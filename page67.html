Joanna Gaines Shares a Sweet Moment at Her Family’s Waco Farmhouse as Her Youngest Helps Celebrate the Holiday Season a ‘Little Early’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Joanna Gaines and her youngest child, Crew, are busy decking the halls of the family’s beautiful Waco, TX, farmhouse.

While some people are staunchly against putting up a Christmas tree before Thanksgiving, the Magnolia co-founder has already brought some seasonal joy into her home.

In a Nov. 15 Instagram Post, Joanna, 48, shared a video of her 8-year-old son excitedly turning on the family’s tree—a stunning display of twinkling lights and metallic-hued baubles.

The "Fixer Upper" star acknowledged the potentially premature timing of the decor with a tongue-in-cheek caption, writing, “For those who like celebrating the season a little early…”

The audio the mom of five selected for the clip really drove the message home, as she selected a song called “Christmas in November,” with lyrics that say, “They say it’s a little early, I say it’s right on time.”

This particular corner of the HGTV family’s dwelling was also featured on the cover of the Winter 2026 issue of “Magnolia,” with Joanna donning a festive red gown as she poses in front of a selection of jars of holiday candy.

The tree makes an appearance on the cover, as well, shining alongside the statement-making picture-frame windows and rustic checkerboard flooring that make the Gaines home so recognizable.

Set on 40 acres, the property has plenty of room for a large family to celebrate together. The family purchased the home back in 2012, and at the time, it was a quaint two-bedroom abode. After countless improvements—which most recently included an extensive kitchen renovation—it's become a home Joanna has come to love, despite being unsure of it initially.

Even though it’s only mid-November, the holiday season has been on Joanna’s mind for quite a while already as she plans ahead for all Magnolia has in store this winter. In fact, she kicked things off with a Sept. 9 post, when she first brought out a variety of wreaths and garlands for a holiday photoshoot.

In a Nov. 10 editor’s note in Magnolia Journal, she wrote about her philosophy as she heads into another merry time of year.

“Every season holds a blend of the big and small, the milestones and the everyday, the photo-worth celebrations and the not-so-picture-perfect moments,” she wrote. “There’s something about the holidays that bridges the gap between these.”

She noted that as the years go by, she’s “come to realize” that the most important memories are the ones that might not be captured with a camera, and exist only in memory.

“Maybe there’s no physical evidence they happened at all, except for the fact that you were there—and you felt it,” Gaines continued. “It’s these softer moments that give us life and ground us in presence. The days that held no agenda, the mornings I took slow, the smiles you won’t see in my camera roll because I was simply bearing witness.”

But that’s not to say she’s not planning make a splash when it comes to home decor.

In a chat with USA Today, Joanna revealed that her approach to holiday decorating has gotten more over-the-top as the years go by.

“The older I get, the more free I feel to deck the halls,” she said. “I do think this is the one time of year where you can really go big if you want to—or keep things timeless and simple.”

Although she’s well on the way to turning her family’s corner of Waco into a winter wonderland, Joanna understands the importance of staying true to one’s own desires.

“There’s no right or wrong because the holidays really are so personal and sentimental,” she said. “It’s just a matter of what you love and how you want your home to feel for your family.”

As excited as Joanna seems to be for the most wonderful time of the year, she doesn’t appear to be blowing past any holidays too quickly. She also has big plans for turkey day, an occasion that she looks forward to for its delicious meals.

“Food really is the center of our holidays,” she told USA Today, remarking that she’s been honing her own traditional recipes since she first married husband Chip Gaines back in 2003. “Thanksgiving especially is the day I can taste all year long because our family really does make the same dishes year after year.”

Robert Pattinson’s James Bond Needs a Killer Cast. Here’s Who Should Join Him.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A great 007 movie isn’t just about the man himself. Now we can start to find the next Bond Girl, villain, and more—and really have some fun.

OUR LONG INTERNATIONAL nightmare is finally over: We have a new James Bond. On Monday, Robert Pattinson was announced as 007, beating out contenders that included Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, and Josh O’Connor. Considering Pattinson is currently double dipping as Bruce Wayne—and the fact that Bond is historically a star-making role, rather than a role for an established star—his casting won’t be everyone’s cup of tea. (My two cents: he would’ve been a god-tier Bond villain.)

Still, it’s hard to deny that Pattinson is going to crush the role. From buzzy indies like The Lighthouse and High Life to blockbusters like Mickey 17 and The Odyssey, he’s long been on a heater. (Nobody using Edward Cullen as a punchline against him is further proof of that.) For Amazon, whose stewardship of the Bond franchise was always going to be met with scrutiny, there’s no shame in making the safe, sensible choice.

But landing on a new 007 is only the beginning. James Bond isn’t just about the title character: The franchise can also be a career-defining move for actors enlisted to play recurring characters (M) and archetypes (the gimmicky henchmen) that bring the world to life. For Pattinson and director Denis Villeneuve, this new Bond entry needs to be populated with the very best supporting players Jeff Bezos’s money can buy.

With that in mind, here’s our dream casting for the seven most important non-Bond roles in the new film.

The Bond franchise has been around for decades, and the films are often a reflection of both the world around them and the current state of moviemaking. The Timothy Dalton era was gritty and grounded as the Cold War wound down; the Daniel Craig movies were serialized in the age of cinematic universes. For a modern-day Bond, then, we should have a villain that feels made for the 2020s. In other words: cast a sinister nerd—and more specifically, cast Samuel Blenkin.

In biopics and documentaries alike, tech bros are cinema’s latest villain du jour. Between the untold economic and environmental damage they've caused in the real world, nobody is going to feel bad about these guys being painted in an unflattering light. In that respect, Blenkin would be a fun casting. If Tenet was a great test run for Pattinson as 007, then Alien: Earth, in which Blenkin plays an impetuous tech bro with the gall to mess around with Xenomorphs (!!!), is proof that he could bring the same (respectfully) punchable energy to Bond. Say what you will about Zack Snyder’s DC movies, but picking the actor who played Mark Zuckerberg to be his Lex Luthor was ahead of the curve. Bond would be wise to meet the moment.

Runner-up: Jensen Huang (with the leather jacket, obviously).

The next M has big shoes to fill. Dame Judi Dench played the character across eight films and two separate Bonds over a period of 20 years, and Ralph Fiennes then made the role his own (as he always does), taking over the mantle for Daniel Craig’s final three films.

But sometimes, the obvious pick is the right one. Emma Thompson is acting royalty with a commanding air of authority, which is exactly what M calls for. I can just as easily picture her chastising Bond for a reckless bit of espionage as I can see her delivering some characteristically bone-dry British wit. Imagine how fucking electric a Pattinson-Thompson tête-à-tête would be.

Runner-up: Kristin Scott Thomas or Fiona Shaw.

We haven’t had an American Bond Girl since Halle Berry in Die Another Day, a film that, unfortunately, doesn’t hold up on rewatch on account of [gestures at Pierce Brosnan surfing a CGI tsunami]. Let’s have Bond dip back stateside with one of Hollywood’s most exciting young stars—and one whose name already sounds like that of a Bond Girl—in Chase Infiniti. Her resume is small but impressive: the emotional center of Paul Thomas Anderson’s Best Picture-winning One Battle After Another, a supporting role in Apple TV’s Presumed Innocent, and the lead of the Handmaid’s Tale spinoff The Testaments. And if Infiniti’s on-screen work isn’t enough to move the needle, she’s rocked enough red carpet looks to prove she knows how to make an eye-popping entrance. For a Bond Girl, that’s half the battle.

Runner-up: Margaret Qualley, mostly because she basically auditioned to be a Bond Girl at the 97th Academy Awards’ Bond tribute.

Aimee Lou Wood’s vibe as a performer is, in a word, cute. In her most notable performances—The White Lotus and Sex Education chief among them—there’s a natural warmth and mischievousness that would make her a fun scene partner for Pattinson’s Bond. Moneypenny is traditionally the one person at MI6 who can get flirty with Bond without worrying about getting a lecture from M after the fact. Wood has the charm and comic timing to make that dynamic sing.

Runner-up if Moneypenny becomes Irish: Nicola Coughlan.

Pattinson, Anne Hathaway, and (quite deservedly) Samantha Morton have been receiving much of the praise for their work in The Odyssey, but Himesh Patel’s beleaguered Eurylochus might be the film’s unsung hero. Now, he can pivot to playing the Bond franchise’s underappreciated MVP in Q: a perpetually put-upon tech genius who has to watch a reckless womanizer repeatedly destroy million-dollar prototypes. Patel has the dry, exasperated delivery down pat, and he’d be a perfect straight man for Pattinson’s particular brand of chaos (and it would mark a more-than-welcome Tenet reunion). Perhaps Q will belatedly give Patel the accolades he deserved for The Odyssey. (Also known as the Rhea Seehorn Principle.)

Runner-up: Matt Berry in character as Laszlo Cravensworth.

This might be recency bias, because I just watched Kelvin Harrison Jr. in The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping (say that three times fast). Harrison Jr. is Beetee Latier in the prequel, who was previously played by none other than … Jeffrey Wright. Credit where it’s due: Harrison Jr. does a mean Jeffrey Wright impression in the film.

However, we don’t need to see Harrison Jr. reheat his Wright impression in the new Bond. What I’ve admired in everything I’ve seen him in is how chameleonic he can be—to the extent that you could conceivably cast him in just about any role and he’d find a way to make it his own. (There’s no better evidence that he could play a killer spy than his performance in the psychological thriller Luce, where his character is eerily hard to get a read on.) I want a fresh take on Leiter, and the one thing we can say for Harrison Jr.: he’ll deliver on that promise.

Runner-up: John David Washington for peak Tenet vibes.

Look, I get it: Do we really need Tormund Giantsbane 2.0 in a Bond movie? But Hivju doesn’t deserve to be defined by that role for the rest of his career. He’s got the kind of outsized screen presence that demands an at-bat as a Bond henchman. (You can totally picture him getting excited by Bond drawing blood during a fight.) Sure, Villeneuve isn’t exactly known for humor in his projects, but Bond films have a long tradition of colorful henchmen, and Hivju could dial up the menace without taking himself too seriously. Give him a gimmick, a tweed suit, and no more than four lines of dialogue, and I promise that Hivju will steal every scene he’s in.

Runner-up: Olivier Richters for that Reacher season 3 fight scene alone.

New hypothesis explains why the right side of the human brain controls the left side of the body

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The brain's crossed wiring runs through every vertebrate group, and two neuroscientists trace it to the eyes of the first fishes.

A stroke on the right side of the brain weakens the left arm and leg. The brain is cross-wired that way, and for more than a century scientists have assumed there was an evolutionary reason for it.

Two neuroscientists have traced the crossing through every major group of living vertebrates. They now propose that evolution may never have selected it for the jobs it does.

On their reading, the crossed wiring may simply be inherited from the way the first fishes’ eyes were wired to their brains, some 500 million years ago.

Martin Wessendorf, an associate professor emeritus of neuroscience at the University of Minnesota, and Daniel Wang, a researcher in the University’s Department of Neuroscience, wrote the review.

It is a hypothesis built from more than a century of published anatomy rather than a new experiment. However, the authors wrote that it may be testable in fossils.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wang said the question reaches beyond his own field.

“This’ll be relevant to anyone who wants to know more about why brain injury on one side of the head often leads to loss of function on the opposite side of the body,” he said.

The crossing begins at the eye. The lens flips the image, so whatever sits to your right lands on the left side of each retina. The nerve fibers carrying the right half of the view then sort themselves at a junction behind the eyes and run to the left side of the brain.

One place that information goes is a structure in the midbrain called the optic tectum. It holds a map of the opposite half of visual space, and it fires fast turning and escape movements.

Every vertebrate examined appears to have one, lampreys and hagfish included, along with the crossing junction behind the eyes.

Living jawless fish carry both, so the authors conclude that the last common ancestor of all vertebrates probably did too. That puts the first crossed wiring in the Cambrian period, among jawless fishes that lived roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

Anatomists call crossed wiring contralateral organization, and its age is what struck Wang.

“What surprised me the most was likely just how old the contralateral organization of the optic tectum is for vertebrates – spanning even to the Cambrian,” he told Earth.com.

Body senses crossed next. Two pathways carry touch, pain, temperature and body position from the spinal cord up to the brain, and both cross the midline in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

The one for light touch and body position is missing in fishes, so the authors trace it to the last common ancestor of the four-limbed animals.

Movement pathways cross too. One runs from the optic tectum down to the spinal cord and turns the body toward whatever just appeared at the edge of vision.

Lampreys and bony fish appear to have no such pathway. Sharks have one that mostly stays on its own side, and rays and lungfish have a crossed one.

Reptiles appear to have lost that pathway. A second crossed pathway, from a cluster of midbrain cells called the red nucleus, is thought to help control limbs.

True mammals appeared about 165 million years ago. By then, those crossed senses and movement pathways had already existed for almost 200 million years.

The pathway that lets you move one finger at a time is the special case. It runs from the cerebral cortex, the six-layered outer sheet found only in mammals, down to the spinal cord, and it crosses in the lower brainstem.

In most mammals, it ends where touch signals enter the cord and adjusts those signals. Few mammals besides primates, raccoons and kinkajous have a version that reaches the nerve cells that drive muscles.

So the circuit that gives humans fine control of their hands appears to be a sensory circuit put to a new use. Wang said that was the finding that surprised his co-author most.

“This demonstrates that circuitry that’s involved in one function can, with just a change or two, become a function that’s entirely different.”

The crossing isn’t universal in this pathway. In goats and elephants, it runs down both sides of the spinal cord, and in hedgehogs and moles it mostly stays on its own side. Even in humans, an estimated 10 to 25 percent of its fibers don’t cross.

Wang pointed to the goats and elephants, “so there’s a decent amount of diversity there,” he said. The authors read that variety as a sign that natural selection wasn’t strongly favoring the crossing in this pathway.

Isaac Newton proposed in 1704 that the junction behind the eyes sorts nerve fibers by visual field.

Ramón y Cajal, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on the structure of the nervous system, went further in an 1898 paper. Once the visual map crossed, he proposed, it would pay for the maps of hearing, touch and movement to match it.

In computer models published in 2014, an animal controlled by the opposite side of its brain was likelier to survive a blow to one side of the head or body.

At least two other research groups have blamed a twist in the body plan, dating to the split between vertebrates and their invertebrate ancestors. That argument goes back to an 1822 dissection of a crayfish.

The authors see a problem with the twist. The visual system was probably crossed in the first vertebrates, but the pathways for touch, limb control and finger control didn’t exist yet.

So the authors doubt that a single ancient twist can explain them.

The authors’ own answer starts with the optic tectum. Its map of the opposite half of visual space sits on top. Beneath it, in register, sit maps of touch and hearing from the same half, and at the bottom sit the cells that fire movements.

Wessendorf and Wang propose that this stack biased the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later toward crossing.

When the cortex evolved, most of the pathways carrying signals into it were already crossed. A cortex wired to its own side would have needed extra crossings to collect vision, touch and hearing from the far side, or a rewiring of every older circuit.

The authors argue that either change would have carried a “wiring cost,” leaving mammals that were less energy-efficient, less intelligent, or both.

Not everything crosses. Smell and taste mostly stay on the same side, and the authors note that neither sense maps space the way vision, hearing and touch do.

The cerebellum, which coordinates movement, works the same side of the body it sits on.

“We propose that contralateral organization didn’t necessarily evolve as an adaptation, but because evolution is an engineer that builds with the foundation and materials it has at hand,” Wang and Wessendorf wrote.

The authors list what would test the idea. Their first prediction is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates had both the crossing junction and an optic tectum.

Cambrian fossils of jawless fish exist, and a fossilized brain cast from one could show whether both were there.

Wang said the finding that would complicate the idea is an early fossil vertebrate whose optic tectum handled vision on the same side rather than the opposite one.

The authors also predict that crossed pathways from the forebrain to the spinal cord will prove widespread in birds. Unfortunately, relatively few bird species have been examined.

Crocodilians appear not to have been checked, and the authors predict they have one too.

Hearing is the one sense the authors treat with caution. It appears to have evolved partly on its own in different lines.

What remains unknown is whether any Cambrian fish skull preserves enough of its brain to show the crossing.

Until one does, the idea rests on living anatomy. The next checks are in the fossil record, and in the birds and crocodilians whose spinal pathways have yet to be mapped.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Neuroanatomy.

DJI spinoff Amflow has spent the last couple of years shaking up the electric mountain bike (eMTB) market with ridiculously high-torque, lightweight models. Today, it’s bringing that same overengineered energy to pavement with the UE Series, its first urban commuter line.

The series consists of two models: the UE Carbon and UE Carbon Pro. Both pack 125Nm of torque, 1,100W of peak power, and an 8x max assist ratio capable of climbing 30 percent grades — all while operating quietly under 45dBA (quieter than a household refrigerator). A swappable 600Wh battery provides up to 132km (about 82 miles) of range and fast-charges from zero to 100 percent in 2 hours and 23 minutes.

While most city e-bikes deliver their power modestly, Amflow is strapping a mountain-bike-grade motor onto a 20.9 kg (46 pounds) upright step-through frame — and pricing it slightly below its flagship trail bikes. The UE Carbon starts at €3,099 (about $3,500 USD), while the Pro will sell for €3,499 ($3,900 USD).

But this isn’t just another commuter bike. With its proprietary Avinox drivetrain, DJI has been ahead of the curve in blending consumer gadget UX — features like wireless phone charging, Apple Watch proximity unlock, Find My integration, and camera controls — with serious hardware specs. That’s one of the reasons why The Verge’s own Thomas Ricker has been so bullish on the brand for the past few years.

With 125Nm of max torque and 1,100W of peak power, the Avinox M2 in the UE Series is slightly more dialed back from Amflow’s 150Nm flagship eMTBs. That said, 125Nm is still massive overkill for flat city streets — but it won’t hurt to have it when encountering any grades over 30 percent. A new Auto Boost mode automatically dumps extra power when starting at a traffic light or hitting a steep hill. And traction control will help prevent any spin-outs on wet asphalt.

The 600Wh removable battery is good for up 82 miles in Eco mode — and likely less than that while relying on higher levels of assist. It’s worth noting that the 1,100W peak power and 8x assist ratios will likely be software-capped depending on where you live and what regulations allow.

As a brand within the larger DJI ecosystem, Amflow loves to integrate a lot of tech into its bikes. A 1.4 inch touchscreen is integrated into the handlebars alongside a 15W wireless phone charger — plus a 20W USB-C charger, if you prefer. The cockpit also features plenty of Apple tie-ins, like the aforementioned Find My tracker and Apple Watch proximity unlocking. The UE can also pair with a heart rate monitor, allowing riders to set a target heart rate zone that will dictate how much pedal assist the bike provides. And, of course, there’s direct recording controls for DJI Osmo cameras, in case you like to film your rides.

Additional security is provided by a built-in 10mm hardened steel wheel lock keyed to match the removable battery. And if the bike is paired with “Lost Mode” in the Amflow app, the user can remotely brick the drive system if the bike ever gets stolen.

There’s plenty of commuter-friendly touches, like a 65mm suspension fork, suspension dropper seatpost, and heavy-duty racks rated for 20kg (44 pounds) on the front and 27kg (60 pounds) on the rear.

The bikes are on sale globally starting today via Amflow’s website and retail partners. The company says a smaller XS frame size is coming in 2027 for shorter riders.
