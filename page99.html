Robert Pattinson cast as next James Bond

📅 2026-11-16  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert Pattinson has been cast as the next James Bond in the upcoming film from director Denis Villeneuve. It will be the 26th James Bond film and the first since Amazon MGM took over the iconic franchise.

Pattinson will be the seventh actor to play the role. Monday's announcement — which came in part via an Instagram post — ends a long period of speculation about who would take over as "007" after Daniel Craig.

"James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honour of a lifetime," Pattinson said in a statement. "I couldn't be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team."

Pattinson has appeared in several big-ticket films this year, including Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" and psychological thriller "Primetime," and he'll soon appear in "Dune: Part Three," also directed by Villeneuve. Pattinson, who rose to fame with the Twilight movie series, is also in production for Matt Reeves' "The Batman Part II," a follow-up to the 2022 film based on the DC comic.

The upcoming Bond film will have a script by Steven Knight and will be produced by Amy Pascal, known for her work on the Spider-Man franchise, and David Heyman, who produced all eight Harry Potter films.

"We were surrounded by extraordinary talent, but there can only be one 007. I am overjoyed to welcome Robert Pattinson as our new James Bond. He has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new. I cannot wait to begin," Villeneuve said.

In 2022, Amazon bought MGM Studios, which had long produced the Bond movies, for roughly $8.45 billion. Amazon MGM then acquired creative control of the James Bond intellectual property rights in 2025, when it closed a deal with longtime producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson.

The film is still untitled, and Amazon MGM hasn't announced a projected release date.

Is there anything I can do to get her to come out?â

At her age, sweet Mitzi has certainly earned the right to enjoy her golden years at her own pace, but I can imagine it is difficult to see her withdraw from the rest of the house, especially when you remember how she used to move around. However, I would not necessarily interpret her reluctance to leave the bedroom as something we need to correct. Losing her hearing can be quite disorienting. Imagine walking into a room where you can no longer hear footsteps, familiar voices, or the little sounds that help you understand what is happening around you. For Mitzi, the bedroom may now feel like her safest little world, where everything is familiar and predictable.

I actually think you have done a wonderful job adapting her environment by bringing her food, water, and litter box closer. Rather than encouraging her to leave right away, I would focus on helping her feel confident enough to explore if she wishes. You could start by sitting with her near the bedroom doorway, offering a favorite treat or a little affection. Gradually place treats a short distance beyond the door, allowing her to decide whether she wants to investigate.

Take care to only approach her within her visual field, avoid startling her while she sleeps, and keep pathways clear and nonslip. Think of it as extending her new world one comfortable step at a time, rather than asking her to return to her old routine.

There is one thing I would not overlook, though. Mitzi has both chronic kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, and at her age, reduced mobility or withdrawal can also be associated with arthritis, high blood pressure, visual changes, or inadequate control of her thyroid disease. I would recommend a senior cat veterinary checkup that includes blood pressure measurement, assessment for arthritis, and follow-up kidney and thyroid testing. Sometimes what looks like a behavioral change is actually a senior cat quietly telling us that they are in pain and something does not feel quite right.

Most importantly, please do not feel that you are encouraging a bad habit by keeping her essentials in the bedroom. You are adapting her home to meet her changing needs, and that is a beautiful expression of your love and care for her. If the bedroom is where Mitzi feels safe, eats well, drinks, uses her litter box, and enjoys your company, that can be a perfectly good quality of life.

At this stage, happiness is not measured by how much of the house she explores, but by how comfortable and secure she feels in the little world she chooses.

'We've Heard You!' - Star Wars Galactic Racer Is Officially Adding Split-Screen Multiplayer

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An incredible addition to an incredible game

Star Wars Galactic Racer is my favourite game of the year so far. It's an incredible racing title that even surpasses the original Star Wars Episode 1 Racer in my view, and I've had so much fun with the campaign and online multiplayer.

There's just one downside that everyone's been pointing out since it was revealed, and that's the absence of local split-screen multiplayer. With that in mind, the development team have found a way to implement it in a new update.

The update isn't available yet, but will be coming to the game in early 2027. Alongside it will be a new mode called Free Play, which is where you'll be able to freely choose your vehicle, character, planet and race track.

"We’ve heard you! Local split-screen multiplayer racing is coming in early 2027. A dedicated team at Fuse Games has been working hard to bring a brand new mode, Free Play, to STAR WARS: Galactic Racer. This new mode will allow you to choose your vehicle, character, planet, and race track to race alone or with up to 3 other players in local split-screen action."

It seems Free Play is the only place where you'll be able to access local split-screen multiplayer in Star Wars Galactic Racer, but that's no surprise considering how solo-focused the main campaign is. They'd literally have to rewrite massive portions of the campaign if they were going to implement split-screen into it.

Anyway, this is just an amazing bonus on top of an already amazing game, and I encourage you to check it out if you enjoy a title like Burnout 3: Takedown - it's fast-paced and frantic, has the same kind of takedown system that you'll remember from that game, and the roguelite campaign is really well designed (online is great fun as well).

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

Tesla confirms €3B Cybercab factory at Giga Berlin for up to 1M a year

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla is going to build the Cybercab in Germany. The company confirmed today a first €3 billion investment in a new production building at Giga Berlin designed for up to 1 million Cybercabs a year, with production planned for 2029.

That’s a lot of robotaxis for a continent where Tesla can’t operate a single one. Under current EU rules, a driverless vehicle model is capped at 1,500 registrations a year.

Plant manager André Thierig announced the decision on X after Tesla told employees on Monday:

If you ever wondered, what we would be doing at Giga Berlin with all the land that we have… We are expanding! Cybercab production is coming to Giga Berlin! Today we announced a first investment of around € 3b in a new manufacturing building with an anticpated start of… pic.twitter.com/vpcDdxoqlW

Per Tesla’s statement, the €3 billion pays for the new production hall north of the existing plant, plus ancillary buildings and a second wastewater treatment plant. Tesla plans to file the building application before the end of the year, break ground in the first half of 2027, and start production in 2029. The company says it will be “the technologically most advanced Tesla factory in the world.”

It leaked a few hours earlier. Steven Scholtysek, an automation engineer at the plant, posted that Elon Musk personally signed off on the project last week and that the new hall will be bigger than the original factory.

The building isn’t new. Tesla laid out a plan to take Giga Berlin to 1 million vehicles a year in its 2023 permit filings and has been clearing land for it since. What’s new is the product. Until today, Tesla had only said the second hall wouldn’t be another Model Y factory.

Giga Berlin still builds only the Model Y. Tesla has been hiring again at Giga Berlin this year as European demand came back, with a goal of 7,500 cars a week. Even at that pace, the plant would fall short of the 500,000 units a year Tesla promised when it opened in 2022.

Tesla is also adding 4680 battery cell production at the site, with 18 GWh of annual capacity planned.

So the new hall alone is rated for twice what the entire existing plant was supposed to build. And it comes after Musk tied the Berlin expansion to the plant’s works council election in March, warning workers against IG Metall’s influence.

The EU has a type-approval path for fully driverless vehicles, but it’s a “small series” scheme limited to 1,500 registrations per vehicle type per year across the whole EU. In March, the Commission lifted that cap for automated valet parking. Robotaxis are still stuck in the small-series track.

Tesla also has no approval anywhere in Europe for its software to drive without supervision. The Dutch RDW approved the supervised version, now called Tesla Assisted Driving, and nine other countries recognize that approval. It still requires a driver paying attention. The Cybercab has no steering wheel.

Rules can move in three years. The Commission has said it wants large-series approval for other driverless uses, and a UN regulation for automated driving systems is in the works. But as of today, Tesla is committing €3 billion to a vehicle it can’t sell in volume in Europe.

Tesla could export from Berlin, of course. But 1 million Cybercabs a year on top of Giga Texas implies a robotaxi business many times bigger than anything on the road today. Texas, where Cybercab production started in April, had 463 Cybercabs registered as of late October. And Tesla just opened personal Cybercab orders at $29,990 plus a mandatory $299 monthly subscription, usable only inside its Robotaxi geofences.

Tesla can’t even launch its driver-assist system in Europe under its own name. What it’s rolling out there isn’t “Full Self-Driving.” It’s “Tesla Assisted Driving,” a level 2 system that needs a driver watching the road at all times, and it took a long fight with regulators to get it approved in 10 countries.

The Cybercab is a much harder sell. No steering wheel, no pedals, so it has to be certified as a level 4 vehicle with nobody inside to take over. That’s a completely different approval from anything Tesla has gotten in Europe. As of this spring, the EU said no automaker had even used its existing driverless approval path.

And the US program doesn’t inspire confidence. Robotaxi is still confined to geofences, still off highways, and it keeps crashing: Tesla had reported 22 incidents to NHTSA by July, and our analysis of the data earlier this year put it at one crash every 57,000 miles, roughly 9 times worse than the average human driver, with a safety monitor on board. The only reason those crashes don’t get more attention is that they happen at low speed. Of course they do. Tesla keeps the cars on slow city streets. Put the same system on the autobahn and we’d expect more crashes, and worse ones.

This is Musk’s modus operandi for the last few years: bet on autonomy before it’s ready. Tesla removed the Cybercab’s steering wheel assuming the software would be solved by the time it mattered. It wasn’t, and now Tesla is putting €3 billion into a German factory on the same assumption.

Good for the workers in Grünheide, and maybe the hall can build something else if this doesn’t work out. But spending €3 billion to build up to 1 million steering-wheel-less cars a year for a market where Tesla can’t legally run a single one, based on a program that hasn’t left the geofence in Texas, is extremely risky.

Will there be one unsupervised Cybercab carrying paying passengers in Europe when the Grünheide line starts in 2029? We wouldn’t bet on it.
