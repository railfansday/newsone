You Can Get This Micro-E-Reader for Under $60 Before Black Friday

Black Friday is usually a great time to grab a new e-reader, but the ongoing RAM crisis has made that a question mark this year: Amazon recently raised the prices of all its Kindles by around $50 and refreshed the entire lineup, which means your odds of grabbing one at a big discount are low. But you aren't totally out of luck: If you haven't noticed, micro-e-readers—I'm talking credit card-sized, with screens that measure around four inches—are all the rage in 2026, and right now, you can pick up one of them for less than $60.

If you too can see the appeal of carrying all of your books with you on a device so small you'll forget it's even in your pocket, it's a great time to jump in: The recently released Xteink X4 Classic is currently 34% off in an early Black Friday sale, but you'll need to act quickly: The discount will soon be gone.

Xteink is a Chinese startup that shook up the e-reading world last year with the launch of the X4, a super bare-bones device that made up for its lack of features and usability with a cheap sub-$70 price point. Since then, a huge online community of fans has formed, sharing tips on Reddit and even coming together to crowdsource an alternate operating system that fixed a lot of early problems. Xteink quickly followed up the X4 with a slate of other devices, including the X3 and the X4 Pro, which adds a front light and touchscreen (at the cost of screen clarity and two physical buttons).

The X4 Classic was released in September, improving on the original X4 with quality-of-life upgrades like a larger battery, relocated page-turn buttons, a real-time clock, and a tilt-to-turn-page feature. It's also 6g lighter and a millimeter thinner than the X4, though there is a trade-off involved there: It drops the USB-C charging port in favor of magnetic pogo pins that require a separate adapter (included).

These upgrades also increased the cost: The X4 Classic typically retails for $89, $20 more than the discontinued X4 and only $10 less than the X4 Pro. But right now, the X4 Classic is $59, or around 34% off, and its lowest price ever. It's a good option for those who prioritize crisp, readable text above the conveniences of a built-in light or touchscreen, as those add extra layers between the inner screen and outer glass, and provided you understand what you're getting into: These devices support only e-books without Digital Rights Management (DRM), which means you can't use it to read your existing library of Kindle books. On the plus side, there's no longer a need to worry about flashing new firmware if you don't want to, as the native version has been totally overhauled and is now one of your best options.

General Motors is searching for outside candidates to succeed Mary Barra

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The automaker is working with an executive search firm and is weighing internal candidates, including its CFO and product chief

General Motors $GM has brought in an executive recruiting firm and is scouting for external successors to CEO Mary Barra, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Internal candidates are still being considered, among them Chief Financial Officer Paul Jacobson, Chief Product Officer Sterling Anderson and Mark Reuss, who has spent 40 years at GM and serves as Barra’s second in command, according to The Wall Street Journal. GM declined to comment.

GM stock rose nearly 1.5% by Tuesday afternoon.

Barra became GM's chief executive in 2014 and holds the distinction of being the first female leader of a global automaker, according to The Wall Street Journal. She is 64, according to The Detroit News. She has spent months preparing to pass the company to a successor, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Pressed in September on whether she might step aside, Barra said she still feels full of energy. "I still feel like I can contribute and continue to build the foundation," she said, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Under Barra, GM has shrunk in size yet grown more lucrative, with its U.S. truck business serving as the foundation, according to The Wall Street Journal. The company now sells several million vehicles fewer each year across the globe than it did 10 years earlier, but its cash generation has hit an all-time high.

Not every bet has paid off. GM lost billions on its Cruise robotaxi division before shutting it down, and it has also scaled back its loftiest electric-vehicle ambitions, according to The Wall Street Journal. The company recorded $7.6 billion of write-offs on EV spending that went unused, spread across 2025 and earlier this year, according to The Detroit News.

Barra chose Anderson to turn around GM's work on self-driving technology, according to The Wall Street Journal. The company now plans to offer highway driving that lets motorists take both their hands and eyes off the road in personally owned vehicles by 2028.

GM forecast last month that its earnings before interest and taxes would land between $14.5 billion and $15.5 billion for the full year.

Lucky Strike Entertainment CEO is proposing a take-private deal at about a 30% premium

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Thomas F. Shannon offered $4.35 a share for all publicly held Class A stock, subject to board and special committee approval

Lucky Strike Entertainment CEO Thomas F. Shannon proposed taking the company private in a letter to its board, offering $4.35 a share for all publicly held Class A stock.

The price is an approximately 30% premium to Monday's closing price, Shannon said in the letter, which is dated Monday. Lucky Strike stock was up about 22% on Tuesday, and at its session high it had gained 35%, reaching $4.48.

Shannon said the proposal would need board approval and agreement on definitive transaction documents. He said he would not move forward unless a special committee of independent and disinterested directors approves it. The documents would also have to include a non-waivable condition requiring approval from holders of a majority of the Class A stock not owned by him or his affiliates.

Shannon said he would not need due diligence and that closing would not be subject to a financing contingency. He said he expects to consider letting preferred investors, and perhaps others who want to invest in a private business, take part in the resulting entity.

The letter describes the offer as an expression of interest that may be withdrawn or modified. No legal obligation would arise until the parties sign definitive documents. If the special committee or a majority of public holders rejects the deal, Shannon said, he intends to remain a long-term shareholder.

Shannon said the stock has generally traded down since an all-time high in 2023 and now trades at a fraction of the price at the company's 2021 de-SPAC listing. He attributed the decline mainly to "market conditions affecting current investors' attitude about site-based businesses generally." He said that has left the stock with very limited institutional and analyst support and a very limited public float.

Shannon said going private would let public shareholders receive a premium and would save the company the cost and distraction of staying public. Jones Day is advising him, and he said the special committee would retain its own legal and financial advisers.

Lucky Strike halted its dividend this month under a plan to pay down debt, and the stock fell to an all-time low as a result, according to Bloomberg. Its loan due in 2032 was put at roughly 80 cents on the dollar, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. That is up from a price under 75 cents at the end of October, which Bloomberg said was far beneath the level that usually marks distress.

S&P Global $SPGI Ratings downgraded the Virginia-based bowling and water park operator in September, moving its grade from B to B-, according to Bloomberg.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. I was afraid of not having enough or of being unable to find another job, and I just had to face that fear.

The AT forced me to confront my own limiting beliefs around money. In the years since that first thru-hike, I’ve gotten a little more relaxed about spending money on things I know I can afford without feeling guilty about it. I haven’t abandoned my frugal tendencies, but they don’t need to dictate every decision I make. I credit the trail for that growth.

“I wonder if I can get another 200 miles out of these shoes before I replace them. I wonder if I should get on a cheaper insurance plan next year. Do I have enough money to buy name-brand peanut butter in the next town? Should I get a different credit card? Should I go stoveless to save on fuel? “

I’ve written a few articles over the years about my peculiar hiking-related neuroses. As always, I want to clarify in the midst of all this doom and gloom that thru-hiking was and is one of the great joys of my life, despite my assorted pathologies and the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

Every thru-hiker will face struggles both external and internal; learning to deal with and overcome them is part of the experience, and this is just one more example. As always, do everything you can to prepare for your thru-hike, accept that you can’t plan for everything, and then enjoy the journey, hardships and all.

Ripple’s RLUSD Supply Hits Record $2.7 Billion, With Ethereum Holding Majority Over XRP Ledger

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

RLUSD supply has climbed to $2.7 billion, indicating rising on-chain demand for its stablecoin, which operates mostly on Ethereum, according to data.

Ripple’s RLUSD stablecoin has achieved another milestone. Its total market supply has reached $2.7 billion, making a new high level in circulation, according to data reported on the X platform. RLUSD supply climbed to the $2.7 billion record on Sunday, Nov. 15, a 57% rise from the $1.72 billion mark seen on Sept. 14, data revealed.

The market supply of the Ripple-backed stablecoin has surged to a new ATH, exceeding the previous $1.72 billion milestone set in September. According to the data, RLUSD’s circulating supply currently stands at $2.7 billion, up from the $1.2 billion level reached in January. This rise signals an expansion of RLUSD’s liquidity across the wider blockchain ecosystem.

The supply expansion indicates rising demand for RLUSD as global customers gradually embrace the stablecoin for payments and DeFi applications. This surge positions RLUSD as a ‘digital dollar’ that allows users to execute cross-border payments and on-chain settlements.

The growth is pushing RLUSD higher into the stablecoin market. Now, the asset ranks as the 7th-largest stablecoin by market supply. The more supply means deeper liquidity across the public blockchain ecosystem, supporting cross-border payments and on-chain utility.

The Ripple-focused institutional stablecoin has reached another milestone, increasing its market supply to $2.7 billion. Interestingly, a large amount of this freshly issued liquidity operates on external public blockchains, not within the XRP Ledger network.

Metrics show that a large share of the new RLUSD supply sits on Ethereum, with Ethereum holding more than those in the XRP Ledger. This indicates rising use of Ethereum as a liquidity and settlement network for the stablecoin, while RLUSD continues to expand across protocols.

On-chain data indicates that out of the $2.7 billion in RLUSD circulation, about $1.73 billion runs on Ethereum. The XRP Ledger holds around $855 million, representing about 30% of the circulation.

Over time, Ethereum’s share of the supply has risen to about 60%. This shift is because Ethereum offers a deeper DeFi market, including lending and more. XRP Ledger is closer to activating its native lending infrastructure.

New hypothesis explains why the right side of the human brain controls the left side of the body

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The brain's crossed wiring runs through every vertebrate group, and two neuroscientists trace it to the eyes of the first fishes.

A stroke on the right side of the brain weakens the left arm and leg. The brain is cross-wired that way, and for more than a century scientists have assumed there was an evolutionary reason for it.

Two neuroscientists have traced the crossing through every major group of living vertebrates. They now propose that evolution may never have selected it for the jobs it does.

On their reading, the crossed wiring may simply be inherited from the way the first fishes’ eyes were wired to their brains, some 500 million years ago.

Martin Wessendorf, an associate professor emeritus of neuroscience at the University of Minnesota, and Daniel Wang, a researcher in the University’s Department of Neuroscience, wrote the review.

It is a hypothesis built from more than a century of published anatomy rather than a new experiment. However, the authors wrote that it may be testable in fossils.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wang said the question reaches beyond his own field.

“This’ll be relevant to anyone who wants to know more about why brain injury on one side of the head often leads to loss of function on the opposite side of the body,” he said.

The crossing begins at the eye. The lens flips the image, so whatever sits to your right lands on the left side of each retina. The nerve fibers carrying the right half of the view then sort themselves at a junction behind the eyes and run to the left side of the brain.

One place that information goes is a structure in the midbrain called the optic tectum. It holds a map of the opposite half of visual space, and it fires fast turning and escape movements.

Every vertebrate examined appears to have one, lampreys and hagfish included, along with the crossing junction behind the eyes.

Living jawless fish carry both, so the authors conclude that the last common ancestor of all vertebrates probably did too. That puts the first crossed wiring in the Cambrian period, among jawless fishes that lived roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

Anatomists call crossed wiring contralateral organization, and its age is what struck Wang.

“What surprised me the most was likely just how old the contralateral organization of the optic tectum is for vertebrates – spanning even to the Cambrian,” he told Earth.com.

Body senses crossed next. Two pathways carry touch, pain, temperature and body position from the spinal cord up to the brain, and both cross the midline in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

The one for light touch and body position is missing in fishes, so the authors trace it to the last common ancestor of the four-limbed animals.

Movement pathways cross too. One runs from the optic tectum down to the spinal cord and turns the body toward whatever just appeared at the edge of vision.

Lampreys and bony fish appear to have no such pathway. Sharks have one that mostly stays on its own side, and rays and lungfish have a crossed one.

Reptiles appear to have lost that pathway. A second crossed pathway, from a cluster of midbrain cells called the red nucleus, is thought to help control limbs.

True mammals appeared about 165 million years ago. By then, those crossed senses and movement pathways had already existed for almost 200 million years.

The pathway that lets you move one finger at a time is the special case. It runs from the cerebral cortex, the six-layered outer sheet found only in mammals, down to the spinal cord, and it crosses in the lower brainstem.

In most mammals, it ends where touch signals enter the cord and adjusts those signals. Few mammals besides primates, raccoons and kinkajous have a version that reaches the nerve cells that drive muscles.

So the circuit that gives humans fine control of their hands appears to be a sensory circuit put to a new use. Wang said that was the finding that surprised his co-author most.

“This demonstrates that circuitry that’s involved in one function can, with just a change or two, become a function that’s entirely different.”

The crossing isn’t universal in this pathway. In goats and elephants, it runs down both sides of the spinal cord, and in hedgehogs and moles it mostly stays on its own side. Even in humans, an estimated 10 to 25 percent of its fibers don’t cross.

Wang pointed to the goats and elephants, “so there’s a decent amount of diversity there,” he said. The authors read that variety as a sign that natural selection wasn’t strongly favoring the crossing in this pathway.

Isaac Newton proposed in 1704 that the junction behind the eyes sorts nerve fibers by visual field.

Ramón y Cajal, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on the structure of the nervous system, went further in an 1898 paper. Once the visual map crossed, he proposed, it would pay for the maps of hearing, touch and movement to match it.

In computer models published in 2014, an animal controlled by the opposite side of its brain was likelier to survive a blow to one side of the head or body.

At least two other research groups have blamed a twist in the body plan, dating to the split between vertebrates and their invertebrate ancestors. That argument goes back to an 1822 dissection of a crayfish.

The authors see a problem with the twist. The visual system was probably crossed in the first vertebrates, but the pathways for touch, limb control and finger control didn’t exist yet.

So the authors doubt that a single ancient twist can explain them.

The authors’ own answer starts with the optic tectum. Its map of the opposite half of visual space sits on top. Beneath it, in register, sit maps of touch and hearing from the same half, and at the bottom sit the cells that fire movements.

Wessendorf and Wang propose that this stack biased the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later toward crossing.

When the cortex evolved, most of the pathways carrying signals into it were already crossed. A cortex wired to its own side would have needed extra crossings to collect vision, touch and hearing from the far side, or a rewiring of every older circuit.

The authors argue that either change would have carried a “wiring cost,” leaving mammals that were less energy-efficient, less intelligent, or both.

Not everything crosses. Smell and taste mostly stay on the same side, and the authors note that neither sense maps space the way vision, hearing and touch do.

The cerebellum, which coordinates movement, works the same side of the body it sits on.

“We propose that contralateral organization didn’t necessarily evolve as an adaptation, but because evolution is an engineer that builds with the foundation and materials it has at hand,” Wang and Wessendorf wrote.

The authors list what would test the idea. Their first prediction is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates had both the crossing junction and an optic tectum.

Cambrian fossils of jawless fish exist, and a fossilized brain cast from one could show whether both were there.

Wang said the finding that would complicate the idea is an early fossil vertebrate whose optic tectum handled vision on the same side rather than the opposite one.

The authors also predict that crossed pathways from the forebrain to the spinal cord will prove widespread in birds. Unfortunately, relatively few bird species have been examined.

Crocodilians appear not to have been checked, and the authors predict they have one too.

Hearing is the one sense the authors treat with caution. It appears to have evolved partly on its own in different lines.

What remains unknown is whether any Cambrian fish skull preserves enough of its brain to show the crossing.

Until one does, the idea rests on living anatomy. The next checks are in the fossil record, and in the birds and crocodilians whose spinal pathways have yet to be mapped.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Neuroanatomy.

Tulum Is Losing Airlines Fast: Why The Hyped New Airport Is Failing

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In late 2023, we saw the opening of the Felipe Carrillo Puerto International Airport (TQO), in Tulum, Mexico. For those not familiar, Tulum is located on the eastern coast of the Yucatan Peninsula, and for years, it was a growing tourist destination.

While it could previously be accessed via Cancun International Airport (CUN), the hope was that by opening a new international airport, visitor numbers would expand massively. Unfortunately what has actually happened is exactly the opposite, as airline after airline has pulled out. What exactly is going on here? I think it makes for an interesting story, and thanks to TravelinWilly for flagging this.

In 2023, we saw airline after airline announce flights to Tulum. Among US carriers, we saw American, Delta, JetBlue, and United, all launch flights to Tulum, while Spirit announced flights, but never launched them.

That was only a small part of the overall international service to the airport, as we also saw flights to Tulum on Air Canada, Air Transat, Avianca, Copa, Discover, and WestJet, along with flights on domestic carriers like Aeromexico, Mexicana de Aviacion, Viva, and Volaris.

Less than three years after the airport opened, where do things stand? When it comes to international year-round service, we’ve now gone from service to 12 airports, to service to four airports, which is an absolutely massive reduction:

Just to put this into numbers, in the first half of 2026, the airport saw a 33.2% reduction in passengers. June 2026 was the worst month to date, with a 44.8% drop, as there were just 47,600 passengers, compared to 86,200 passengers the year prior.

It goes without saying that for a newly built airport, this is rather catastrophic, and not what you hope to see.

In some ways, it feels like the whole concept behind Tulum Airport was sort of unnecessary. That’s also easily explained by the fact that the airport was well over a decade in the making, so while I can see how it might’ve seemed like a good idea at some point, tourism trends do evolve over time.

The reality is that there was a point where Tulum was cool, edgy, and a bit off the beaten path, compared to… whatever it is now. But when it comes to the struggles that airlines have had with flying to Tulum, it seems like the problems have been endless:

It’ll be interesting to see if Tulum can reverse any of these trends, and regain visitors…

A few years ago Tulum Airport opened to some fanfare, as a good number of airlines rushed to add service there. Unfortunately the excitement didn’t last long, as the airport has gone from a dozen year-round international routes, to just four.

This reduction in demand particularly evident from the United States, as soon there will just be nonstop service on American and Delta, and even that will be with reduced frequencies and routes.

Tulum Airport is facing endless challenges, not the least of which is a decline in visitors to Mexico from the United States, combined with increasing competition for those fewer visitors. Mexico just isn’t a value destination in the way that it used to be in places like Cancun, Tulum, Los Cabos, etc., and that’s understandably impacting demand.

I’m curious if Tulum can find a way to turn things around, but it’s not a good situation.
