GTA 6’s Best-Kept Secret Is the Most Important Part of the Game

The prologue of GTA 6 starts with a shock

Considering how much of Grand Theft Auto 6 leaked before its launch, you’d think that Rockstar’s latest wouldn’t have anything left to hide. I mean, we’ve really seen it all. But as players immediately discovered when starting their save file, Rockstar (kind of) managed to keep one secret — and it was a huge one.

[Ed. note: This article contains spoilers for the prologue of GTA 6.]

GTA 6 kicks off with a short prologue chapter that sets a few things in motion. First, we get to see the moment Jason and Lucia met, as they both got crossed trying to jack the same car. A rom-com montage ensues, but things take a turn fast. We fast-forward to Lucia sitting in a restaurant as she prepares to pull off a robbery across the street. After nervously stalling​​​​​​​, she works up the courage to stick the place up. Unfortunately, it doesn’t go well. The cops quickly intervene and arrest her.

The twist comes as she’s being put into the back of a cop car. A panicked Lucia begs the cops to take her back to the restaurant where she was holed up before the robbery, because she left something there. The cops discover something that the camera was carefully hiding earlier in the sequence: Lucia’s infant son, Tommy, who she left unattended.

As we soon learn, Tommy is Jason and Lucia’s son — unbeknownst to Jason, until Lucia tells him upon getting out of prison. Tommy now lives with a separate guardian after getting taken from Lucia’s custody; Jason and Lucia only get to see him through occasional photos that get texted to them. It turns out that Jason and Lucia’s whole motivation for making cash in GTA 6 is to fund a family savings account in hopes that they can reunite with their son and give him a normal life.

It’s a shocking revelation that Rockstar desperately wanted to keep hidden as long as possible. No pre-release trailers for the game so much as teased that there was a kid in the picture. When I spoke to Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson just ahead of the game’s launch, he seemed relieved that the detail had mostly gone unspoiled, despite a cavalcade of leaks. (It actually did leak about a year ago, but it faded into a forgotten rumor in a sea of them by the time GTA 6 actually launched.) For Nelson, that initial shock is an important part of an experience that’s designed to challenge what you think you know about its antiheroes at every turn.

With Tommy comes a mess of complicated character dynamics and anxieties that you’ll see throughout GTA 6’s story. While Lucia seems like an impulsive loudmouth who doesn’t have a care in the world, it becomes increasingly clear that she’s harboring serious insecurity after having Tommy taken from her. She quietly worries that she doesn’t deserve to be a parent after leaving her kid to go pull off a stick-up. While Jason wasn’t part of that, he holds the same fear that someone like him isn’t responsible enough to be a father. Those feelings bleed out whenever the two receive a text with a photo of Tommy and are reminded of the kid who is growing up somewhere without them.

Tommy also adds some welcome tension into Jason and Lucia’s love story, considering that the romance can be largely free of friction otherwise (depending on how you engage with the romance system). At one point, the two broke into a spontaneous conversation about their family. Jason noted that Lucia’s mother doesn’t seem to like him very much, which prompted Lucia to reveal that she lied to her mom and told her that Jason didn’t want to be involved in Tommy’s life. Though that isn’t exactly true given that Jason didn’t know his son existed, Lucia then recalled a conversation where Jason told her he didn’t want to have kids. That exchange gives us context that explains why Lucia didn’t tell Jason about Tommy upfront. For Nelson, those little details help build a nuanced story of two reluctant parents trying to figure out how to adapt to an unexpected responsibility.

It’s sympathetic to Lucia’s circumstances, making a point in a flashback to show her struggling to live as a single mother in a world that is hostile to her specific needs. It is careful about letting Jason face some very human insecurities without casting him as a hothead who can only deal in tough love. GTA 6 tells us that two people can survive a bad situation if they’re driven by a love that provides stability in uncertain times.

As players are bound to experience for themselves, Tommy changes so much about the psychology of GTA, even from off-screen. Could Earth have been collecting dark matter for billions of years?

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Heat measurements from dead stars helped physicists narrow down how dark matter might interact with ordinary matter.

Physicists have spent decades building dark matter detectors deep underground, and they still haven’t caught a single particle.

A new study suggests that Earth itself, along with the Sun, Jupiter and a handful of dead stars, might trap certain kinds of dark matter better than any lab instrument.

These bodies could have been collecting slow-moving particles for billions of years.

The researchers used neutrino data and heat measurements to rule out some combinations of particle mass and interaction strength that laboratory experiments couldn’t probe.

The work was led by Alejandro Ibarra from the Technical University of Munich, Merlin Reichard from the Korea Institute for Advanced Study and Gaurav Tomar from the Sogang University in Seoul.

About 85% of the matter in the universe is dark matter. We know it’s there because galaxies spin faster than their visible matter can explain and light bends more than it should as it passes them.

Beyond that, very little is known about it. Physicists are stuck in a frustrating loop.

You can’t identify something you can’t see, and you can’t design a way to see it without some idea of what it is. So they make a guess, build an experiment to test it, and so far the answer has always been no.

One guess is that dark matter isn’t completely dark. Its particles might respond to electricity and magnetism very faintly, carrying a tiny fraction of an electron’s charge or acting like microscopic magnets.

The paper describes five such properties. One of them, the “anapole moment,” is a doughnut-shaped kind of magnetism.

Any of the five would let a dark matter particle occasionally bump into an atom.

Underground detectors wait for those bumps. A slow particle taps an atom so gently that the instrument doesn’t notice.

In practice, these detectors are more sensitive to faster dark matter particles.

Planets and stars work the other way around. A fast particle shoots straight through them. A slow one only needs to lose a little speed in a collision before gravity grabs it and won’t let go.

Over billions of years, trapped particles would sink toward the center and crowd together. Once packed tightly enough, they could meet and annihilate, turning into other particles.

Some of those would be neutrinos, which pass through rock almost as if it weren’t there. The rest would deposit their energy as heat.

“In this work, we considered four celestial bodies: Earth, the Sun, Jupiter and white dwarfs,” Tomar said.

“There are dedicated experiments, for example IceCube at the South Pole, looking for neutrinos from the Sun and Earth. For Jupiter and white dwarfs, we considered the heating possibilities.”

For the Sun and Earth, the team used limits from IceCube, a detector built into the Antarctic ice, and from its smaller companion detector, DeepCore.

That included ten years of IceCube data on neutrinos coming from Earth’s core. There was no excess.

Neither Jupiter nor the white dwarfs generate heat through nuclear fusion.

The Cassini spacecraft measured how much heat Jupiter leaks from its interior.

The white dwarfs are burnt-out stellar cores in M4, a cluster of ancient stars. Each one holds about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass in a ball with a radius of roughly 1,250 kilometers.

If a candidate type of dark matter would make these objects give off more heat than they actually do, it can be crossed off.

Earth turned out to be the best test for dark matter with a tiny electric charge. For particles heavier than about ten protons, it set the strongest limits yet.

There are two reasons. First, a charged particle’s pull reaches a long way, so it is easily drawn in. Second, Earth’s core is full of iron, whose atoms each carry 26 protons, which makes collisions more likely.

The white dwarfs set leading limits on two of the more exotic properties, the charge radius and the anapole moment, across a wide range of particle masses.

For very heavy particles, they also beat earlier limits on dark matter’s magnetic dipole moment.

The anapole limits matter for one popular candidate whose only possible electromagnetic property is the anapole moment.

For that candidate, the white dwarfs reach places that particle colliders and underground detectors struggle to get to.

Jupiter was a letdown. In general, it didn’t improve on limits that already existed. None of the four bodies beat earlier limits on the electric dipole moment either.

Underground labs sit beneath a kilometer or more of rock to block cosmic rays. If dark matter interacts strongly with ordinary matter, that same rock could slow it down or stop it before it reaches the detector.

The shield could be hiding the very particles the experiment is looking for.

Planets and stars don’t have this problem. The more strongly dark matter interacts, the better they catch it.

There is one important caveat. The more dark matter surrounds a star, the more of it the star can capture, and nobody has measured how much dark matter M4 contains.

The team had to rely on estimates from models.

“We do not know the dark matter density there. We used estimated values, so if those values change, our limits could become weaker or stronger,” Tomar noted.

Tomar suggests two places to look next. One is even colder white dwarfs in regions with more dark matter, where any extra heat would be easier to detect.

The other is beyond the Solar System, where exoplanets could serve as dark matter traps too.

The study is published in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics.

Directing duo Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo stretch a love story across the Spanish Civil War, three generations and.the great beyond.

The film, co-written with Conejero, braids three timelines. In 1932 Granada, Carlos (Milo Quifes), a young upper-class Andalusian, is blackballed from an exclusive casino because he’s gay — the premise of Lorca’s unfinished play La Bola Negra, of which only four pages survive. In 1937, Sebastián (singer- songwriter Guitarricadelafuente) keeps watch over Rafael (Miguel Bernardeau). And in 2017 Madrid, Alberto (Carlos González) learns that he is Sebastián’s grandson. Sebastián has left him the rest of Lorca’s play — pages the film imagines into being — which he had promised Rafael he would save. Those pages — verified by Glenn Close, who plays a Spanish-speaking American scholar named Isabelle Durand — lead Alberto and his relatives to discover that Sebastián spent most of his life secretly living with another man.

“This is a movie about what we pass on to the next generation,” Ambrossi says. “It’s about inheritance.”

The co-directors, known as Los Javis among their collaborators because of their shared first names, reassembled their usual crafts team to begin shaping each timeline: cinematographer Gris Jordana, editor Alberto Gutiérrez, production designer Roger Bellés, costume designer Ana López Cobos, composer Raül Refree and casting directors Eva Leira and Yolanda Serrano.

The directors were already keen on Guitarricadelafuente and Bernardeau for their respective roles, as well as González for the part of Alberto, which Ambrossi calls “a creation.”

“He’s so comedic and flamboyant, but we wanted to give him a role where he can prove the actor that he is because sometimes queer people are given the same [stereotypical] roles,” Ambrossi says.

Quifes, who like Guitarricadelafuente makes his screen debut in the film, was found through an open casting call. “He has a sense of melancholy about him, but it’s not about hopelessness; there’s a sense of hope and beauty,” says Leira. “And he has such a beautiful gaze. It’s a gaze that comes from ancient times.”

Los Javis also knew they wanted to shoot in 35mm from the outset, which aligned with DP Jordana’s approach. “You don’t have to fight the format to get images,” she says of 35mm film. “If you use the right light direction, the rest just comes together. It’s like magic.”

Light also drove the design of the opulent casino in Granada, the only set built from scratch. Everywhere else, the production restored existing buildings, hiring local artisans who still work with prewar methods. “At first glance, it looked like something crazy and unnecessary when we built it in the middle of Spain, but when you open the windows, you see Spain, not a green screen,” says production designer Bellés, who commissioned artist Ignasi Monreal to paint the mural on the ceiling in the style of Joaquín Sorolla, “one of the great painters of Spanish light,” he explains.

The windows were made of sugar glass so that they could shatter safely when the casino explodes near the end of the film after being overtaken by a mob as Carlos makes a final case for admission. The remaining structure was then filled with fake snow as the scene transitions to Carlos lying on a mountainside, realizing he has died from the bomb’s explosion. The shot, captured at an altitude of 3,000 meters (more than 9,800 feet high) in the snowy region of the Sierra Nevadas, brought to life elements described in Lorca’s poem — like the skiers on the slopes — which also inspired Guitarricadelafuente’s original song, “La Nieve (The Snow),” that plays in the background.

“With Federico García Lorca, there’s a lot of powerful imagery and symbolism, like in the beginning of the film, the horse running past Sebastián is a representation of untamable desire,” Guitarricadelafuente explains. “ ‘La Nieve’ symbolized the end of a journey and approaching death. I had to leave the emotional baggage of Sebastián to fully understand what he was carrying to be able to write that.”

Refree took a hands-on approach with Penélope Cruz, who portrays Nené Romero, a cupletista cabaret singer. “She told me, ‘I’m not a singer,’ but as soon as I heard her singing … I fell in love with her singing,” he says. “The problem was that she hadn’t created the character yet.”

Over the course of several days, Refree went to Cruz’s home for rehearsals, and she began to craft the persona of Nené, who serves as a portal to a world in which Sebastián can be free to be his true self when he sees her perform “Las Tardes del Ritz (Afternoons at the Ritz)” and “Batallón de modistillas (Seamstress Battalion)” on the base.

“My goal was to have this kind of music that she could be influenced by when talking to the soldiers and to create this evocative thing, like making two men play together as a couple,” Refree explains of the first number. “The second one needed a dirty energy that would make the soldiers lose control and want to touch her. So we created something very rhythmic and feminine at the same time.”

Wardrobe also helped shape Nené. Cobos drew from research on 19th century cuplé singers, like María del Consuelo Vello Cano, to sketch Cruz’s feathery, embellished ensemble.

“When she saw the white dress, she said, ‘This is it; she has to be wearing white,’ and Gris also said it would be helpful because we don’t have a lot of lights in the theater,” says Cobos. “[For the second number,] because I know Penélope’s a big fan of Mugler, I went through their images and found these overalls and dresses that reminded me of military costumes. So I did my own interpretation of them, and it worked really well because it helped her move and it was very comfortable for her to do the choreography with the tank and all of the other dancers.”

In the final scene of the film, Sebastián reads a poem with the words, “Mira, que nos acechan todavia (Beware, they still haunt us),” after being forced to shoot Rafael. It’s the final verse of “Love Sleeps in the Poet’s Breast,” one of the Sonetos del Amor Oscuro (Sonnets of Dark Love), written by Lorca for Rafael, expressing his forbidden desire for him amid the looming threat of societal persecution.

For Calvo, the warning is just as relevant today. “We had it on the whiteboard, and it was kind of a guide [for us],” he says. “Putting it [at the end of the film] was perfect and so significant because they do still haunt us.”

Apple HomePod mini 2 review: A necessary, but underwhelming update

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I'd be willing to forgive you if you forgot about the new HomePod mini. The overhauled smart speaker played a supporting role at best during Apple's Welcome Home event last month, behind the HomeView, and probably, in some minds, behind the updated Apple TV 4K. Still, the company provides a few upgrades in the HomePod mini 2 ($149), ensuring that it's ready for Siri AI and the assistant's newfound ability to offer personalized context for your queries. The main problem? The speaker doesn't yet support the recently upgraded virtual assistant Apple spent years improving.

The most notable update on the HomePod mini 2 is on the inside. Apple swapped in its S10 chip from the Apple Watch Series 10, kicking the nearly antique S5 silicon to the curb. For a Watch comparison, that's like going from a Series 5 or first-gen Watch SE, which debuted in 2019 and 2020 respectively, to the 2024 edition. It's a big leap, and it's one Apple says was necessary for the HomePod mini to work with Siri AI.

The recently updated assistant isn't all the S10 is helping with here. Apple says the component can run real-time music evaluation "to optimize dynamic range, loudness and acoustic performance," with a hand from "advanced computational analysis." When connected to the newly introduced fourth-gen Apple TV 4K, you can use four HomePod mini 2 speakers as a surround sound setup. That S10 chip helps the speakers recognize their positions in a room and adjust the lag, convey that info to Apple's streaming box, and audio levels accordingly.

I only have two HomePod mini 2s, so I wasn't able to test this configuration, but I did hear it during Apple's event in October. Even with that brief experience, I can tell you using two second-gen HomePods as the front speakers and two HomePod minis as the rears is a much better setup. The HomePod mini doesn't muster enough bass to be a great home theater speaker on its own, and those full-size HomePods bring the boom when an action scene demands it.

Before now, you could send audio from a Mac to a HomePod mini, but Apple has expanded the functionality so that the second-gen model can serve as persistent default speakers. This means, in theory, they'll stay connected to your Mac as the primary output rather than just relying on a temporary AirPlay link. This allows you to use the HomePod mini 2, or a pair of them, for everything on your computer — from FaceTime calls to games — without having to think about connecting each app or session to the speaker(s).

The HomePod line has always had lights on the top, but they haven't really done much. Before now, the top touch panel would light up if audio was playing or display an animation when you chatted with Siri. With a new feature called Sound Glow, those lights now show hues that compliment a song's album artwork. The lights move in real time, reacting to the bass and treble in each tune. If you opt for a soundscape, Sound Glow reacts to that too, showing an animation that resembles the pitter patter of rain drops, for example. It isn't a game-changing feature, but it's a nice use of lighting that was barely utilized previously.

Lastly, there are the new colors. The HomePod mini 2 comes in pink, green, blue, white and midnight (dark grey), with those first three coordinating well with Apple's iMac colors. That list means the company retired orange and yellow from the previous generation, both of which looked great in their time. As you can see, my review units are green, and I've enjoyed the pop of color they've added to my desk over the last few weeks.

Given that Apple saw fit to bestow a "2" on this HomePod mini, I expected some upgrades to overall sound quality to be in the mix. Unfortunately, I can't notice a difference between old and new models. Sometimes I think there's some variation, but it's not substantial enough that I'm sure, and it's not consistent across various musical styles. I do like the audio performance of the HomePod mini for its size, but it still has a sound profile of crisp highs and prominent mids offset by bass tone that lacks dynamics and depth. It's good enough for casual listening or background tunes, but not for anything more than that.

Despite the lack of Siri AI, I was hoping the S10 chip would provide a performance boost for the old Siri experience you have to settle for right now. However, the assistant's responses to basic questions took the same amount of time as they do on the first-gen model. To be clear, they're quick on both devices, I'm merely pointing out there's no difference in this regard. Just don't try to ask the old assistant to dive into Apple's core apps for you, or try to have an on-going conversation — neither request will bear any fruit.

The biggest issue with the HomePod mini 2 right now is its biggest omission: Siri AI. The recently updated assistant isn't available on the speaker yet, and Apple hasn't provided a timeline for its arrival either. The upgraded HomePod mini is equipped to support the now-current-gen Siri, thanks to the S10 chip. However, you'll have to wait for Siri's newfound contextual awareness to dive into your Messages, Calendar and other apps on your iPhone or iPad. And to me, that's a huge miss for Apple on an overhauled smart speaker. You'll also have to wait for the more conversational chats with Siri as the HomePod mini 2 doesn't yet support those either.

I like the idea of using a HomePod mini 2 or a stereo pair of them as a persistent computer speaker with a Mac, but it's not a great experience in practice. Any time you do anything — start a song, pause a song, skip tracks — there's a 1-2 second delay in the audio responding. The lights on the top of the speakers, including the Sound Glow effect, will stop shining immediately, but the audio has to catch up. There's also a slight delay between speakers if you're using a stereo pair, which sounds like every song has added reverb. For these reasons, I found the Mac speaker feature more frustrating than enjoyable, and I quickly moved back to AirPlay over a nearby Sonos speaker for my productivity tunes. And I haven't even mentioned the fact that I can't get the persistent connection to stick with my MacBook Air either. I'm constantly reselecting the HomePod mini 2 as the audio output.

Another item that's disappointing is something else that's not here: a substantial overhaul. The original HomePod mini is six years old at this point, so I expected a much bigger redesign for the second-gen version (until the rumor mill squashed that). If you have a first-gen HomePod mini, you'll miss out on the updated Siri, whenever it arrives, and new features like Sound Glow and the home theater option. And if you're hoping to create a stereo pair, you'll need two of the same generation HomePod minis to do that; you can't pair a first-gen speaker with a second-gen model for that purpose.

Lastly, there's the price. The previous version was $99 before Apple raised it to $129 in June when the company hiked the prices on pretty much all of its hardware. Now, I'm well aware of the global economic factors at play, especially the AI-fueled cost increase on RAM and other components, but asking $50 more than the first-gen model for incremental updates is tough. Had Apple kept the price at the June rate, the HomePod mini 2 would be a much easier recommendation.

It's baffling to me that Apple would announce an updated HomePod mini without support for the latest version of Siri ready to go. The speaker's primary use is a hands-free conduit to the company's voice assistant, after all. The only decent conclusion I can come to is that Apple wanted to announce what it considered was a full suite of home products around the HomeView debut, and it felt like the Apple TV 4K, HomePod mini and a collection of smart home devices were the best way to do that. I would be interested to know what the hold up is, because the Apple Watch Series 10 supports Siri AI already, and those wearables are also running the same S10 chip as the HomePod mini 2. But, of course, Apple isn't talking specifics.

After spending a few weeks with the HomePod mini 2, I mostly wish Apple had just waited. At a higher price, this isn't as compelling as it used to be in terms of getting Siri in your home. And with Siri AI missing, and some of the new audio features proving to be unreliable, it's clear a bit more polish was needed here.
