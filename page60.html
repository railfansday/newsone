Google Adds Computer Use to Gemini on Mac for Multi-Step Tasks

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google announced on November 19, 2026 that computer use is rolling out in the Gemini app for Mac, allowing the assistant to carry out multi-step work across local files, desktop applications and websites. After a user describes the desired workflow, Gemini moves through menus, clicks and types within apps, and fills in forms rather than merely supplying directions.

Gemini performs the work in an isolated background window, with a live preview in the chat. The user’s main screen and cursor remain free for other activity. Users choose which apps it can access, can watch and pause its progress, and approve key actions before they happen.

Google’s suggested uses emphasize transferring information between programs. For a shipping quote, Gemini can extract weights, dimensions and addresses from local PDFs and enter them into a multi-box form. It can also gather metrics and screenshots from live dashboards across multiple regions and turn them into a Google Slides presentation.

Creative examples include batch color-grading photos in design software and setting up complex 3D scenes. A resale workflow can analyze clothing photos from a local folder, research pricing, record item details and stage listings on resale platforms, combining local images, web research and listing preparation in one task.

The feature is rolling out in English globally, except in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Nigeria. It is limited to personal Google accounts for users aged 18 or older with a Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra subscription.

The Mac must have Apple Silicon and run macOS Sequoia 15.0 or later. Google’s general installation requirements also call for at least 8 GB of RAM, 200 MB of available disk space for installation and a stable internet connection.

Local access is narrower than an unrestricted view of the Mac. Google’s task documentation says Gemini can view only files or directories specifically added to the Connected folders toolbar. Users select Task below the text box, optionally add a Mac folder, and can disconnect an item added by mistake.

With permission, Gemini can view and edit most folders, including Downloads, Desktop, Documents and Photos. It makes requested changes to files in connected folders by default. Folder selection therefore determines which local material enters a task, while the separate app permissions govern the programs involved in the workflow.

When Gemini works with computer files, it creates temporary backups. Users can recover those backups before moving to another task, but they are deleted when a new query or task starts or after 24 hours. That makes the recovery window dependent on what the user does next as well as the clock.

Google cautions that Gemini can make mistakes, permanently delete Workspace or computer files, and send or share files with third parties. It is designed to request permission before those actions.

The recommended precautions include checking file contents and recipients before approving sharing, avoiding tasks involving information or actions the user considers sensitive, and keeping sign-in and payment details out of the task thread.

Public cloud drives add another exposure. Once connected, their files can be accessed, edited, shared and deleted, and Gemini can also share data from other connected folders and apps, signed-in websites and the user’s location with the drive. Anyone with access to that public cloud drive can see data shared there.

The practical trade-off sits in those permissions: the access that lets Gemini finish work across local and online services also determines what it could alter, delete or disclose if it makes a mistake.

Zoning reform is remaking parking lots into a housing paradise

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Parking lot redevelopment is gaining momentum as zoning reforms expand by-right and ministerial approvals on commercial land. The tradeoffs include labor and affordability rules, tighter lending, and continued lawsuits over eligibility and process.  AI Summary

Developers across the country paved over paradise to put up a parking lot. But changes to zoning laws to increase housing supply now have developers paving over parking lots to create a housing paradise.

In California, a series of state laws has made that easier. Florida lawmakers have gone after the same land. San Diego suburb National City took matters into its own hands. Because of its high housing costs, California has been the leading model for how reforms can work. But the Golden State is also a model for how neighborhood advocates and cities fight the reforms, often rendering them worth less than the paper they’re written on.

Much of the zoning reform push across the country focuses on where ground-up developers could build residential density, particularly infill in urban areas. The pressure is on land.

Underused parking lots are one option. The median share of downtown land used only for parking is about 26% in U.S. cities with more than 500,000 people, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation.

California’s Assembly Bill 2011 created a ministerial approval path, exempt from environmental review, for apartments on sites where office, retail or parking is a principal use. AB 2243 expanded those tools for commercially zoned property when it took effect last year.

Florida’s Live Local Act requires cities and counties to allow multifamily rentals by right on commercial, industrial and mixed-use land when a share of the units is affordable. Lawmakers have amended the law multiple times, including this year, since enactment in 2023.

National City shows how the local action can work. The city loosened height limits in its mixed-use zones last year, making underused lots more workable for housing.

National City Investment Group has broken ground on Market View, a 120-unit workforce housing project that will replace a parking lot beside Manolo Farmers Market in National City. This small grocery chain has locations in San Diego, where leaders have instituted reforms that have faced neighborhood challenges and lawsuits that new state law quashes.

Opponents still turn to the courts, even though reforms were meant to curtail or preclude lawsuits. And they are getting more inventive.

In San Francisco, neighbors are suing over an 848-unit project at 15 Marina Blvd. The towers would replace the Safeway’s parking lot and rise above a newly built store. They say the city wrongly ruled it eligible for AB 2011 and denied them a hearing.

To get the case into federal court, local opponents invoked an 1871 law, commonly known as the Ku Klux Klan Act. The developer wants the federal court to dismiss the case.

In Los Angeles, the fight is over habitat. Planners rejected 398 homes at the Woodland Hills Country Club in August, citing mountain lions. The state Department of Housing and Community Development said the city violated AB 2011. Pro-housing groups have sued the city while the developer threatens its own lawsuit for damages that could exceed $20 million.

Under AB 2011, a qualifying project gets ministerial approval, which means municipal staff checks it against objective standards and no hearing is required. Residents lose the chance to comment on the project or its environmental effects, said Sheri Bonstelle, a land-use attorney at Greenberg Lusker who represents developers, told HousingWire TBD.

“Neighbors are really upset that they no longer have a voice at all,” Bonstelle said.

Developers will also pay for the faster path. AB 2011 projects must meet labor standards, including prevailing wage. Bonstelle said that can add roughly 30% to construction costs, which developers weigh against discretionary review and the risk of a lawsuit under the California Environmental Quality Act.

“It’s literally a monetary analysis,” Bonstelle said.

The math has worked for some of her clients, she said. They’ll trade off higher costs for the elimination of entitlement risk and confidence that a project can be built. Bonstelle said lenders also favor the ministerial path. That’s especially crucial now, given the tight lending environment.

How much access neighbors get depends on the city. Los Angeles has a detailed AB 2011 application, Bonstelle said, so developers must file substantial information that neighbors can request and review. The city knew these projects were coming and was thorough about what had to be filed. Smaller cities are the bigger problem, she said.

“It’s more of an issue with the smaller cities that haven’t had the projects come through yet,” Bonstelle said.

Some cities hand developers a checklist meant for a building permit, she said. Her firm then goes through item by item with city attorneys to explain that most of those materials are not due at the planning stage of ministerial review.

Other cities believe state law should not apply to them and decline to process applications, Bonstelle said. In one case, her firm is working with a small city that has been “incredibly difficult.” The firm is asking the state Department of Housing and Community Development for a technical assistance letter explaining what the law requires.

Florida’s Live Local Act addresses similar commercial land. Cities and counties must approve multifamily rentals administratively on commercial, industrial and mixed-use land, with no rezoning or public hearing, if at least 40% of units are affordable for 30 years. Lawmakers have amended the law three times since 2023, most recently in 2026.

The law covers parking lots zoned for those uses, Patrick Chopson, an Atlanta architect and co-founder of Cove, told HousingWire TBD. “That’s a big deal,” Chopson said. Cove, which has a proprietary AI platform, recently acquired Deerfield Beach, Fla., architectural firm Gallo Herbert Architects. The firm has done a lot of work statewide in affordable housing.

The act also funds projects. In St. Petersburg, Nashville-based Elmington Capital Group plans Skyway Flats, a 174-unit affordable apartment building on surface parking next to an assisted living facility in the city’s Skyway Marina District.

The five-story building will have 28 one-bedroom, 100 two-bedroom and 46 three-bedroom apartments. All will target households earning 30% to 80% of the area median income, and 18 will go to young adults aging out of foster care.

Pinellas County commissioners unanimously approved $6 million in March to buy the land. The county will require affordability for 99 years. The $77 million project also draws on federal tax credit equity, tax-exempt bonds and a state loan through the Live Local Act.

Skyway Flats is in predevelopment. Elmington expects financial closing and a construction start in early 2027.

Similar to Pinellas County officials, National City planners acted on their own. They changed zoning to allow more density on parking lots. The change fits within the California law context. San Diego County alone needs more than 171,000 homes, regional officials say.

The city’s planning commission began reforming the code in July 2025 to allow four stories. Previously, the code capped buildings at three.

That cap limited how many units a project could hold, Andrew Regenberg of Gafcon, the firm working with the owner, told the commission in a public hearing. Chris Dallo, who heads the investment group, backed the change.

“We believe that more housing means more opportunity not just for our market, but for local businesses,” Dallo told the commission.

Market View is scheduled for completion in the first quarter of 2028. It will have three buildings, mostly studios, one-bedrooms and two-bedrooms, linked by open space and the market. RDC designed it, and R.D. Olson Construction is the general contractor.

Councilmember Ditas Yamane, who represents the district, said the groundbreaking followed a long review.

“This milestone is the result of a long, thoughtful process and extensive due diligence to bring this vision to life,” Yamane said.

Mortgage delinquencies rise to 4.42% in Q3 2026

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mortgage delinquencies increased in Q3 2026 to 4.42%, MBA reported, with serious delinquencies rising to 2.12% without accounting for seasonality. FHA and VA foreclosure rates reached 1.89% and 1.06%, respectively, their highest levels since 2017 and 2016.   AI Summary

Mortgage delinquencies increased in the third quarter of 2026, with the share of loans at least one payment past due rising to 4.42%. That’s according to the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA)’s National Delinquency Survey, released Thursday.

The seasonally adjusted delinquency rate for one- to four-unit residential properties was up 5 basis points from the second quarter and 43 bps from a year earlier.

Foreclosure starts also increased, with the share of loans entering the foreclosure process rising 1 basis point from the previous quarter to 0.21%. The percentage of loans already in foreclosure reached 0.71%, up 4 bps quarterly and 21 bps year over year.

“Loans 90 days or more late or in foreclosure increased for the fifth consecutive quarter and are 51 basis points higher than the third quarter of 2025,” said Marina Walsh, MBA’s vice president and head of industry analysis.

Walsh said the deterioration in government-backed loans is contributing to higher foreclosure activity. The Federal Housing Administration (FHA) foreclosure rate reached 1.89%, its highest level since the first quarter of 2017, while the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) foreclosure rate climbed to 1.06%, its highest since Q4 2016.

By delinquency stage, the 30-day rate increased 4 basis points from the previous quarter to 2.25%, while the 60-day rate rose 1 bps to 0.74%. The 90-day delinquency rate remained unchanged at 1.43%.

Performance varied by loan type. The conventional mortgage delinquency rate increased by 1 basis point from the second quarter to 2.73%, while the FHA rate declined 5 bps to 11.74%. The VA delinquency rate rose 11 bps to 5%.

All three loan categories posted annual increases. Conventional delinquencies were up 11 bps from a year earlier, while FHA delinquencies increased 96 bps and VA delinquencies rose 50 bps.

The seriously delinquent rate, which includes loans at least 90 days past due or in the foreclosure process, reached 2.12% on a nonseasonally adjusted basis. That was up 6 basis points from the second quarter and 51 bps year over year.

FHA loans recorded the largest annual deterioration in serious delinquencies, with the rate rising 197 basis points. The rate increased 13 basis points for conventional loans and 60 basis points for VA loans.

The five states with the largest quarterly increases in overall mortgage delinquencies were Wyoming, up 72 basis points, followed by West Virginia and Louisiana, both up 32 basis points. Arkansas saw its rate increase by 31 basis points, while Kentucky’s rose by 27 bps.

Walsh said the overall mortgage delinquency rate closely tracks the national unemployment rate, which stood at 4.1% in October.

Although the labor market has remained resilient, Walsh pointed to several pressures on household finances, including wage growth lagging inflation, declining personal savings, and rising balances on credit cards, auto loans and student loans.

Borrowers who took out mortgages in recent years at higher interest rates may also face affordability challenges that make timely payments more difficult, Walsh said. Changes to mortgage relief programs over the past year, including the removal of some COVID-era flexibilities, could be adding to financial pressures for some homeowners.

Patient safety watchdog The Leapfrog Group released its latest collection of hospital safety grades hand-in-hand with calls for policymakers and stakeholders to consider, or directly tie, patient safety reporting with price transparency.

The twice-yearly release brought top marks for 900 out of a total 2,366 graded hospitals, and the lowest “F” score for just three. There were 774 hospitals that earned a “B” grade, 655 a “C” grade, and 35 a “D” grade.

No grades were assigned to another 447 hospitals, a decision the group made after a judge’s ruling in a lawsuit involving the scores it would assign to facilities that did not voluntarily submit their quality data to Leapfrog’s survey (Leapfrog appealed that decision in September). Leapfrog’s decision not to score these has generally shifted its letter grade distribution upward, and the organization has said it is reviewing a potential methodology change for upcoming gradings to incorporate non-submitting hospitals.

Still, “a record number of hospitals are choosing to publicly report their safety and quality performance, and we applaud them for holding themselves accountable to their patients,” Leapfrog President and CEO Leah Binder said in a statement.

At the state level, Connecticut maintained its claim on the largest portion of hospitals earning an “A” grade (64.3% of its total), followed by Utah, New Jersey and South Carolina. Leapfrog also called out Rhode Island and Maine for jumping up the rankings—from eighteenth to seventh for the former, and from seventeenth to eighth for the latter. No hospitals were given an “A” grade in five states: Iowa, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont and Wyoming.

A hospital’s assigned grade is calculated by reviewing recent data on up to 22 patient safety measures, including a 10-part Medicare composite of reported patient safety and adverse events. Safety scores for specific hospitals can be searched on the group’s website.

Leapfrog and Binder, in a news release outlining its latest scores, made the case that its grades and similar independent measures warrant a seat at the table in a transparency conversation primarily focused on hospital costs.

“Progress on price transparency is important, but there’s no good price for bad care,” Binder said. “We are calling on the Trump Administration and Congress to ensure price transparency is integrated with patient safety and quality transparency.”

The group backed its calls by pointing to a recent analysis (PDF) conducted by the Purchaser Business Group on Health (PBGH) that reviewed employer claims, federal price transparency data, and quality and safety measures including Leapfrog’s. It “found little to no correlation between negotiated prices and standardized quality measures,” as hospitals that received high safety grades often charged cheaper prices than those in the same market with lower grades.

Leapfrog and Elizabeth Mitchell, president and CEO of PBGH, said the analysis—currently being expanded for practical usage by healthcare purchasers—is an example of how a more wholistic view of prices and care delivery should be considered when weighing a hospital.

“Employers have an appropriate duty to pay a fair price for high-quality care, and they can’t meet it by looking at price alone,” Mitchell said in the release from Leapfrog. “When we put the data together, some of the most expensive hospitals were not the safest. Our members want to buy high-quality, safe care that is fairly priced. That shouldn’t be too much to ask.”

Aetna is rolling out a new program that aims to provide key end-to-end care for pregnant women, new mothers and infants.

The offering, called Aetna For You and Baby, will be available to 90% of expectant mothers enrolled in an Aetna plan. Through the program, moms will be connected to a prenatal visit in their first trimester as well as a postpartum visit within 12 weeks of the baby's birth.

Women in the program will also be screened for depression both during and after pregnancy to identify any risks.

In addition to supporting the mother both during pregnancy and postpartum, the program aims to give babies a healthy start by providing at least six well visits through their first 15 months, which offer critical tests and screenings, checkups and vaccinations.

The news of the launch was provided first to Fierce Healthcare.

Katerina Guerraz, Aetna's chief operating officer, told Fierce that maternal care often focuses mostly, if not completely, on labor and delivery, though prenatal and postpartum visits play a critical role in driving positive health outcomes.

In its announcement, Aetna notes that data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention show that about 90% of pregnancy-related deaths could have been prevented, with a lack of access to prenatal care and more complete support making for a key barrier.

"This is a national access issue, a quality issue and it's an affordability problem," she said.

In addition, it's not uncommon for programs that aim to drive better maternal outcomes to lack services for the babies, who in the first year of life undergo a wide array of critical developmental milestones, Guerraz said.

Aetna, as the third-largest insurer in the U.S., sees about 200,000 deliveries a year among its members. That means the success of this program could have a significant impact in a short amount of time, supporting at least 1 million babies over the next five years, she said.

Aetna for You and Baby also builds on a broader push within the company to build a more personalized and simple experience for members. For instance, the company established a program that embeds Aetna nurses in the hospital to support discharge planning and transitions for members, and Guerraz said that holds potential in the maternal care space.

For example, if a baby needs to stay in the neonatal intensive care unit after birth, having an Aetna nurse in the NICU providing additional support to the clinical team and the member would pay dividends, she said.

It’s been almost seven months since artificial intelligence disease detection and care coordination platform Viz.ai entered into a partnership with the National Rural Health Association (NRHA).

The partnership aims to help rural healthcare leaders better understand and implement AI tools across systems. That's been a personal and professional passion of Viz.ai Chief Clinical Officer Andrew Ibrahim, M.D., who discussed with Fierce Healthcare at HLTH his key takeaways from the collaboration thus far and forward-looking goals as it approaches its one-year mark.

Ibrahim said that the two organizations have done three educational events for rural health systems since the partnership kicked off in April. These series aim to show rural health administrators the specifics of AI—and whether they should adopt it, he said.

He noted there’s “sometimes a very big mismatch” when smaller rural health systems onboard multiple vendors.

“We've learned [that] many rural hospitals don't have the IT expertise and infrastructure to adopt some of these tools, and so it's actually been a force function to strengthen their hub spoke relationships,” Ibrahim said.

Strengthening these relationships, according to Ibrahim, has been key.

“It's actually been beautiful to see the hub-spoke relationship strengthened with the AI tool as the conduit,” Ibrahim said. “And it gives them a reason to interact more, and a way for the rural hospitals to strengthen their capabilities.”

He continued, “there's an authenticity about the moment where it's like ‘Yeah, we do need to help each other out.’ We're all kind of figuring it out as we go.”

In addition to the educational series, Ibrahim said frameworks are in development to aid rural health systems in navigating, implementing and deploying AI—which will be built into a toolkit.

One of Ibrahim’s personal goals for the one-year mark of the partnership is to ensure any rural health administrator can understand what an AI tool is when pitched.

“I think that would be a huge milestone,” he said. “Getting to 100% of hospitals at year one would be ideal—probably unrealistic, but that's probably the ultimate goal.”

And when it comes to metrics for measuring the collaboration, Ibrahim said he's looking for numbers that show participants are “keeping more care local” and retaining their workforces.

“It would be great to be able to connect the dots [and understand whether] adoption of an AI tool give[s] rural clinicians confidence, enthusiasm, willingness to stay in their community as opposed to dropping the services and moving back,” he said.

Another initiative from Viz that has “disproportionate benefit in rural communities,” according to Ibrahim, is its new partnership with Wolters Kluwer Health to integrate the Viz.ai One platform into UpToDate Expert AI.

“I'm pretty excited about being able to bring their level of clinical decision support into our rural hospitals,” he said.

How to Plan Your Holiday Meals in a Year of Unprecedented Recalls

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Following a series of high-profile food recalls and outbreaks this year, many Americans may be concerned about keeping their families safe from foodborne illness during the holidays.

In fact, according to a recent national survey by Orlando Health, around 60% of U.S. adults say they’re more concerned about foodborne illness this year than in previous years.

Although several major foodborne illness outbreaks have ended and their associated recalls are no longer active, food safety remains an important concern. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that 48 million people in the United States experience foodborne illness each year.

However, there are simple steps you can take to reduce the risk of foodborne illness when preparing holiday meals and storing leftovers.

While there were several food recalls in the United States over the summer, two of the largest involved Salmonella linked to jalapeños and Cyclospora linked to lettuce.

These outbreaks prompted widespread recalls and raised concerns about the safety of certain foods, particularly fresh produce.

Although health officials have since declared the major outbreaks over, some consumers may still be hesitant to purchase foods associated with previous recalls.

Even with this declaration, you may still feel nervous about purchasing certain foods, but experts say you don’t need to worry.

“Here in the United States, we have the safest food supply in the world. The recent recalls show that our food safety system is working,” said William Schaffner, MD, Professor, Division of Infectious Diseases at Vanderbilt University.

Michelle Routhenstein, preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished, told Healthline that past recalls do not mean entire food categories need to be avoided. The important thing is whether the specific product you have matches a current recall, based on details such as brand, lot number, package size, and expiration date.

“You should enjoy familiar foods, including properly cooked poultry, washed fruits and vegetables, and pasteurized dairy products,” she said. “The key is checking relevant recalls and practicing safe food handling.”

“Before shopping or cooking, review FDA and USDA (U.S. Department of Agriculture) recall information and compare any relevant notices with the labels on your ingredients,” said Routhenstein.

Check the pantry and freezer, too: something purchased months ago may still be affected.

“Stay alert to any news reports about food recalls and use good food preparation practices. Those simple actions should keep your holidays happy and safe,” said Schaffner.

Consumers can sign up for food recall alerts through the FDA or visit FoodSafety.gov for recall information from both the FDA and USDA.

Schaffner emphasized the importance of following safe food-handling practices when preparing everyday meals and holiday gatherings.

“Remember the longstanding advice: keep food hot or cold. Avoid long times at room temperature,” he said.

Routhenstein advised focusing on four basics: clean, separate, cook, and chill.

Schaffner noted the importance of refrigerating leftovers promptly rather than leaving them at room temperature for extended periods. He recommends clearing the table and refrigerating leftovers soon after the meal ends.

Routhenstein reiterated that you should refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours rather than leaving them out to cool.

Divide turkey into smaller portions and transfer sides and gravy into shallow containers to help them cool quickly, she advised.

Leftovers can typically be stored in the refrigerator for 3–4 days. If you don’t plan to eat them within that time, consider freezing them.

The important thing is to keep an eye out for any new recalls, practice food safety, and enjoy your holiday meals!
