A Change of Tune: How the 2000s Changed How We Listen to Music in Cars

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From the CD changer to the iPod and aux jack, the decade saw audio options shift—for better or for worse.

These days, each of us is in our own little world. We’ve forfeited a largely shared reality and replaced it with an almost infinite series of more private universes. And I’m going to be honest with you: Results have been mixed. The seeds of our collective solitude were planted in the decades before, and the worst of it wouldn’t hit us until the years after, but 2000–09 was the era when the monoculture really began to fracture and fragment into microcultures. The great American mainstream was dwindling in the Aughts, a narrative expressed through our music. New technology changed the way people across the world listened. And as Americans, we in particular experienced this historic shift through our car stereos.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Once we shook off Y2K anxiety, the new millennium began as normal. Fueled by boy bands, blockbuster hip-hop, and, somehow, Limp Bizkit, the recording industry was flush in 2000. What was big was huge, and whether you liked the songs or not, you knew them. My main musical memory of the year 2000 took place in a rental car, on a drive up to the mountains with my roommates. The mid-size sedan had a mere cassette player, so we made do with the radio. On that October afternoon, some station played the comeback single from a band that hadn’t released a studio album in three years: U2’s “Beautiful Day.” We turned it up, we nodded along, and we were impressed that a band could sound this good despite having members who were now 40. Best of all, we shared the experience with however many strangers were also tuned in to this frequency.

As the Aughts progressed, I found myself sliding into my car’s CD slot fewer store-bought discs and more homemade mixes I burned using my iBook after downloading music from iTunes. It was easy, it was cheap at 99 cents per song, and it was as frictionless as a click on the touchpad. I recently found a box of these discs in my garage, and each one is packed with music I have not thought about since I put them there. These early-­Aughts songs left me no physical album covers to remember them by; the playlists were not yet saved for posterity on streaming services. They vanished.

Then there was the iPod. Other MP3 players predated it, but the iPod was sleek and sexy and held up to 1000 songs, which I swear sounded like a lot in 2001. The forward-thinking among us might carry a special cord in our bag in case we found ourselves in a car with an auxiliary input. Traditional radio stations suffered as we ditched the DJs and did their job for free.

Satellite radio launched an effort to fill the void—XM in 2001, Sirius in 2002, then SiriusXM in 2008, with hundreds of hyperspecific-genre stations on the digital dial. Satellite radio broke us in to the concept of paying a monthly fee to listen to music we don’t own, and Spotify and Apple Music would run with that idea in the decades ahead. Now we all have access to virtually every piece of music ever recorded, but who is guiding us through it? It’s chaos. I find myself longing for those days before endless choices, before Bluetooth or aux cables, when I’d trust a stranger to play something for me while I was driving. When I’d be on a road trip and an unknown station would fuzz in and play a country song and a public service announcement, when a human being in a studio said something that streaked on radio waves across the desert air, and we were connected, until the station fuzzed out.

Of course, I can still do that. But the convenience culture that has continued to creep in since the Aughts has conditioned me to pull out my phone, wirelessly connect it to the car, and put on a mix or a podcast. I actually have to remind myself that those days were real, when the radio was once our go-to.

I miss those times. The new stuff from U2 is pretty good, though.

TSMC announced at its 2026 Europe OIP Ecosystem Workshop this week that its A14 (1.4nm-class) fabrication technology is ready for customer design work and version 0.9 of its process design kit (PDK) is ready. TSMC also detailed Boost Cells, a way for the new node to deliver 14% higher performance compared to N2P, and how customers can migrate their designs to A13 in the future. Furthermore, Cadence and Synopsys have both completed full design-flow certification on an Arm Cortex-A725 CPU core, which gives chip designers two validated toolchains to build their 14A chips.

"A14 0.9 design ecosystem and the PDK are ready for design in," a TSMC spokesman said on stage at the 2026 Europe OIP Ecosystem Workshop.

A14 uses TSMC's 2nd Generation nanosheet transistors and its NanoFlex Pro standard cell architecture, which supports Boost Cells to enable higher performance as well as Low-Power Cells to reduce power consumption. Designers can place compact high-speed Boost Cells on critical paths (which resembles Intel's Turbo Cells supported by its 14A fabrication technology) and use Low-Power Cells with narrower nanosheets for general logic or parts of the chip that do not need high performance.

In general, TSMC promises A14 with NanoFlex Pro optimizations to deliver a 14% speed gain at the same power or a 23% reduction in total power at the same speed compared to N2P with NanoFlex. In a separate comparison against baseline N2P, TSMC put A14 at 50% higher energy efficiency, 22% higher drawn transistor density, and 23% higher logic density.

A14 also gives customers a path to A13. The foundry says customers can cut die area by about 6% without changes to their A14 design flow. Furthermore, to take even more advantage of A13 and get 5% higher energy efficiency, all the designers have to do is re-engineer the M0 metal layer, reshape OD, and use VT Flex cells (cells that can use different threshold-voltage options for their NMOS and PMOS transistors).

TSMC expects to start risk production using A14 in the second half of 2027, with mass production following in the second half of 2028. Chief executive C.C. Wei said in October that the company had already seen more customer activity on A14 than on N2 at a comparable stage of development and expects A14 to become a bigger node than its 2nm-class predecessor. A13 is set to reach the high-volume manufacturing stage in the second half of 2029.

One of the key parts of the TSMC announcement is the readiness of the 0.9 version of the PDK and the electronic design automation (EDA) tools ecosystem. What matters about v0.9 is that the industry considers this revision ready for design work, which means that customers can start designing chips with confidence that they will meet their performance and power goals. Furthermore, TSMC has validated two complete flows — by Cadence and Synopsys for A14, with Siemens EDA's flow coming later.

Live Science asked its readers what type of archaeological find they'd want to discover.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

From people's backyards to famous excavation sites, archaeological discoveries found the world over give clues about the cultures and lives of ancient peoples. For instance, the discovery of 1,200-year-old pennies in Denmark revealed that the Vikings melted down silver from the Muslim world, while an excavation of a 2,800-year-old tomb with sacrificed horses in Siberia revealed ties to the nomadic Scythians.

Other archaeological discoveries are important due to their historical significance or extraordinary preservation. For example, the 40,000-year-old "Lion Man" statuette was carved by humans in what is now Germany during the last ice age, and it may be the earliest known evidence of religious belief in the world.

Meanwhile, ancient texts can provide clues about mysterious and long-lost languages, such as the Linear A writing system from the ancient Minoans of Crete. Other texts, like the Rosetta stone, helped crack the code of Egyptian hieroglyphics, allowing researchers to better understand this ancient culture.

Each type of archaeological discovery unearths something new about the ancient world. This week, Live Science polled its readers on which of these types of findings they'd most like to uncover.

Over 290 readers weighed in, with 69% choosing that they'd like to discover an ancient text "that helps decipher a language."

The comments also reflected this trend, with one reader writing: "The interpretation of a newly deciphered language can lead to the discovery of other things like tombs and documents, as was the case with the Rosetta stone. Once Egyptologists could read hieroglyphs, they could discover other information which helped them in their research and digs."

Other readers highlighted the importance of finding ancient weapons, which 4% of participants voted for. As one commenter wrote: "War has always been responsible for where people gather and settle down together. Weapons therefore can reveal the ebb and flow of people across the countries, which for many years never existed. This therefore reveals a lot about the specific development in many different areas and ultimately perhaps as to why we actually live where we do."

In a similar vein, one commenter wished that they could "discover Attila's grave site, since no one has found [it] as of today."

Another reader reflected on the importance of finding ancient burial sites, writing: "I think that tombs are the most valuable finds in archaeology."

Do you agree or disagree with these readers? What would you most like to find? Let us know in the comments!

From Saturn's magnificent rings to the waning gibbous moon, here's how to turn your Thanksgiving celebrations into a family skywatching adventure.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

If your household is like most American families, then on Thursday you likely will all be sitting down with family and friends for a traditional Thanksgiving turkey dinner during the mid-to-late afternoon hours.

Then as darkness falls, some will probably migrate into the living room to catch some football or other holiday fare on television. But if you have a large gathering of family and friends at your home — and if your skies are mainly clear — why not invite everyone outside to gaze at the evening sky?

If you have binoculars, or better yet, a telescope, you just might end up turning this into a memorable family tradition of its own.

If I may paraphrase the mysterious voice from the 1989 movie, "Field of Dreams": "If you set up your telescope, they will come." And you'll get a big kick out of your Turkey Day guests uttering all the "oohs" and "ahs" or exclamations of "Wow!" with just one peek through the eyepiece.

As darkness falls, Saturn will glow at first magnitude, shining with a sedate, yellow-white light and appearing noticeably brighter than the star Fomalhaut, about 40 degrees (the equivalent of four fist-widths) to its lower right.

Its famous ring system is now tilted only about six degrees toward Earth but may be visible with steadied (or image-stabilized) high-power binoculars or through the eyepiece of a telescope magnifying over 30-power. The rings will seem to glow with an inner light and for those who have never had the pleasure of seeing them for themselves, that first view always elicits astonishment; kids especially most often will say: "Awesome!" or "No way!" I've even had people accuse me of using a slide of Saturn, to which I, of course, reply with, "No . . . that's the real thing."

Then, along about 6:25 p.m. local time, the waning gibbous moon, 2½ days past full phase and 91% illuminated, will begin to emerge from beyond the east-northeast horizon. Give it another hour and it will stand about 10 degrees above the horizon — equal to the width of your clenched fist held at arm's length. By then, the moon will be in a better position for evening study.

Even in binoculars, the moon is a striking sight to see. It's such a special object because it doesn't matter whether you live in a rural area or in the middle of a brightly lit city, for the moon can always be readily observed. It always looks spectacular whether you're using binoculars or a telescope. It never looks precisely the same no matter how often you view it and can be observed even on hazy or partially cloud-covered nights.

Pay particular attention to the line of darkness — called the terminator — features near that border stand out in bold relief; the shadows become stronger and details are more easily seen. Take note on this night of the circular dark area, bisected by the terminator: Mare Crisium — the "Sea of Crises." Immediately bordering above it is a 50-mile (80 km)-wide ring of very rugged mountains, rising as high as 14,400 feet (4,389 meters), which now resembles a huge crater partially in shadow when the sun is low.

Probably the one object that your Thanksgiving guests will want to see is the biggest and brightest of all the man-made objects orbiting the Earth: the International Space Station (ISS). Except for those living across the southern US, for most everywhere else, the ISS will be making visible passes on Thanksgiving evening.

Circling the Earth at an average altitude of 260 miles (420 km) and at a speed of 17,500 mph (28,200 kph), it can appear to move as fast as a high-flying jet airliner, sometimes taking as much as six or seven minutes to cross the sky. It can easily be confused with aircraft lights. Nominally, it appears white with a slight yellow tinge and at its very brightest it can sometimes appear to shine as bright as magnitude -5.6, which is twice as bright as the planet Venus! Kolodziejczak creates objects in marble, precious stones, and bronze at his workshop in Carrara, Italy.

Kolodziejczak, who started as a jewelry designer in Paris for brands like Givenchy and Saint Laurent, began making decorative objects and furniture after a move to Milan. His exhibition at Ateliers Courbet (on view until January 16, 2027) was inspired by fragments of leftover stone he found in the marble yards of Carrara, not far from his workshop. “Every time I go there I find myself buying new stones,” Kolodziejczak says.

The Further Mis-Adventures Of Cliff Booth Doubles Down On A Once Upon A Time In Hollywood Controversy

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Warning: This article contains spoilers for "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth."

It simply wouldn't be a Quentin Tarantino movie if it didn't double down on itself. After wowing audiences everywhere in 2019 with "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood," the quirky and uber-talented filmmaker returned to the scene of the crime (so to speak) once more with his long-awaited sequel/spin-off film, "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth." This time, the buddy comedy act with Leonardo DiCaprio's Rick Dalton and Brad Pitt's Cliff has been dropped, focusing solely on the latter as the stuntman embarks on his latest round of mischief. Though Tarantino stepped aside from his directing duties to make room for David Fincher, well, let's just say that the script is as purely QT as it gets.

That quickly becomes evident as we're dropped back into this alternate-history world of 1970s Los Angeles, and there's one recurring gag that references a certain controversy from before. You see, despite so much acclaim from critics and fans alike, "Once Upon a Time" drew the ire of many with its irreverent treatment of martial arts legend Bruce Lee, portrayed by Mike Moh in the film. Even Lee's family weighed in on the matter, objecting to how easily Cliff puts the arrogant Lee in his place during a fight scene on the set of a film. Not surprisingly, "Cliff Booth" returns to this fraught topic on several occasions, and it certainly feels like a meta-textual jab at the real-world backlash from before.

Was this in poor taste, or is Tarantino merely poking fun with a self-deprecating running joke? Moviegoers will undoubtedly have strong takes on either side. Here's where we land on our second go-around with this little debate.

You know, there's something to be said for addressing an uncomfortable matter head-on instead of dancing around it — or even ignoring it altogether. Quentin Tarantino has never been one to shy away from a fight, a trait he happens to share with washed-up stuntman Cliff Booth. As a master cinephile, a brilliant bartender, a hit with the ladies, and a ruthless killer all in one, the power fantasy of it all isn't exactly hard to see in "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth." Perhaps that's what made the Bruce Lee moment in "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood" stand out all the more (a fight that almost ended very differently). It's one thing for Cliff to be the coolest person alive in his world, and quite another for him to intrude rudely upon our own.

So how does "Cliff Booth" address this elephant in the room? Although the incident is brought up on a number of occasions in passing, Yahya Abdul-Mateen II's buzzy actor Rodrick "Hot Rod" Harrison is positively fixated on it once he meets Cliff — a fact that also seems to catch the eye of talent manager Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki). Intriguingly, Cliff himself almost seems embarrassed by the attention, perhaps self-reflexively brushing it off as the brash actions of a young man. Maybe, like Tarantino himself, he's a little older and a little wiser and less apt to repeat the same mistakes.

Less generously, the fact that this fight is emphasized so much in "Cliff Booth" could make the exact opposite point. It's treated almost like a legend or a myth in this world, giving Cliff a dangerous sense of aura and mystique that belies his charming appearance.

Either way, this is just one ingredient tossed into a much larger stew. "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth" may struggle to reach the transcendent heights of "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood," but isn't that part of its point, too? "What is Cliff Booth without Rick Dalton?" Quentin Tarantino's script seems to be asking. Not dissimilarly, is a Tarantino movie with David Fincher at the wheel really a Tarantino movie anymore? Who can say how much ownership any of us have over our own art — or our lives in general?

Perhaps that's why "Cliff Booth" feels so much colder and mean-spirited than its predecessor. Although both films build to violent climaxes, the one in "Hollywood" is shot to be much more over-the-top and darkly funny than the one here. Before, Rick Dalton and Cliff were fighting to preserve a very specific way of life — one that the Manson family threatened to overturn. Here, Cliff very easily could've avoided the bloodshed with killers Eddie (Matt Groove) and Fernando Verdusco (JB Tadena), but he pursues this anyway out of ... ego? Principle? A sense of right and wrong? Cliff is a man of both simplicity and contradictions, so perhaps it's only fitting that the filmmaking style reflects this through Fincher's very different approach, as well.

That's where the repeated jabs about the Bruce Lee fight come in, as a moment that Cliff has no choice but to own. "Cliff Booth" is a movie about our attempts to define our own stories — whether it be himself, the troublemaking TV star Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), or Karen. And maybe Bruce Lee, too.

BOWIE, Md., Nov. 26, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- This holiday season, Linkind, a smart lighting brand specializing in connected lighting solutions, donated and installed permanent outdoor lights at Crossfire Worship Center in Bowie, Maryland.

Following installation, Linkind hosted a special lighting reveal at the church on Friday evening, bringing together members of the congregation and local community. Families counted down together and cheered as Linkind's holiday light show began. When the carolers started singing, bands of color moved across the church walls in time with the music. Christmas services bring families to Crossfire Worship Center during the holidays. In the months that follow, the same lights can welcome neighbors back for family gatherings and community events.

As part of its holiday community initiative, Linkind provided Crossfire Worship Center with a complimentary outdoor lighting installation, helping transform the church's exterior into a welcoming illuminated space for its congregation and surrounding community. The lights are permanently mounted along the edge of the church roof, so the church can change its display each holiday without taking them down and rehanging them.
