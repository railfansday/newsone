11-term House rep who mocked a kid’s letter about EVs has lost her seat (Upd.)

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

An 83 year old Congressional representative who sent an embarrassing letter to a well-meaning 10 year old constituent on the topic of electric cars and climate change, where she seems to brag about saddling his generation with debt through a recent climate rollback she voted for, has lost her seat.

Update: This article was originally posted on May 14, but we wanted to update it with news of the election result. After a recount, Chuck Hubbard has defeated Virginia Foxx by 330 votes, and will go on to represent NC-5 in Congress.

Christian Mango is ten years old, and was encouraged by his elementary school class to write a letter about a topic of his choice, and mail it to a decisionmaker. Christian chose his Congressional representative, 83 year old Virginia Foxx.

Foxx is an 11-term Congresswoman in North Carolina, one of the most gerrymandered states in the nation, and was expected to win a 12th term in the heavily republican district, having previously proudly touted an endorsement from an enemy of the US who tried to overthrow the government. Her opponent is Democrat Chuck Hubbard, a former journalist and native of the district.

For the topic of his letter, Christian chose electric cars – which is what makes this story relevant to us here at Electrek. Christian laid out several points about EVs, stating that “they’re better than normal cars. They’re better for the environment. They pay less. And there’s no gas.” (All true! well done Christian).

Christian suggested that electric cars could be incentivized with a $5,000 tax rebate – lower than the $7.5k EVs used to get, but better than the $0 they get federally today (compare to the ~$20k in ignored costs that gas cars still benefit from).

Christian went on to suggest action on climate change, stating “maybe there is still a chance for 6.5 million people to not die anymore and for the weather to get better just maybe glaciers stop melting.” Christian was probably referring to the number of people who die from air pollution globally each year (though several estimates suggest that number is actually higher, and Christian did conflate climate and weather… but we’ll give him a pass on this one, his heart is in the right place).

Christian was proud of his letter, as was his mother, Emily Mango, who said “he researched, cited facts, and made good arguments in his essay – an essay that had nothing to do with politics. He was proud of his essay.”

But then he got a response, and that’s when things took a turn for the worse. Christian’s mother Emily angrily shared the response on Instagram, where it gained traction.

Emily said Foxx’s screed was “disrespectful, hostile and unkind” and called it “demoralizing and reprehensible.” She said she had to speak to her son and teach him that this response was “not okay,” and that “nobody should talk to a child like that and nobody should talk to a teacher like that.”

I’d also add that it’s full of disinformation, and that it even seems to brag about the massive debt that Foxx has imposed on Christian’s generation.

Foxx includes six links, all from propaganda publications that deny the science of climate change. 3 are from from WSJ (2 of which are opinion pieces) and 1 is from Fox, both of which are owned by the same climate denier, Rupert Murdoch. 1 is from Washington Times, a tabloid founded by a religious cult (yes, really), and 1 is from another right-wing climate denying publication, National Review.

Notably, none of the six links have anything to do with electric cars.

In the text of the response, Foxx touts republicans’ “all of the above” energy policy, which is ironic given that the party whose energy policy she touts has spent most of the last year forcing broken dirty coal plants open (which they are still doing after ruled illegal) and trying to shut down clean cheap ones. Apparently “all of the above” is code for “fossil fuels and nothing else.”

Foxx’s letter does not directly address the strengths of electric cars, despite them being the subject of Christian’s letter. Notably, EVs actually can receive energy from any generation method, whether it be wind, solar, or even coal or gas – whereas gas-powered vehicles can only use one type of energy, oil. Thus, an “all of the above” strategy should probably find EVs the preferable route for transportation.

But in apparent contradiction to Foxx’s surely sincerely held belief that we should pursue an “all of the above” energy policy – which electric cars would help to achieve – she in fact has voted against EVs when given the opportunity.

Foxx claims in her response that she can’t support tax credits on electric cars, because those go to “mostly people with higher incomes who are able to purchase ‘new’ electric cars.”

But she in fact voted against, and later voted to repeal, incentives which were limited to lower-income-earners and were only available on cars up to a certain MSRP cap. Further, they were available on used vehicles, not just new ones – suggesting that Foxx doesn’t know what she voted against.

Foxx was one of 218 republicans to vote in favor of a ~$4 trillion giveaway to wealthy elites. Her vote raised electric bills and threatened US energy security and surrendered US manufacturing jobs to China. Some of the money given away to wealthy elites in the bill was clawed back from energy efficiency programs that had been going to regular Americans.

Those energy efficiency programs had been instituted by the Inflation Reduction Act, the Biden-era law which established tax credits for EVs and other efficiency rebates. The Inflation Reduction Act was actually revenue positive, which is to say it reduced the national debt (by $238B), rather than adding to it, because of provisions to increase enforcement against wealthy tax cheats.

But most of the IRA was repealed by the bill that Foxx voted for, meaning the US lost out on those deficit reductions. All in all, the bill Foxx voted for would add $4 trillion to the national debt – which, incidentally, is another point Foxx touches on in her response.

In one sentence, she seems to directly mock Christian, telling him that “YOU and your classmates will be responsible for that debt” as part of a warning about the dangers of government debt.

But I read this sentence as mockery, rather than as a sincere warning, given that Foxx voted to add $4 trillion to the national debt, which she, at 83 years old, knows she will not have to pay off. Christian, who is 10, will likely spend his whole life saddled with the debt Foxx voted for.

He will also have to pay for her inaction on climate change, which will have increasingly worse effects on the world Christian will grow up into. Foxx’s generation has already felt the heat, with the massive floods that devastated her state in 2024, from a hurricane that was made 200x more likely due to climate change. Now, imagine how much worse it could get if Foxx’s policies remained in place until 72 years from now when Christian will be her age.

Foxx then closes out the letter with disparaging remarks about teachers, claiming that they are “too interested in indoctrinating you,” and warning Christian about “propaganda.” Which I guess shows where Foxx’s attitude comes from – if she has spent her 83 years never trusting anyone who knows more than her about a topic, that would certainly lead to the ignorance reflected in her letter.

Foxx – who somehow has a doctorate in education and currently sits on the Congressional education committee – could have turned this into a nice moment to teach the child who wrote her something about democracy and responsive government, but instead turned it into a moment to teach the world what’s wrong with her.

When we first wrote this article in May, Foxx had a projected 97% chance of winning in a district which voted for her by a 19% margin in 2024.

But that was before America (and its short memory) was reminded of how incapable republicans are of leading anything.

After a pile of economic crises caused by Donald Trump’s incompetent “leadership,” and his continued participation in an 8-month-and-counting war of choice that is spiking energy prices worldwide, bringing oil profits to record highs (and attempting to distract the news from talking about the Epstein files, which he is heavily featured in and has tried to cover up), America was once again fed up. And his incompetence took his entire party down with him.

Safe republican seats were flipped around the country in the biggest “wave” election we’ve seen in recent times, with the Democrats gaining control of both the House and Senate.

And it looks like Democrat Chuck Hubbard, a Wilkes county native and former journalist, who also ran against Foxx in 2024, will now get his chance to represent the people of North Carolina’s 5th district, after winning by 330 votes in a recount.

The situation in North Carolina is particularly interesting, given that republicans had done an emergency redraw of district lines in an attempt to cheat their way to winning more seats.

Generally, district lines get redrawn after each Census, which happens on the decade mark, rather than mid-decade. But republicans saw ahead of time that they were likely to lose the House due to their unpopularity, and wanted to fudge the numbers in several states instead of going for a fair fight.

So they engaged in Gerrymandering, a process wherein district lines are redrawn to favor a particular political party. The idea is to put as many of your opponents’ votes into a few safe seats as possible, while spreading out your own votes to a wider range of seats that you win each by a small margin.

Foxx’s main argument against Christian’s suggested $5,000 EV incentive, is increasing national debt. Yet in just one tax bill, her approval increases the national debt over 10%. This is not Christian’s “responsibility”, this burden is forced on him, along with climate change.

But it turns out that republicans were even more unpopular than they thought, and as a result, their Gerrymandering backfired. Those narrow margins they sought ended up flipping more seats than would have otherwise flipped, turning North Carolina almost completely blue in what analysts have called a “Dummymander“. (NC-5 itself was not one of the redrawn districts, though – it just swung 19 whole points to the Dems)

After a nail-bitingly close election, Foxx requested a recount. The margin is extremely thin, just 0.12% of votes, but today Foxx called to concede, making it the last decided House race of the election, giving Democrats a 258-seat majority.

Foxx previously vowed to run again in 2028, hoping for a less hostile political environment. She will be 85 years old at the time of that election.

Gary Neville believes there has been a “remarkable” drop in Manchester United’s standards after the Red Devils sank to a 4-0 thrashing by Liverpool on Sunday.

The club were looking to propel themselves out of the Premier League relegation zone, helped by a bounce from the arrival of new manager Mauricio Pochettino, who took charge of his first match at Anfield.

The Argentine, however, was dealt a nightmare start, as Leny Yoro was sent off inside eight minutes for denying Alexander Isak a clear goalscoring opportunity, and Dominik Szoboszlai, Florian Wirtz, Rio Ngumoha and Victor Munoz got the goals for Andoni Iraola’s brilliant hosts.

“This is the worst Manchester United have ever been in 53 years.

"The club standards have dropped so far, it's incredible.

Amorim proud of ‘intelligent, courageous’ Milan, world-class Rabiot and Ramos

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Ruben Amorim insisted that he and his AC Milan team are not getting carried away despite hammering Frosinone, and are simply focusing on the next game.

Milan were in a rampant mood tonight at San Siro, as they put five goals past Massimiliano Alvini’s side and it could have been more in truth. Goncalo Ramos, Ardon Jashari, Alphadjo Cisse and Christian Pulisic all scored, in addition to an own-goal that opened the scoring.

It was important that the Rossoneri build on their victory over Genoa before the break, especially given that Lazio, Roma, Como and Juventus all won earlier in the round. In addition to that, several individuals shone and the team showed the levels that they are capable of.

Now, the challenge is to follow it up and get back on course in the Europa League in Greece on Thursday night. Amorim spoke to Sky after the final whistle to reflect on what was a very positive night for his side, with his words relayed by MilanNews.

Is that the best Milan performance since you have been here?

“It’s a good feeling, but it’s not just the result: it’s also about consistency throughout the 90 minutes of the match, but now we’re thinking about the next one. It’s always like that at this club, always thinking about the next one.

“Tonight we played well, we were intelligent, courageous, and it showed towards the end of the match. We were tired, yet Frosinone continued to make transitions, but we managed to control them well.

“We could have even scored more goals. Perhaps in the middle of the first half we struggled a little and we need to improve in one-on-one situations defensively, but anyway, it was a good night for us tonight.”

Are you happy for Gonçalo Ramos? How is he?

“We can’t lose any more players. All our players can play every game, but we have little time to recover for the Europa League, so we can’t risk anything. It was better to take him off.

“I imagine he wasn’t happy, but I’m still happy for him, for his goal, especially for the 2-0. It was a goal that made the difference in a match against a team that is very strong in one-on-one transitions.”

Is Rabiot one of the best you’ve ever coached?

“I don’t want to say he could have had a better career because he played at PSG. Now he’s here at Milan and we want him to stay.

“Obviously, he’s a player who can play as a number 10, as an attacking midfielder, he can also play as a number 8, he can also play as a slightly deeper playmaker, even if he doesn’t like it.

“He’s definitely one of the best players I’ve ever coached, he’s one of the best midfielders in Europe right now.”

“The first thing I’ll say is that we have to win the match: if we don’t, then it will be really difficult to qualify for the next stage. Then I think that with Rabiot and Pulisic’s characteristics of playing between the lines, when they can’t play, we have to play and approach the game differently.

“But that’s not an excuse. I said the same things against Ferencváros: we should have played a different game and gotten a different result, but what I’m trying to say is that if we don’t win the match, it will be really difficult and things will go badly for us.

“Then, if we exit the Europa League, we’ll only play once a week and many players won’t play enough. However, I think that if we stay in Europe, we can play more and everyone will continue to grow.

“Furthermore, if we don’t qualify, we will fail to achieve an important objective: so that’s why we have to start the match differently than Benfica and Ferencváros. We’ll see.”

What can you tell us about Cisse as a striker?

“I think he can play very well in that role, in that position. Tonight I chose Cissé to play in that position and not Camarda because against Frosinone, one-on-one, Cissé has these characteristics more. Tonight the goal wasn’t to keep possession, but to run and attack the space.

“Cissé is better at this than Camarda. We wanted to make more transitions than Frosinone, so it was a choice dictated by the player’s characteristics, but I think Cissé is good both as an attacking midfielder and as a central striker, and not just as a winger.”

Figma vs. Fastly: Which Cloud Stock Is a Better Buy for 2027?

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Figma's revenue growth outpaces Fastly's by a wide margin, yet Fastly's lower multiples raise a sharper question about which story deserves your money.

By Brendan Coffey – Updated Nov 22, 2026 at 2:43PM EST

Rapid digital transformation is forcing organizations to prioritize speed and seamless team collaboration. Choosing between Figma (FIG -3.01%) and Fastly (FSLY -11.57%) requires weighing high-growth creative tools against specialized infrastructure.

Figma revolutionized the design process by moving collaboration into the browser, enabling teams to build digital products in real time. Fastly focuses on the delivery side, using its edge cloud to ensure those products reach users quickly and securely. Both companies provide essential services for the modern internet economy, making them frequent points of comparison for investors.

Figma provides a platform for teams to build digital products through collaborative design and prototyping. The company serves designers, developers, and collaborators across various industries, including a significant presence outside the U.S. In its latest annual report, the business highlighted a shift toward enterprisewide procurement and specialized programs like its education platform to drive deeper integration.

In the 12 months ended Sept. 30, 2026, revenue reached about $1.4 billion, which represents a growth rate of approximately 44.4% compared to the prior year. This period saw a net loss of around $562.5 million. The growth trend among tech stocks like this highlights the current demand for specialized design software.

As of its December 2025 balance sheet, the current ratio is roughly 2.6x, indicating the company has sufficient short-term assets to cover its obligations. The debt-to-equity ratio is approximately 0.1x, and free cash flow reached about $246.2 million in the fiscal year ended Dec. 31, 2025. Note that stock-based compensation represented roughly 544% of operating cash flow, meaning reported cash generation is heavily inflated by this non-cash add-back.

Fastly operates an edge cloud platform designed to improve the speed and security of web applications. Major customers include media giants like TikTok, specifically the TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC, and it maintains a commercial partnership with Comcast (CMCSA +0.16%). Customer concentration like this adds a layer of risk to the business.

In the 12 months ended Sept. 30, 2026, revenue was around $719.1 million, reflecting growth of roughly 21.5% over the previous period. During this time, the company reported a net loss of approximately $62.9 million. This performance suggests a stabilization in operating results as the company scales its delivery and security services.

As of its December 2025 balance sheet, the current ratio is roughly 2.6x, and the debt-to-equity ratio is approximately 0.5x. In the fiscal year ended Dec. 31, 2025, free cash flow was around $65.8 million. Note that stock-based compensation represented roughly 124.2% of operating cash flow, meaning reported cash generation is heavily inflated by this non-cash add-back.

Figma faces intense competitive pressure from legacy players like Adobe Systems Inc. (ADBE -1.79%) and larger tech giants like Microsoft Corp. (MSFT -2.77%). The company is also navigating regulatory scrutiny, including a voluntary disclosure regarding potential export control issues. Managing rapid growth and the transition to usage-based pricing for AI features creates uncertainty around future revenue visibility.

Fastly carries significant customer concentration risk, as a large portion of its revenue depends on a few major clients. The company faces stiff competition from public cloud vendors like Amazon.com Inc. (AMZN -4.10%) and specialized rivals such as Cloudflare (NET -9.46%). Operational risks like service interruptions or supply chain delays for hardware components could also impact its ability to deliver performance.

Fastly appears to be the more value-oriented choice based on its lower multiples, while Figma trades at a premium that reflects its faster revenue expansion. Figma has seen its user experience design solution become the industry standard for digital asset and mobile application development. The company uses this niche dominance to introduce additional modules, helping transform it from a single-point solution into a full design platform.

One differentiator from competitors is that Figma has done an excellent job not only presenting itself to developers, but also to marketers and product managers who have to collaborate on designs. That gives the company more champions in an organization for its products.

For the current fiscal year, revenue should grow quickly, at about 39%, to $1.47 billion. Look for a dramatic slashing of its net loss, from nearly $1.3 billion to about $560 million. Wall Street still sees similarly sized losses ahead for the foreseeable future, however.

Meanwhile, Fastly processes over 5 trillion requests a day, a huge number that is actually just a small part of the total addressable market (TAM) for the business. Management sees the TAM as $22 billion, giving it a long runway for growth. The company has posted three straight quarters of more than 20% growth (year-over-year).

One of the company's competitive edges, it says, is that it delivers its resources dynamically, allocating them in real time to whatever the global load is. So, for example, one Thursday night, its network might be delivering Thursday Night Football. On Black Friday, the exact same network is handling e-commerce. The next week, it might be handling a video game release. Fastly says that makes it a much more efficient network than competitors' siloed networks for security, compute, and delivery, et cetera.

For the current fiscal year, look for sales to grow to $747 million, a 20% rise over the prior year, with a much narrower net loss of about $56 million.

So which cloud tech stock is the better buy? Keep the decision simple by purchasing growth stocks at more reasonable prices. In this case, it is Fastly, which has the lower forward P/E and P/S ratios of the pair.

6 Underrated Mountain Towns to Visit This Winter

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

You know the big names. Jackson, Wyoming. Stowe, Vermont. Telluride, Colorado. These are the mountain towns that dominate our winter daydreams. The famous ski towns that make us consider taking a sabbatical, buying a van, and living the Aspen Extreme dream for three months in the heart of winter. But there are other winter adventure spots that deserve the spotlight.

I cover mountain towns for Outside, and every year I start plotting out my own winter getaways right about now. Sure, I enjoy hitting up established destinations like Park City as much as the next skier, but the towns on this list get me excited because they fly under the radar. They don’t receive a ton of hype, but they have access to amazing powder stashes, lengthy trail systems, and resort lines that are far shorter than their better-known counterparts’.

A couple of these towns are my longtime favorites, while others are still on my “must-go” list. Here are six often overlooked winter mountain towns that you need to visit once the snow starts to gather.

A legitimate winter town in the South? Yes indeed. Boone is a vibrant college town that grows when Appalachian State’s roughly 20,000 students are in session. It has all of the hallmarks of a fun-loving mountain town, with plenty of dive bars and burrito joints to keep you satiated. But it also sits in North Carolina’s High Country, surrounded by 5,000- and 6,000-foot mountains, which get some of the heaviest snowfalls in the region. There are three ski resorts within 30 minutes of downtown and some of the most consistent cross-country skiing in the South on a series of 6,000-foot peaks near the Tennessee border.

Winter Adventures: You have three downhill resorts to choose from, but Beech Mountain Resort is the highest ski resort in eastern North America, with a summit elevation of 5,506 feet. It also has a bar, the 5506′ Skybar, at the top of the mountain and its own brewery in the base village. As for the terrain, the mountain has 95 acres and just over 800 feet of drop, so you’ll need to do laps to get in your vert. But the terrain park gets better every year, and skiers can usually count on about 80 inches of snow every winter. Full-day adult lift tickets start at $57 on weekdays.

But the best winter adventure near Boone is cross-country skiing Roan Mountain, a high ridge that tops out at 6,285 feet, gets heavy snowfall, and has a variety of terrain to ski, from high-elevation grassy balds to singletrack through rhododendron tunnels. The only catch is you’ll need to bring your own skis, as rentals are scarce in the South. Start at Carver’s Gap and head up the gated Forest Service road, which climbs gradually for about two miles to the Rhododendron Gardens. You’ll get long-range views in both directions and the option to hop onto more challenging trails that branch off the road.

Duluth is a longtime shipping and industrial city at the western tip of Lake Superior that has found new life as an adventure hub, with a world-class mountain bike trail system traversing the hills on the edge of downtown. But the city’s location in northern Minnesota means winter hits the city hard. Duluth gets about 86 inches of lake-effect snow each winter, and that snow sticks around because high temperatures stay below freezing on more than 100 days a year. The constant cold temps are ideal for ice climbing, and the consistent snow and cornucopia of mountain bike trails mean Duluth becomes a fat-biking and cross-country ski mecca during winter.

Need more reasons to visit? How about downhill skiing at nearby Spirit Mountain, which has 175 acres of terrain with 700 feet of vertical drop.

Winter Adventures: Northern Minnesota has a legitimate ski culture, and Spirit Mountain has some of the best blue cruisers in the Midwest, many of which have long-range views of downtown Duluth and Lake Superior. The mountain also has two terrain parks, one for beginners looking to progress and one for shredders looking to send it. There’s a welcoming uphill policy for anyone who wants to earn their turns on touring gear, too. Adult tickets start at $83.

But Duluth is a bike town, and when the snow falls that means it’s time to roll out the fat tires. Many sections of the Duluth Traverse, an epic 40-mile-long route that runs from one side of the city to the other, are groomed for fat bikes in the winter. The machine-built trails in Mission Creek, with their berms and rollers, are particularly fun with a layer of packed snow. Local outfitter Day Tripper offers guided fat-bike tours and rentals.

New England got hammered with snow last winter, with the highest elevations in the mountains getting more than 200 inches of snow, and the tiny town of Jackson was poised to make the most of it. This picture-perfect village, which comes complete with a general store and covered bridge, sits on the edge of White Mountain National Forest with fast access to downhill skiing at Black Mountain and roughly 90 miles of cross-country ski trails scattered throughout the valley.

Jackson doesn’t have the cachet of some other ski towns in New England, but American ski culture got an early boost here when Black Mountain opened in 1935, making it one of the first lift operations in the country. And that culture is still thriving. Case in point: check out Friday Night Lights, a weekly uphill party where skiers put on headlamps and ski to the top of the mountain en masse.

Winter Adventures: The Jackson Ski Touring Foundation grooms and maintains about 150 kilometers of trails, all surrounding a touring center with rentals and lessons. The trails traverse the community throughout three consecutive river valleys over 60 square miles. You’ll get views of Mount Washington from open meadows, tight singletrack through woods, and even the chance to hit trailside restaurants in certain spots. Day passes start at $29.

No trip to Jackson is complete without some laps at Black Mountain, a proudly independent ski resort with 140 acres of glades, narrow groomers, and nonexistent lift lines. You’ll get 1,100 feet of vertical drop, with the steepest terrain at the summit, and a lively pub (the Shovel Handle Pub) at the base of the mountain for après-ski activities. Adult lift tickets start at $49.

The San Juan Mountains are home to some of Colorado’s most iconic mountain towns and most well-regarded ski slopes, but most skiers don’t explore the region beyond Telluride and Silverton. Dig deeper into the mountains of southwest Colorado and you’ll find tiny Pagosa Springs, which sits on the banks of the San Juan River. The hamlet is best known for its geothermal springs, but the mountains surrounding this hot-water hub are even more enticing. Pagosa Springs is 23 miles from Wolf Creek Ski Area, which is known as one of the snowiest mountains in Colorado, pulling down an average of 430 inches of snow every winter.

Want more variety? Summer waterfalls turn into ice-climbing playgrounds when the temperature drops, and the local Nordic club grooms more than 50 kilometers of skate and classic ski trails near town.

Winter Adventures: Forget the champagne huts and concierge service you find at many ski resorts these days. Wolf Creek is a no-frills operation that’s all about the snow and the terrain, with 430 inches of annual snowfall that blanket wide groomers and steep trees. OK, a lot of trees. Wolf Creek has a significant amount of expert terrain, which plays out in a series of steep bowls at the top of the resort and seemingly endless glades all over the mountain. And you won’t have to take out a loan to fund this trip; full-day adult lift tickets at Wolf Creek are right at $110.

The 50 kilometers of cross-country ski trails begin in town, with a mile-long loop inside the town park that’s perfect for beginners. But this is a great chance to dip a toe into fat biking. There’s an 8.5-mile loop that’s groomed for fat bikes when snow allows, winding through a ponderosa pine forest. Pagosa Mountain Sport rents cross-country skis and fat bikes.

Park City, Salt Lake City…these are the ski towns that get the press, but Ogden? Ogden doesn’t typically make the list. But it should. This historic city, which was the first permanent settlement by people of European descent in the state of Utah, sits between the Great Salt Lake and the Wasatch Mountains, just 40 minutes north of Salt Lake City, but seemingly a world apart. Downtown Ogden is anchored by 25th Street, a historic main street with upscale Wild West charm, while three ski resorts known for deep powder are less than 30 minutes away. Powder Mountain anchors the trio, with more than 8,000 acres of terrain, but Snowbasin is continually ranked one of the best ski resorts in the country by Ski magazine and the smaller Nordic Valley has a local-hill vibe complete with night skiing. Bonus: You don’t have to fight Salt Lake City’s notorious canyon traffic to make your turns.

Winter Adventures: You’re here to ski, so get after it at Powder Mountain, the largest ski resort in the Lower 48. Powder has 2,800 acres of lift-served terrain with more than 2,000 feet of vertical drop, but the real gem here is Powder’s sidecountry. Head through the Powder Country or Woody’s World entrance gates from the lift-served side of the mountain and you’ll have 1,200 acres of ungroomed, off-piste action at your ski tips. The best part? A shuttle bus picks you up at the bottom of your line and takes you back to the front of the mountain, so there’s no boot-packing involved. Adult lift tickets start at $99.

You might also want to try your hand at sledding AKA snowmobiling while you’re in Ogden. The Monte Cristo area, east of the Ogden Valley, has some of the best snowmobile terrain in the state, including a 63-mile groomed loop that’s perfect for beginners and has long-range views of the Ogden Valley. Club Rec North offers tours and rentals next to the trailhead. Tours with rentals start at $229.

This former gold-mining post in the North Cascades really leans into its historic roots, with a downtown that’s lined with Old West-style false fronts and wooden boardwalks. And that western heritage is still alive and well. A number of outfitters offer horseback adventures and the annual Ride to Rendezvous, a four-day horse-packing trip complete with a wagon train that rolls into town for Winthrop’s ’49er Days parade. But make no mistake, Winthrop has found new life as a ski town. Well, a skinny-ski town. The Methow Valley surrounding Winthrop has one of the most extensive cross-country trail networks in the country with more than 120 miles of groomed, marked and maintained paths to explore.

Looking for a cozy vibe for the whole family? Take a spin around Winthrop Rink, the town’s outdoor ice-skating rink that serves as the social hub for the season.

Winter Adventures: If you’re spending time in Winthrop, then you’re here to ski the Methow Trails. With more than 120 miles of obsessively groomed paths, there is something for everyone in this valley. Want to ski from lodge to lodge? That’s an option. Want a pretty loop around a lake? No problem. Want to spend the day climbing an obscene amount of vertical? They have you covered. The Methow Trails organization runs clinics and camps all season if you want to improve your kick-and-glide form or maybe try your hand at biathlon. Day passes start at $32.

Loup Loup Ski Bowl is a lift-served mountain with 300 acres that’s operated by a nonprofit. There are 10 runs with 1,240 vertical feet of drop. You’ll find beginner trails and expert trails that drop from the top of the mountain and a very welcoming uphill ski culture if you want to earn your turns. And you’ve got to check out the Ski Bowl’s luge course, where you guide an old-school sled down groomed trails. Adult lift tickets start at $80.

Graham Averill is Outside magazine’s national parks columnist. He loves warm-weather adventures, but he gets absolutely giddy when ski season approaches, and writing articles like this helps him plan his ski trips.

Movement can change how quickly you learn from the same visual clues

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In a virtual reality study, swinging a lightsaber or throwing a punch helped volunteers learn visual patterns that required combining two features.

In the textbook account, learning happens in the head, and the hands do nothing more than report the answer.

Volunteers learned to sort striped discs more accurately when they answered by swinging a virtual lightsaber or punching a virtual target.

The advantage appeared when they had to judge the angle and thickness of the stripes together.

In the punching experiment, volunteers kept their lead when they switched to button presses immediately after training.

For sorting tasks that could be solved with explicit rules, bigger movements gave volunteers no clear advantage.

Luke Rosedahl is an assistant professor of biomedical engineering in Florida Atlantic University‘s College of Engineering and Computer Science.

Professor Rosedahl conducted the experiments at Brown University with Yuka Sasaki and Takeo Watanabe.

The 160 participants were young adults recruited around Brown, sorting striped discs in a headset, so how far the result reaches outside a lab is still open.

“We found that larger movements substantially improved learning when people had to recognize patterns by integrating multiple features, but provided no meaningful benefit when people learned categories using explicit rules,” Rosedahl said.

“Even more intriguing, the benefit occurred whether participants swung at the target or punched a separate response box. The movement itself appeared to matter, not whether it directly interacted with what they were learning.”

Some categories come with a rule. If it has whiskers and meows, it’s a cat. Psychologists call these rule-based categories, and people learn them by testing hypotheses, which is thought to lean on the front of the brain.

Other categories can’t be put into words. A radiologist’s sense that a lesion looks aggressive comes from weighing several features at once, and so does a driver’s read on whether a road is icy.

People learn these by comparing each new case with ones they’ve seen.

That kind of learning is thought to run through the striatum, a deep brain structure tied to movement and habits.

Both kinds were tested with the same material: gray discs striped at different angles and thicknesses, shown one at a time in a virtual reality hallway.

For the rule-based version, thickness and angle could be judged separately. For the other version, the dividing line ran diagonally through both, so no single feature gave the answer.

In the first experiment, 80 people each sorted 600 discs into two groups over 12 blocks, with the disc flashing green or red after every answer. Half answered by pulling a controller trigger.

The other half swung a virtual lightsaber and hit the disc.

On the hard, no-rule categories, the swingers pulled ahead by the second block and stayed ahead for most of the session.

On the rule-based categories, the swingers did very slightly worse, by an amount that could have been chance.

A swing takes longer than a button press, and it touches the thing being judged. Either could have been the real cause.

In a second experiment with 80 new volunteers, some punched a virtual response box separate from the disc, while others pulled a controller trigger.

Every disc stayed on screen for 2.5 seconds, however quickly they answered.

The rule-based task was also made harder, with two rules to discover instead of one, in case the first version had simply been too easy to show a difference.

Punching still helped on the no-rule categories and still did nothing for the rule-based ones. Slower answers went with worse accuracy, not better, so taking extra time wasn’t the reason.

In the final 100 training trials of the task that required combining both features, volunteers who punched got 90.7% of answers right, compared with 81.0% for those who pressed a button.

Then everyone switched. The punchers moved to buttons and scored 89.5%, almost unchanged. The button group moved to punching and scored 83.1%.

Whatever the big movements had built was still there when the movement stopped, which points to better learning rather than a boost at the moment of answering.

The authors can’t say what the arm is doing for the brain, and they said so. They offered two possibilities.

One possibility is that more effortful movements increase dopamine release in the striatum, which could help reinforce learning.

Effortful actions have also been shown to steer attention toward the features that matter. And movement has helped in other corners of learning, the authors wrote, such as writing by hand to learn to recognize letters.

The selectivity is the clue. If swinging had simply made people more engaged, Rosedahl’s team wrote, both kinds of categories should have improved.

The benefit appeared only when people had to combine visual features, a kind of learning thought to involve motor circuits.

The result also raises a question about decades of earlier work, nearly all of it done with button presses.

Those studies found that no-rule categories are learned slowly and that written instructions on how to perform them don’t help.

The authors suggested that the buttons themselves may have been holding that learning back.

The team didn’t record which hand participants favored, and controllers were handed out in no particular order, so the role of handedness and motor experience is unmeasured.

Other category types, such as rules with exceptions, may respond differently. And everything happened with participants seated and a headset on.

The team wrote that the next studies should test the effect in real training, in fields such as radiology and aviation.

There, the judgments that can’t be put into words are the ones that take years to learn. Rosedahl framed the stakes in one line.

“Incorporating movement could offer a simple way to improve learning in situations where conventional approaches fall short,” he said.

Whether a bigger swing of the arm can speed up a radiologist’s eye, and not only a student’s sorting of striped discs, is the test that comes next.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
