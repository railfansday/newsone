Defamation Counterclaim Arising out Reagan National Helicopter Crash Case Can Go Forward

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

[A.] From Ellis v. Wallace, decided today by Chief Magistrate Judge Scott Varholak (D. Colo.):

On January 29, 2025, a Black Hawk Helicopter and American Airlines Flight 5342 collided mid-air near Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport. Hours after the crash, several posts and articles appeared claiming Plaintiff was the helicopter pilot in the midair collision ("the rumor"), including:

Defendant made his first X post addressing the rumor on January 31, 2025, at 1:00 a.m. Eastern Time. And Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this matter on April 9, 2025, alleging that Defendant's posts were defamatory. Plaintiff then proceeded to appear as a guest on several podcasts. On July 18, 2025, she was interviewed on the Kill Tony podcast. During the interview, the hosts asked Plaintiff about the collision rumor and her pending litigation. Plaintiff explained that Defendant was the "[f]irst one to tie my likeness to the rumor. We don't know if he started the rumor, but he had millions of followers. He did the most what we can determine as damage."

Ten days later, Defendant filed the Special Motion to Dismiss Plaintiff's Complaint, attaching copies of X posts and articles linking Plaintiff to the crash hours before Defendant commented on the rumor. Plaintiff then appeared on a second podcast, the RVA Magazine podcast, on September 30, 2025. Plaintiff was again asked about the rumor and her pending litigation. In response, she stated:

[W]ithin 24 hours of the crash, I was finding out that I was being named as as the pilot as, like, the unnamed female pilot that was the supposed DEI hire that Trump, President Trump had alluded to at a press conference that "we know it's DEI because we just know." And so that kind of sent the online world into a frenzy of like, let's figure out who this pilot is. And, from what we can tell, [Defendant] picked up on that and used a picture of me and the [Michael] Smerconish article and, um, kind of put it all together. And with his following, he had, I think, two and half million followers or something like that, we believe he did the most damage. Like it would not have had the legs it had if he hadn't posted about it.

Finally, on January 12, 2026, Kim Congdon interviewed Plaintiff on The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast. Congdon asked Plaintiff if she was suing the individual that started the rumor. Plaintiff responded:

I don't, yeah, I don't know if he started it, but he definitely, like, he pushed it. He's the first one to tie my likeness to it. Yeah. His name's Matt Wallace and, uh, he, uh, millions of followers on Twitter, and he pushed it pretty hard and we believe he did the most damage and so I'm suing him in federal court."

On April 6, 2026, Defendant filed the Counterclaim alleging that these podcast statements were defamatory.

Defendant alleges that Plaintiff made repeated defamatory statements that: (1) Defendant "was the first one to post [Plaintiff's] likeness in connection with the helicopter crash," and (2) that Plaintiff believes Defendant's "statements caused the most damage." {Plaintiff alleges that there is a material difference between claiming that Defendant was the first to name Plaintiff in relation to the tragedy, and that Defendant was the first one to post Plaintiff's likeness in connection with the collision. The distinction is unclear to the Court. Accordingly, the Court assesses Plaintiff's "likeness" statements as conveying Defendant was the first to connect Plaintiff to the collision.}

[B.] The court rejected Plaintiff's arguments that Plaintiff's statements were protected by the "litigation privilege." That privilege bars defamation lawsuits based on statements in court filings and in-court statements, but doesn't apply to statements about the lawsuit made outside the litigation.

(Recall that, though in a typical case it would be a defendant who's arguing the statements were privileged, opinion, true, etc., and plaintiff would be responding, here the roles are flipped because the defendant is counterclaiming against plaintiff based on plaintiff's statements.)

[C.] The court also rejected Plaintiff's arguments that the statements were nonactionable opinion: It concluded that both statements were "susceptible of being proved true or false," and were framed as factual assertions rather than using "loose or hyperbolic language that provided the impression that her statements should not be taken seriously."

[D.] The court concluded that Defendant had introduced sufficient evidence that the statements were false:

First, with respect to Plaintiff's "likeness" statements, Plaintiff repeatedly claimed that Defendant was "the first one to tie [her] likeness to the rumor." However, Defendant provided several pieces of evidence showing that other X users linked Plaintiff to the rumor before Defendant….

Second, Defendant has shown a reasonable likelihood of proving by clear and convincing evidence that Plaintiff's "most damage" statements were false. Defendant declares that "the rumor was spread primarily by much larger voices than [Defendant's,] such as Ann Coulter's, Alex Jones's, and others." He claims that these "prominent individuals and entities" had a "far greater reach than anything [he] posted" about Plaintiff. If true, this declaration could provide clear and convincing evidence that Defendant's "most damage" statements were false. At the very least, it creates a question for the jury since Plaintiff has offered competing evidence. …

[E.] The court also concluded that Defendant had introduced sufficient evidence that Plaintiff knew the "likeness"/"first one to tie" statements were probably false (which is required by Colorado law in such cases):

First, Plaintiff's statements on the RVA Magazine podcast and The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast occurred after Defendant filed his Special Motion to Dismiss and Plaintiff filed her response. In his Special Motion to Dismiss, Defendant included evidence of other X users linking Plaintiff to the rumor before Defendant's first post. And Plaintiff's podcast statements referred to the ongoing litigation. Thus, it is plausible to infer that Plaintiff had a high degree of awareness of the probable falsity of her statements.

Second, Plaintiff's statements reveal inconsistencies in her account of the underlying events. During her interview on the Kill Tony podcast and The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast, Plaintiff repeatedly stated that she did not know whether Defendant started the rumor despite claiming he was "the first one to tie [her] likeness to it." Plaintiff repeated these conflicting statements in her declaration. was the first to blame [her] for this horrific crash because [she] clearly knew that information was incorrect.")] Consequently, such inconsistencies weigh in favor of finding that Defendant has shown a reasonable likelihood of proving actual malice by clear and convincing evidence….

But the court concluded there wasn't enough evidence of knowing or reckless falsehood as to the "most damage" statement:

Defendant declares that at the time Plaintiff made her public statements, she knew "prominent individuals and entities" had discussed her connection with the crash and that those individuals had a "far greater reach than anything [Defendant] posted." However, such evidence does not support an inference that Plaintiff entertained serious doubts as to the truth of the statements or was highly aware of their probable falsity. Further, Defendant provides no other evidence to support his actual malice claim here.

On the other hand, the mere fact that the actor stated that the podcaster did the most harm—even knowing the New York Times had a broader audience—does not necessarily equate to actual malice; the actor could easily believe (even if wrongfully) that the podcaster caused the most harm because the podcaster gave credibility to the false rumor and thus led others to run with the story. Here, Defendant has only presented evidence that those with broader audiences likewise posted the false story. But, as the hypothetical shows, this does not necessarily mean that Plaintiff's statement that Defendant caused the most damage was made with actual malice.}

[F.] Finally, the court concludes that Defendant had sufficiently alleged damages, as required by Colorado law:

Specifically, he alleged that Plaintiff's statements have caused: (1) "significant stress and strain in [his] family relations," and (2) stress from his alleged tarnished reputation. Although Plaintiff claims that that Defendant asserted these damages "in conclusory fashion," the Court finds the presented evidence sufficient to satisfy Defendant's burden at this stage.

The court thus denies Plaintiff's motion to dismiss as to the "likeness"/"first one to tie" statements, which means Defendant's counterclaim can go forward (though of course it doesn't mean the Defendant's counterclaim will ultimately prevail).

Opinion: Arne Slot axed Liverpool star who is perfect for Andoni Iraola

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arne Slot's time as Liverpool's manager was fairly mixed.

He won the Premier League title during his first season in charge but then, just a year later, he was relieved of his duties.

Most fans wanted his sacking to occur months earlier but, Fenway Sports Group chose to stick by him and, luckily, he managed to get his men to qualify for the Champions League.

His fifth-place finish was clearly miles away from where Liverpool actually needed to be which is why Andoni Iraola was brought in.

And, while a lot of people thought his time at Anfield would be smooth-sailing, there are still a lot of changes he needs to make before his team are properly ready to compete for silverware again.

The main issues that need to be rectified are the performances of some players with the January transfer window the best way for them to fix the issues that they're suffering with.

But, while Slot was in charge, he had the answer to one of Iraola's main problems but, instead of keeping him around, he chose to move him on with fans now wondering why that decision was ever made.

Liverpool must figure out what they're doing with their midfield

Liverpool's main problem area among their first-team squad at the moment is their midfield.

They currently have four options to choose from as their number sixes and, while Alexis Mac Allister and Trey Nyoni have put in some solid performances, Dominik Szoboszlai and Ryan Gravenberch have both failed to do the same.

The duo were the two midfielders offered long-term contracts by FSG last season with each of them being handed bumper new deals.

Clearly, Liverpool expected them to be their regular midfield duo but, that has been far from the case.

Mac Allister and Nyoni have both performed far better than their teammates and, it feels like they should have been the ones given new contracts instead.

However, that isn't what happened and now, Iraola and Julian Ward seem to be stuck between a rock and a hard place.

So many positions within Liverpool's squad still need upgrading but, if Slot and Richard Hughes had kept one player they sold in the 2025-26 summer window, they'd have one less position to worry about.

Tyler Morton would have been perfect for Andoni Iraola

As mentioned, Liverpool's clear-out last season was taken differently depending on who you asked.

Some people believed that selling players such as Luis Diaz, Tyler Morton and Jarell Quansah was the best thing to do.

But, looking back now, all three of them had incredible campaigns last season and each of them would have been a huge help to Slot.

And, of the lot, Morton is probably the one that Liverpool wish they didn't let go, especially since he's the perfect defensive midfielder for Iraola's system.

Not only can he win duels, keep his line and add extra stability to a team but, he can also create chances and score them if needed.

Alongside all this, he's also a really hard-working central midfielder so, the fact that each of these skills line up perfectly with Iraola's system is a real kick in the teeth.

Obviously, what's done is done and these things can't be changed now but, in hindsight, selling a Liverpool-born midfielder who wanted nothing else but to play for his childhood club was maybe not the Reds' best idea.

That is, especially when they already knew that their midfield was going to be a problem for them.

Cold Plunges Are Finding a New Place in Residential Design

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Once relegated to backyards and home gyms, cold plunges are finding a new place in residential design. As the wellness practice grows in popularity, homeowners are increasingly looking to incorporate cold therapy directly into their bathrooms, transforming what was once a standalone piece of fitness equipment into an integrated feature of the home.

The interest goes beyond athletic recovery. Cold-water immersion has been associated with potential benefits ranging from reduced muscle soreness to improved sleep and stress management, although research into its broader health effects remains limited. Still, the appeal is evident. According to Houzz’s 2024 Summer Trends Report, searches for “cold plunge” increased 74% year over year, while searches for “indoor sauna” rose 69%.

In the Heber, Utah, residence by Berg Design Studio, Hydro Systems’ Marquis bathtub demonstrates how traditional freestanding tubs can accommodate emerging wellness technologies, including cold plunging. Photography by Steve C. Wilson.

That interest reflects a larger shift toward wellness-focused residential spaces. The company’s 2026 report found that searches for “wellness room” had increased 164%, suggesting that homeowners are thinking more intentionally about how their environments can support relaxation and well-being.

For interior designers, these changing priorities are introducing new considerations into residential projects. Arizona-based designer Stephanie Larsen, who incorporates cold plunges, infrared saunas, and integrated aromatherapy systems into her work, has seen clients place greater emphasis on these features. “Today’s clients are craving homes that heal and inspire,” she told Luxe Interiors + Design. “They want spaces infused with wellness technology.”

Accommodating those requests means thinking beyond individual fixtures to how different wellness experiences function together. Cold plunges, for instance, are often paired with saunas for contrast therapy, which alternates between hot and cold exposure. Bringing both into a residential setting requires careful consideration of available space, plumbing, and the relationship between the different elements. Manufacturers, meanwhile, are developing more integrated systems that offer greater flexibility.

Hydro Systems integrates cold-plunge technology into freestanding bathtubs, bringing cold therapy into residential bathrooms without the need for separate equipment. Photography courtesy of Hydro Systems.

“We’re seeing wellness become a more intentional part of residential design, with dedicated spaces that bring together multiple hydrotherapy experiences in a single tub, from cold plunging and whirlpool to oxygenated microbubble therapy,” says Ashley Steinhardt, VP of Sales at Hydro Systems.

The California-based bath manufacturer has developed its Hydro Chill technology to incorporate cold plunging into customizable bathtubs, eliminating the need for a separate fixture. Its latest iteration also introduces smart-home functionality, allowing users to adjust water temperatures and schedule plunges remotely.

Hydro Systems is also exploring how multiple hydrotherapy experiences can share the same footprint. Its new Hydro Chill Ultra delivers both hot and cold water through a self-contained system that generates its own hot water. Because it doesn’t require a traditional hot-water connection, the technology offers additional flexibility for installations in pool houses and dedicated wellness rooms.

Featured in an Atlanta Homes Showhouse, Hydro Systems’ Slate bathtub features a custom color and polished finish, with integrated Hydro Chill cold-plunge technology. Paired with a wood-paneled sauna, the tub demonstrates how cold therapy can become part of a thoughtfully designed residential wellness space. Photography courtesy of Hydro Systems.

While expansive home spas provide room for separate saunas, plunge tubs, and other amenities, integrating hydrotherapy into an existing bathroom requires a different approach. Multifunctional fixtures can help reduce the space needed for these experiences, making them easier to accommodate within conventional residential layouts.

“Wellness doesn’t have to be confined to a dedicated room,” Steinhardt says. “Hydrotherapy can also be incorporated into smaller bathroom footprints, bringing wellness into everyday routines.”

Hydro Systems’ Hydro Chill cold-plunge system is incorporated into a home gym alongside weight-training equipment, bringing cold-water immersion into a dedicated fitness and recovery space. Photography courtesy of Hydro Systems.

Cold plunges may still be a relatively niche residential amenity, but their growing visibility is prompting designers to reconsider what a bathroom can accommodate. As more wellness features make their way into the home, the challenge will be integrating them without compromising the functionality, comfort, and aesthetics of the spaces around them.

Follow This ‘Map of Quietude’ to the Most Silent Trails in Sweden

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Taking in one of the many gorgeous views along the quiet Swedish coast (Photo: Andrea Bussell)

The invitation piqued my interest as soon as it landed in my inbox: “A beachfront cabin where you can stay for free,” it read, describing a remote spot on the Baltic coast in southwest Sweden. “The only condition? You have to stay quiet.” Surrounded by apple orchards, with the sea just out the door, this idyllic place would be available to a few lucky travelers—as long as we maintained an air of “quietude” during our respective stays.

Outfitted with sound-measuring technology to monitor noise levels inside, the unique setup of the Stay Quiet Cabin is part of a broader initiative by Visit Skåne (pronounced skaw-nuh) to combat one of Europe’s most significant environmental health issues: noise pollution. According to the European Commission, prolonged exposure to loud, chronic noise is the second largest cause of health problems contributing to cardiovascular disease, sleep disturbances, and stress-related health concerns. Spending time in naturally quiet environments can help relieve those symptoms.

Skåne is a region renowned for its restorative pastoral and seaside landscapes, unique rural culture, farm-to-table restaurants, and heritage traditions. Within it, The Map of Quietude—a list of 26 places mapped by a professional sound engineer—leads to many spots along Skåne’s 1,000+ miles of hiking trails. Each spot’s silence has been measured to ensure distinctly healing experiences.

I found that pausing in them to take in the stillness and beauty added something beyond a typical travel or hiking experience. It gave me insight into the true frequency of a place and what it offers: peace, an immersion in rejuvenating slowness, and awe. After my days in silence on the coast, I’ve rounded up some best trails where you can easily experience these locations of quietude, while also taking in interesting communities, historic sites, and some of Scandinavia’s most beautiful wildernesses. Sweden’s nature-centric way of life includes the right of public access, or allemansrätten, which allows hiking or camping almost anywhere in the countryside with the expectation that you will respect the land.

Distance: 7.5 miles Part of the Öresund Way, this loop is worth a detour from inland Skåne to discover a car-free island where people come to bike and hike. You can also get some of the region’s best cardamom buns at Hven Durum, a family-run bakery and mill.

A quick ferry will bring you to this tiny slip of seaside heaven. Walk along the coastline and through a species-rich nature reserve for sweeping views of the Öre Sound and Copenhagen skyline. Ven’s southern headland, södra udde, is a nature reserve and designated place of quietude. See an active, mid-20th-century lighthouse nearby. Or, check out the site of 16th-century Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe’s observatory.

Distance: 7.4 Miles (bonus summit extends to 11.4 miles)

Starting at the edge of the Kulla peninsula in northwest Skåne, you’ll trace roughly seven and a half miles of mostly flat shoreline south from the small fishing village Mölle. One of Sweden’s first seaside resorts at the turn of the 19th century, city dwellers came here for their nature fix. Back then, Mölle was steeped in scandal when, after long hikes, men and women began swimming together in the sea nearby, something that was unthinkable at the time.

The route explores nature reserves and gnarled pine forests and winds past beaches and historic towns. You’ll even pass remains of Kulla fälad, a vast historic pasture that served as a shared grazing land for livestock for centuries. In some places, ancient drystone walls are still visible. You’ll end in Höganäs, a globally renowned center of stoneware and ceramics production. It’s also a great place to explore a burgeoning creative community. Stop by The Hub Höganäs to experience local life, where artists sell heritage-steeped crafts, and locals gather for events. If you’re looking for a great place to eat, try Hubben, a local spot run by friendly Swedes.

To visit a place of Quietude, start in the spectacular Kullaberg Nature Reserve. From there, head to Håkull, the region’s highest peak. The summit offers panoramic views of the sea and Danish coastline. It will add about four miles to your trek.

Distance: 117 miles For over 100 miles, the Österlen Way traverses the southeast corner of Skåne. This is Sweden’s famed culinary region. It’s also where my Stay Quiet Cabin was located. Starting in the medieval town of Ystad, the Österlen Way follows the Baltic Sea coast. It passes through nature reserves, national parks, and fishing villages, with inland stretches through ancient forest.

Among other fascinating stops, you’ll pass the hilltop stone circle, Ales Stenar. This is often referred to as “Sweden’s Stonehenge” for its similarly mysterious origins. The trail has two spots of Quietude. The first is Meditationsplatsen, a circular enclosure where people come to meditate. There’s also Gislövshammar Quarry, a 19th-century limestone quarry on the coast of its namesake fishing village. This area is known for its many orchards, farms, and excellent independent shops and bakeries.

Distance: 113 miles This breathtaking route hugs the wave-swept coastline of the Öresund Strait. From north to south, it passes through historic fishing villages and dramatic landscapes. It combines previously mentioned trails and quiet spots (Håkull, Mölle to Höganäs, and Ven Island) with two additional places of Quietude. There are the historic open-air cold bathing houses Pålsjöbaden and Hilleshögs dalar valley.

Hikers pass beaches, meadows, dunes and cliffs, and enter pine forests. The trail varies in difficulty before finishing with a loop around the Falsterbonäset Peninsula. Stone Age graves, medieval castle ruins, and views of the Danish coast keep things interesting. The final legs of the trail provide ample opportunities for birdwatching. It enters nature reserves that are internationally renowned habitats for many of Northern Europe’s migratory birds.

Distance: 994 miles At just under 1,000 miles (1,600 km), the region’s Skåneleden trail is a long-distance route that traverses a vast amount of the countryside’s changeable terrain. You’ll encounter rocky coast, verdant forest, national parks, and nature reserves.  There are also historic sites like castles and stone circles.

The Skåneleden Trail is divided into seven sub-trails by geographic area. There are 148 sections (encompassing all the previously mentioned routes). Many individual sections make for great day hikes. Or, combine them for longer treks over multiple days or weeks. Several sections are near train or bus stations, making transportation easy. Basic campsites with wind shelters and bathrooms are available along the way for those seeking a longer adventure.

**You can hear 60-minute field recordings of birdsong, waves, and wind from each of the places of quietude mentioned above on Visit Skåne’s website or Spotify.

What You Need to Know About Japanese Knives

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The popularity of Japanese knives in home and commercial kitchens has exploded in the last few years, and for good reason: A lot of them outperform their Western counterparts in kitchen tests. Western-style knives are bulkier, all-purpose kitchen tools—years ago, it would be common for many chefs to use the heavy bolster near the heel of the blade to crack open a can of tomatoes during service. Japanese knives (and Japanese-style knives produced by manufacturers elsewhere) are designed with precision cuts in mind and usually have a specific purpose. However, because they’re designed with that specificity in mind, it can be tricky to know what you need and what you’ll use.

Thankfully, we’ve reviewed a wide variety of Japanese-style knives for Bon Appétit and have assembled a round-up to help you identify exactly what your kitchen needs. While there are more styles of Japanese knives than those listed here, these options have all been thoroughly tested, giving us the confidence to speak to each knife’s utility. Before we jump into specific styles, however, it’s useful to give a quick rundown on some of the broad strokes of what makes a Japanese-style knife unique.

Many Japanese knives are designed for push cuts or pull-through slicing rather than the rocking chop of a heavy, Western chef’s knife. They tend to have a straighter edge than their Western counterparts, making them slightly less adept at speeding through a mince of herbs, but more precise in their cuts. Most Japanese-style knives also feature a sheep’s foot tip that curves down to the point, giving the user more control over fine-point work.

Japanese knives are often made with a harder steel than their Western counterparts. Harder steel helps a knife’s edge retain sharpness, which lets manufacturers use steeper sharpening angles for a finer edge. This helps most Japanese knives slice more delicately than Western knives, though harder steel can chip on material like ice or bone.

Traditional Japanese-style knives are only sharpened on one side, giving them a much finer edge than the 50/50 bevel of Western-style knives that are sharpened on both sides. Some modern Japanese knife makers have pivoted to a 50/50 style bevel for ease of sharpening; however, when you’re shopping, you’ll encounter plenty of knives with 70/30 bevel with the inside edge (the left edge for a right-handed knife) sharpened at a steeper angle. A 70/30 bevel is easier to maintain than a single-bevel but offers a sharper edge than a 50/50 bevel.

Many traditional Japanese kitchen knives feature a wa-style handle—an octagonal wood handle that envelops a hidden knife tang (the metal part of the knife that connects to the handle). Wa-style handles help keep knives lighter and nimbler, while many Western-style knives use full-tang construction, adding weight and heft to the blade. Many modern Japanese knifemakers, though, combine full-tang knife handles with Japanese blade shapes to create a hybrid style knife that’s both durable and adept at slicing.

The gyuto, or Japanese chef’s knife, originated in Japan in the early 1900s, when French cooking began to gain popularity in a rapidly modernizing country. This knife style took cues from the traditional French chef’s knife and combined them with elements of traditional Japanese knife-making. The resulting Japanese take on a chef’s knife was primarily designed for slicing meat, though it also features a curved edge that makes it easy to chop herbs. Compared to a Western chef’s knife, the gyuto has a straighter blade and a drop point tip with a lower overall profile. This is primarily designed so that the user can place the knife's tip on the cutting board and draw it through food, taking advantage of the gyuto’s sharp edge and thin blade for precision slicing. But while the gyuto excels at slicing meat, its general shape makes it an overall great pick to anchor your knife collection, and many home cooks opt for a gyuto as their daily driver over a traditional chef’s knife.

We’ve long been fans of the gyuto fan here at Bon Appétit, with MAC’s Professional Hollow Ground Chef’s Knife topping our chef’s knife review for years. For a wicked sharp, more traditional option, we really like the Kazan Arare by Shibata gyuto, which impressed us so much we had to give it its own review.

Increasingly common as a part of brands’ knife sets, the santoku knife is a relatively modern knife shape, first appearing in the 1940s or so. Nicknamed the “three virtues” knife, the santoku draws on the gyuto, nakiri, and deba to create a well-rounded utility knife that’s great for cutting meat, fish, and vegetables. Typically, these knives feature a pretty steep sheep’s foot tip for precision point work, a tall heel for push cuts, and just enough curve for pull-through slicing and rock-chopping. A santoku knife is great for well-rounded utility work—think cutting cheese and an apple for a snack, slicing leftover roasted chicken to make a sandwich, or dicing an onion for a curry.

Typically, we like a santoku knife that’s between six and seven inches, so it contrasts with the longer gyuto. Our favorite overall model is the MAC Professional Santoku Knife, but if you’d like a razor-sharp version with a traditional wa-style handle, we love the Kazan Ginsan Nashiji Santoku.

Nakiri knives have a straight edge and a sturdy spine, specifically designed for chopping vegetables. Sometimes referred to as a “vegetable cleaver,” the nakiri excels at push cuts, making it great for both matchsticking carrots and breaking down tough-skinned squash. Since most gyutos feature thin blades and fine edges, the nakiri also holds a spot in the Japanese knife lineup as a tougher, heavier knife for more demanding tasks. Traditional nakiri knives may also feature a blunt, rounded tip, which can be rotated against the cutting board to create a smooth, chopping motion.

The Tojiro DP Damascus Nakiri is by far our overall favorite tested nakiri knife for its extremely sharp edge and overall balance, but it’s also pricey. We also really like the Tojiro Classic nakiri, though it can have stock issues due to its popularity and price point.

The deba knife is a traditional Japanese knife designed for fish butchery. Unlike a Western fillet knife, which is designed to be flexible and nimble, the deba is a heavy, sturdy blade with a thick spine that gives the heel of the blade a cleaver-like feel. But a deba knife is also designed for precision slicing, and usually features a traditional single-bevel edge. That single-bevel edge allows the knife to come to a finer point than a double-bevel edge, and the sheep’s foot tip adds to the deba knife’s ability to portion out delicate fish fillets, as well.

We haven’t tested deba knives because they require a bit of knowledge and skill to use them for breaking down whole fish, but if you’re intrigued by the shape and want a cleaver-like knife for your kitchen, the Seki Mago Roku Deba from Kai is an affordable, solid option. Otherwise, we think most people would be better served looking to a nakiri or santoku knife.

Named for the French word “petite,” which means small, the petty knife is often a replica of the gyuto, just in a much, much smaller form. Most petty knives are around five inches long and feature a thin, lightly curved blade with a drop point tip and heel just tall enough to keep your fingers from bashing into the cutting board. Petty knives are great utility knives for small tasks, like dicing a shallot, slicing garlic, or slicing herbs, but they’re also long and nimble enough to be great slicers. Unless you do a lot of handheld turning and peeling, a petty knife is a great replacement for a paring knife in your lineup.

We found that both the MAC Professional Petty Knife and the Tojiro Classic Petty Knife were sharp enough to slice a steak while still being the perfect size to slice strawberries for your morning yogurt bowl.

Traditionally a fisherman’s knife used for all sorts of purposes on the boat, the funayuki is an old-school knife shape that predates the gyuto and santoku, and it likely helped inspire both shapes. The traditional funayuki is a shorter knife, around seven inches long, with a narrow, pointed tip and a tall heel. They are santoku-sized but gyuto-shaped. Unlike the deba knife, which was a heavy, single-bevel knife used for fish butchery, the funayuki was designed to be light and nimble so that fishermen could fillet their lunch while at sea. These days, most home cooks might pick up a funayuki as a secondary, shorter gyuto, or opt for this shape over a santoku as an all-purpose utility knife with a narrower tip.

Though it’s not as common in modern kitchens, one of our favorite Japanese chefs' knives is the Akiru-Saku Funayuki Gyuto, which is more traditionally shaped like a funayuki but is advertised as a gyuto since the two shapes are so similar.

The kiritsuke is a Japanese slicing knife with an angular “sword tip” that allows the user to make precision cuts. The initial design combined a long slicing shape and tall heel, making the kiritsuke a hybrid knife, similar to the santoku. For most people, the kiritsuke will be a version of an all-purpose chef’s knife that could take the place of a gyuto as a daily driver, with the big difference coming in the shape of the tip.

When reviewing chef’s knives, we found that the Shun Classic Kiritsuke was a great all-purpose chef’s knife with a unique tip that some people might prefer over a gyuto, since it curves upwards more like a Western chef’s knife and allows for more of a rock chop motion.

Boning and fillet knives are often unitaskers that don’t see much use unless you’re a fan of home butchery, but the Japanese honesuki is an even more specialized boning knife shape. Designed specifically for poultry, the honesuki is a steep, wedge-shaped knife with a very fine point and a tall heel. The goal is to have a knife that’s delicate enough to slip between a chicken joint while being sturdy enough to cut through cartilage. While the honesuki was designed for a specific purpose, we find that this shape is more versatile and can be swapped in for a petty knife for other small chopping and dicing tasks in ways that a curved fillet knife can’t.

We like the MAC Professional Honesuki as a great option that could tackle most boning-knife tasks while remaining sharp and versatile enough for utility work. If you find yourself breaking down a whole chicken while also wanting a strawberry-slicing knife, the honesuki could be a nonconventional multitasker.

FAA Orders Nationwide Ground Stop

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Flights were grounded across the U.S. Friday afternoon over suspected air traffic control communications issues.

The FAA issued a highly unusual mass ground stop shortly after 3 p.m.

“Due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety, attention all aircraft operators, by order of the Federal Aviation Command Center all airports are ground stopped,” the agency said in an advisory. “All departing traffic should remain ground stopped until further notice. Inbound international flights will be managed on a case by case basis.”

In a separate statement, the FAA said a communications issue affecting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers is to blame. Agency technicians are now investigating the issue.

According to ABC News, this is only the second time since the 9/11 attacks that departures across the country have been stopped. Flights currently in the air are believed to still be in contact with ATC.

National media outlets showed footage of commercial airplanes queued up but not moving at numerous major airports, including Los Angeles, Boston, Reagan National, New York-LaGuardia, Houston Bush, Dallas/Fort Worth, and Raleigh–Durham.

The ground stop will almost certainly cause a cascade of cancellations and delays lasting into the weekend.

Dawn Greenhalgh, Voice of the Elephant Queen Celeste in ‘Babar’ Cartoons, Dies at 93

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The stage veteran and mother of ‘Anne of Green Gables’ star Megan Follows also appeared in films for David Cronenberg and Sophia Coppola.

Dawn Greenhalgh, the Canadian actress who provided the voice of the spirited Queen Celeste for two animated series that centered on the beloved children’s character Babar the elephant, has died. She was 93.

Greenhalgh died Nov. 6 at her home in Toronto, her family announced.

Survivors include her youngest daughter, actress Megan Follows, who starred as Anne Shirley in three Anne of Green Gables miniseries and as Catherine, Queen of France on the 2013-17 CW series Reign. Greenhalgh started out on the stage, where she was a founding castmember of the renowned Stratford Festival in Stratford, Ontario. Later, she studied with Uta Hagen, Herbert Berghof and Mira Rostova in New York and appeared in theaters from Manhattan to the West End.

On the big screen, she portrayed the agent who reps actress Havana Segrand (Julianne Moore) in David Cronenberg’s Maps to the Stars (2014) after working in such other films as Sophia Coppola’s The Virgin Suicides (1999), Avenging Angelo (2002) and Welcome to Mooseport (2004).

Greenhalgh voiced Celeste over five seasons (1989-91) on Babar, the CBC-Nelvana series based on the books by French writer-illustrator Jean de Brunhoff (her husband the king was played by Gordon Pinsent). The show also aired on HBO in the U.S. and in more than 150 other countries.

She and then Pinsent returned in 2010-11 with the computer-animated sequel Babar and the Adventures of Badou.

Hazel Dawn Greenhalgh was born to British parents, Elizabeth and Laurence, on Sept. 25, 1933, in Shanghai, China. She and her family were held in a prisoner of war camp during the Japanese occupation of the city.

“It was there she discovered that in the midst of such abject misery, her love of music, theater and dance could offer happiness, purpose and temporary respite, both to herself and to her audience,” her family noted.

At age 14, she came to Canada accompanied only by her guardian, Albert Mah, and attended Montreal High School. She joined the fledgling Stratford Festival in Stratford, Ontario, five years later and was a founding member of the Neptune Theatre in Halifax and Theatre Plus in Toronto as well.

In 1969-70, Greenhalgh was a regular on the syndicated-CBC soap opera Strange Paradise, and she portrayed Mrs. Cadbury alongside her daughter on the 1985 CBC miniseries Anne of Green Gables, which attracted huge viewership numbers in Canada.

She was married to fellow actor Ted Follows from 1958 until their 1979 divorce.

In addition to Megan, survivors include two other daughters, Samantha Follows, also an actress, and Edwina Follows, a screenwriter and producer; her son, Laurence Follows; and grandchildren Lyla, Aaron, Russell, Samuel, Mikayla and Rowan.

Going Green: Miss Monique Debuts Her Nature-Centric Biorhythm Show in the U.S. This Weekend — With Bigger Plans For America to Follow

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

"I feel like my relationship with the U.S. is having an amazing start," the Ukrainian producer says ahead of her Nov. 13 show at Brooklyn Pacha.

On a recent afternoon, Miss Monique was in her studio in Lisbon when she got the news: Her upcoming headlining show at Brooklyn Pacha was sold out.

The Ukrainian producer, known for her progressive house productions and signature green hair, has already played key U.S. festivals like Ultra and EDC Las Vegas. But news of a sold-out headlining set hit different.

“I’m very appreciative of the U.S. crowd for the support,” the artist born Alesia Arkusha tells Billboard over Zoom from her home in Lisbon. “To bring your own show, that’s a completely different thing… you have more responsibility. But we’re sold out, so I think it’s another great sign of our relationship with the U.S. market.”

Certainly it points to real momentum toward Miss Monique — already famous throughout the European club scene — raising her profile in the U.S., which she cites as a major goal for 2027. “I would say I’m at the very beginning,” she says of breaking in the States. “For me, the U.S. crowd and market is completely different from everything I’ve done before.”

But with a sold-out show in one of the country’s key dance markets, she acknowledges with a smile: “I feel like my relationship with the U.S. is having an amazing start.”

She’s arriving with something special to offer: Biorhythm, the immersive audiovisual show she debuted at Tomorrowland’s Freedom Stage in July and brought to Argentina last month. (She next brings it to France later this month.) The show’s imagery weaves together the industrial and natural worlds — jellyfish, forests, iron structures — as a subtle meditation on what gets lost when technology crowds out the organic world.

“I started understanding that maybe I have a small power to remind people to be protective of nature,” she says. “I don’t want to give people a lesson, but Biorhythm is that reminder that we shouldn’t forget about nature.”

The concept is personal. Miss Monique grew up in Ukraine surrounded by parks and open land, and says she’s felt nature receding from her life as she’s moved through the churn of touring during her rise over the last 15 years. Biorhythm is her attempt to hold onto something she finds sacred, pairing it with the music she finds equally profound. “I love to live next to nature,” she says, turning her camera to show the forest outside her window. “Even here, I try to find places where I can sit and see something peaceful.”

Finding this peace hasn’t been easy. When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, Miss Monique says she “woke up in bed at 4:00 a.m., because of the explosions.”

After the war started, she moved from Ukraine to Portugal and continued producing her signature progressive house and touring the world behind it, while her friends and family lived amid war. That dissonance has been painful. “My heart is broken,” she says. “Until the war is over in Ukraine, I cannot fully enjoy this life. I have amazing people around me; I’m based in a fantastic country. But I know my home is going through hell.”

In the war’s early days, she would often cry when audience members held up Ukrainian flags or offered words of support. She’s since “learned how to keep my emotions inside,” but the weight of the situation is never far from her mind.  ”I still always have that mark on my heart,” she says. “It’s the same for every Ukrainian.”

She takes solace in music, which she says with gravitas “is also medicine for me” as much as it is for those who see her play. “Music saved my life so many times,” she continues. “It saved me emotionally in the hardest moments. Music has always been there for me.”

She’s had a lot of new music to speak of this year, with her latest single “Undertone” with Belgian producer Paradoks out today. It follows “Beauty in Us,” her September collaboration with English pop legend Robbie Williams. The two first connected in 2023, when Miss Monique remixed a song from Williams’ electronic trio Lufthaus. She says working with him again on a full track “was an absolute honor.”

This spring, Miss Monique intends to spend one or two months living in the U.S., not just touring through it, as she works to raise her profile in the country.  “When we travel, we are huge fans of exploring, not just coming in and out,” she says of her and her partner. “I want to feel how people live, the culture, the mentality. I want to understand what it’s like to be based there.”

She’s already started doing her homework. In 2024 she made a trip to the Grand Canyon, where she recorded one of her signature outdoor livestreams. Standing at the canyon’s edge as the sun set behind her, she was moved to tears by the beauty. “I never even dreamed that I would be there one day,” she says. “That stream was one of the best of my life.”

It’s fitting that one of her biggest shows in America arrives with a nature theme. Green, after all, has long been her signature color. What started as teenage hair experimentation eventually became her brand, which was cemented when she tried going back to her natural hair color and fans revolted.

“They were fighting with me,” she says with a laugh. She called her stylist the next morning. The green has stayed ever since, spreading from her hair to her visuals to the cascade of green heart emojis that follow her across social media.

“Step by step,” she says, “I realized green is my color.”
