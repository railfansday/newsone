A pledge to make AI less of an environmental disaster is floating around United Nations climate negotiations this month, but it’s just lip service.

At this point, reading through the latest splashy pledge to use AI for the good of the environment feels like downing a nonalcoholic beer. Admittedly, I’m suspicious from the onset. How can this be good? But I love beer and our planet enough to give it a whirl — and hey, why not try a little harm reduction?

Negotiators from more than 190 countries around the world are in Antalya, Turkey, this month for the annual United Nations climate summit, called COP31 (the 31st “Conference of the Parties” that ratified the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change). Turkey previewed its Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge ahead of the convening, inviting delegates from other countries to endorse the pledge before its official launch on November 16th.

Like that nonalcoholic beer, it’s deeply unsatisfying

The pledge actually includes some common sense measures that could curb planet-heating pollution from energy-hungry AI data centers. Unfortunately, like that nonalcoholic beer, it’s deeply unsatisfying.

At first glance, many of the ingredients you’d expect from a real commitment on AI’s environmental impact are here. It sets out to ensure that data centers are increasingly powered by clean energy, calling for internationally recognized standards for responsible AI development. Similarly, it pushes for reporting requirements so that there’s greater transparency into the impact data centers — which run primarily on fossil fuels — already have on the environment. It even acknowledges the need to manage the tons of e-waste created by the generative AI boom.

And yet, there is no there there because, as the text states clearly, “these objectives of the pledge are voluntary and non-prescriptive, create no new obligations, and are to be implemented in accordance with national circumstances.” In other words, these are voluntary commitments that countries can choose to follow or not follow based on their whims.

There’s also no way this pledge will garner the support of the most influential player in this game (at least for now). The US has way more data centers than any other country, but Donald Trump has pulled the nation out of the global climate accord and has no official delegates at the summit in Turkey.

The last point in the pledge is to ensure that advances in generative AI can be used to create more renewable energy and reduce greenhouse gas emissions, particularly in economically developing countries. To that end, research published in a Nature journal in August found that AI causes more carbon emissions by boosting fossil fuel production than it would help avoid through gains in renewable energy.

When it comes down to it, this pledge offers a placebo effect at best. In the face of data centers demanding tremendous amounts of electricity and spurring the development of more fossil fuel infrastructure, negotiators can’t be seen as doing nothing about the issue. And with global average temperatures still on the rise, we could all use an emotional support beverage.

Superman Sequel Will Officially Introduce the DCU’s Wonder Woman, Report Says

Man of Tomorrow is reportedly bringing in another Justice League hero

On Monday, James Gunn and Peter Safran stepped down from their leadership roles at DC Studios. Skydance — which recently finalized its acquisition of DC parent company Warner Bros. Discovery — tapped Jeremy Latcham to lead DC. He used to work at Marvel Studios, and coincidentally was a producer on Gunn's Guardians of the Galaxy.

When sharing his story about the news, The Wall Street Journal's Ben Fritz seemingly confirmed details about Gunn's next movie, 2027's Superman sequel Man of Tomorrow. In a post on X, he wrote that Gunn and Safran will debut Wonder Woman in next summer's blockbuster.

Fans have long speculated that Princess Diana of Themyscira would appear in Man of Tomorrow. Adria Arjona (Andor) was cast in the Superman sequel in April, with some reports pegging her as playing the relatively unknown character Maxima while Gunn remained tight-lipped about her role. Arjona hasn't confirmed who her character is either, but her training regimen for this fall's Onslaught and Man of Tomorrow has led fans to think she's Wonder Woman. Some outlets, like Cosmic News, have definitively stated that she will be Wonder Woman, which seems more plausible after Fritz's post. He doesn't specifically say Arjona will be Wonder Woman, but no one else in the cast fits the bill.

Wonder Woman's alleged appearance in Man of Tomorrow isn't the only notable part of Fritz's post. He also wrote that The Brave and the Bold is slated to come out in 2028. This film would introduce the DCU's Batman in live action. While we've known DC Studios has been planning The Brave and the Bold, the film has been without a release window.

Fritz also said that Gunn and Safran were "aiming for Gunn-directed Superman sequels" in 2029 and 2031. Apparently, those sequels "low-key evolved into Justice League" movies at some point. While Gunn recently confirmed he was working on his next movie after Man of Tomorrow, he hasn't officially announced what that film will be.

For now, it's unclear what the future of the DC Universe will look like with Latcham stepping in to lead the charge.

Alphabet’s Wing to invest $125M in new delivery drone factory

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Wing Aviation LLC today announced plans to spend $125 million on the construction of a new delivery drone factory.

The initiative is part of a broader investment that also encompasses the Alphabet Inc. unit’s research and development teams in California. Additionally, Wing stated that it plans to invest in the communities where it operates. It currently offers deliveries in five U.S. cities and intends to bring its service to 14 more markets soon.

Wing is building its factory in Fort Worth, Texas. The 15,000-square-foot facility is expected to create up to 100 jobs. Wing says that it will have capacity to make tens of thousands of delivery drones per year, which should significantly speed up its market expansion efforts.

The Alphabet unit’s drones reportedly have a wingspan of about 4.9 feet. They’re based on a frame made of styrofoam, a foam that mostly consists of trapped air, and carbon fibers. Styrofoam is lightweight and repels water, which comes handy when a drone is navigating challenging weather. The frame’s carbon fibers, in turn, boost its sturdiness.

The drone frame is equipped with 16 electric rotors that enable it to reach speeds of up to 60 miles per hour. Workers at stores that use Wing’s delivery service attach merchandise to its drones via a tether. After a drone takes off, it reels in the tether until the package comes into physical contact with its frame. When it reaches the customer’s house, the drone descends to an altitude of 20 feet and lowers the parcel.

Wing has built a software stack that automates much of the work involved in coordinating deliveries. It picks the most suitable drone for a given delivery run, plans its flight path and ensures that it won’t interfere with other drones. Human safety teams monitor for weather and air traffic changes that may make it necessary to adjust flight plans.

Wing plans to make its delivery service available to more than 40 million Americans by 2027. The Alphabet unit is pursuing the growth initiative in partnership with retailers such as Walmart Inc., which uses its drones to process e-commerce purchases. Last month, the latter company announced plans to make Wing deliveries available at more than 270 stores.

The Alphabet unit also intends to offer its service in Japan. Last month, it teamed up with Tokyo-based internet provider KDDI Corp. to make drone deliveries available to local consumers in 2027.

Although the drone delivery market is in an early stage, Wing already faces a significant amount of competition. In August, Amazon.com Inc. announced plans to make its Prime Air service available in nearly 500 U.S. markets by year’s end. More recently, drone delivery startup Zipline International Inc. raised $1.5 billion in funding at a $21.5 billion valuation.

Welcome to college football's new reality: Make the 24-team playoff or get fired

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As support grew for the College Football Playoff to expand from 12 to 24 teams, money was not the primary factor.

Yes, the new five-year, $1.7 billion deal finalized Monday will add a few million for each school in the SEC and Big Ten and a smaller amount for everyone else. But replacing conference championship games with a bunch of first-round playoff games was not, on its own, a big enough pot of gold to blow up the entire sport and risk losing the sanctity of the regular season.

What was worth it? Access. As coaches and administrators experienced the 12-team playoff, they came to the conclusion — and probably correctly — that nothing resonates with fans anymore besides getting into the field. They increasingly saw their own job security tied to the black-and-white standard of being in or out, so they simply changed the standard to include far more programs stuck in the middle of the two richest conferences.

But will more playoff access really calm down the "Fire Everyone" culture that seems to be increasingly pervasive in college football? Here's a prediction: It'll make it far, far worse.

Though it was a slow start to this year's coaching carousel, business is picking up with four power conference openings and several more that could materialize in the coming weeks. The real surprise, though, is how many first-year coaches are already under pressure.

Michigan's Kyle Whittingham, California's Tosh Lupoi, Washington State's Kirby Moore, Colorado State's Jim Mora and Tulane's Will Hall have been so disappointing in Year 1 that it wouldn't be a shock if their schools pulled the plug and started over. Even at places like Arkansas and Florida, where the record is no different from what was expected, first-year coaches are struggling to convince fans they are on the right track.

Traditionally, firing a coach after one year was taboo unless there was an off-field scandal. Even if it was immediately clear to university administrators that they hired a coach who was in over his head, it was almost too embarrassing to admit such a mistake without at least two years of on-field evidence.

But if that culture is indeed changing and compressing the timeline for coaches to prove themselves, the 24-team playoff is going to be like dumping kerosene on a bonfire.

If you thought the 12-team playoff created a world of FOMO for everyone who didn't get in, just wait until two-thirds of the power conference schools realize every November that they landed on the wrong side of a pass-fail test.

When the standard is really, really high to make the playoff, you can at least rationalize not making it. Even now, in the 12-team version, we have seen good teams like Texas, Notre Dame and Vanderbilt last year all fall short because there wasn't enough room and the margin for error was small. If you fell short of the cut line, you were in good company.

So when you change the threshold so dramatically, you are of course including more teams worthy of a shot. But you are also creating a new standard for what constitutes a successful season. It's inevitable that, starting next year, fans and boosters will consume college football in a much more binary fashion: You're either in or you're out, and it's a big problem if you're out.

At the same time, college football is undergoing an Overton window revolution on what kind of season is acceptable for a new coach. It used to be, the first year was a free pass. Especially at programs that had lost for a few years, the results didn't matter. All you had to do was show that you've changed the locker room culture, be a little bit more competitive than the last coach, and secure a good recruiting class to keep fans happy.

Those days are gone. Even if Curt Cignetti getting Indiana to the playoff in Year 1 and winning a national title in Year 2 is the extreme example, he's far from alone in using the transfer portal to engineer an immediate turnaround. When fans see Bob Chesney get UCLA to 9-0, Will Stein put Kentucky into the CFP mix, Eric Morris win quickly at Oklahoma State with players he coached at North Texas and James Franklin put the best Virginia Tech team on the field in nearly a decade, everyone has a right to ask, "Why not us?"

If you're trying to figure out what's fueling so much anger right now at a place like Florida, which is 5-4 and has not been bad overall in Jon Sumrall's first year, look no further. Under a traditional timeline, Florida fans would generally be in agreement that Sumrall needed time to rebuild the program after five straight mediocre (or worse) seasons and wouldn't start rendering any judgments until Year 3.

But now that it's proven you can flip any program in a year, that becomes the expectation. Nobody can afford to play the long game anymore. At 5-4 with wins over Texas and Ole Miss, Sumrall is not on the hot seat and has largely been fine. But will the chatter about his job security move from the online world to the real world if he's not on track to make the 24-team playoff at this time next year?

The collision of those two factors — expanding the playoff and the recent track record of coaches winning in Year 1 — will inevitably accelerate all the conversations fans are having when they start to wonder if they've got the right guy. And as we see around the country every year, prohibitive finances are no match for administrative self-preservation.

For weeks, Florida State officials were adamant that they wanted to bring back Mike Norvell, largely because his approximate $50 million buyout was such an issue. When the noise got loud enough, though, the school president caved. We could see the same thing starting to unfold at Nebraska, where Matt Rhule signed a new contract last year with a $63 million buyout but now finds himself at the center of a fan mutiny after losing four straight.

These are not healthy developments, but Newton's Third Law of Motion always wins in the end. For every action (massive coaching contracts, playoff expansion, transfer portal free-for-all), there will be an equal and opposite reaction (new expectations, more pressure, less patience to build from the ground up). The old rules no longer apply.

But there is a real problem with what they've done here. At 24 teams, there are still going to be more losers than winners at the end of every college football season; only now, it will make the line between the two that much brighter. Everyone will feel that much more pressure to be on the right side of the divide because it will be viewed as unacceptable to fall short.

There are plenty of reasons why expanding the playoff makes sense. Increased parity means more than 12 teams deserve a chance, donors are being asked to pour $40-50 million into rosters without enough postseason rewards and there was no way to rescue the bowl system given all the focus on the CFP. The conference championship games had also gotten stale since realignment helped form 16- and 18-team mega-conferences, so there is some logic in replacing them with first-round playoff games.

But if coaches and administrators think that a team squeaking into the playoff as the No. 22 seed will spare them from the "Fire Everyone" culture they seek to tame, it will prove to be a delusion. And if God forbid they miss the 24-team playoff, they will soon get a lesson in unintended consequences.

Victor Wembanyama's Spurs teammates catching his alley-oop fever

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

PHOENIX - A feathery pass lofted toward the basket from the free-throw line followed by a perfectly timed explosion skyward for a vicious one-handed slam.

The sensational alley-oop dunk the Spurs executed late in the third quarter of their 124-109 win at Sacramento on Friday was a thing of beauty - but it came with a twist.

It was a Victor Wembanyama-to-Stephon Castle connection, not the other way around.

The Spurs entered Monday night's game against the Suns boasting the top alley-oop combination in Castle to Wembanyama with 14 such plays, but in Sacramento, it was the Wembanyama feed to the explosive third-year guard that went viral.

"It's cool," Castle said after shootaround ahead of Monday night's game against the Suns.

"To be able to have that kind of connection with the big fella, whether it's me throwing a lob or him throwing it to me and it being so seamless, it's a details thing. But I think with how talented we are, it produces highlights like that."

The play also exemplified a trend of late for the defending Western Conference champions in that it's not just Wembanyama throwing down alley-oop slams with authority.

In Saturday night's 125-103 win at Portland, Luke Kornet and Tarris Reed Jr. had slams courtesy of alley-oop feeds from Keldon Johnson.

In the 112-111 win over Dallas on Wednesday at the Frost Bank Center, Devin Vassell set up Dylan Harper for an alley-oop dunk, and Kornet got one on an assist from Julian Champagnie.

"It's fun," Wembanyama said of the Spurs' diverse alley-oop avalanche.

"We have to keep finding ways, keep experimenting," the 7-foot-4 center added. "But the lob is the best shot in the game, so if we got good positions and good passes, there's no reason that anybody shouldn't catch a lob."

"We're so talented, we have so many scorers, we have so many people that can create that when we do play the right way, when we do play with our teammates, first off it's fun and second off it looks beautiful," he said.

Spurs coach Mitch Johnson said the widespread alley-oop dunking highlight's the team's athleticism, offensive depth and unselfishness.

"We have nine guys on our team who, in my estimation, have been leading scorers, and remain (high-level) offensive players in this league," the coach said. "If we don't acknowledge and value and celebrate passing, it's going to be hard for us to have the right type of harmony on the offensive end and as a team. I know there's some mumbo and jumbo in there, but it's a real thing when you start talking about the ecosystem of a team in the locker room.

"And when we can acknowledge and value passing and understand and what that does for our team and then celebrate it so people feel a part of plays where I might have made the pass to you and you made the pass to the person making the shot, that's when you start talking about high-level talent playing that style of basketball that's really hard to guard."

Castle said all the alley-ooping also reflects team chemistry.

"It's just the way we like to play off of each other," he said. "We know how hard we are to guard when the ball's popping, with how much talent we have on the court at all times."

With so many weapons, alley-oops and lobs seem to be coming from all directions these days.

"The ability to do that makes us very unpredictable," Wembanyama said.

Of course, it all begins and ends with the 22-year-old French superstar, said De'Aaron Fox, who ranks second on the team in alley-oop feeds to Wembanyama with 11.

"Vic's probably the best roller ever to play the game, probably the best lob threat ever," Fox said. "People try to keep him away from the rim as much as possible, but when he does get behind the defense, I think everybody knows - us and them - it's hard to stop or there's nothing they can do."

Wembanyama's 40-plus dunks and 30-plus alley-oop slams leads the NBA. He entered the game against Phoenix ranked fourth in the league in scoring with a career-best-29.1 average and he finished last season averaging 25.0.

"We're doing a better job running the floor, spacing the floor, and he's just doing a better job of asserting himself in the right positions," Champagnie said of Wembanyama's increased scoring. "The intent behind how he's playing is a little different. He's going to dunk the ball a lot more. So the more and more he does that, the more and more he does his thing in space, it kind of just opens up. Teams have to either guard the three, guard the dunk."

And when it comes to dunking, especially of the alley-oop variety, so many other Spurs players are getting into the act.
