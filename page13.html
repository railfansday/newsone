Donovan Mitchell on 9-9 Cavs' inconsistent effort: 'I'm trying to figure out how not to end up on ESPN'

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Chicago Bulls entered Thanksgiving week as one of the NBA's worst teams: 4-12, getting outscored by nearly eight points per 100 possessions, with the second-worst defense in the NBA. And yet, they controlled the bulk of Monday's matchup with the Cleveland Cavaliers, leading for nearly the entire final three quarters — and by as many as 24 points — in a 122-108 win.

This, despite the Cavs coming in with a rest advantage, with the Bulls on the second night of a back-to-back after going to overtime against the Nuggets on Sunday. And despite Chicago being without leading scorer Norman Powell, who's stuck in street clothes nursing a strained right hamstring, and 2026 lottery pick Caleb Wilson, out with a broken right hand. And despite a pair of Chicago starters — point guard Josh Giddey and swingman Matas Buzelis — getting benched, again, late in the fourth quarter by first-year head coach Tiago Splitter, who elected to close with backup point guard Tre Jones and intriguing two-way reserve Jaylin Sellers. (Giddey did not seem particularly pleased about that.)

And, perhaps most importantly, despite the Cavs … well, frankly, they're just supposed to be better than this.

Donovan Mitchell (right), James Harden and the Cleveland Cavaliers have stumbled to .500 a month into the season. (Melissa Tamez/Icon Sportswire via Getty Images)

Yes, Cleveland's without Evan Mobley, an integral component of its operations on both sides of the floor. And yes, Kenny Atkinson's club wound up on the business end of , with Chicago going 20-for-49 from 3-point range while the Cavs made just eight of their 31 triple tries. Not exactly easy to win when you're outscored by 36 points from long distance.

It's not just the simple, cruel vicissitudes of shooting variance, though. Aside from Mobley, the Cavs had the entire rest of their preferred rotation against a four-win team … and they still got drilled. A Cleveland squad that made it to the Eastern Conference finals last spring enters Wednesday's matchup with the New York Knicks — the team that, as you might recall, annihilated the Cavs in that series en route to winning the NBA championship — at 9-9, in seventh place in the East.

That's a long way from where the Cavs — one of the most expensive rosters in the sport — want to be … and, from the sound of things, they've got some pretty big questions to answer before they can get there. From Danny Cunningham of The Inside Shot:

Donovan Mitchell, who had a game-high 28 points, was asked about the effort being inconsistent from quarter-to-quarter or game-to-game this season.

Mitchell thought for a full five seconds after the question was asked.

"I think," he said before pausing for eight more seconds. "I'm trying to figure out how not to end up on ESPN."

"I think the biggest thing now is just figuring each other out. We're not here in the locker room panicking, and all I can say to that, is what we got going forward. Nights like tonight are bad. It's a bad night, and we got to respond to that. We've got to respond to that and just keep playing our game. There's so many different things you can point to, but at the end of the day for us, it's within us. I have all the confidence in everybody in here, and we've got all the confidence in each other. We've got to go out there and execute it. That doesn't really answer your question, but yeah."

If there's one thing I'm qualified to assess as a long-time professional blogger, it's this: That was a lot of words to not really make any point or address the issue at hand. Which, come to think of it, might constitute a pretty loud statement about the state of affairs in Cleveland right about now.

Mitchell wasn't the only Cavalier searching for an answer to the issues that have characterized the club's fits-and-starts opening to the season. Peyton Watson, the swingman who came over from Denver in an offseason sign-and-trade intended to plug Cleveland's long-yawning hole on the wing, offered a somewhat more direct answer than the All-NBA scoring guard.

"I think if we knew the answer, we would just do it," Watson said in the locker room. "I think we're still searching for that. You know, I'm always trying to communicate with my teammates and try to help make their job easier and just be a guy that connects the team in a lot of ways. I think I try to just go out there and lead by example by playing as hard as I can every night. I just think that everybody has got to look inward and look themselves in the mirror and ask themselves, 'Am I giving it 100% every single night?' I think that if that's not the case, then we need to go to the drawing board and really, like I said, look in the mirror and evaluate ourselves."

That doesn't exactly seem like the most stirring vote of confidence for the competitive makeup of the Cavaliers, especially considering they've already gone through a few moments that would seem to invite such sober self-reflection.

Blowing a 29-point lead against the 76ers on opening night. Dropping both ends of a home-and-home to the post-injury-apocalypse Thunder. Getting boat-raced by the Knicks in the second half at home, an extension of the pain of the conference finals. Getting blown out by a Hornets team playing without Brandon Miller or Kon Knueppel. Getting out-executed down the stretch by a Grizzlies squad playing without Ty Jerome. And now, Monday's scattershot effort in Chicago.

It just feels like something's missing in Cleveland, some spark, some urgency — and it starts on the defensive end.

The last half-decade of success for the Cavs — a climb out of the post-LeBron basement that began with the Mobley/Allen/Darius Garland unit under J.B. Bickerstaff, and that continued after the trade for Mitchell made it a "core four" — had been predicated on consistently stout defenses, with Cleveland ranking in the top eight in points allowed per possession each season from 2021 through 2025. That began to shift when Atkinson's arrival supercharged the offense, and more firmly tilted after dealing for Harden; Cleveland posted a top-five offense after the trade deadline, but a below-average defense, and got routinely torched by the Knicks in the four-game conference finals sweep.

The Cavs now sit at 21st in defensive efficiency — on pace for they're first bottom-10 finish on that end since 2020-21. They're conceding shots at the rim and from beyond the 3-point line more regularly, and they give up wide-open 3s at a bottom-10 rate. (Nearly half of Chicago's 3-point attempts on Monday came without a Cavalier defender within 6 feet of the shooter.) They don't force turnovers, and they're average-at-best at clearing the defensive glass and guarding without fouling. That's a recipe for underwhelming results, unless you can balance out that bottom-10 defense with a No. 1-with-a-bullet offense … which the Cavs, currently a good-but-not-overwhelming seventh in points scored per possession, don't have at the moment.

Maybe they will. Get Mobley healthy, get Harden — who followed up his 51-point Friday masterclass by shooting 5-for-14 from the floor with seven turnovers in Chicago — more consistently off the schneid, and get Watson (a career-low 48% on 2-pointers and 27% on 3s) thawed out, and maybe these Cavs have the ingredients they need to build on last season's trip to the final four and put themselves in position to make a bid to represent the East in the NBA Finals. They believe they have the talent to do that.

Right now, though, they feel like a team that's less than the sum of their parts — a group that's playing like they're just kind of going through the motions until the real season starts, rather than one that needs to earn its postseason spot in an East that's gotten more crowded at the top. And if they want to have a shot at being the last team standing come springtime, they need to start finding answers as fall turns to winter.

"You can't just turn it on in April or May," Cavs guard Sam Merrill told reporters. "I think building the right habits only makes it easier to play that way once you get to later in the season and in the playoffs. We continue to talk about it. That's got to continue to be our mindset."

Talking about it is one thing. Their next chance to do something about it comes Wednesday in New York. One bit of good news for Mitchell and Co., at least? The game won't air on ESPN.

The Golden Rules of Affordable Family Travel, Illustrated by Mona Chalabi

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

My first Thanksgiving was a lavish 12-person dinner in Connecticut in 2014. I had moved to the US in March of that year and had planned on working from home until a friend generously invited me to go spend the day with her family; an act of unguarded openness that I’ve realized is one of the many things that differentiates Americans from Brits. As soon as we got off the train in Cambridge, Massachusetts, I realized that even though my friend lived in a nasty, overcrowded apartment in Brooklyn, she came from a family that had immense wealth. After dinner, as I helped wash plates in the kitchen, my friend’s mum asked her why she wouldn’t just move into the lovely building they had bought for her. She turned beet red and handed me another dirty plate without looking at me.

Take it from an immigrant: this holiday is full of weird contradictions. Of course, there’s the annual reminder of colonization which requires a careful forgetting of the people who have been colonized. And then there’s the financial aspect of it. At first blush, it seems like the least capitalist holiday of the year—there isn’t the expense of Christmas or Hanukkah gift-giving or the extravagance of Halloween candy. Yet the holiday also marks the start of one of the year's busiest travel seasons when families are taking great expense to reunite, vacation, and explore outside of their every day lives.

As we approach the last Thursday of November, with money very much on our minds, the below well-traveled parents share their hard-won wisdom on pulling off affordable family travel—including how to find treasure in New York City’s Chinatown without paying an arm and a leg, seek out free entertainment in the streets of Oaxaca, and embrace the joys of accessible public transportation in Europe. All of these stories say something about the wealth of the person writing it, whether or not they wanted to reveal that information. I’m obsessed with getting to the truth about someone’s class (it’s why I wrote my book on the subject), and so these family dispatches, which serve as equal parts advice and confession, are a treat I’ll come back to when I’m not busy washing a rich family’s plates. —Mona Chalabi, Pulitzer Prize-winning writer and illustrator

I know the secret to parenting and I’m willing to share it: snacks. Snacks are everything—they’re an activity, a mood changer, and a window into culture for kids. Hit a local farmers market on your first day or if you’re somewhere truly out of their comfort zone even a local convenience store—like 7-Eleven in Tokyo—for something that can feel both familiar and novel. If your hotel room has a refrigerator you can store any pre-cut fruit that you buy, or opt for items that don’t require refrigeration like apples, bananas, and clementines. Bring a lightweight backpack, refill each morning, and watch the hangry meltdowns (mostly) vanish. —Dana Kravis, global executive director, CNT video

Last December in London, we stayed in a quaint Airbnb in Primrose Hill and explored different neighborhoods on foot. When our kids, then eight and three, got tired, we hopped on a double-decker bus and headed upstairs, hoping for front seats. Suddenly, getting home became an amusement park ride: Christmas lights, bustling streets, panoramic views. The kids loved it; as a photographer, I loved the vantage point. London caps daily bus fares when you use the same card or phone, and children under 11 ride free. We’ve done this in Singapore, too. Our rule: skip the sightseeing tour (or Uber) and make public transportation part of the family adventure. —Tanveer Badal, photographer

Breakfast is where family vacations are made, or broken. My moody teenagers can’t be pried out of bed no matter what city they wake up in, so instead of wringing my uncaffeinated hands and hoping to get us all on the same schedule, I just bring my own breakfast, coffee included. Whether in Paris, Rome, or a tent, you will find me hand-grinding my coffee beans, brewing a pour-over, and snacking on a pastry left over from the previous day while I peruse headlines. Avoiding the time and expense of a sit-down breakfast, and indulging in a bit of morning self-care, means we can focus on the adventures and meals we are actually going to remember. —Chris Morocco, food director of Bon Appétit and Epicurious

My parents and I meet in Aruba every winter for a family vacation that’s warm and relaxing when everyone else is cold and miserable. It’s a classic only child/parents trip where we spend most of our time eating lunch talking about where we’re going to eat dinner. We spend our days mostly just reading parallel to each other. I’ve never been an e-reader user, so I have to bring several physical books to read to get me through the week. The key is that my mom and I have some shared taste in books, so when we finish our own we can swap and save ourselves buying extra books (or lugging even heavier luggage through the airport). —Alison Leiby, comedian and writer

Finding creative ways to entertain wiggly one-year-olds with limited attention spans can be daunting, but it turns out all you need is a good window. From London to California, staying in rooms on low floors with windows overlooking lots of activity below has resulted in seemingly endless engagement—waving at commuters striding by at the Peninsula London, or golfers strolling a few feet away at the Ritz-Carlton Half Moon Bay? Bonus points if the room has curtains with electronic controls—that guarantees an extra hour of ooohs and aaahs, at least. —Sarah Khan, contributing editor

I have a thing for seeing “The World’s Biggest or Oldest,” and so here we were at Missouri’s Oldest Wooden Suspension Bridge. “Don’t tell your mom,” I said to my kids, because any bridge that was built from wood during the Civil War should be treated with caution—and, also, all good dad stories start that way. We started up the van, piled everyone in, and began the adventure. The wood cracked and echoed as we drove over the river. It sounded like a popping spinal cord. We swayed, and for a moment, I actually questioned myself. I should have just taken them to the park. Thankfully, I can now confidently say that Missouri’s oldest wooden suspension bridge can hold the weight of a mini-van. —Shannon Carpenter, humorist

New York is an expensive city and the best way to keep it cheap is to throw petty cash at it. Instead of buying tickets to an overpriced kid’s experience, I give my children a few twenty dollar bills and take them to Chinatown for a shopping spree. They love the atrocious arcade, and there are fake Labubus, squishy dumplings, a plastic “jade” green dragon that my son now keeps on his bedside, and a 'gold' necklace my daughter thinks is treasure. —Stephanie Danler, author

As a seasoned travel writer, I know that any hotel that’s gotten media coverage is often pushing $1,000/night. My trick for affordable family lodging is loading up Google maps, figuring out which area we’re going to stay in, and picking a cheerful place with good reviews in the $250 range. It’s almost never one you’ve heard of, but they’re always clean and cheerful, usually family-owned, and we always leave with big hugs and promises to keep in touch. That doesn’t happen at the Georges V. My kids (12 and 8) loved a converted windmill in Rome, a townhouse two blocks from the Rijksmuseum and—most recently—an orange grove near Siracusa where they spent so much time chasing the owner’s cats they even forgot Wi-Fi existed. A steal at any price! —Priyanka Mattoo, filmmaker and author

People tell you about a million things you “need” before your baby is born, but there's one that has come in the most handy, and I've had a true love/hate relationship with it since day one: A travel car seat. After a ton of research—some may say over-research—we bought a Maxi Cosi Romi. It's rated high for safety, but it's also easy to strap into a car and also an airplane. We always bring it on the airplane, and that's where the headache comes in. While the Maxi Cosi Romi is considered lightweight, it's also big and bulky, and the only way to get it through the airport and onto the plane is by wearing it in a special backpack. I'm pretty sure that I'm one of the most annoying people in any airport as I'm walking through it since I can't really see if I'm bumping into people in the TSA line or getting to the gate. The thing is that, after I considered the numbers, it's actually a really wise investment because most rental car places charge anywhere from $10 to $15 a day to rent a car seat, so we've already started saving money because of it. Now, that's about a hundred or more dollars we're not spending when we go out of town and bring the Maxi. It's wildly annoying and inconvenient, and I look like I'm wearing a jetpack as I'm walking through the airport, but it's worth it. —Jason Diamond, 'Figuring' family columnist at The Cut

From bustling markets to neighborhood festivals, I've learned that some of the best family entertainment happens in the streets. In Oaxaca, my four-year-old was mesmerized by calendas, raucous community processions with brass bands, towering puppets called monos de calenda, and rag-covered revelers known as tiliches. Paradegoers tossed my kid candy, fireworks exploded overhead, and the party raged late into the night. We didn't pay a peso. Before booking pricey family activities, research local festivals, celebrations, and public performances. Sometimes the most exciting show in town is happening right on the sidewalk. —Ashlea Halpern, contributing editor

On our last family vacation to Rajasthan, a passionfruit lassi from our five-star hotel nearly ruined the trip. We’re talking emergency roadside bathroom stops and several days of Zofran. We’ve been taking our kids—Vijay, Vik, and Meera—to Rajasthan since they were babies; they’re teenagers now. And we’ve learned one unexpected lesson: the sickest we’ve ever been in India was after eating at our five-star hotel. More than once.

So we’ve adopted a new philosophy: pack the right medicine and embrace street food. In Jaipur, Vineet Sharma, founder of Jaipur Walks, led us on a street food tour around the bustling market, introducing us to piping-hot pyaaz kachori and creamy, saffron-rich kulfi. His advice? Choose clean stalls with high turnover, and favor food cooked fresh and served hot over raw offerings. At busy stalls, food is often prepared continuously and served straight from the heat—sometimes a more reassuring bet than a meal at a luxury hotel or high-end restaurant. And if your stomach’s going to revolt, at least make the meal memorable. —Christine Chitnis, author and photographer

One day, and soon, I know I won't want to do this much more. But for now, I'm taking every chance to do so. —Erin Florio, global features director

How to Deal With Holiday Arguments and Guilt Trips

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ideally, the holidays are a time of happiness, cheer, and positive relationship interactions. In practice, however, we often need to manage some bad with the good. There are usually a few “bones to pick” over the Thanksgiving turkey and “elephants in the room” along with the Christmas tree…

So, to maximize your holiday joy (and minimize your angst), I have compiled some strategies to deal with dysfunctional games, family patterns, arguments, and emotional manipulation. By the end, you should have a good idea why Aunt Ruth keeps giving you a guilt trip, how Uncle Stu keeps making you lose your cool, and what exactly you can do about all of it. Then, maybe, you can enjoy yourself, too!

To start, according to Eric Berne in his book Games People Play (1964), people generally try to manipulate us because they want something from us—but they don’t believe they can get it honestly or fairly. These negative beliefs (called existential positions) come in two forms. On one hand, they can believe that you’re not OK and try to bully you because they think you don’t deserve their fair treatment. On the other hand, they can believe that they’re not OK (i.e., I’m not okay) and try to guilt trip you into feeling that you owe them something for nothing.

Thus, people play games, argue, and manipulate when they believe they are not OK, other people are not OK, or both. This allows them to get what they want without giving back or risking being fair and trusting. Such manipulations can have secondary benefits for them, too, like being excused from standards or responsibilities (I'm not OK), or feeling morally superior (You're not OK). Unfortunately, those bad beliefs and manipulative games also cause many of our hurt feelings and dysfunctional relationship patterns in the process as well.

How these game-playing relatives and friends push your emotional buttons was explored by Niven, Totterdell, and Holman (2009) and defined as negative affect regulation strategies. Put simply, they use emotional manipulation tactics to make you feel bad, so you give them what they want. There are four general tactics they can employ to emotionally manipulate, but they all share one common theme—they reject you and hurt your feelings, while also putting their feelings first.

Beyond that, they might choose to emotionally bully you by being disrespectful and insulting or bragging and trying to make you feel bad by comparison. This would fall under the “You’re not OK” belief and allow them to feel superior at your expense. In contrast, they might decide to guilt-trip you by criticizing how you treat them or withdrawing until you feel bad and comply with their wishes. This would be part of an “I’m not OK” belief and allow them to be excused from standards and responsibilities—like doing something worthwhile for you back. In either case, unless you know how to defend yourself, you might be left trying to choke down negative emotions, while they munch on dessert!

Fortunately, there are things that you can do to defend yourself—and possibly even improve your relationship with that unruly relative, partner, or friend, too. To begin, though, you need to decide whether you want to deal with the manipulation indirectly by diverting attention and emotion away from the problem, or directly by engaging attention and emotion toward addressing the problem. In other words, can you make light of Cousin Bessie’s snotty comments and change the subject, or do you need to talk it out and come to a better arrangement?

To help you decide, consider some indirect and direct softening techniques suggested by Overall, Simpson, and Struthers (2013). They were initially devised for addressing problems with emotionally avoidant or argumentative romantic partners—and serve as a foundation for dealing with relationship problems in my book Attraction Psychology (Nicholson, 2022). Beyond that, I have found them helpful with family and friends as well, especially when they believe one of us is not “OK” and are emotionally hurtful about it.

Dealing with issues indirectly requires that you “soften” by temporarily downplaying the severity of the problem, until their bullying or guilt-tripping lessens. To do so, it helps to build positive rapport by communicating your care, acceptance, and regard—while also minimizing their emotional manipulation attempts with positive feelings and humor (i.e., kill them with kindness). From there, you can redirect them toward other positive aspects about your relationship with them and points of agreement. Essentially, this is a lot like dealing with a toddler having a tantrum—make light of the outburst and find a more pleasant topic to change their mind and improve their behavior.

Dealing with issues directly, however, requires that you “soften” by acknowledging some aspect of their point of view as a base of agreement—without surrendering to the negative emotions from their bullying or guilt trip. Pretend you’re a cool TV lawyer who says, “We can agree that there is a problem here, but it would help us both to keep working on a better solution.” From there, you can show optimism about solving the disagreement and having a good relationship with them. Where you are able, also acknowledge some of their positive efforts in the past toward connecting and compromising with you. This can help improve their emotional outlook and motivate them to find healthier ways to interact with you in the future as well.

Whatever strategy you choose, I hope you have Happy Holidays and a Good New Year, too!

As inflation remains elevated—with the latest Consumer Price Index (CPI) coming in hotter than economists’ expectations—“resilient” growth in home prices continues to be eroded.

The September S&P Cotality Case-Shiller Home Price Index was up 0.2% month-over-month, recording a 2.1% annual gain—the same as last month. This is compared to annual inflation readings of 3.4% in both the September CPI and Personal Consumption Expenditures price index.

“Nominal home prices remain resilient, but inflation is once again widening the gap between nominal and real housing returns,” said Rebecca Kaufman, associate director of Commodities at S&P Dow Jones Indices. She noted that this marks the “16th consecutive month in which U.S. home prices declined in real terms.”

Realtor.com® Senior Economist Anthony Smith also noted that this month’s data “reflects sales closing from July through September, a period when mortgage rates edged higher, with Freddie Mac’s 30-year fixed rate averaging 6.54% in July, near 6.7% in August, and 6.86% in September.”

The 10-City Composite was up 0.2% month-over-month to a 3.5% annual gain (down from 3.6% last month), while the 20-City Composite was up 0.1% month-over-month to a 2.6% annual gain (same as last month).

Kaufman also noted that this month’s report “revealed a widening gap between seasonal weakness and underlying price momentum,” as the monthly numbers pre-seasonal adjustment were declines across the board for the National, 10-City Composite and 20-City Composite Indices (-0.3%, -0.5% and -0.5%, respectively).

“That contrast suggests that some of the apparent monthly weakness reflects the normal fall slowdown rather than a broad deterioration in home prices,” she explained.

Smith agreed that this was reflective of “the normal fall slowdown,” as the “market’s momentum has weakened since September closed.”

Taking a look at the 20 cities that make up the index, Chicago once again led the pack (for the 6th consecutive month) with a 6.9% annual gain. Following behind were New York City at 5.8% and Miami at 4.6%.

Kaufman called out that Miami’s growth is “particularly notable,” as “after moving into fourth place among the 20 cities in July and August, it has now climbed into the top three, a sharp reversal from its comparatively weak performance in 2025.”

In terms of declines, Seattle was again the leader for the 4th consecutive month with an annual fall of 1.9%. Denver (-1.0%) and Dallas (-0.9%) followed behind.

For the picture ahead, Smith said that the “rate and demand backdrop has become more challenging since September closed,” as mortgage rates are now above 7% and inflation remains fairly elevated—with “expectations for a Fed rate hike in December.”

“If financing costs stay elevated into the winter, the plateau in price growth could give way to renewed softening, particularly in supply-heavy Southern and Western markets,” he continued.
