Casino Mogul Steve Wynn Sells His $70 Million Central Park Condo as Manhattan Luxury Market Heats Up

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Casino billionaire Steve Wynn has finally unloaded his Central Park duplex penthouse, making it the top luxury sale of a record-breaking week in Manhattan.

The mogul's prewar condo at 50 Central Park South—first listed in 2022 for $90 million, the most expensive in the country at the time—eventually saw its price drop to $70 million.

Even at this steep discount, it still took two years for the condo to find a taker. The duplex went into contract last week with Serena Boardman of Sotheby's International Realty as the listing agent (co-exclusive with Deborah Grubman of Corcoran).

The sale means the casino magnate failed to turn a profit on the 10,875-square-foot condo. But given that, according to Forbes, Wynn is worth $4.3 billion, he likely isn't sweating it too much.

The Billionaires Row duplex takes up the 30th and 31st floors within the 36-story Ritz-Carlton Residences, formerly the Hotel St. Moritz, which opened in 1930. The top 12 floors are the private homes.

The penthouse, formerly owned by real estate developer Christopher M. Jeffries, comes with an esteemed pedigree.

It was Jeffries' Millennium Partners that purchased the Emory Roth–designed building in 1999 and reimagined it along with the Ritz-Carlton Hotel Co. Jeffries was once married to Princess Yasmin Aga Khan, the only child of Prince Aly Khan and movie legend Rita Hayworth.

Yet the real selling point of the duplex is its sweeping Central Park views. With floors that aren't too high, from the vantage of the 45-foot long terrace, it feels as if one is sitting in the famed park's trees—a "bird's-eye perspective," as the listing puts it. Another terrace along the western wall stretches 28 feet.

With three bedrooms, four full baths, and two half-baths, the prewar unit features 10 grandly arched windows, a marble staircase, 15-foot coffered ceilings, and a living room that used to be the St. Moritz ballroom.

Modern touches include a chef's kitchen; a breakfast room with its own terrace; a primary bedroom with Central Park views, en suite bathroom, gym, and dressing room; a staff room with laundry; and a library.

As the condo sits atop the flagship Ritz-Carlton hotel, it comes with access to the amenities of the global luxury name, including the La Prairie spa and health club, a business center, the Contour Bar and Lounge, in-room dining services, and housekeeping services.

The unit has $31,362 in monthly dues on top of $15,500 in taxes.

As for why a property offering such grandeur may have taken so long to move, luxury broker Michael Reisor with Compass-affiliated Reisor.Team, who is familiar with the building, says, "Properties like this are wonderful but specific. There's a more limited buyer pool."

Despite the park views and architectural significance, it's still a century-old building, and buyers at this price range have their pick.

However, "when buyers at this level want prewar charm and detail, there is only one way to get it authentically, and that is to buy of that era," he says. "Are there people in droves wanting to pay at this level for prewar? No, but there are people who do. It's usually a more sophisticated buyer.

"They want that, 'This is classic New York.'"

The final price isn't yet known, but Reisor says, "We can assume it was right around [the ask price], if not right on."

The buyer hasn't been revealed, and listing agent Boardman did not return a call for comment.

One unique aspect of the 50 Central Park prewar is that it is a condo, not a co-op. Prewar buildings—built between the late 1880s and 1939—are typically co-ops.

In general, condos outsell co-ops each week—and after two weeks of the gap between them being slim, for the week ending Nov. 8, condos were far ahead, 34 contracts versus 7. Co-ops tend to be cheaper, with an average ask of $8.6 million last week versus $10.5 million for condos, according to Olshan Realty.

Another unusual feature of the sprawling duplex is that it has only three bedrooms. Often condos at this scale have expanded to four or more. Last week's top sale, a $31 million penthouse condo at 111 Murray St. in Tribeca, had five bedrooms.

"It's more of a psychological sticking point than an actual need," Reisor says of a high-end buyer's desire for plentiful bedrooms. "They think, 'At a certain price, I should be overflowing with bedrooms.' Even if they never need them."

Whoever snapped up the trophy unit likely wanted to be in that particular building, which is low-density, has only 11 private residences inside of a global luxury brand, and comes with a glamorous history and illustrious architect.

"It could be someone high-profile," Reisor speculates of the mystery buyer. "Someone high-profile would want something like this."

The Wynn sale was one of a slew of high-end contracts last week, with 45 signed, a big leap of 11 over the week before, and the biggest luxury sales week for the entire year. Of that, an astonishing 14 sales were priced over $10 million.

The New York City pied-à-terre tax doesn't seem to be squeezing the top end of the market. Reisor says last week's surge of luxury (over $4 million) buys can be attributed to the midterm elections.

"We always see a freeze-up before elections," he says. "The enemy to any financial market, including the housing market, is uncertainty. The biggest thing in play is that the election happened; it's over."

Additionally, he thinks that the "blue wave" was a positive for the high-end market, speculating that President Donald Trump's somewhat chastened stance on immigration is good news for monied buyers.

"This is especially relevant to New York City because of international buyers," he says, theorizing that affluent foreign buyers are wondering, "Will this be more friendly to me as an international purchaser?"

The second-highest contract of the week was a triplex penthouse with an asking price of $25.36 million. The condo, EA at 95 Madison Ave., is also in a 1912 prewar building. Although smaller at 8,355 square feet, the condo came with six bedrooms.

Formerly the Emmet Building, the Flatiron building is a quick stroll to Madison Square Park. Its Gothic facade is made from limestone and marble, reflecting the "architectural Golden Age," says the listing, which was held by Vicki Zhi Saali of Global Real Estate Ventures Group.

In a key coastal area of Peru, violence was low despite the practice of human sacrifice.

Despite engaging in human sacrifice, the people who lived in the Lambayeque Valley of northern Peru had low levels of violence, a new study of their remains finds.

Human sacrifice occurred in the valley over the millennia. It's thought that at least some of these mass sacrifices were attempts to appease the gods in the wake of El Niño, a periodic weather phenomenon that can lead to intense rains and flooding in Peru and droughts in other parts of South and Central America.

"We have one example at the site of Sicán where around [A.D.] 1050/1100, around 200 individuals were killed and ritualistically buried during an intense El Niño event," study co-author Haagen Klaus, an anthropology professor at George Mason University in Virginia, told Live Science in an email.

Other human sacrifice findings include more than 200 children found near the modern-day town of Huanchaco who were sacrificed sometime between 600 and 800 years ago, possibly during an El Niño.

However, despite the occurrence of human sacrifice, levels of violence unrelated to sacrifice were actually quite low in the Lambayeque Valley. In the study, scientists analyzed the skeletal remains of 1,519 people from the Lambayeque Valley who lived between roughly 1500 B.C. and A.D. 1800, and whose remains were preserved enough to detect signs of violent trauma, including interpersonal violence.

A skull showing blunt force trauma that dates to the Chimu period, roughly 1375 to 1490.

Of these skeletons, "only 25 (1.6%) exhibited traumatic lesions consistent with interpersonal conflict, and 77 exhibited post-cranial traumatic lesions consistent with accidental trauma (5.1%)," Klaus and co-author Thomas Snyder, a bioarchaeologist at New York University, wrote in the paper, which was published Nov. 8 in the journal Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology.

The team didn't find any link between a person's sex and violence, they noted. The findings mean that despite the presence of human sacrifice, the amount of violence unrelated to sacrifice was low. This leaves a question: Why?

"Ritual violence may have alleviated non-ritual violence by creating spaces where social tensions could get worked out in a few different ways," Klaus said.

It's important to remember that other areas of Peru also practiced human sacrifice but didn't have the same low levels of interpersonal violence. Klaus noted that areas in the Peruvian highlands sometimes had higher rates of interpersonal violence. In their paper, Klaus and Snyder noted that societies that originated in the highlands, such as the Tiwanaku, Wari and Inca had to grapple with poorer soils and cold temperatures, and these challenges may have been a factor in increasing the levels of interpersonal violence.

The "evidence of sub-lethal and lethal violence forceful enough to break bone is significantly higher in some areas of Peru's highlands," Klaus said. He and Snyder noted in their paper that the inhabitants of the Lambayeque Valley may have had better access to marine and water resources, such as seafood and rivers that could be used for agricultural irrigation, than those who lived in highland areas. Access to these resources may have helped alleviate interpersonal violence in the valley by providing more food resources, they wrote.

Aside from practicing human sacrifice, people in the valley may have engaged in other practices that helped lower levels of interpersonal violence, researchers who were not involved in the new study told Live Science.

Environmental conditions may have helped reduce conflict or there could be "really unusual cultural practices in this region of avoiding or mediating conflict — both between and within groups," Elizabeth Arkush, an anthropology professor at the University of Pittsburgh, said in an email.

Constanza Ceruti, an anthropologist and explorer with the National Geographic Society, said in an email that she's glad that researchers are studying human sacrifices "after decades of being ignored as a taboo topic in academic discussion." She praised the study’s large sample size and noted that human sacrifice is a "phenomenon primarily associated [with] the manipulation and concentration of power by elites."

Jordi Rivera Prince, an anthropology professor at Tulane University, said that the findings dovetail with her excavations in the region, where she has "found little evidence of violent trauma during the Salinar (400-200 BCE), a period of great social change in the Andes."

Weston McCool, an archaeology professor at Cal Poly, said in an email that the newly published paper "is an interesting study that provides additional evidence for a sharp break in patterns of violence between the Andean coast and highlands." He noted that it "supports the hypothesis that coastal populations were buffered from terrestrial environmental downturns in ways that highland populations were not. The nature of that resilience, however, remains an important question for future research."

Thomas Massie files articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY), seen here on September 16, 2026 at the U.S. Capitol, filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Monday. Roberto Schmidt/Getty Images hide caption

Rep. Thomas Massie has reintroduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, setting up a vote in the House of Representatives in the next two days.

The Kentucky Republican's resolution accuses Hegseth of high crimes and misdemeanors and lists eight specific charges against him.

Pete Hegseth greets British Defense Secretary Wes Streeting at the Pentagon on Oct. 15, 2026. Hegseth says any effort to impeachment him will fail. Finn Gomez/Getty Images hide caption

Hegseth defended his record at the Pentagon in a video released last Thursday, dismissing Massie's effort as "sham articles of impeachment."

"Didn't work against President Trump and it won't work with me," Hegseth said.

Massie has long been a staunch critic of Hegseth. During the GOP primary, Hegseth campaigned on behalf of Ed Gallrein, who went on to defeat Massie.

He first sought to bring the impeachment resolution to the floor before the midterm elections, prompting Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., to call an early recess to avoid the vote. That caused the resolution to expire, which is why Massie reintroduced the resolution Monday.

Presumptive next House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., has already signaled support for an impeachment push against Hegseth.

Even Republican members who oppose impeaching Hegseth have said the Trump administration could do a better job making the case to Congress and the public about what the plan is for the Iran war going forward.

Rep. Warren Davidson, R-Ohio., indicated to NPR that impeachment would be hypocritical given that administrations going back to the Korean War have largely ignored Congress' war powers efforts.

"I think trying to scapegoat Pete Hegseth is just the status quo politics in Washington," he said. "I think it would be a mistake to impeach."

Davidson said lawmakers who want changes are better off trying to leverage the power of the purse to withhold money from the conflict.

There could likely be sufficient votes to impeach Hegseth in the House, with the support of all Democrats, Massie, and a handful of Republicans. However, it is unlikely to clear the higher Senate threshold for convicting and removing Hegseth from office — which requires two-thirds of the Senate.

After months of skirmishing, the Trump administration removed WuXi AppTec, a key contract developer and manufacturer for the pharmaceutical industry, from its list of “Chinese military companies” operating in the U.S.

The U.S. Department of Defense took this step to comply with a court ruling last August, according to a notice with the Federal Register. At the time, a U.S. District Court judge temporarily blocked the administration from pursuing the designation after WuXi filed a lawsuit arguing it was arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act.

Two months earlier, the administration had placed WuXi AppTec on the list over concerns that the company has ties to the Chinese government and military, and that U.S. biotech leadership is at risk due to growing competition from Chinese companies. WuXi is seen by China hawks in Washington as a rising threat to national security.

James Gunn And Peter Safran's DC Exit Exposes A Bigger Hollywood Problem

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

James Gunn and Peter Safran are officially out as DC Studios co-heads in what is a major shock to both the industry and fans expecting much more from their still burgeoning DC Universe. The pair were announced as the shepherds of DC's cinematic enterprise back in November 2022. But four years later, their journey has come to a premature end. What comes next is anyone's guess. All we know for sure is that former Marvel Studios executive Jeremy Latcham is taking over and that Gunn will still release his "Superman" sequel "Man of Tomorrow."

In fairness, Gunn and Safran's approach seemed ill-advised from the outset. The now shuttered DC Extended Universe, or "Snyderverse" as it was known, went out in 2023 with a string of flops that proved audiences were tired of what Warner Bros. and DC were providing. Instead of starting from scratch, though, Gunn and Safran rebooted the franchise with a few caveats, retaining elements of the previous cinematic universe while jettisoning most of the major characters and storylines. At a time when superhero movies were already starting to lose their box office appeal, it seemed like an odd approach, at the very least.

On the plus side, Gunn was allowed to take big swings by green-lighting projects based on little-known DC characters. That, at a time when popular IP is being mined for all its worth, was refreshing, if risky. Now that he and his DC Studios co-head are gone, however, we'll almost certainly see much less of that and a lot more focus on well-known characters with proven box office appeal. It's a complex and nuanced situation that ultimately leaves DC in a precarious position ... and audiences even more confused and frustrated than they already were.

The Warner Bros. Discovery/Paramount merger became official in October 2026, after which it looked as though James Gunn and Peter Safran were relatively safe in their roles as DC Studios co-heads. David Ellison, CEO of the newly-christened Skydance,  previously seemed to support the pair's vision for the DCU going forward. He even flew to the "Man of Tomorrow" set prior to the merger becoming official, in what appeared to be tacit approval of Gunn's DC roadmap. Alas, it seems Ellison had other plans all along.

At this point, we don't know the details of Gunn and Safran's departure. As we await further information about what Jeremy Latcham has in-store, it seems almost certain he won't be green-lighting shows such as "Creature Commandos," the animated series that Gunn used to kick off his DC Universe, or movies such as the box office flop "Supergirl." Instead, we're likely to see DC double down on its marquee characters: Superman, Batman, and Wonder Woman.

On the one hand, that is a real shame, as it reinforces Hollywood's fixation on established IP and suggests filmmakers with more interesting creative visions will be sidelined. On the other, you have to wonder what Gunn and Safran were thinking with these projects based on characters of whom mass audiences had little to no awareness. By all means, make an animated "Creature Commandos" show and talk up your movie based on the "Authority" comics. But maybe save them for after we've gotten over the whole superhero fatigue issue and restored DC's cinematic fortunes?

Ironically enough, former Warner Bros. Discovery CEO David Zaslav, who appointed James Gunn and Peter Safran as DC Studios co-heads, couldn't get enough of IP. In his first investor conference, he told shareholders that Warner Bros. was "the greatest treasure of global IP," prior to approving a Harry Potter reboot series that surely stands as the most conspicuous symbol of Hollywood's obsession with IP and nostalgia. When Gunn laid out his vision for the DC Universe, he wisely touted "Superman" as the centerpiece of his plans. But he also spoke enthusiastically about projects based on characters nobody outside of comic book fandom had heard of.

Since then, he's overseen some hits: the aforementioned "Superman," the HBO series "Lanterns," and the most recent DC Studios movie, "Clayface." But at the same time, he's overseen "Supergirl" flopping in spectacular fashion, and his animated series "Creature Commandos" didn't make the kind of splash that the DCU needed (despite being well-received by those who watched it). What's more, we've been getting podcasts in which he discusses whether his 2021 film "The Suicide Squad" is canon to the DCU or not. I think this is the sort of stuff that, viewed in hindsight, will seem so obviously misguided.

Meanwhile, the threat of a DC cinematic universe populated by bland, big-budget movies designed to capitalize on audiences' awareness of IP looms. Viewed from any perspective, this is a pretty dire situation that will do nothing to help bring superhero movies back to prominence, and it may even actively hasten their decline. And to think, all we needed was a full DC reset in 2022.

Lindsay Clancy is one step closer to possibly facing a retrial over the deaths of her three young kids ... after a Massachusetts judge rejected her attempt to get the murder charges tossed.

According to court docs, obtained by TMZ, the judge denied Clancy's motion to dismiss the case, ruling a second trial would not violate her constitutional protection against double jeopardy.

The judge determined there was manifest necessity to declare a mistrial in September after jurors failed to reach a unanimous verdict.

In a separate ruling, the judge also denied Clancy's request to investigate the holdout juror over allegations of misconduct, including claims the juror lied on a questionnaire and improperly used a cellphone in the jury room.

Her attorneys argue she was suffering from postpartum psychosis and was not criminally responsible for the killings.

Toukie Smith—Model, Actress and Robert De Niro's Ex—Dead at 74

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Toukie Smith, a model and 227 star who also shared two kids with ex Robert De Niro, died Nov. 4, her and De Niro’s children confirmed in a statement. She was 74.

A model has left the world after making a tremendous impact.

Toukie Smith, a model who was also known for her role as Eva Rawley in 227, died Nov. 4 after a “long, courageous battle” with multiple sclerosis, her and ex Robert De Niro’s children Airyn and Julian confirmed to The Hollywood Reporter. She was 74.

Smith, born Doris A. Smith, was born in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania in 1952 and made her debut in the modeling industry in the 1970s, inspired by her brother Willi Smith, a renowned fashion designer and often used her as his muse.

In 1989, she scored the role as Eva on the final seasons of the NBC sitcom that also starred Regina King, Marla Gibbs and Helen Martin. She went on to appear in Joe’s Apartment, Miami Vice and Goosed but later admitted it was hard for her to break through in Hollywood.

"Everybody has to get it right," she told the New York Times in 1992. "I am an actress first. I'm not a sometime model.”

Nevertheless, she joked, “I thought modeling was rough with rejection. Huh! Welcome to the real world of acting!”

But Smith’s life couldn’t simply be narrowed down into one career. In addition to acting as well as appearing in modeling campaigns for Chanel, Versace and Yves Saint Laurent, she also opened a restaurant, Toukie’s, in 1994. The establishment sat on West Houston in Manhattan and donated some of its profits to help those fighting AIDs in honor of her brother, who died from illness related to the disease in 1987.

“I opened this place because big parties are one thing,” she told the New York Times after the restaurant opened, “but now it's time for us to come together, to connect.”

Smith and De Niro—who began dating in 1988—welcomed Airyn and Julian in 1995 through a surrogate. The former couple split about a year later, but while they often stepped out together, they rarely shared more insight into their romance. As she told the Los Angeles Times when asked about the romance in 1990, “What’s great about a private life is it’s private.”

What Smith was most known for, however, is synonymous with her nickname, which she said her grandmother gave her from a children’s song about a determined fire engine.

As she told the Los Angeles Times of the moniker, “I’ve always had lots of energy.”

Robert De Niro met his first wife, Diahnne Abbott, while making Martin Scorsese's Taxi Driver, in which she had a bit part as the ticket taker at a porn theater Travis Bickle patronizes in the gritty 1976 classic.

The couple also appeared together in Scorsese's 1977 tortured-romance drama New York, New York and his 1982 satire The King of Comedy.

The actor adopted Diahnne's daughter Drena, born Sept. 3, 1967, when they got married.

Drena's first Instagram post is a black-and-white snap of her as a child out to dinner with her parents and then-couple Jack Nicholson and Anjelica Huston, captioned "At this point all I wonder is WTF was I doing there ???"

She caught the creative bug early, though, and remained in show business as an actor and producer.

Growing up, "it definitely wasn't the typical family lifestyle," she told Everything Zoomer, "but there was a lot of warmth, fun, craziness." She described her father as "a non-judgmental, open-minded person, always accepting of what people feel their paths are."

International order is not the absence of conflict, nor is it the rigid defense of the status quo. Rather, it is the existence of predictable mechanisms to manage inevitable disagreements. And the primary mechanism tasked with upholding international peace and security—the United Nations Security Council (UNSC)—is no longer fit for purpose     .

The UNSC still resembles the world of 1945, giving privileged status to the victors of World War II and the holders of colonial empires. Without reform to give rising and middle powers a status commensurate with their growing influence, states will eventually see more sense in pursuing their security interests unilaterally or through exclusive, destabilizing blocs. For now, with a few notable exceptions, states remain committed to speaking the language of multilateralism. But make no mistake: Reforming the UNSC is not a matter of aesthetics or desirability; it is an absolute necessity.

Three dynamics have held up reform. But each may be slowly giving way, creating a rare window for genuine change.

The first and most commonly cited obstacle is the Security Council’s five permanent veto-wielding members, or P5—the United States, China, Russia, France, and the United Kingdom. The P5 countries are wary of admitting rising powers as fellow veto wielders, as it would diminish their own influence in the body and make it more susceptible to deadlock.

However, without reform, the council’s legitimacy in the eyes of the global majority will deteriorate, rendering it more and more irrelevant. The P5 will be the biggest losers if the UNSC fades into insignificance, as they have the most to lose: their veto prerogatives. Consequently, the P5 are only likely to allow for reform when the council appears      to be nearing      the brink of collapse—a point that the world may soon      approach      if the P5 continue to wield their vetoes with impunity.

The second issue is the deadlock between the two main reform groupings: the G4 coalition of Germany, Japan, Brazil, and India, which favor enlarging the permanent membership of the UNSC to include themselves, and the Uniting for Consensus group, which favors only adding additional elected seats to the council. The uncompromising competition between these two blocs has prevented other proposals from gaining steam. However, Germany’s recent failed bid for a two-year term on the council has put into question the viability of its claim to a permanent seat—and the viability of the G4 proposal as a whole. This enables a possible shake-up that allows for the emergence of new and more creative reform ideas.

The third factor is that discussions over which reform model would best enhance the UNSC’s efficiency, effectiveness, and legitimacy has, to date, been largely speculative. Neither side has been able to predict with any meaningful accuracy the implications of their proposed reforms.

This, too, is now changing. Thanks to artificial intelligence, complex simulations can be conducted that calculate potential shifts in the council’s performance. We have conducted such a simulation to determine precisely what each proposal would achieve—and what it would risk. The simulation shows that the G4 model would dramatically increase gridlock over the next five decades. Council business would slow down as decision-making speed per resolution would grow from 28.5 days to 42.1 days, and veto use would grow by more than 60 percent. Despite giving them a seat at the table, the resulting paralysis would encourage leading powers to work outside the UNSC, defeating the purpose of reform entirely.

Yet while the Uniting for Consensus group’s proposal would limit the damage to the council’s efficiency and effectiveness, it fails to resolve its legitimacy deficit. The UNSC cannot effectively serve as a long-term custodian of the international order if Britain continues to enjoy a more privileged position than India—the world’s most populous country and likely soon to be its third-largest economy.

How, then, to resolve this conundrum? The AI simulation points to two mechanisms.

First, if more countries are to gain the veto, then this must be balanced by creating a veto override mechanism: for example, a veto wielder must secure one or two concurring negative votes among sitting council members, otherwise, a two-thirds majority in the General Assembly can overturn the veto.

The simulation shows that this would reduce veto usage by up to 40 percent, even before any cast vetoes are overturned. It would also speed up decision-making on average from 28.5 days to 21 days because veto wielders would either know that they have the necessary concurring vote(s) and cast their veto without delay, or know that they don’t and be incentivized to compromise to avoid a potentially humiliating General Assembly override.

Skeptics argue that the P5 countries are unlikely to agree to any reform that dilutes their veto privileges. But the simulation projects that successful overrides would occur only 28 percent of the time (or as low as 14 percent of the time, if the vote to override takes place by public roll call rather than by secret ballot). Agreeing to this limited mechanism, which would only take effect when a great power is isolated to the point of having no allies on an expanded council, is a small price to pay to ensure the council’s long-term legitimacy—and the permanent members’ privileged position that comes with it.

Second, a pool of “semipermanent” members, serving multiple lengthy terms and rotating on and off the council a few countries at a time, would offer middle powers that fail to earn a permanent seat a substantial consolation prize. According to the simulation, it would also triple small states’ likelihood of election to an ordinary council seat by taking these middle powers out of the running.

Countries such as Japan, Brazil, Argentina, India, and Pakistan currently monopolize the council’s nonpermanent seats, serving on average more than one term per decade, leaving smaller countries to earn a place only once every 30-50 years. The simulation shows that this proposal, if tabled by a strong coalition of small and developing states, has a 78 percent chance of being adopted and contributing to breaking the logjam.

The “international order” may be an abstract concept, but it takes concrete form in the institutions through which states manage their differences, constrain the use of force, and prevent conflict from descending into chaos. None matters more than the UNSC.

For decades, the presumed trade-off between legitimacy and effectiveness has been an obstacle to meaningful reform. Our simulations suggest that this is a false choice: A more representative council can also be a more effective one. What is, in fact, impossible is a new and stable order without a Security Council designed for it.

A Sam Altman parody offers an amusing lesson in memory management

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We verified the RAM numbers are real without sending any emails to OpenAI.

Sam Altman’s recent comments on the Cheeky Pint podcast inspired developer Iain Kay to create a satirical script that allows users to email the OpenAI CEO once per day if more than 10 percent of their system RAM is reported as available. Ram-Altman is described by its creator as a “parody project” launched in response to Altman’s viral remarks about RAM in your personal PC.

As reported by The Verge, on the October 29 episode recorded with Stripe President John Collison, Altman said, “A lot of people want local inference. And somehow, they assume that the problem is solved now because we are going to have open source models, we’re going to be able to run them on our computers, and so we’re not going to need all the stuff in the data center. But from a global allocation perspective, actually, it’s quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world’s limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer and being used 5 percent of the time.”

Altman has since explained that his comments were taken out of context and briefly changed his display name to “RAM Altman” on X, which Kay calls on the project GitHub, “objectively excellent and where the project gets its name.”

In an email to Ars, Kay said, “Ram-Altman is a bash script that takes MemTotal minus MemAvailable from /proc/meminfo, so the figure includes page cache and reclaimable slab but leaves out tmpfs and shared memory.”

If the script only showed what Linux reports as “free” RAM, it would paint an incomplete picture. Linux often reports a surprisingly small amount of “free” RAM, as it doesn’t count memory that is currently claimable for use. Linux will fill available RAM caches with file data for quick access, but the kernel can reclaim that usage at a moment’s notice when needed. The MemAvailable figure is the kernel’s own estimate of how much memory it could hand over without swapping.

Users can schedule the script to ask the kernel for a status update every five minutes, and if more than 10 percent of RAM is available, it triggers a joke email from one of several joke templates. The script limits outgoing emails to one per day.

The GitHub page for the RAM-Altman project includes a dry-run option that any Linux user with a Bash terminal should be able to paste and run without the need for email trolling. When the threshold is exceeded, each dry run output features one generated joke email, so repeated runs produce different amusing examples.

A test run of the script on my Fedora 44 KDE machine confirmed the author’s measurements. Inside the System – Info Center of my Fedora desktop, the system reports 14.9 GiB of total usable system RAM after the kernel and firmware take their needed slices of the 16 GiB of physical memory. This reflects the total amount of capacity the system has to work with, not the amount available at any given time. The Ram-Altman script shows that of that 14.9 GiB total pool, 10 GiB is used, with 4.9 GiB available (33.1 percent). That figure isn’t the simple sum of the “free” and “cache” lines that the script displays, but a kernel-calculated figure that accounts for the full amount of memory that can be reclaimed.

The project concedes the nuance that Altman was referring to how much time memory spends being utilized, while the script measures how much capacity is free at the time of measurement. While the script might tell Altman that 33 percent of your system RAM is available, it doesn’t outline how often that’s the case. The concession steps on the joke slightly, but it keeps everyone honest.

The GitHub is wise to point out that “--dry-run is the best way to enjoy this. It costs nothing and all the jokes are there.” Used irresponsibly, this cheeky joke could morph into a harassment tool, and Kay quickly points out that the script does not include anyone’s email, including Altman’s. Users who deploy their own operating version are encouraged to only use their own real email addresses and honor any requests to stop, as is outlined at the bottom of the script-generated output.

If you’re interested in taking a look at some of the emails generated without having to spin up a Linux machine that reports the system status every five minutes, the ram-altman.lol project website rotates through sample emails generated by the script. Kay writes on the website that, “In fairness, he says the remarks were taken out of context, posted ‘i want you to have so much ram’, and briefly went by Ram Altman on X. That makes this script a daily progress report.”

Inspired by Fall Marathon Season? Here’s What You Need to Do to Prepare for 26.2

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Follow these nine coach-approved training tips now for a strong finish later.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

The spirit of the fall marathon is undeniably contagious: the roaring crowds, the crisp autumn air, and massive community energy. If you spectated this year, watching the hundreds (if not thousands) of runners race toward the finish line might have you yearning to lace up your shoes and hit the starting line next season. But before you jump into training, hit pause and make sure you’re prepared for success.

“Training to run a marathon will revolutionize your life, but it will also test you in brand-new ways,” says Susie Crossland-Dwyer, an avid marathoner and ultramarathoner, coach, and founder of studio s in Cincinnati. “A lot goes into a healthy, successful training season and what you do leading up to it.”

Here are nine tips that coaches want every marathoner to know to get to race day healthy, strong, and fast!

You’re about to set out on four-plus months of intense weekly mileage. That’s really tough on your body. To sidestep an overuse injury, having a steady foundation is crucial. A review of research on runners’ injuries published in the Journal of Sport and Health Science found that the injury incidence rate among runners is 40.2 percent, and overuse from ramping up mileage or intensity too quickly causes more than 70 percent of the injuries.

“As you begin your marathon season, start with base building and don’t just jump right into doing all the miles and all the speed workouts,” advises Crossland-Dwyer, who recommends spending at least three months gradually working up to 20 to 25 miles per week before starting an official marathon training program.

“Strength training, specifically your core and hips, is the most effective way to prevent injuries,” says Jeff Gaudette, owner and head coach at RunnersConnect. “As you ramp up your mileage for the marathon, this will be essential to you staying healthy through the training segment.”

Studies have found that strength training can also boost your performance by improving your running economy. You don’t need a certain base of strength before starting your training plan, he says, but you should include two to three weight workouts each week.

Along with weight training, “try plyometrics to get your body used to absorbing impact, single-leg exercises to prevent muscle imbalances, and mobility drills to keep your range of motion and joints healthy,” advises Meg Takacs, NASM-CPT, a run coach and founder of the Movement & Miles app.

Carb-loading before race day is what most novice runners are familiar with when starting on their marathon-training journey, but it’s only one aspect of your diet that’s important.

“Nutrition is key for successful training and a successful race—from carbs to water, electrolytes, and salt capsules,” says Takacs. “The goal is to stay ahead of any deficits before they happen because once you’re in a hydration or nutritional deficit, it’s almost impossible to come back.”

Buy water bottles to carry with you on long runs (sip water or a sports drink every 10 to 15 minutes), plus gels or gummies to nibble on every 30 minutes to stay fueled and strong through your long runs ahead.

We’re not talking about the race itself—though, yes, you’re going to be on your feet for a long time for that, too. But it’s important to know the kind of time commitment you’re going to be looking at for the next 16 or so weeks leading up to race day.

You’ll be devoting a couple hours, at least, likely every Saturday or Sunday morning to a long run, plus your other weekly runs. But also, says Crossland-Dwyer, “Training for a marathon isn’t all about running. In fact, a big portion of your weekly training hours should be spent preparing, with prehab exercises and prerun activations, doing strength-training, and recovering with mobility work, adequate rest and refueling, and extras like compression and cold therapy.”

Yes, destination races are popular, and if you’re pretty sure this whole marathon thing is going to be a one-and-done experience for you, you might as well pick an epic course. But don’t ignore races in your backyard.

“It’s so fun to see your city streets opened up for you,” says Erin Beck, vice president of innovation, product, and experience at Xponential Fitness, who’s run 21 marathons. “Seeing your city from a different vantage point than usual can keep your mind off of the treachery of 26.2 miles.”

Yes, you have several months until race day. But don’t wait to find your favorite clothing and shoes. “Purchase the gear you’ll need now, including shoes for training, the shoes you’ll race in, your potential race kit, and options for different weather conditions,” Gaudette says. “That way, you can test everything out during a few training runs to ensure it’s comfortable.”

Don’t forget about accessories: Having the right details for you, from the fuel belt that bounces the least on your hips to the socks that help you sidestep blisters, will make training all the more comfortable.

If you’re not sure where to start, check out a local running store or online forums for recs from knowledgeable contributors.

Runners joke about bloody nipples for a reason. Rubbed-raw skin there—or your inner thighs or armpit or your upper arm where it shifts against your shirt or sports bra—is inevitable at some point (or several) during marathon training.

Blisters from ill-fitting shoes or the wrong socks could be in your near future too. “Unfortunately, the only way to really know what clothing will contribute to this to test it out, which is why it’s good to have everything you might wear early,” says Gaudette.

In the meantime, buy an anti-chafing stick or cream now and lube up before every long run ahead to protect your skin and slash the chances that you’ll be in a lot of pain later.

All the anticipation of gearing up for your race will likely be mixed with nervousness, too. Remind yourself that you can do this because so many people like you have done the same.

“You don’t have to be Eliud Kipchoge to run a race—and you don’t have to know who that is to run a race, either!” says Beck. “The only thing you have to be in order to be a marathoner is bold enough to try one.”

Every successful marathoner has a training plan to follow. So before you dive into marathon training, you’ll want to find a plan that spans at least 16 weeks and fits your fitness level and your goals.

Do you just want to finish? Are you looking to break three, four, or five hours? Can you meet the weekly mileage in week one of the plan? Think about these questions and find the plan that works to guide you to the starting line and finish line feeling strong.

Go Inside Miu Miu’s Handbag Atelier In Tuscany

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Twenty years in, the brand’s matelassé leather remains a luxury calling card.

March 4, 2006, was perhaps like any other day in the mid-aughts. Myspace was the top website in the U.S., while Beyoncé’s “Check On It” was No. 1 on the Billboard charts. Cult-classic teen mermaid drama Aquamarine had just premiered the night before. And in Paris, during the fall/winter fashion shows, the date would prove to be the unexpected start of a new era—not just for one brand, but for fashion as a whole.

At the 18th-century Left Bank restaurant Lapérouse, Miu Miu presented its first-ever show in the French capital—now its permanent runway home—signaling a crucial differentiation from its sister brand Prada in Milan. Paper-thin knits, ultra-mini dresses, peplum jackets, chunky platform wedges, and mismatched-lace oxfords walked the intimate runway, on models adorned with white eyelashes and poppy-red lips. On the handbag front, an experimental leather-making technique was introduced, one that would become Miu Miu’s signature and a staple of aughts aesthetics: matelassé.

Originating in early 18th-century Marseille, the quilting technique was traditionally used in bedding or upholstery. In Miu Miu’s collection, it was developed in the form of a leather handbag—the Coffer—a slouchy shoulder tote in chocolate brown with a twisted handle and a bold metal clasp. A counterpoint to the youthfully eccentric clothes, the bag itself was a subtle debut, with only three shown on the runway and in only one colorway. Nonetheless, it was an immediate hit, carried by Anne Hathaway, Lindsay Lohan, and Isla Fisher in its early years. For the relatively young but increasingly iconic brand, a calling card was born.

Twenty years since that collection, Miu Miu has transformed its matelassé technique into a rainbow of colorways and style offerings. The seemingly seamless Wander and boxy Arcadie styles boast prime spots in the brand’s portfolio of leather goods, especially when it comes to the matelassé iterations—just take a look at the brand’s 2026 campaign starring Gigi Hadid.

The handbags come to life in Miu Miu’s leather goods workshop in Tuscany, the architecturally award-winning headquarters called The Garden Factory, built in the Valvigna region near where Miuccia Prada’s husband Patrizio Bertelli grew up, and designed by Guido Canali in the mid-1990s. Completed in 2015, the building itself is a physical manifestation of the Prada Group’s minimalist aesthetic.

The Garden Factory’s nickname derives from the property’s one-to-one ratio of building to greenery, a complete balance of cool steel and native vines, and plenty of glass windows to bridge the two. Inside is row upon row of accessories—66,000 leather goods and 77,000 pairs of shoes—carefully filed from the collections over the years to serve as both record and inspiration. Instead of season or collection, the cabinets are organized by product category, and matelassé has its own section.

Despite its creation 20 years ago—or perhaps in part because of it—the very tactility that makes Miu Miu’s matelassé leather so appealing is hitting a peak in current luxury collections across the board. To a consumer, the in-person experience was always a factor, but in the Instagram age, brands are turning to hyper-visual signifiers of craftsmanship to keep up with the market. Be it ASMR-inducing sequins, fluffy feathers, slick latex, or impossibly buttery leather, touch and feel appeal, even through an iPhone screen, is increasingly important. To see a matelassé handbag is to wonder how it feels, and to feel one is to experience its pillowy, permanent spring, an effect somewhere between plissé and origami. That effect is intentional, yet the product itself is softer in practice. That, too, is intentional.

The quilted technique is achieved by layering elastic Lycra with thin padding and nappa lambskin leather, sourced from Miu Miu’s tannery in France. Strategic patterns are stitched onto the layers and then the Lycra is removed, allowing the leather and padding to fold back into itself without losing the valleys created by the stitches. Thus, the matelassé effect comes to life. The smaller the product—like a card holder—the harder it is to craft. 3-D modeling is never used. Leather products are always assembled by hand, an important standard for the brand, and inspected manually by experts who know every detail of the bag’s design.

How does one become an expert in Miu Miu bags, you may ask (besides simply being a loyal fan)? Prada Group has its own leather goods academy where craftspeople spend six months learning about the brands’ luxury practices and standards. Even after those six months, it takes five to 10 years for a craftsperson to know how to make an entire bag themselves, start to finish.

From anywhere in the building, you have a view of the gardens outside, along with 45-degree-angled ceilings that let light in without creating too much heat. Stations are bustling with artisans, most of them women, in teams of two. They wear white lab coats emblazoned with the Prada Group logo.

The building’s minimalism, it seems, isn’t a lack, but an open space for creation to take place. And like the windows bridging steel to vine, or the archives linking past and present, or even the stitched valleys of a matelassé handbag, Miu Miu has mastered the spaces in between.
