The CFTC opens the door to Bitcoin reserves at US exchanges with a 20% catch

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CFTC guidance lets exchanges count Bitcoin toward operating reserves, exposing annual funding buffers to crypto price swings.

On Nov. 23, Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) staff said US futures exchanges and derivatives clearinghouses may count Bitcoin, Ethereum, and qualifying payment stablecoins toward a rolling year of operating costs after applying valuation discounts.

A hypothetical selloff shows how a firm can lose part of that buffer while its liquid assets still cover six months of expenses.

The agency's announcement described the letter as staff guidance. Existing rules remain in force, and the letter offers no assurance that staff would decline to recommend enforcement.

Under the rules for designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations, exchanges and clearinghouses need resources to cover at least one year of projected operating costs on a rolling basis.

Within that annual pool, a separate liquidity test calls for enough unencumbered cash or highly liquid securities to cover at least six months of expenses.

The rules allow committed credit lines or similar facilities to make up a liquidity shortfall under certain conditions. The crypto assets in the advisory do not count toward that six-month test.

For the annual calculation, staff says they would raise no objection to the approach in the advisory, which includes a minimum valuation discount of 20% for Bitcoin and Ethereum and a 2% discount for qualifying payment stablecoins.

These discounts, known as haircuts, apply to current market value, so the amount a firm can count moves with the price.

Take a hypothetical exchange with $12 million in annual costs spread evenly across the year, $6 million in unencumbered cash or other eligible liquid assets, and a Bitcoin position worth $7.5 million.

At a fixed 20% haircut, the Bitcoin counts for $6 million. Together, the liquid assets and the discounted Bitcoin position count for $12 million. That matches the annual cost estimate and leaves the exchange with zero headroom.

A 25% drop in the Bitcoin price reduces the position to $5.6 million before the haircut and $4.5 million after. Once the position is valued at the lower price, the firm has $10.5 million in counted resources, $1.5 million below the annual cost estimate.

The $6 million in eligible liquid assets still covers the six-month floor.

Staff also flag the risk that operating losses could coincide with falling crypto prices. In that case, a firm's reserve could lose value just as it needs more resources to absorb those losses.

At the stated benchmarks, $1 million worth of Bitcoin or Ethereum counts as $800,000, while $1 million in qualifying payment stablecoins counts as $980,000. The smaller discount for payment stablecoins comes with conditions on which tokens qualify.

Before the GENIUS Act takes effect, staff limit the stablecoin treatment to dollar-denominated tokens from a state-regulated money transmitter or trust company, or a national trust bank.

The issuer must back the tokens with reserves limited to cash, US Treasuries, or overnight Treasury repurchase agreements, held directly or indirectly.

Monthly attestations must cover what the reserves hold and whether their fair value matches the amount in circulation.

Once the law takes effect, the advisory applies its definition and requires a permitted issuer or a foreign issuer that meets the law's applicable conditions.

Customer margin is posted against trading positions, and clearinghouses separately set aside resources to cover clearing-member defaults. The clearinghouse rule bars them from counting the same resources toward both default coverage and operating costs.

Private Edtech Large File Browser Upload: Multipart Presigned Access Control

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use a small application control plane to authorize a private upload, create a multipart session, and issue short-lived presigned URLs for each part. Let the browser send bytes directly to object storage, then let the control plane complete or abort the session. This keeps access control in the application without making the application server carry the file.

That is the least complex design that still gives an edtech product a useful recovery story for a large lecture recording, assignment archive, or dataset. It also makes the important failure visible: an upload is a state machine, not a single request.

Before choosing an SDK or storage service, decide which side of the boundary owns each fact. The application owns identity, policy, and the upload record. Storage owns part transfer and assembly. This decision survives a provider change; a provider-specific URL format does not.

The production scenario is bounded and familiar: a student selects a private lecture recording, the browser uploads every part, and the UI shows success. A later download request still needs a fresh authorization decision. If the application treats the upload URL as proof of read access, or if it marks the row complete before storage confirms completion, the system has confused delivery with authorization. I've seen enough missed jobs and duplicate deliveries to distrust a green progress bar on its own.

The invariant is simple: the application owns identity and state; object storage owns byte transfer. Keep those responsibilities separate and a retry remains a retry instead of a second delivery. It sounds obvious. Production has a way of making obvious rules expensive.

The browser should never decide who may read or write an object. It asks the application for an upload session. The application authenticates the user, validates the declared size and content type, chooses an object key, and records the owner, expiry, expected part count, and status before returning URLs.

The browser uploads each numbered part directly to storage. It sends the resulting part identifier back to the application. The application verifies that the part belongs to the session and, only after all expected parts are accounted for, completes the upload. Download access is a separate decision: issue a short-lived presigned GET only after the user is allowed to see that object.

That session row should contain more than an object key. Persist the authenticated owner, expected byte size, part size, expected part count, storage upload ID, application deadline, and terminal status. Those fields let a reconciler distinguish a slow transfer from a client that vanished. They also let support answer the only question that matters during an incident: who was allowed to finish this upload, and what did the system believe had happened?

Here is the control-plane shape in Go. The storage client is intentionally behind the workflow; the HTTP handlers around it should enforce authentication, authorization, size limits, and origin policy.

The example omits one import for readability of the workflow: types is github.com/aws/aws-sdk-go-v2/service/s3/types. In a real service, persist the session before returning any presigned URL, and generate those URLs from the same bucket, key, upload ID, and part numbers. The browser may retry one part, but a retry should replace that part's recorded ETag. It should not create a second logical part.

One more rule matters. Completion must be idempotent. Store the terminal object metadata and return it when the same client request is repeated. A timeout after storage completes is normal network ambiguity; treating the retry as a new upload creates the duplicate-delivery incident the design was meant to avoid.

Three requests commonly arrive in an inconvenient order: a part retry, a user cancellation, and completion. Define the allowed transitions in the database: pending -> uploading -> complete, or pending/uploading -> aborted. A terminal row stays terminal. Completion checks ownership, session expiry, the exact part set, and the current status in one transaction before calling storage.

That last detail deserves emphasis. A transaction cannot roll back a storage completion. Use a small reconciliation job for the uncertain window: if storage reports completion but the application timed out before recording it, inspect the session and repair the row. If cancellation wins, call abort and mark the row aborted. The background job should retry an abort and report the attempt; it should not silently delete application evidence.

The hard case is a timeout after the storage service has accepted the final request. The browser sees no response, retries, and may race a support-triggered cancellation. A useful repair record captures the session ID, the last known part list, the completion attempt, the cancellation attempt, and the final storage observation. The reconciler then applies one rule: a confirmed completed object is recorded as complete, an unconfirmed session is left visible for another check, and an explicitly aborted session is never reopened by a late browser callback. That is more state than a one-request upload, but it is the state that explains why a private student file is downloadable, still uploading, or intentionally gone.

I have been paged for missed jobs and duplicate deliveries, so I put this sequence in the runbook, not in a comment nobody reads. The useful alerts are sessions past expiry, completion requests failing repeatedly, and objects whose application status does not match the recorded upload result. Your mileage may vary on thresholds; measure transfer duration and cancellation behavior before choosing them.

Storage lifecycle cleanup is a backstop. It is not a substitute for the cancel path, especially when users expect an abandoned upload to stop consuming resources quickly. Keep the application deadline shorter than the storage lifecycle window and make the difference explicit in operations documentation.

For private student uploads, multipart is a good fit when files are large enough that retrying from zero is painful and the team can own a session table. It is not suitable when the product only accepts small files, cannot run cleanup, or needs a single atomic request with no client-side coordination. Stick with a single presigned PUT for the small-file path; keep the multipart path for the failure mode it actually improves.

Access control also has a boundary that is easy to miss. A presigned URL is a bearer capability for its lifetime. Keep its lifetime short, bind it to one object and one part, avoid putting sensitive data in the key, and never use an upload URL as a download URL. For downloads, authorize the current user at request time and issue a new read URL. Private means the application still makes the decision.

Test the ugly cases first: an out-of-order part list, a duplicate part number, one part retried with a new ETag, an expired session, a completion retry after a client timeout, cancellation during completion, and a user attempting to finish another user's session. Verify that every rejected request leaves a diagnosable status.

The browser needs the storage origin's CORS policy to allow the methods and headers used by the presigned requests. The server needs request limits and content validation. The object key should be unguessable, but that is not authorization. Observability should include a session ID, user ID, object key hash, part count, and terminal transition, while avoiding raw bearer URLs in logs.

The postmortem question is simple: could the team tell which state the upload was in when the browser disappeared? If the answer is no, adding another retry loop will not fix the system. Record the state first.

greenServeFE programming language (open-source project)

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve been working on an open-source programming language called greenServeFE. It is a programming language with its own syntax, compiler, virtual machine, and native modules, written primarily in C.

The project started with a simple idea: I wanted to build something of my own rather than always depending on existing programming languages and frameworks. I was interested in understanding how programming languages actually work behind the scenes. How does a language read source code? How does it understand expressions and functions? How does it execute instructions? How can it interact with the operating system?

Instead of just reading about these concepts, I decided to build them.

Over time, greenServeFE grew beyond a small language experiment. I developed its lexer, parser, compiler, bytecode system, and virtual machine. I also started building native C modules that extend what programs written in the language can do.

One of the most interesting parts of the project has been the HTTP server module. My goal was to let developers build server-side applications using greenServeFE without needing Node.js or another runtime for the application itself.

The server module supports HTTP routes, request and response objects, middleware, static files, HTML responses, JSON responses, templates, and other server capabilities. I have also been working on improving its underlying implementation.

I wanted greenServeFE to be more than a syntax demonstration. I wanted people to be able to write actual programs with it.

I decided to make the project open-source because I want other developers to explore the implementation, understand how it works, report problems, and contribute ideas. I also want the source code to be useful to people who are interested in learning how programming languages and virtual machines are built.

At the beginning, I expected the hardest part would be designing the language syntax and getting the compiler to work.

I thought that once the parser and virtual machine were working, adding features would become relatively straightforward. I expected to write a few native modules, create examples, and gradually make the language more capable.

But developing a programming language is different from writing an ordinary application.

Every feature introduces new interactions with the existing implementation. A change in the parser can affect compilation. A compiler change can affect bytecode execution. A change in the runtime can affect native modules. Even something that looks simple from the user's perspective can require changes across several parts of the system.

I also expected that documenting the language would be relatively easy. In reality, keeping documentation, examples, module APIs, and actual implementation behavior consistent is another challenge.

One experience that made this especially clear was developing the HTTP server module.

I could register routes and start the server, but that did not necessarily mean every request-handling feature worked as expected.

For example, I created an HTML form that submitted customer and product information to a route. The page loaded, the form submitted, and the route executed. However, the values received by the handler were null.

At first, it looked like a small problem with accessing the request object. But it made me realize that I needed to understand the actual server implementation and its request-parsing behavior rather than assume that an API worked in the same way as a familiar framework.

I started comparing the documentation, examples, and C implementation. I found differences between what was documented and what the implementation supported. I also had to consider whether the installed native module matched the source code I was examining.

That experience reminded me that a programming language is not just its syntax. The runtime and native modules are equally important.

I have approached greenServeFE by building and testing individual components rather than trying to implement everything at once.

I started with the language itself: lexical analysis, parsing, compilation, and bytecode execution. I then expanded the language with functions, variables, control flow, arrays, objects, and other runtime features.

After that, I began working on native modules written in C. These modules allow greenServeFE programs to use functionality that would otherwise require implementing everything inside the language.

I have worked on modules for mathematics, JSON, computation, validation, strings, file operations, cryptography, and other utilities.

The HTTP server module has been a particularly interesting challenge because it connects the language to real network requests. It requires handling routes, request data, responses, static files, and server configuration.

I have also spent time improving the developer experience. I want installation to be straightforward, examples to be understandable, and documentation to explain the actual APIs instead of presenting features that do not exist in the implementation.

Not everything has worked on the first attempt. Some syntax produced parser errors. Some module functions behaved differently from what I expected. Some features required changes to the underlying C implementation.

I have also had to deal with development-environment limitations. I primarily work on Windows, while much of the building and testing happens in Linux cloud environments. Network reliability and limited computing resources sometimes make it difficult to follow the development workflow I originally planned.

One thing I have learned is to avoid treating a successful build as proof that a feature is complete. I need to test the actual behavior of the language, including how its components interact.

I have used AI coding assistants as development tools, especially when investigating C implementation details, reviewing compiler behavior, understanding errors, and exploring possible designs.

However, I still make the architectural decisions for greenServeFE. I decide what the language should look like, what features belong in it, how its APIs should be designed, and which problems need to be solved.

I also learn by reading source code, comparing implementations, testing examples, and investigating unexpected behavior.

I am interested in hearing from experienced systems programmers and programming-language developers because there are many areas where I would benefit from a more experienced perspective.

In particular, I would like feedback on the architecture of the virtual machine, the native module system, memory management, and the HTTP server implementation.

greenServeFE is an open-source project with an existing compiler, virtual machine, language syntax, and a growing collection of native modules.

The HTTP server module is also part of the project. Developers can import it and define routes using greenServeFE functions.

The idea is to write the server application in greenServeFE while the underlying HTTP functionality is implemented in C.

The project is still evolving. I am continuing to improve the runtime, expand native modules, fix inconsistencies, write examples, and make the server more reliable.

I do not consider it finished just because it can execute programs. There is still a lot of work involved in making a language dependable and comfortable for other developers to use.

The source code is publicly available, and contributions, bug reports, and technical feedback are welcome.

I would especially appreciate advice from people who have built programming languages, virtual machines, or native runtimes.

How do you decide when a language's core architecture is stable enough to focus on expanding its ecosystem?

What approaches do you recommend for testing a custom virtual machine and native modules, especially when the language and its modules are developed together?

I would also like to hear about maintaining consistency between documentation, examples, and actual implementation as a project grows.

Most importantly, I want to know what experienced developers think makes an independently developed programming language worth adopting and contributing to.

I started greenServeFE because I wanted to understand how programming languages work by building one myself. Now I hope it can become something other developers can learn from, use, and help improve.

When ubiattach Fails with "error 22 (Invalid argument)": The Cause Is in the Kernel Log

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Are you stuck because a UBI attach failed? Or do you want to know when a UBI attach can fail?

When an attach fails, ubiattach may print the following error:

error 22 is EINVAL (Invalid argument). But this only tells you that the kernel's attach returned -22. It does not necessarily mean that the arguments you gave ubiattach are wrong.

The cause is written in the kernel log, before this line. In this article, I reproduce three situations in which attach fails with error -22, and show which log lines appear before it in each case.

The test environment is Linux 6.6 running on QEMU's virt machine (ARM64), with nandsim, a kernel module that imitates a NAND flash chip. The NAND is 128 MiB, and an erase block (PEB) is 128 KiB.

Look at the kernel log, not at the output of ubiattach.

Go up from the failed to attach mtd1, error -22 line, and look for lines that contain ubi0 error:.

Each time ubiformat formats the flash, it chooses one number, image_seq, and writes the same value to the EC header of every PEB. At attach time, if UBI finds a PEB whose value (other than 0) differs from the value of the PEB read first, it gives up attaching.

The log above means that the image_seq of PEB 205 (1311134312) differs from the value of the PEB read earlier (597359688).

I stopped the ubiformat process in the middle of reformatting. ubiformat rewrites the PEBs in order from the start, with the new image_seq. If it stops partway, the PEBs not yet rewritten keep the value of the previous format. In this example, PEB 205 onward still had the old value.

UBI records which volumes exist (such as their names and sizes) in the volume table. It keeps two identical copies of the table, in LEB 0 and LEB 1 of a dedicated volume.

bad CRC at record 0 means that the CRC of record 0 of the table does not match. It appears twice because it did not match in either LEB 0 or LEB 1. If only one copy is corrupted, UBI restores it from the other copy and continues attaching (the log shows volume table was restored). If both copies are corrupted, UBI cannot tell which volumes exist, so it gives up attaching.

In both PEBs that hold the volume table, I changed one byte of the volume name.

A PEB assigned to a volume has a VID header after the EC header at its start (in this environment, the VID header starts at byte 512). The VID header records which LEB of which volume the PEB is assigned to. PEB 257 contains corrupted VID header means that the VID header of PEB 257 is corrupted.

When the VID header is corrupted, UBI cannot tell what the PEB is. Something is written in the data area (it is not all 0xFF), so UBI keeps this PEB without erasing it (preserved), so that a person can look into its contents.

In this example, the two corrupted PEBs were the PEBs of the dedicated volume that holds the volume table (the layout volume). UBI finds no PEB of the table, so it gives up attaching with the layout volume was not found.

In both PEBs that hold the volume table, I changed one byte of the VID header.

error 22 (Invalid argument) from ubiattach alone does not tell you the cause. In the kernel log, look at the ubi0 error: lines that appear before failed to attach.

When you investigate a returned product or similar, take a dump of the flash, including the OOB (nanddump -o --bb=dumpbad), before you format or attach it. Reformatting makes attach possible again, but neither the contents of the volumes nor the way the PEBs were corrupted remain. Even attaching alone can make UBI write to the flash: it may rewrite a corrupted volume table or move data to another PEB. By default, nanddump skips bad blocks when it dumps, so the PEBs after a bad block shift to earlier positions in the dump.

This article collects the attach failures from Chapters 7 and 8 of my book, Hands-On Embedded Linux UBI with QEMU: Stop Writing Off Flash Corruption as "Cause Unknown", and rewrites them.

In the book, with no real hardware, using just one PC, you load nandsim into Linux running on QEMU and put UBI on top of it. You dump the contents of the flash and compare them with the structures in the kernel source. Then you deliberately cause failures that could happen at any time on real hardware, and check in the logs and the contents of the flash what UBI does.

An appendix lists the UBI and NAND logs that appear in the book, along with when they appear.

Furthermore, the bill seeks to bar elected officials and policymaking staff from trading on such platforms, citing concerns over the use of non-public information. It would also establish penalties for election-related crimes carried out on prediction market platforms.

Your own bank takes cash free on a personal account, so use its branch or one of its ATMs that accept deposits. If your bank is online-only, hand the cash to a partner store’s cashier, often for a few dollars, or deposit it at a local bank or credit union and transfer it to your online account.

Paying a few dollars to deposit your own cash adds up fast when tips or cash sales come in every week. Where you make the deposit decides whether you pay anything at all.

Your bank’s own ATMs also let you avoid ATM fees when you take cash out. If your credit union belongs to the Co-op Shared Branch network, other credit unions in it will take your deposit too, at more than 5,500 locations across the country.

Large deposits come with a federal rule. Your bank or credit union must report cash deposits of more than $10,000 in a day to the Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, whatever the reason for them. It will ask for your ID and Social Security number to file that report. Depositing that much is allowed, but splitting it into smaller deposits to avoid the report is a crime called structuring, even when the cash came to you legally.

Many online banks and apps take cash at a store’s register, and the store may charge for it. With the Chime® app, cash deposits are free at Walgreens and Duane Reade, but other stores can charge up to $4.95 each time1. Capital One takes cash free at partner stores such as CVS and Walgreens for its checking and savings customers.

The free workaround needs a second account. Picture a server who deposits her tips at her credit union’s ATM every week, then moves what she’s saving to an online savings account. At a store charging $4.95, those 52 deposits would cost her about $257 a year instead of nothing.

If cash comes in every week, open a no-fee checking account at a credit union or bank near work and link it to your online bank.
