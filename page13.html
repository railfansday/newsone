AI visibility can hide evidence gaps that weaken buyer recommendations as requirements get specific, making decision readiness a crucial GEO metric.

For much of the past two years, the AI search conversation has focused on visibility. Teams want to know whether their brand is mentioned, where it appears in recommendations, how often competitors surface, and whether they are being cited across ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Google, and other AI surfaces.

Those are useful metrics, but I have increasingly questioned whether they tell us enough about the corresponding business outcome.

Nearly two years ago, I began using the term “eligibility gates” to describe the detailed decision criteria that determine whether a company or product remains qualified as a buyer moves through the decision process. I later expanded on that idea when I wrote Decision Coverage: whether an organization has exposed enough information and evidence for AI to evaluate, compare, qualify, and confidently recommend.

That led to two related arguments tied to previous articles. In my article Beyond Content Parity, I argued that using tools and processes that simply match competitor content may create parity without creating any decision advantage. In Beyond Brand Sovereignty, I argued that the knowledge AI needs often exists beyond the marketing site, scattered across product, sales, support, engineering, legal, customer success, and external sources.

A recent project allowed me to test those ideas against an actual business problem.

The company did not have an AI visibility problem. It frequently appeared in AI-generated consideration sets. The concern was what happened afterward, when prospective buyers continued the conversation and began asking more specific questions about requirements, workflows, integrations, outcomes, evidence, and competitive differences.

The question was not “How do we get seen in AI?” It was, “Why, with all this visibility, is our lead pipeline shrinking?” The reality we found was that as AI took a prospective buyer further down the consideration set, they became less competitive.

To prove my thesis and identify the problem, we created 38 detailed, criteria-based prompts across several product categories and buyer segments, and evaluated them on five AI surfaces, yielding 190 responses.

These were intentionally different from broad “best vendor” prompts. They reflected the kinds of requirements buyers use to narrow a field: workflow fit, integrations, regulatory requirements, interoperability, operational conditions, analytics, platform breadth, and other decision criteria. I had to create a detailed explanation of their purpose as diagnostic-only because the brand and their SEO agency argued that no human would ever use that complex a prompt. We actually found that some prompts were even longer after talking to actual procurement managers.

The initial numbers looked excellent – even better than their current visibility scorecard. Our research found the company was mentioned in 98.4% of evaluated responses and appeared as either a primary recommendation or shortlisted option 93.2% of the time. For many GEO programs, that would be the headline of the week and champagne for everyone. The brand was almost always visible and almost always treated as a serious option. But visibility was not the problem we were investigating.

When we evaluated those same responses against the decision criteria in the prompts, 73.7% had weak or missing evidence supporting important claims or recommendations about the company. In 15.8% of runs, an important association, decision criterion, competitive distinction, or discovery signal contained what we classified as a Significant or Critical Gap. The more we refined our evidence-scoring criteria, the higher this number rose.

The critical finding was that AI systems generally knew the company belonged in the conversation. However, they were far less consistent at substantiating why it should remain there once the buyer began applying specific eligibility criteria. That is the distinction that matters and explains their declining lead pipeline.

There is a meaningful difference between asking which vendors operate in a market and asking which vendors can satisfy a specific combination of technical, operational, regulatory, and business requirements.

A model may know a company is a legitimate vendor, understand its main products, and have enough general information to put it on a shortlist. But once the buyer asks how an integration works, whether a workflow is supported, what happens under a particular operating condition, what limitations apply, or what evidence supports an outcome claim, the information requirements become more demanding. Those are not merely additional keywords. They are evidence requirements.

That explains how a brand can achieve a 98.4% mention rate and 93.2% primary-or-shortlist rate while still having weak or missing evidence in nearly three-quarters of the responses. The numbers are not contradictory. They measure different stages of the decision process.

No one can dispute that AI visibility gets the company into the consideration set. Decision readiness determines whether AI has enough information to keep it there.

One of the more important findings was that weak evidence did not necessarily mean the company lacked the capability. In many cases, the capability appeared to exist, but the available information did not explain or substantiate it well enough to satisfy the AI surface’s confidence level.

A marketing bullet might claim an end-to-end process without ever showing the actual process, connections, or how the data flows between systems. We found efficiency claims but did not have any “proof” from internal tests or customer testimonials of the efficiency gain.

Sometimes the information was simply fragmented. A model might need to connect a product page to an integration page, then to another interoperability page, before it could infer how the pieces satisfied the buyer’s requirement. While an AI system could or should be able to make those multi-part connections, each additional inference creates another opportunity for incomplete representation or for a competitor with clearer evidence to become the safer recommendation.

We also found that the strongest content was not necessarily the longest or most buzzword-integrated, but rather content that made the relationships among capability, workflow, buyer requirements, and outcome explicit.

An interesting finding was that only first-party evidence was not inherently a weakness. Many claims lacked third-party validation, but this was not necessarily flagged as a defect. We found that authoritative product documentation may be the best evidence that a feature or integration exists. However, every claim involving superiority, effectiveness, adoption, scale, measurable outcomes, or competitive differentiation did benefit from independent corroboration by customers, industry, government, analysts, or other parties.

That means some evidence gaps are not content-writing problems at all, but does anyone else agree with the evidence?

The findings reinforced the concern I raised in my earlier work about how easy-button AI content-generation systems create “me-too content parity.” As I explained to this brand, most of the GEO efforts to date were what I would call SEO-adjacent GEO. It is a newly rebranded SEO process that leverages AI speed for traditional content-gap analysis. Teams look at competitor coverage, People Also Ask questions, common prompts, and pages receiving citations, then create content to close those gaps. There is nothing inherently wrong with that. In this case, it appears to have worked extremely well at establishing this company’s category presence. The limitation appears when everyone says roughly the same thing, and you don’t offer details.

If every vendor claims to be end-to-end, highly integrated, AI-enabled, scalable, flexible, and focused on efficiency, those statements may help establish that each belongs in the category. They do much less when the model has to determine what “end-to-end” covers, what “integration” connects, what the AI actually does, or what evidence supports the efficiency claim.

Parity can help a company qualify for the conversation without providing enough information to survive deeper evaluation.

Running the prompts was relatively straightforward. There wasn’t a tool or off-the-shelf framework for what we were trying to measure. We needed to build a multi-variable evidence scorecard capable of determining whether the model had enough support to evaluate the company against the criteria that actually mattered in the buying decision.

That meant defining what counted as strong evidence, what first-party documentation was enough, and when independent corroboration became important. The bigger challenge was thinking beyond the “we are great” organizational mindset to determine when an evidence gap or omission was serious enough to affect consideration or selection. That turned out to be even harder than getting SEOs to agree on ranking factors.

Those judgments then had to be translated into a repeatable scoring system and applied consistently across the prompts and five AI surfaces. It also required conversations beyond content. Sales had to help identify criteria and objections that affect deals. Product had to validate capabilities and limitations. Customer-facing teams helped identify real outcomes. In some cases, customers themselves were the best source for understanding what evidence mattered.

This is why I do not believe there will be one universal GEO evidence scorecard that every organization can download. The methodology can be reusable if you can honestly define the decision criteria and what constitutes sufficient evidence, then evaluate whether AI can connect the brand to those criteria.

As we evaluated eligibility criteria and evidence requirements, many findings no longer looked like conventional content gaps. Looking at this massive evidence gap matrix as one manager exclaimed, “that no human will ever read,” it was obvious this would become a team effort to make the changes. Content teams cannot make up content, nor will they necessarily know how everything works.

Some of the knowledge required to close these gaps is organizational in nature. We had to pry it out of product, engineering, and sales, and then run it through marketing and compliance. In some cases, we had to loop in external and partner relations when stronger third-party or attributable proof was required beyond what we could create.

The content team still has a critical role: organize that knowledge, make relationships explicit, create coherent evidence paths, and expose information in ways humans and AI systems can understand.

Most importantly, this project validated and reinforced my belief that measuring only the first AI response is insufficient. True, those broad PAA-like prompts are valuable for generating a list of options worth considering. The problem is the current tools cannot maintain state like a human workflow and aggregate the sequence of prompt turns most users leverage. Each subsequent prompt digs deeper into the criteria for consideration, maintaining preferences, and memory, and culminating in the multivariate result that I affectionately call prompt math.

This means that GEO measurement needs to become more sophisticated and account for both visibility and decision readiness. Many tools now have answer accuracy monitoring, which is a step forward. I argue that we need to put more effort into the element that is much closer to the business problem organizations should actually care about.

There is an obvious danger here because once marketers conclude that AI systems need evidence, we will probably see an explosion of “evidence-shaped content”: longer pages, superficial diagrams, weak surveys, synthetic research, and AI-generated case studies created primarily because someone believes those formats will influence an LLM, just like the explosion of Reddit submission services. That misses the point. The objective is not to manufacture artifacts that appear persuasive to AI, but to provide real content that reduces uncertainty and risk for the buyer.

If the information would not help a knowledgeable customer make a better decision, it probably should not exist merely to improve a GEO score.

As noted, the company had already achieved what many GEO programs would consider an amazing success: 98.4% visibility and 93.2% primary-or-shortlist consideration, yet 73.7% of those same responses had weak or missing evidence. Unfortunately, our analysis could not prove that this evidence gap caused every lost sale or every subsequent AI exclusion. What it does show is that a visibility-only measurement model would have missed the weakness we were asked to investigate.

Any company can succeed in entering the conversation but fail to offer enough credible evidence to remain qualified as the conversation becomes more specific.

Is Pilates Effective For Weight Loss? Instructors Share How To Incorporate It Into Your Routine

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

It’s nearly impossible to scroll through social media right now without seeing some celebrity or influencer rave about the perks of Pilates. But even with all the buzz surrounding this popular practice, details are a little fuzzy on whether Pilates is an effective workout for weight loss.

First off, know that the health and fitness benefits of having a Pilates practice are so much more than that. This form of exercise is known for improving your posture, breathing, flexibility, and core strength. As Claudette Sariya, CPT, a personal trainer and fitness educator based in New York City, notes, “any movement that you can enjoy regularly and consistently can help you lose weight.”

So, doing Pilates (or any workout for that matter) to stay active paired with a nutrition plan is likely to help you with weight loss. But it probably won't help expedite your weight loss in the same way that, say, lifting heavy weights would. (We'll get into why!)

The focus of Pilates is not weight loss, experts point out. "Pilates should be considered part of your self-care routine," says Jacquelyn Brennan, CSCS, pilates instructor and co-founder of Chicago-based Pilates and Coffee. "We all need to move our spines more, move our joints through every range of motion, and everyone needs to be breathing better."

All of those healthy practices together can aid weight loss, though. Here's everything to know about doing Pilates when you're trying to lose weight.

One form of Pilates isn't superior to another to drop pounds, experts agree. "For weight loss, I don’t think one type of Pilates is necessarily best,” says Berkow. Yet, there are different flavors—and finding one that you enjoy and will keep coming back to can, in turn, help you commit to a fitness routine and lose weight.

When someone mentions Pilates, they're often referring to Romana’s Pilates, which features exercises that Joseph Pilates (the founder) developed, explains Gabbi Berkow, RD, CPT, a nutritionist and Pilates instructor. Some studios are dedicated to classical or Romana’s Pilates (which is what Berkow is trained in) but there are studios that feature different Pilates-based workouts, which means they use Pilates-inspired moves but don't follow the classical version to a T. Some Pilates classes take place on a mat (a.k.a. mat Pilates), while others occur on a reformer, which is basically a sliding, spring-loaded platform.

You also might hear the term "athletic Pilates," but that is usually a description of the pace, says Sonja R. Price Herbert, a certified Pilates instructor and founder of Black Girl Pilates. These classes are designed to be fast-paced and high-intensity, and may even have some high-intensity interval training (HIIT) exercises and elements in the mix, which would make them better for weight loss due to the way HIIT training can help you burn more calories during and after the workout.

But if you really want to maximize your Pilates practice for weight loss and strength, it’s a good idea to use both the reformer and Cadillac machines, says Nofar Hagag, a Pilates instructor and creator of the Nofar Method. “By incorporating cardio-focused movements—such as squats, planks, and Pilates burpees, your heart rate increases, boosting calorie burn and metabolism,” she says. “These high-energy moves are paired with strength-training exercises, like side leg lifts and abs teasers, to build lean muscle and improve body composition.” (Reminder: Loading your muscles with resistance is key for muscle growth—and more muscle mass helps you burn more calories at rest and, in turn, lose weight.)

What you also want when it comes to Pilates and weight loss: an instructor who knows what they’re doing, is committed to safe movements, and who will challenge you, says Berkow. “You want to make sure your Pilates class is a workout. It shouldn’t be just a series of slow, easy stretches. Pilates has to contribute to your total daily calorie burn for it to make a difference in weight loss,” she says.

If you’ve ever gone to a class, you know how insane the core work is (seriously, the famed Hundreds exercise is not for the faint of heart...or abs).

While you cannot spot-reduce fat, Pilates can help you learn how to properly engage your pelvic floor and deep core muscles, says Brennan. "Pilates will teach you how to engage your muscles correctly, which will lend to increased activation during core exercises and lead to [the appearance of a] 'tighter tummy,' " she says.

However, to see abs definition, you do have to lose the fat that sits on top of your muscles, which requires proper nutrition, says Berkow. Think: a diet rich in whole, fresh foods and a limit on overly-processed foods and snacks.

There is some research to suggest that you can lose weight doing Pilates—but it's tough to give an exact number of pounds considering so many individual factors play into weight loss, such as genetics, sleep and stress, lifestyle habits, and more.

A meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials published in Frontiers in Physiology in 2021 found that pilates “dramatically” reduced body weight and body fat percentage in adults with overweight or obesity. And the longer the person practiced Pilates, the bigger the weight loss. Older research has had mixed results, though, with one small study concluding Pilates was indeed helpful for weight loss, while another found it had no impact on weight loss in post-menopausal women.

Again, traditional Pilates isn’t a modality designed for weight loss. Generally, traditional classes do not pack enough of a cardio or strength training component, Herbert says: “It is not a steady-state [cardio modality] like running, jumping, or walking. There is also not enough weight [or resistance] to aid in a decrease in body fat.” Instead, Herbert says that “Pilates focuses on awareness of the body, efficiency of movement, and breath.”

Pilates will, however, deliver far more benefits than just a lower number on a scale that may indirectly help with weight loss. For instance, Pilates involves breath work, encouraging deeper belly breaths that are in direct contrast to the shallow breathing many of us do on the regular. “Shallow breathing leads to increased cortisol levels, which contributes to weight gain. One could say that through pilates and learning to breathe better, you’d have lower cortisol—and thus, stress—levels, something that can help you lose weight,” Brennan says.

Feeling better, both mentally and physically, will also encourage you to take care of your body, says Berkow—and Pilates can definitely be a key part of achieving an overall weight-loss goal.

World Children's Day 2026: 6 ways to help kids feel heard

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The backpack hits the floor, and "How was your day?" gets a one-word answer: "Fine." Kids don’t always say what’s on their mind, but a little time and attention may help them open up.

World Children’s Day is a good moment for parents, caregivers, and teachers to ask kids how they’re feeling and listen without trying to fix anything. The day honors children’s rights, including the right to be heard.

Here’s a look at what the day is about, plus a few activities that can help kids feel understood, at home or in the classroom.

World Children's Day is a global day of action for children, run by UNICEF. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 20. This year’s theme is “My day. My rights. My future.” It invites children to tell world leaders what kind of future they want.

The day celebrates children and the rights they’re born with. In many places, children “take over” high-profile roles in media, sports, and government for the day. Landmarks light up blue to show support for children's rights.

November 20 marks two milestones for children’s rights. On this date in 1959, the UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of the Child, a statement of shared ideals. Thirty years later to the day, it adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1989, a treaty that nations can commit to follow.

The Convention sets out the rights every child has, including the right to be safe, to play, and to have their opinions taken seriously. Nearly every country has formally agreed to follow it, and it’s the most widely ratified human rights treaty in history.

Countries around the world mark World Children’s Day. Many also celebrate a separate Children’s Day, most notably June 1, International Children's Day.

Some countries do pick dates of their own. Turkey celebrates on April 23, and India on November 14. The dates differ, but the idea is the same: it’s a day dedicated to the youngest members of a community.

You don’t need a big event to mark World Children’s Day. A meta-analysis on children and teens linked understanding emotions with better social skills and fewer behavior problems. Kids can build that understanding in everyday moments, like the ones below.

Kids may not know they have rights, but those who learn about them may feel more confident speaking up. UNICEF’s child-friendly version of the Convention explains the rights in plain language.

Try: Read one or two rights together, then ask which feels most important to them.

Not every child has the words for how they feel. Invite them to sketch their day from morning to night, then have them walk you through it.

If they’re up for it, ask which part they loved most and which part felt hard. A drawing gives kids something concrete to point to as they talk, and that can feel easier than answering a broad question such as “How are you feeling?”

Try: At home, pin the drawing to the fridge so your child can talk about it again if they’d like. In class, create an “Our Day” wall where kids can choose to share their drawings and spot the moments they had in common.

When a child melts down, get curious about the feeling underneath. Help them put it into words: “It looks like you’re frustrated that it’s not your turn. Is that right?” Then listen carefully to their answer before you reassure them. “You’re fine” can sound dismissive, even when you mean well. When you talk about feelings this way, kids often learn to do it too.

Try: Post a chart of faces showing common feelings where kids can see it, and invite them to point to or say how they feel at the start of the day. Welcome any details they want to share.

💙 Once a big feeling has a name, here are coping skills for kids to try.

Set aside a few minutes at dinner or morning circle time for everyone to share one high, one low, and one thing they’re thankful for. Take a turn yourself so kids feel more at ease sharing.

Just listen as kids share, and try not to talk them out of a hard feeling. Kids tend to manage feelings better when you respond with warmth instead of brushing them off.

Try: Pass around a talking object, such as a small stuffed animal. Whoever holds it has the floor, grown-ups included.

A few minutes of meditation can be a soothing way for kids to slow down and settle. Sessions can focus on anything from breathing and body awareness to gratitude and kindness, so there’s room to follow your child’s interests or needs. Kids can sit, lie down, or stand, and they can stop whenever they’d like.

Try: Belly breathing is a gentle place to start. Have your child lie down with a stuffed animal on their belly and watch it rise and fall as they breathe slowly.

Reading together gives you some time to connect, and stories can spark conversations about feelings. Kids may find it easier to talk about how a character feels than how they do.

Pick a book where a character is going through difficult feelings, then pause and ask, “What do you think she’s feeling?”

Reviving an Enduring Emblem of Simplicity, Loll Designs Launches the Here and There Collection

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The best designs almost always come out of necessity and quiet observation. In the 1940s, legendary ecologist Aldo Leopold constructed a remarkably straightforward wooden bench so that he could sit outside his Wisconsin farmstead and contemplate nature’s primal force; the fundamental need for its conservation. The back-slanted seat was constructed from readily available dimensional lumber and almost immediately presented itself as an act of unpretentious, serendipitous genius: the quick assembly of a functional furnishing devised for a specific need but ultimately, one with universal potential. The particular brilliance of the design was its x-shaped self-bracing geometry, zero waste configuration, and uncomplicated assembly.

“Anyone that’s handy with a saw and some fasteners and lumber could easily build an Aldo bench,” says Eric Swanson, president of Duluth-based outdoor furniture brand Loll Designs. “That’s the beauty of it.” The Minnesota manufacturer recently teamed up with Michigan industrial design studio Argenta Park to turn the foundational artifact into the Here and There Collection. Named for Leopold’s belief that our appreciation for the natural world should balance between what’s nearby and farther afield, the cohesive and complementary suite of furnishings includes a dining chair, dining table, and of course, a bench.

This contemporary reinterpretation was twelve years in the making. For his outdoor wedding in 2014, Argenta Park designer Ricky Biddle was faced with the same practical challenge Leopold surmounted decades earlier: creating sturdy, durable, and comfortable outdoor seating. He didn’t want to rent generic, flimsy event furniture, so he spent his bachelor party assembling rudimentary Aldo benches. It was almost as if influential Italian designer Enzo Mari had sanctioned the task as one of his open-source “Autoprogettazione” self designs.

The pieces have remained a fixture of Biddle and his family’s everyday life ever since. This permanence prompted him to reconsider how its raw genius might be retooled using advanced manufacturing, elevated ergonomics, and year-round durability. He brought the idea to Argenta Park founder Churu Yun and the Loll Designs engineering team.

The best type of historical preservation is the kind that implements the latest technologies and refined strategies; doesn’t focus on creating replicas merely out of nostalgic reverence. “Not all historical designs lend themselves to being evolved into a modern-day furniture piece,” says Brian King, Loll Designs Brand and Marketing Director. “It’s more about being hyper selective in a design that has meaning, history, or a deep-rooted memory and identifying what’s most valuable.” Loll had done this many times before, most notably by bringing the ubiquitous Adirondack Chair up to contemporary standards.

While still carrying the original design’s understated silhouette, the re-edited and expanded collection carefully addressed the physical limitations of simple timber construction. Softly contoured surfaces replace flat planks, streamlined fastener assembly, and the use of 100% recyclable high-density polyethylene (HDPE)—available in Black, Charcoal Grey, Fog, and Sage—simplifies ongoing maintenance.

This calculus responds to changing demands: contemporary homeowners are increasingly looking for low-maintenance solutions that can withstand harsh seasonal shifts without sacrificing aesthetic refinement or tactile comfort. “With historical designs comes historical maintenance and poor ergonomics,” Swanson adds. “People want to enjoy their time outdoors sitting in their furniture more than working on it, which is why using alternative materials to traditional wood is a benefit; why a curved seat and backrest is so important; and why the right proportions—the height of the dining table—make all the difference.”

Leopold is widely considered the founder of wildlife ecology and his bench stands testament to that; allowing people to enjoy nature for as long as they want. The new Here and There Collection carries on that legacy.

The Aircraft That Beat The A350 As Airbus' Best Value & Most Operationally Practical Incremental Upgrade

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Airlinephoto | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

All of the widebodies in production: the A330-900, Airbus A350 family, Boeing 787 family, and upcoming 777X family have the potential to offer airlines the best value for money. Which, in the real world, is highly contingent on missions, existing inventory, requirements, and a large range of other factors specific to individual airlines.

To say the A330-900 beats the A350 in value for money is narrowly true only in these cases where the stars align. And align they do, as the A330-900 has attracted an impressive 500+ orderbook, including some cases where the A350-900 may have been a viable alternative.

Some airlines (such as Delta) are replacing their A330ceo with a mixed fleet of A330-900s and A350s. Others (such as Qantas) are replacing them with A350s and 787s, while China Eastern is replacing its A330ceos with A330neos. Still others may go another way and partially replace them with long-range narrowbody A321XLRs on thinner routes. Here are some of the benefits of the A330-900 specifically (not the failed A330-800).

Airbus originally intended to produce the widebody A350 in three variants: the shortened A350-800, the standard A350-900, and the stretched A350-1000. Some airlines, such as Hawaiian Airlines, placed orders for the A350-800. However, Airbus then decided to cancel the A350-800 variant and upgrade the legacy A330ceo in two variants, the shorter A330-800 and stretched A330-900, collectively the A330neo.

Hawaiian switched its order to the A330-800. However, as time went on, it became clear that the A330-800 was unpopular, and unpopularity has a habit of breeding more unpopularity, with airlines getting cold feet about being left with a variant few other airlines wanted.

Ultimately, Hawaiian (and other airlines) canceled its orders for the A330-800 and purchased the Boeing 787-9, only to see its future parent, Alaska Airlines, snatch them away. The real story of the A330neo is not the dismal failure of the A330-800, but the success of the A330-900. As an upgrade to an existing aircraft, its development was much cheaper and faster than developing a clean-sheet aircraft.

By September 2026, Airbus had accumulated around 511 firm orders for the A330-900, with the remaining A330neo orders being the residual 13 A330-800 orders. It is somewhat ironic that this article is dedicated to the success of the A330-900 when the sister A330-800 is one of history's worst-selling airliners.

The A350 is not a failure in any way. It is one of the world's most successful twin-engine widebody aircraft families ever developed. To date, it has accumulated around 1,628 firm orders, around three times more than the A330neo (almost all of which are for the Airbus A330-900). However, the expectations of the two aircraft are different.

The A330neo comes with new wings, new engines (Rolls-Royce's new Trent 7000 engine), modernized avionics, and other improvements, but it remains, at its core, a 1990s airliner. Airbus elected to capitalize on the low-hanging fruit that two decades of technological development offered, and forego the marginal benefit of a clean-sheet design.

While the clean-sheet A350 (and rival 787) is substantively made up of advanced composites and titanium, allowing the aircraft to be lighter, more efficient, and have a higher cabin pressure, the A330neo remains a mostly aluminum aircraft. Airbus says the A330neo offers around a 25% improvement in fuel burn per seat compared with older-generation aircraft like the Boeing 767-300ER.

It also says the A330neo offers up to 7% lower operating costs per seat than the rival Boeing 787. Caution should be taken with this second claim, and it would heavily depend on assumptions, missions, and other specific factors. Boeing likely runs its own calculations with a different set of assumptions and comes up with an inverse conclusion.

One advantage the A330neo boasts is that it lacks the high development costs of a clean-sheet design. For airlines, the trade-off is that the aircraft may be a bit less fuel-efficient than it could otherwise be, but also somewhat cheaper to purchase or lease. This is a trade-off that has been judged worthwhile by many airlines, although list prices of aircraft can be misleading given that airlines tend to receive discounts, especially in bulk.

It is better to focus on market values and lease rates: these are what airlines actually spend on their aircraft, rather than the fancy figures that manufacturers put in their brochures. A recent analysis by Acumen shows the five-year market value of Boeing 787-9s, Boeing 787-10s, A350-1000s, and A350-900s clustering around $120 million to $130 million.

The market value of the Boeing 777-300ER is lower at around $110 million, possibly on account of its relatively poor fuel economy relative to the previous aircraft. Meanwhile, the shortened 787-8 sits at just under $100 million.

The A330-900 is priced at around $87 million, making it considerably cheaper in capital terms. According to the 2026 ICLG Aviation Finance & Leasing report published in June 2026, a new A330-900 costs around $118.5 million. This is much less than the A350-900 at $168 million, the A350-1000 at $181 million, the Boeing 787-9 at $160 million, and the Boeing 787-10 at $168 million.

Many airlines lease their aircraft, and these rates largely mirror the current market value. For factory-new aircraft, the going lease rate for the A330-900 is within the range of $902,000 to $975,000 per month. Meanwhile, its A350-900 counterpart is around $1.19 million to $1.25 million a month, and the stretched A350-1000 is higher still.

When it comes to the relevant Dreamliners for this comparison, the Boeing 787-9 is being leased at around $1.07 million to $1.15 million a month, and the stretched 787-10 is generating $1.13 million to $1.23 million a month in leasing revenue. Naturally, the market values and lease rates for legacy Boeing 777s and Airbus A330-200s and A330-300s are much lower.

However, these aircraft are much less fuel-efficient, and their maintenance expenses rise as they age. The quad-engined A340-300 and A340-600 are also no longer relevant as a point of comparison for the A330-900, as the A340 family has almost completely retired as scheduled passenger aircraft in mainline airlines.

A few A340-300s remain to be phased out, while Lufthansa has finally withdrawn the last of its A340-600s. Some of these old A340s are finding an epilogue in sanctioned countries like Iran, but this is irrelevant to where market forces are operating freely.

One of the most important things airlines look for when structuring their fleets is commonality. Maintenance is one of the leading expenses for maintaining aircraft and commonality is one of the main ways to reduce the expense. Extreme examples of airlines pursuing commonality are Southwest and Ryanair (only Boeing 737s) and IndiGo (A320s).

The older bones of the A330neo are a disadvantage in fuel efficiency, as discussed above, but it does come with an upside beyond basic cost. The A330neo shares around 95% of its airframe spare parts with older A330ceos. This allows the almost 150 airlines already flying the A330ceo to add or switch to the A330neo with minimal training and lower marginal maintenance burden.

Notably, pilots going from the ceo to neo only need half a day of computer-based self-study. This commonality can save airlines around $6 million per aircraft in maintenance, although exact savings will vary considerably. The A330neo also boasts commonality with the clean-sheet A350, even if it's less than with its A330ceo predecessor.

This is relevant for airlines like Delta, which operate both the A330 and A350. Naturally, all this means the A330neo faces an uphill battle convincing airlines already operating Boeing aircraft to switch. The argument inverts.

Paradoxically, one of the strengths of the A330neo is precisely that the A350 and Boeing 787 are successful and popular. The massive popularity of those two aircraft has created massive backorders, with Airbus and Boeing struggling to ramp up production. An airline placing a new order today may have to go to the back of the queue and expect to get the aircraft sometime in the mid 2030s.

Still, the three-times greater A350 order book is muted by the fact that its production rate is higher. Assuming a new order goes to the back of the queue and using Airbus' September 2026 production guidance, new orders for A350-900s will be delivered in 6–8 years (2032-2034). The A330-900 ordered today may be delivered around a year earlier (2031-2034). Planned production rates are 12 for the flagship A350 and five for the A330neo.

The A330-900 is not a direct one-for-one equivalent to the A350-900. Forgetting costs and fuel efficiency, they are different aircraft, with different capacities and ranges, and have been optimized for different missions. There is overlap, but that only goes so far.

My Grandma Got Me Through the Water Temple. She Would’ve Loved the Ocarina of Time Remake

On Saturday nights, I’d take my Nintendo 64 cartridge upstairs to Mémère’s apartment. She always knew where I needed to go next.

When my older brother started grade school in the early ’90s, I spent a lot of time at her house. We called her Mémère (pronounced “mem-may” by French Canadians like us), and she’d fire up the Nintendo to keep me entertained.

For a while it was the NES. I’d shoot ducks until I got bored, and I hated Mario because it was too hard. What I really remember fondly was watching Mémère play The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past after she upgraded to the SNES. She’d tell me who she was fighting and why, and following along felt like having one of my favorite people read me a book. When it was my turn to play, I’d just charge up Link’s sword and mow every blade of grass I could find. Then I’d go looking for chickens. Then I’d ask what she had for snacks. She always had good snacks for me.

By the Nintendo 64 era, Mémère had moved into an apartment in a house my uncle owned. He lived on the first floor, she lived on the second, and my cousin lived on the third. Most Saturday nights, the whole family would get together. While the adults played cards (always Pitch, if you're curious), my brother and I would drift between floors. Grand Theft Auto or Dynasty Warriors on the first? Dino Crisis, horror movies, or kung fu flicks on the third? Nope. For me, it was usually Ocarina of Time with Mémère on the second floor.

I can still picture her lying sideways on her bed with that plain gray controller in her hands. The TV was only a few feet away, and the N64 sat right on the corner of the dresser beside it. By then, I had my own N64, and I was always excited to tell her where I had been and which Spiritual Stones or Medallions I still needed. I usually brough my own cartridge over, and she’d help me through a part I was stuck on. Yes, including the Water Temple. I remember being in awe when she showed me how to get the Fire Arrows at Lake Hylia. How was I supposed to know you could shoot the sun? And when the skeletons rose from the ground at night in Hyrule Field, I'd hand over the controller and cower behind her as she challenged them head-on.

Mémère owned a beat-up copy of Prima’s official strategy guide. With it, she’d played through the whole game and found every heart piece. Then she did it two more times. When she finished the third file, she went back to the first slot and started over. I have no idea how many times my grandmother beat Ocarina of Time, but it was probably more than you have. All three of her save files always had the same name: Adeline.

I thought it was kind of weird. I didn’t know anybody named Adeline. I asked her about it a few times, and she always told me to ask my mother. When I was finally old enough to hear the answer, I cried. Adeline was Mémère’s first daughter, the only sibling older than my mom. She died from complications related to spina bifida when she was only a few years old. My mom’s three younger siblings were either too young to remember her or hadn’t been born by the time she died. My mother and grandmother were the only ones who could tell me much about “Addy,” and they rarely talked about her.

I’d spent all those Saturday nights watching Mémère play, asking her questions about practically everything on the screen. I could ask her how to get through the Water Temple, and she’d walk me through it. When I asked her about Adeline, she didn’t answer. And every time she started a new game, she typed in her daughter’s name again.

I bought her Majora’s Mask once. She told me she’d played it, though I’m still not sure how far she got. All she had to say about it was that it was “just not the same.” That alone made me pretty sad about the whole thing, and it made me rethink Mémère's whole Zelda ritual. Even though she never really talked about it, she was thinking about her lost daughter every single day as she ventured into Hyrule to fight the growing darkness and defeat Ganondorf. She was content with that same adventure, replayed endlessly. A lot of people might argue that Ocarina of Time is just that good. Mémère would certainly say there's nothing else like it.

That's what makes the remake's release on the Nintendo Switch 2 feel so bittersweet. Mémère died back in 2017 at the age of 70, but not before my brother had his first daughter, a child he named Adalyne. During one of the last times I visited Mémère, she still had that same dusty old N64 next to her TV. My mother told me that Mémère “talked” to Addy every day. I wondered if Mémère meant she was playing Ocarina of Time. Maybe all of these complicated memories help explain why I’ve never been a huge fan of the Zelda franchise at large. Yes, I’ve played over a hundred hours between Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom. They’re excellent in many ways. But I can’t stand the fragile weapons and the clunky feel of combat. I also don’t like how they tend to, even unconsciously, make me a little bit sad.

If Mémère were still alive today, what would she think of the remake? Would she shed a tear seeing her beloved video game lovingly remade in such a beautiful way? She was always shockingly savvy for her generation when it came to technology. Though I’d probably have to hook the Switch 2 up to the TV for her. (Her eyesight was always fairly bad). Honestly, it makes me almost happy that I don't have a Switch 2 myself to experience the new Ocarina of Time, because even just thinking about it makes me emotional. If she were still alive, I’d probably have tried harder to go out of my way to get the console and game just so we could game together.

I do know one thing: If I ever do get around to it, I know exactly what I'll name Link.

Crypto and card access. Users can fund their accounts with crypto wallets, standard payment cards as well as local payment options. That lowers the barrier for users who are new to digital assets, while keeping settlement on-chain.
