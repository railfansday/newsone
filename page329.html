Prince William and Kate Middleton’s Full Relationship Timeline

The last few years have seen a lot of enormous changes in the British Royal Family, an entity known more for tradition than rapid growth. After the loss of Queen Elizabeth II, who ruled over the U.K. for most of the 21st century, some people may be looking for some stability. If there’s any couple in the family who offers that, it’s Prince William and Kate Middleton.

William and Kate have had what appears on the outside to be a bit of a fairytale romance, from the storybook wedding to the picturesque family of five they have now. While they’ve faced hard times like everyone else, there’s something about them that has captivated people since the beginning of their relationship. Here’s a look back at their journey, from college sweethearts to royal power couple.

Prince William and Kate Middleton met while both studying at St. Andrews University in Scotland. Rumor has it that William was won over after seeing Kate modeling at a student fashion show for charity (a show for which he reportedly paid £200 for a front-row seat).

Other rumors suggest that her reason for being at St. Andrews was entirely related to meeting her future husband.

The Crown historian Robert Lacey wrote in his 2020 book Battle of Brothers: William and Harry—The Inside Story of a Family in Tumult, that Kate was trying to manifest her future relationship with William when she was only 19.

“Kate was very strategic,” Lacey said. “She gave up her place at one of Britain’s premier universities, Edinburgh, and opted for another one [St. Andrews] that probably didn’t have the same academic standard. She had already been to Edinburgh to get her flat accommodations sorted out but switched universities and switched her courses to history of art because that was William’s. She delayed her own education by a year and even takes the chance she won’t get into St. Andrews.”

She was not the only person making these choices. Applications to the school reportedly went up by 44 percent after it was announced that was William’s choice of university. They might not all have been marriage hopefuls, but some probably were like Kate.

Hello! magazine reported that Prince William was moving from his dorm into a flat with three college friends, one of them “a pretty 20-year-old brunette” who turned out to be Kate Middleton.

He allegedly started out as just her friend, before seeing her walk in a fashion show in this see-through dress on March 26, 2002.

“She’s there and about to walk down the runway on the fashion show, and she strips off an extra layer of clothing,” Lacey explained. “Here is a very strategic lady who knows what she wants and has got it.”

William and Kate were photographed together for the first time during a ski trip in Klosters, causing then-Prince Charles to threaten to sever ties with The Sun, the outlet that published the photos. Charles was said to have been “very disappointed” by the breach in an agreement between the press and Buckingham Palace that ensures royal children can continue their education without having their privacy intruded upon.

Both William and Kate graduated from St. Andrews. The Middletons later shared a photo of them together on the day.

The couple attended Camilla’s daughter Laura Parker-Bowles’ wedding, marking one of their first big public appearances together. It also sparked speculation that they were serious enough to one day walk down the aisle themselves.

In 2007, William and Kate took a time-out and broke up for a couple of months. Kate took a vacation to Ireland with her friends afterwards, while William awkwardly fielded questions about “how things are going” with Kate.

Kate later said in a 2010 interview after their engagement that in hindsight, she thinks it was healthy for them to have time apart.

“I think I at the time wasn't very happy about it, but actually it made me a stronger person...You find out things about yourself that maybe you hadn’t realized,” she said. “Or I think you can get quite consumed by a relationship when you’re younger. I really valued that time for me as well although I didn't think it at the time, looking back on it."

By July of that same year, rumors began to swirl about a possible reconciliation as Kate was seen attending a Princess Diana tribute concert—though she sat two rows behind William with her sister, Pippa.

The two were seen together again, back where it all began: the ski slopes. Photos show Kate and William together in Klosters as sources told newspapers that they were very much back together.

William graduated from his RAF training, with Kate at his side.

William proposed to Kate while on a trip to Kenya, with Princess Diana’s sapphire engagement ring. “We had a little private time away together with some friends, and I just decided it was the right time really,” William later said in their engagement interview. “We’d been talking about marriage or a while, so it wasn’t a massively big surprise, but I took her up somewhere nice in Kenya.”

William and Kate got married in a fairytale ceremony at Westminster Abbey witnessed by nearly 72 million people worldwide. The bride did her own makeup, the groom wore sweat pads so that he wouldn’t sweat out of his uniform, and Ellie Goulding performed at the reception.

In marrying William, Kate gained the title the Duchess of Cambridge, the Countess of Strathearn, and Lady Carrickfergus.

After the wedding, the married couple settled in Wales and adopted a dog, an English cocker spaniel named Lupo.

Kate had not reached the 12-week stage, but the Palace made the announcement because they felt they couldn’t keep the pregnancy under wraps from the press for much longer. Once again, Kate suffered from extreme morning sickness.

Is Durin Related To Gimli In The Rings Of Power?

📅 Jan 20, 2023  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Given the similarities between Lord of the Rings' Gimli and Rings of Power's Durin IV, it's not surprising that they are related. Introduced as a member of the Fellowship of the Ring, the Dwarf warrior Gimli has become a fan-favorite character in the novels and Peter Jackson's film trilogy thanks to his altruism and humorous personality. It seems that this is a trait that has passed through the generations, as the Dwarven Prince of Khazad-dûm, known as Durin IV, in Prime Video's prequel series, Rings of Power, also shows several similar mannerisms.

Rings of Power doesn't shy away from Durin IV’s royal lineage, while the Appendix of Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings also states that Durin inherits the legacy of the much-venerated Durin I (the founder of Khazad-dûm and one of the oldest Dwarven rulers). Gimli’s background, while canonically hinted, isn’t discussed that much in the films, so the links between the two might not be as obvious. However, both characters are significant for building relations outside the Dwarf community, managing to earn the respect of their kin and the other communities.

Gimli is indeed a distant relative of Durin IV, as his father Glóin traces his origins from the line of Durin of the Dwarves of Erebor. These were the Dwarves who dwelt beneath the Lonely Mountain and were one of the seven kinds of Dwarves (known as Khazâd). Tolkien’s anthology, The Silmarillion, provides more context behind these Dwarves whose leaders belonged to the House of Durin, hence also being referred to as Durin’s Folk. The first king of this Dwarf lineage was the legendary Durin I. Having lived a long life of more than 2,300 years, Durin I was the eldest of the Fathers of the Dwarves.

Also known as Durin the Deathless, he was succeeded by seven kings, all of whom took his name. Durin IV is introduced in Rings of Power, but obviously, there’s no mention of Gimli, given that he would be born several generations later. However, Gimli does seem to be proud of his ancestors in the books. For instance, in The Two Towers, Gimli tells Legolas about the Glittering Caves and how important the cave’s mineral ores are for the Durin’s Folk, who inhabited the region. To directly quote Gimli, “None of Durin's race would mine those caves for stones or ore, not if diamonds and gold could be got there.”

Just because Gimli traces his lineage from Durin doesn’t necessarily imply that he’s of royal blood, as Rings of Power is set about 4,959 years before The Lord of the Rings. Gimli is the son of Glóin, who is introduced in The Hobbit as a member of Thorin Oakenshield’s company, also being one of his four third cousins. Thorin himself is a direct descendant of Durin I, even becoming the King of Durin’s Folk in his lifetime. Going by Tolkien’s Appendix A on Durin’s Folk in The Two Towers, Gimli was a royal and remote descendant of Durin I (and Rings of Power’s Durin IV in this process).

Gimli’s ancestor, Borin, was the brother of Dain I, who was crowned King and carried on Durin’s lineage. Borin had two grandchildren, one of whom was Gróin, Gimli’s grandfather. Gimli is of royal blood in the books and movies but is quite distant from having any chance of becoming King. However, Gimli hardly harbors any royal ambitions. Instead, he plays out as a dutiful protector of the Dwarves, his Elven allies, and others.

The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 2 introduced another canon dwarf character, and while he isn't a blood relative of Gimli, there is a connection here. Narvi, played by Kevin Eldon, was credited in Tolkien's appendices for creating the Doors of Durin for the West Gate of Khazad-dûm. Of course, these are the doors that the Fellowship had to speak "friend" to, and through which Gimli entered the home of his ancestors for the first time in The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring.

Narvi creates the Door of Durin with the help of Celebrimbor, just as we saw in The Rings of Power season 2. It's through this dwarf that the kindly relationship between the elves of Eregion and Khazad-dûm is fostered. Of course, little did they know that centuries in the future, Durin's descendant would rekindle the friendship between elves and dwarves, with Gimli and Legolas walking through that very door together in Lord of the Rings. There's an intriguing literary parallel here, as Narvi is another Dwarf with no greater ambitions than to serve and defend his people.

The Rings of Power season 3 does especially well at demonstrating how far Narvi will go to serve both the throne, regardless of who sits on it, and his own conscience. Durin IV's fiery temper certainly brings Gimli to mind, as does his determination. Still, there's a sort of wisdom beneath Gimli's skin that is more reflected by Narvi. One way or another, there's little doubt that the legacy of Khazad-dûm fuels Gimli as much in The Lord of the Rings as it does Durin's Folk in Rings of Power.

Extra-Fluffy Cinnamon Rolls

📅 Dec 22, 2022  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

At first glance, these homemade cinnamon rolls have all the classics: buttery, golden-brown dough, a gooey cinnamon-and-brown-sugar filling, and a generous swoop of cream cheese frosting. But a couple of smart moves make them especially fluffy, with a tender crumb that stays soft well into the next day (assuming there are any left).

One is tangzhong, a Chinese technique that cooks a small portion of flour and milk into a thick paste before it’s added to the dough. The precooked starch helps the dough hold on to more moisture, resulting in pillowy, supple cinnamon rolls.

The other is brown butter, which gives the filling a nutty, toasty edge. The tangy cream cheese frosting balances all that richness; add the optional lemon zest for extra brightness.

Can I make these cinnamon rolls the night before?

Yes. You can refrigerate the dough after its first rise or assemble the rolls in their baking pans and refrigerate them before the second rise. Cover tightly with plastic wrap to prevent the dough from drying out. The next day, let the chilled dough sit at room temperature for about an hour before proceeding with the recipe, making sure the shaped rolls have doubled in volume before baking.

This is a soft, moisture-rich dough, but it should become smooth and elastic as you knead it. For the most consistent results, weigh your flour rather than measuring by volume. Also, make sure the tangzhong has thickened into a paste before adding it to the dough: You should be able to see the tracks of your whisk across the bottom of the saucepan. Avoid adding excessive extra flour, which can make the finished rolls dense.

Can I make these cinnamon rolls without a stand mixer?

Yes, though kneading this soft, enriched dough by hand takes considerable time and effort. Several readers have reported success doing so. If using a stand mixer, keep an eye on the motor during the extended kneading time. If it begins to strain or overheat, stop the machine and finish kneading by hand.

Can I use active dry yeast instead of instant yeast?

This recipe calls for instant yeast, which can be mixed directly into the dough. Active dry yeast generally requires activation in warm liquid and may affect rising times, so we recommend sticking with instant yeast for the recipe as written. Mocha Brown Hair-Color Season Is Here—and It’s Universally Flattering

📅 Nov 18, 2021  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There’s something about this time of year that makes everybody want to go darker. From their nail polish to their wardrobe, it’s a turn toward rich, darker shades. So it’s only natural that hair color would follow suit, going deeper and darker too. This winter in particular, it’s all about mocha brown.

Also called mocha chocolate, the rich, glossy shade was first popularized by celebrity colorist Jenna Perry, whose multidimensional brunette wizardry has fans like Bella Hadid and Kaia Gerber. Since then, the smooth and chocolaty hue—which combines darker espresso tones with lighter, milkier brunette shades—has become popular with a much wider section of the A-list, with Hailey Bieber and Daisy Edgar-Jones among the latest devotees.

“It reminds me of a chocolate mousse or a rich chocolate cake,” Perry tells Vogue. “That’s where the name came from. I thought mocha chocolate resembled the decadence of this shade of brown.” In line with sweater weather, Perry has churned out individualized takes on what she calls the “universally flattering brunette hair color” for a slew of stars, including longtime client Emily Ratajkowski and Amelia Gray. “A deep, rich brunette is chic,” Perry says. “The color reflects warmly in the sun, so it doesn’t ever look drab.”

According to Stephen Buller and Anita Rice, founders of Buller and Rice salon, these cacao tones feel luxurious for winter, particularly when the color isn’t completely flat. The key? Having a varied tone. “This is important throughout the lengths and ends so it reflects light and feels expensive rather than looking like one solid block of dark color,” they say. “This also gives clients who have had years of balayage an opportunity to go darker without feeling as though they’re simply covering everything up.”

Celebrity hairstylist Lorraine Dublin says that these indulgent, chocolaty shades look especially beautiful on textured and curly hair: “Think deep espresso brown with subtle ribbons of mocha, creating a rich, multidimensional finish,” she previously told Vogue. “It works beautifully on textured hair because the tonal variations catch the light, highlighting the shape and movement of curls, coils, and kinks.” She recommends asking your colorist for a “rich espresso base” with “delicate mocha or cinnamon brown accents,” which will impart a soft, seamless color melt.

Another important part of mastering the mocha chocolate look is keeping hair strong and shiny. “What makes this hair color so incredible from the start is having healthy hair,” explains Perry, who adds that preserving pigments with a color-safe shampoo and conditioner is key. Try Matrix Total Results Brass Off Shampoo to nix brassy or red tones. You can also use an at-home gloss or color conditioner, like Glaze Supergloss Color Conditioning Hair Gloss Chocolate Gleam or John Frieda Brilliant Brunette Color Vibrancy Conditioner, to prolong your hair’s vibrancy.

10 Best Office Chairs of 2026—We Tested 70 to Pick Them

📅 Nov 10, 2021  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There Are Three things you don’t skimp on if you want a happy life: a good mattress, a good pair of shoes, and the best office chair you can find. Each is key to a world free of unnecessary pain. Finding the right ergonomic chair can be the difference between a satisfying and focused home office life and a squirmy day filled with cricks and small dissatisfactions.

The best desk chair is almost always an adjustable chair—one that can be tailored to your body’s unique shape and that offers enough lumbar support to avoid back problems even after long hours at the grind. Many chairs also utilize materials that can dissipate body heat, so you’re not stuck in Zoom meetings with a sweaty back. But the chair options out there can be bewildering. Luckily, the WIRED Reviews team has spent more than seven years sorting them out. WIRED Gear editor Julian Chokkattu has tested more than 60 chairs at his last tally. Reviewer Mathew Korfhage and other staffers have tried more than a dozen more, and most of our top picks have been tested by multiple people with different heights and body types.

WIRED’s favorite office chair for most people is the Branch Ergonomic Chair Pro ($499) because of its balance between value, features, comfort, and adjustability. That chair, and its lower-budget Ergonomic Chair ($369) cousin, have been tested and attested to by multiple WIRED reviewers. But we also offer chairs for every budget, from the preternaturally comfortable Steelcase Gesture ($1,510) to a $150 chair from Staples that leaves cheap Amazon brands in the dust.

We all have differing body types and needs, which is why we’ve put together a broad collection of recommendations—and why we enlist people of all sizes to keep testing our top picks for comfort and durability. Hopefully, we have a home office chair for you here.

The price of a good office chair often inspires actual gasps, easily cresting $1,000. This is typically justified. Most cheaper chairs reveal their shortcomings quickly and uncomfortably. Branch’s Ergonomic Chair Pro is WIRED’s top pick for most people in part because it’s available at an attainable price while still offering comfort, ergonomics, and build quality. At $499, it’s not egregiously expensive and it has a decent warranty period.

One of the main markers of a good office chair is how well it can be adjusted to fit your particular shape and size. This is where Branch’s Ergonomic Chair Pro excels. The armrests can move up and down, forward and back, and angle inward or outward. But unlike most comparable chairs, the arms of the armrests can also move in and out to accommodate wider or narrower bodies, and lock into place.

The seat depth can also be pushed out if you’re tall, or pulled in if you’re short. The Pro can lock into the upright position, or at a few different reclining angles. This seat tilt allows for a more active seating position when you’re grinding away—a vanishingly rare feature. The high-density foam cushion is decently soft, noted tester Julian Chokkattu, who appreciated how the mesh backrest was able to follow the curve of his back. The lumbar support can also be adjusted deeper or shallower. (Chokkattu and I both tend to like more aggressive lumbar support, but it can take getting used to for a lot of people.)

The stock Branch seat might be a bit short for those above 6 feet tall, but this is fixable with a $29 add-on Tall Cylinder. You can also add a headrest, though Chokkattu did not find it was needed. Other options include personalized picks on fabric, vegan leather, or leather-leather.

It’s sturdy and well-built, with a powder-coated aluminum base and casters that roll easily on hardwood. Drawbacks are few, though Chokkattu found that the recline tension knob didn’t do much in particular. —Matthew Korfhage

Steelcase’s Gesture is comfortable, no matter how you’re sitting. It’s an attractive chair, too, with several colorful upholstery options. The seat is cushy, and while some might find it thin, it feels plush compared to the even pricier Herman Miller Embody (see below). I like how you can extend the seat depth forward with a twist of a knob—super elegant, which is a good descriptor for the Gesture on the whole. The recline knob smartly offers a series of clicks to indicate the angles you can lock, and even adjusting the armrests—whether you want to move them up and down, left and right, or forward and back—is fluid without any jerky movements.

The backrest aligns nicely with my back, and you can grab the tabs to adjust the lumbar support to hit the curve of your spine. All this makes for a comfortable sitting experience, even more so with the headrest upgrade. I typically don’t care for a headrest, but I used it here more than any other chair. It sticks forward quite a bit, so I don’t need to lean my head back as far to let it rest. Assembly was easy, too; it’s simply placing the seat pan on the legs because the backrest and seat were already preassembled. The only thing to note is that since this is an all-foam chair, you may run warm, especially in the summer, unless you can crank the AC to keep your home office cool.

Another downside is that the Gesture’s upholstery may not hold up well over time, at least not as long as a seat like the Herman Miller Embody. I haven’t seen this on my test model, but our original reviewer noted it, and a friend who has owned a Gesture for several years also complained about it. —Julian Chokkattu

The Branch Ergonomic Chair was WIRED’s previous top pick for most people, before getting displaced by the much-improved adjustability and lumbar support of the Pro. But the Ergonomic Chair is still a great place to start if you’re on a tight budget. The chair assembles in minutes, with the help of the—surprisingly rare—actually intuitive instructions.

While the original Ergonomic does not offer the 14-point adjustability of the armrests on the Pro, you can still push the armrests forward and back, up and down; extend or push the seat in; or lock the recline. Adjustable lumbar support is also on offer, and the chair looks sleek—with a number of colors to choose from and an optional headrest. At its price, this is an impressive offering.

Note that the Pro’s forward tilt option is absent here, and so active sitters might find that the chair doesn’t keep your back as upright as you’d like. The OG chair also doesn’t offer a longer cylinder for taller people. But after years of testing, 6’ 4” tester Chokkattu still found the chair comfortable —as did his 5’ 1” partner. The double-woven nylon mesh backrest feels nice to lean against. The seat is made of firm, comfortable high-density foam, and doesn’t trap heat as much as many other foam seats we’ve tested.

Fair warning, though: In Chokkattu’s experience, the chair’s material can be a magnet for pet hair. If you have a cat or a dog who loves you too much, you may want to invest in a nice pet vacuum. The fabric on the front end of the seat can also pill a bit. —Matthew Korfhage

It might take you a week or two (or even a month) to get used to the Herman Miller Embody, but it’s well worth your patience. I always return to the Embody after testing other chairs, and it feels like a breath of fresh air every time. Its upright positioning supports my back well and eases lingering back pain from years of sitting in a cheap gaming chair. The seat feels rigid at first, but eventually becomes surprisingly pillowy, and the armrests stay firmly in place. It does a nice job of whisking heat away from my body, though not as well as all-mesh options. It’s also one of the most adjustable models around: You can pull out the seat, change the height and angle of the armrests, and tweak the Backfit adjustment to follow your spine’s natural curve.

Did I mention it’s pretty? I’d argue this is one of the most eye-catching designs around, especially with the rib-like pattern on the back. Not to mention the surprisingly small footprint. I know, I know, it's incredibly spendy—I bought it for roughly $1,465 back in 2020, and the price has skyrocketed since. But what’s amazing is that after more than five years, it feels good as new with barely any squeaks. Herman Miller offers a 12-year warranty that covers every part of the Embody, and the chair arrives completely assembled. Pick one of the Medley upholstery choices with the graphite base finish to see the lowest price.

Herman Miller has a “Gaming” version of the Embody, and while it looks the same, there are some minor changes. It has a copper-infused foam layer designed to accommodate a more active sitting position and wick body heat away. —Julian Chokkattu

If you have to sit at a desk for a living, I strongly urge you to save up and buy a nice, high-end chair. Buy once, cry once, right? But sometimes that’s just not possible. If you need a chair now and have a tight budget, get the Staples Dexley (6/10, WIRED Review)—the office supply company hasn’t steered me wrong yet. The Dexley is the best budget chair, miles better than most cheap chairs on Amazon. Does it have flaws? Of course. But at its frequently discounted $150 price, you can’t find much better.

The all-mesh seat is great for airflow and keeping you cool, though it is a little rough on the skin. The chair is decently adjustable, includes a headrest, and I put it together in about 15 minutes. I previously recommended the Staples Hyken here, which is still available and might be better suited for shorter people. One thing to note is that the seat may sink in a little over time, something that happened on the Hyken too; I’d be surprised if the Dexley lasts a decade, but hey, it’s less than $200. —Julian Chokkattu

An even more affordable Staples chair: The Staples Essentials Task Chair ($120) no longer offers a huge price break from the Dexley at its usual price, but it sometimes goes on sale for less than $80. With a fixed back and static armrests, it’s not as adjustable as the Dexley, but assembly is a cakewalk. Build quality is what you’d expect for a low-cost chair, but the bottom cushion offers ample support, and the mesh back feels solid but still flexible, noted WIRED contributing reviewer Henri Robbins. Just don’t expect it to adjust to your body’s shape. For a little more, the Dexley is a better budget seating experience.

The Herman Miller Aeron has long been a recommendation in this guide, with a few of my WIRED colleagues swearing by it. It’s durable, supportive, and airy; it’s also an iconic office chair that graces workspaces all over the world, updated this June with new dark olive Jasper and dark blue Nightfall color options. Last year I finally decided it was time for me to give the GOAT its due and try it for myself. Initially, I found the Aeron a little too bouncy—the proprietary Pellicle mesh is very firm—but after a few days, I didn’t mind this as much.

The levers on the Aeron are not where you’d expect. The seat height lever, for example, isn’t all the way on the underside of the chair, but on the right edge of the seat. The arm height lever is at the very back of the arm frame. It feels a little like I’m operating a spaceship with all these knobs and levers. I highly recommend watching the adjustment tutorial after setting up the chair to maximize comfort.

It comes in three sizes—A, B, and C, with C being the largest. That’s what I tested, with the Adjustable PostureFit SL, Tilt Limiter, Seat Angle, and Fully Adjustable Arms. The PostureFit SL is the lumbar support and is controlled via two knobs that push the support in or out, depending on your preferences. It worked well for me, but you can’t adjust it vertically. If the positioning doesn’t land on your back in the right spot, it could pose a problem. It may be safer to go with the Adjustable Lumbar Support instead, or try it in a retail store if possible. The Fully Adjustable Arms are convenient and don’t move around much, and while I like having the option of a forward tilt for a more active sitting position, I preferred to keep the chair on a gentle recline to keep my body moving ever so slightly.

I sat in the Aeron for close to a month, more than eight hours a day, and I found it supportive. It also regulates my body heat well, thanks to the mesh. The build quality is impeccable, and my only major gripe is that I don’t think it’s as attractive as chairs like the Anthros V2 or Herman Miller Embody (also featured in this guide), that fall in the same high-end price range. Before you buy a brand-new Aeron, there's a very good chance you can find one for significantly less at a local furniture reseller, on eBay, or Facebook Marketplace. I’m constantly reviewing office chairs, which means I get a lot of ads served my way. Anthros is one that kept cropping up in my Instagram feeds. At Herman Miller–pricing, the brand promised pain relief and an even comfier experience. Color me skeptical. But after nearly a month in the Anthros V2 (8/10 WIRED Recommends), I’m a believer.

The Anthros V2 looks nothing like most office chairs and is significantly more compact. The upper- and lower-back support are two separate pieces, and you can adjust each to accommodate your body type and achieve a natural lumbar curve. You rotate the knobs on each side to make adjustments; it initially took me a bit to get used to it, but now the chair feels natural. After you buy your seat, every customer gets a video consultation with an Anthros ergonomics therapist, who will go over the adjustments and help you get the right fit. I found this useful, as the therapist recommended a few adjustments I wouldn’t have made on my own.

Overall, I’ve felt well supported in this chair, though note that you can’t extend the seat depth. I don’t think the Anthros has quite supplanted the Embody as my favorite chair, but it's one of the rare options that has come close. What Should I Look for in an Office Chair?

A good rule of thumb is that the more adjustable a chair is, the better. This gives you the possibility to mold it to your body shape, which hopefully makes it a comfier and more supportive seat. Here's what you should be able to adjust on a chair.

Armrests: Most chairs let you move the armrests side to side, up and down, forward and back. It's even nicer when you're able to lock the armrest into place so that they don't slide around as your elbows rest on them. The armrest should naturally allow your palms to slightly hover over the desk. You'll often see descriptors like “4D” and “5D" adjustable armrests, which denote how many directions you can adjust them.

Seat height: It's worth checking a chair's maximum and minimum height before you buy because they might not work for your current desk (unless you have a standing desk where you can adjust the height). This is usually controlled with a pneumatic lift lever under the chair—pull the lever and get off the seat to let the chair go up, then sit on it and pull the lever to bring it down to your preference. Your feet should be planted on the floor at a 90- or 100-degree angle. If your chair arrives and it doesn't get as high as you'd like, don't worry, there's a very simple fix. Some office chair brands offer “tall cylinders" for not a lot of money, and if not, you can usually find third-party options on retailers like Amazon (make sure you buy for your specific chair!). Simply swap your existing cylinder with the tall version, and you'll have a greater height range to work with. You may be able to choose this option before checking out, or reach out to the company to see if they can swap it for you.

Seat depth: Seat depth is important for taller folks. There is usually a mechanism that lets you pull the seat out so that your thighs get the proper support. There should be one to two inches between the front of the seat and the back of your knees. Taller people with longer legs may not get the best support if they can't adjust the seat depth.

Seat tilt: This isn't as common as the above adjustments, but tilting the seat up or down may help posture and prevent back pain, though it can take some getting used to.

Lumbar support: Most office chairs offer lumbar support, which can look like a separate piece attached to the backrest that can slide up and down or back and forth. This piece supports your lower back (the lumbar region), specifically maintaining the curve of the spine to maintain good posture as you sit and, ideally, warding off back pain. Check your chair manufacturer's assembly instructions to learn how to adjust the lumbar support, and place the support on the curve of your lower back. What I like to do is run my hand along my spine until I reach the natural dip, then I try to make sure the lumbar support sits at that exact location. Some chairs also let you adjust the depth of the lumbar support, pushing it further inward or outward. Make sure it feels natural and that it's not digging in. Play around with this until it feels comfortable. Your shoulders should align with your hips, and you shouldn't feel like you're leaning forward.

Recline: Almost every office chair lets you lock or unlock the recline, but some go a step further and let you tweak the tension of the recline. This allows you to use more or less force when you lean back, which comes down to how much resistance you want when you lean back.

Does My Office Chair Need Lumbar Support?

In short, probably. If you sit in a chair all day, you likely want a chair with lumbar support.

We lead unnatural lives, evolutionarily speaking: The human body was not meant to sit for eight hours at a time in the same position. Unless you, blessed creature, have perfect posture—a difficult achievement when typing at a keyboard and looking at a monitor—the lower curve of your back might need a little extra support when sitting for long spans.

A relatively flat-backed or concave chair will often grant excellent support to your shoulders and back, but leave your lower back to fend for itself. Eventually, it complains.

“Your back has this natural curve to it, and if you're sitting in a chair that doesn't help support that natural S-curve, over time, it's going to feel a little bit less comfortable,” said Kristianne Egbert, an ergonomist at Briotix Health, in an interview with WIRED about lumbar support in late 2025. “When you're sitting in a forward, hunched position, and don't have that curve in your lower back, it increases the forces on the discs in your lower back, definitely resulting in discomfort.”

Experts are pretty univocal on the benefits of lumbar support. But not all lumbar support is created equal. Look for lumbar support that's adjustable, especially vertically so that the curve of the chair can line up with the curve of your back. Horizontal adjustability can adjust support to the level that's most comfortable for you. Cheaper chairs are less likely to offer adjustable support, and can instead make you feel like you're being pushed.

This said, if you can't afford a chair with excellent adjustable lumbar support, you're not doomed to a life of pain. A low-cost lumbar pillow like this one from LoveHome offers a workable fix.

Engineered to contain flames and burn fuel more completely, smokeless fire pits deliver ambient warmth with a lot less smoke in your eyes than a traditional campfire. They achieve this through two points of combustion: The first happens at the bottom, where airflow feeds burning wood; the second occurs at the top, where the fire’s smoke and gas output are burned off. And while no smokeless fire pit is 100 percent smokeless, if you use best practices (like burning dry wood) they come pretty darn close.

We tested smokeless fire pits in our New York and Pennsylvania backyards, building fires rather than judging them on specs alone. Whether you want a smokeless fire pit to heat your outdoor space or be more of an ambient seasonal moment is an important consideration. For long nights by the fire with family and friends, you’ll likely want to invest in a larger model that measures 24 inches in diameter. In testing, models that size and up exuded warmth for several feet, and could easily warm four to six people.

They could also handle 14- to 16-inch-long logs. The trade-off: Models this size are a lot heavier and harder to move. If you’re looking for more of a warming accent, narrower fire pits work wonders. You can also maximize some models’ output by adding a heat deflector that sits above the flames and pushes heat out laterally, instead of letting it escape upward.

Ideally, the smokeless fire pit should work right out of the box, with limited-to-no setup, besides setting the pit on a base. It should also break down easily. If you’ll be storing it between fires or seasons, a model that’s easy to disassemble, carry, and put away is key.

The shallower and wider the smokeless fire pit, the easier it is to make a fire from scratch. You simply have more base room to work with to set up your paper, kindling, and wood, and less depth to maneuver to light your fire once ready. Burning pellets instead of wood was the closest we got to a truly smokeless fire during our testing. Made of compressed wood waste like sawdust, chips, and shavings, pellets are easy to light, always bone dry, and produce a hotter, more consistent flame than logs. There are trade-offs, though: They lack the classic crackle and pop of a wood-burning fire and that wonderful smell.

Bags of the stuff also typically cost more than a handful of logs at the hardware store, yet burn about the same length of time. But, if you’re allergic to smoke, don’t want your sweaters to smell of it, have wet wood drying out, or want a reliable and even fire every single time, pellets are an excellent way to go. Some portable options may only be wide or deep enough to handle twigs (pellets may be a better option for those).

Pro tip: Wet or green wood will always be slow to burn and emit a lot of smoke; use a moisture meter to suss out whether your logs are dry enough to light up before putting a match to them.

If you plan to use your fire pit regularly, easy ash removal should be a deciding factor. The last thing you want to have to do is pick up and tip a heavy steel drum over a trashcan in order to empty it of ash. Instead, look for a removable ash pan or tray that allows you to lift the ash out cleanly once the pit has fully cooled (usually 12 to 24 hours after a fire has ended). Otherwise, you’ll need a shovel to scoop out the ash.

If you don’t have space to leave your fire pit outside all year, think about who will move it and how often. Even a small pit designed for camping trips can weigh as much as 20 pounds. Fortunately, these options usually come with a carrying bag, so you can sling them over your shoulder. Meanwhile, larger models can top 45 pounds (the 24-inch Breeo weighs just over 60 pounds), which matters when you have to move it under cover or into the garage at the end of the season. Units that come apart make this easier; having a dolly or hand truck on hand helps, too.

Material affects how long your new pit will look the way it does on arrival. While stainless steel looks shiny and modern from the start, heat from the flames will discolor it, and potentially quite quickly, as some testing showed. (You can restore its original shine with a stainless-steel cleaner such as Bar Keepers Friend, but that’s extra work.) Corten-steel usually costs more but forms a protective rust-colored patina that looks attractively weathered over time. Painted or powder-coated steel usually costs less, but can scratch and chip with repeat use.

Whatever finish you choose, keeping your smokeless fire pit empty of ash and covered when it’s not in use will help extend its life. Rainwater sitting in a fire pit full of ash can create a corrosive, alkaline slurry that accelerates rust and can damage the metal. Some models come with a cover as part of their bundle; others charge extra for it. Our reviews note whether a cover is included in every pick.

Smokeless fire pits can be a lot more than a place to warm your hands on chilly nights. In some cases, you can turn them into an outdoor kitchen. Definitely check the accessory lineup before you buy your pit; they’re often model-specific. A pair of fire-resistant gloves is always a good investment.

While product photos often depict smokeless fire pits sitting on wood decks or backyards, it’s worth noting that their bases can get hot enough to scorch or discolor lawns, wood decks, composite, and asphalt. Ideally, yours should sit on brick, concrete, stone, or sand. But in the absence of that, a dedicated base (sometimes included, other times sold separately) helps raise the unit off the ground. For an even safer setup, consider adding a fireproof mat underneath. You should also keep a bucket of water or a fire extinguisher nearby and check for local burn bans before lighting.
