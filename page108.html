Alex Stamos says AI has pushed cybersecurity into a dark age

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Alex Stamos, Cognition’s security chief, says the cost of defence is not falling as fast as the cost of attacks. He wants Google and Amazon to fork the Linux kernel and rewrite it in Rust.

Alex Stamos speaking at Fortune Brainstorm Tech in Half Moon Bay, California, in 2021.

Every company needs to attack its own systems with AI hacking tools within about 90 days, or hackers will get in first. That is the warning from Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition. He told Nilay Patel on The Verge’s Decoder podcast that open-weight AI models now give low-skilled attackers much of the skill of elite hackers.

Stamos also lectures at Stanford University’s Cyber Policy Center. He said open-weight models can now automate most of the kill chain. That is the series of steps an attacker takes, from finding a flaw to stealing data or money. Attack tools now find bugs and write exploits in real time, he said.

“We are entering a dark age. Things are going to get more bleak,” Stamos told Decoder.

He pointed to ARTEX, a Chinese open-source attack tool with an English translation on GitHub. Attackers pair it with a version of the open-weight GLM-5.3 model that has its safety limits removed. That setup can break into a large number of organisations, he said.

The attacks began with three South Korean banks, according to Stamos. CrowdStrike linked those breaches to AI tools in October. Attackers hit about 200 more companies in the week before the interview, he said. He has been helping some of them.

Attackers no longer need frontier models, whose makers keep logs that police can reach, he added. Stamos said he runs a cut-down GLM-5.3 on his own Mac Studio.

Some attacks still rely on people. North Korea’s hackers took Binance mostly through social engineering, Stamos said. Binance has said a fake job interview led to its $6.3bn hack. Break-ins at AI companies look like the work of what he called China’s “cottage access industry”. These small contractors sell stolen access to larger Chinese firms.

“The cost per token for attackers is going towards zero, and the cost of defense for defenders is staying quite high,” Stamos said.

A colleague at a large security company told him that one investigation with a frontier cyber model costs $900. Security teams will have to hand their first levels of monitoring to AI, he said. Cheap classifiers would come first and pass alerts up to larger models.

At Cognition, the company’s own security agent can switch off staff accounts, he said. It stops at a fixed number until a human approves more. He also discounts the technical views of anyone who talks about AI attack “swarms” by about 80%. The attacks he sees use one agent, maybe two.

Every company will need to attack itself within 90 days, Stamos said. It can use ARTEX, a similar AI red-teaming product, or a hired firm.

“If you do not, you will get breached,” Stamos said.

Such tests could cause downtime, he said, but they would not steal data or shut down blood bank refrigerators, as happened in France. A cyberattack earlier this month pushed French blood bank freezers to extreme temperatures.

Patching alone cannot keep up, Stamos argued, because each new frontier cyber model brings another wave of Linux kernel fixes. He called on Google and Amazon to fork the Linux kernel for server hardware and rewrite it in Rust, a memory-safe language, at a cost he put at $100m to $1bn. The PHP flaws in October were a near miss, he said, because they proved hard to exploit in the wild.

Stamos wants AI makers to carry liability when their models escape and attack people directly. But he warned that OpenAI and Anthropic already refuse some requests from defenders. The labs treat legitimate companies in Europe and Asia “much more poorly” than American ones, he said. He would be fine with slowing down frontier AI so cheaper defensive models could catch up.

He also said chatbots should not have personalities. On Instagram, he said, Meta should verify real photos straight from the camera rather than try to detect AI-made ones.

Stamos, who joined Cognition in September, said he plans to build a “dark age index” that tracks patch volumes. Microsoft’s monthly Patch Tuesday has grown from about 150 fixes to more than 900, according to Stamos.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/carelon-agrees-pay-10m-settle-ghost-network-coverage-denial-allegations-new-york" hreflang="en">Carelon agrees to pay $10M to settle ghost network, coverage denial allegations in New York</a>

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Carelon agrees to pay $10M to settle ghost network, coverage denial allegations in New York" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Carelon will pay more than $10 million across two settlements in New York over mental health networks and coverage denials.

The company, a division of Elevance Health, will pay $2.35 million in penalties, fees and costs as well as up to $8 million in restitution for members who paid out-of-pocket for out-of-network mental healthcare, to reimburse those whose claims were improperly denied or to overhaul its networks.

New York Attorney General Letitia James said in an announcement that the settlements resolve two investigations from her office that determined the company misled members by directing them to seek care from providers that were out-of-network—through so-called "ghost networks"—and improperly denied coverage for treatment for substance abuse disorder.

James noted that Carelon administers behavioral health benefits for multiple plans, including Anthem Blue Cross, EmblemHealth, Independent Health and the Empire Plan, which covers state and local government workers.

Across these insurers, Carelon manages behavioral health benefits for more than 4 million people in the state, James said.

The alleged behavior also goes against a 2015 agreement with the AG's office, where the company agreed that it would maintain accurate provider networks and "use approved criteria" when denying substance abuse treatments.

"When someone is ready to seek help for a mental health condition or addiction, every day matters," said James. “Carelon left New Yorkers calling provider after provider who could not see them and made it harder for people to get the treatment to which they were entitled."

"These settlements will help people get the care they need and put money back in New Yorkers’ pockets," she said.

Elevance Health said in a statement to Fierce Healthcare that it is committed to ensuring patients have access to the most up-to-date provider information.

"Carelon Behavioral Health is committed to helping people find and access high-quality mental health and substance use disorder care. Accurate, up-to-date provider information is an important part of that effort," the company said. "We continue to invest in tools and processes that support accurate provider information and work closely with providers and other partners to help connect consumers with the care they need."

Carelon was hit with a lawsuit in April over the alleged ghost networks and their impact on the Empire Plan in particular. In the case, plaintiff's attorneys conducted secret shopper calls to analyze Carelon's network, calling 300 providers. Just 17% of those contacted took insurance and were open to new patients.

These "ghost networks" have also drawn concern in other markets, such as for maternal care services in Medicaid and in Medicare Advantage. A study released last month found that commercial plans also routinely have inaccurate provider directories.

CoreWeave CEO Intrator tells CNBC he has canceled his planned share sale program

CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator told CNBC on Wednesday that he has canceled his 10b5 stock sale plan after the company reported strong revenue growth and beat Q3 expectations.

"Until there is a material change in in, in our equity pricing, my 10b5 has been sidelined," he told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street."

He said any further selling would be "just normal tax stuff."

Under Intrator's 10b5-1 plan announced in November 2025, the CEO has sold 8.26 million shares at an average price of $96.14 per share for a total of $794.2 million.

Intrator and his co-founders have faced criticism for their stock sales as the company's stock has struggled, with shares down 18% so far in 2026 despite a 20% post-earnings rally on Wednesday.

The company has faced further pressure from the sell-off in the aftermath of last week's sweeping electoral victory for Democrats, but Intrator told investors on the earnings call that the company isn't worried about the political changes on the horizon.

"We do not anticipate any impact from the seismic regulatory shift that's occurred," Intrator said on his company's earnings call late Tuesday, in response to a question about new pressures after the election. "We believe that what has occurred is a statement around how you build. We want to build responsibly."

Intrator's comments came after CoreWeave reported 168% year-over-year revenue growth, beating estimates, and lifted its forecast for 2026. The stock rallied in extended trading, as the report offered some relief to investors, who have pushed the shares down about 27% over the past month.

Rising interest rates and an unfavorable political environment have fueled concerns that the artificial intelligence boom is poised to cool and that companies like CoreWeave will struggle to raise capital.

In the Nov. 3 midterm elections, voters sided in favor of greater restrictions on data centers in 40 out of 41 ballot measures in 10 states, according to Ballotpedia. Democrats won control of both houses of Congress and were victorious in governors' races in critical AI infrastructure states like Texas, Ohio and Georgia. In each state, the winning candidate favors some restrictions on new data centers.

J.P. Morgan Asset Management wrote in a report after the election that data center projects from 2028 onward are "most exposed," and "face greater uncertainty around permitting, power availability and financing."

But CoreWeave says it's not slowing down. The company said it still anticipates 1.85 gigawatts in active power by the end of 2026, and it's now targeting $37 billion to $39 billion in full-year capital expenditures, implying 155% growth at the middle of the range. Analysts polled by Visible Alpha were looking for $37.69 billion.

"We are very comfortable with our pipeline," Intrator said, adding that the message from voters isn't a deterrent because the company is always conscious about "being good neighbors."

"We think that that is an opportunity for us to continue to scale our business within the new political reality that exists," Intrator said.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/caresource-launches-1m-grant-program-support-ai-powered-solutions" hreflang="en">CareSource launches $1M grant program to support AI-powered solutions</a>

CareSource launches $1M grant program to support AI-powered solutions" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Nonprofit insurer CareSource is launching a $1 million program that aims to back responsible AI solutions in healthcare.

The initiative, launched through the CareSource Foundation, is seeking proposals for ways to advance and identify deployments for AI that can drive better outcomes and address barriers for individuals enrolled in Medicaid or who otherwise struggle to access care.

The program will provide up to $1 million over the course of two years to ensure that the tool can deliver on meaningful and scalable benefits for the communities it serves.

Erhardt Preitauer, CEO of CareSource, said in an email to Fierce Healthcare that AI offers the potential to improve not just patient care but the experience for providers too as they navigate complex conditions.

"I'm a big believer that when technology, healthcare and the people in our communities rally around a shared purpose, magic can happen," said Preitauer. "Leaving no stone unturned is the only way we can make healthcare work better for people, and AI gives us the power to explore every possibility."

The program will include two phases. First, interested applicants can submit shorter proposals, with the goal of generating interest. Applications that are selected to move on will be tasked with submitting a more fleshed-out proposal, including details on implementation, AI governance and sustainability.

CareSource is aiming to identify solutions that are ready for implementation and has the potential for scale. Potential focus areas include care coordination, member engagement, behavioral health and rural healthcare. Solutions should use AI to support care delivery, but it should not replace human judgement or oversight, the insurer said.

To be eligible, a qualified nonprofit organization must be leading the effort, according to the announcement. Technology companies, academic researchers, healthcare organizations and community-based groups can also play a role as partners.

Applicants moving to the second phase of the program will also be required to showcase data privacy and security, solutions to address and prevent bias and ongoing performance tracking.

Measles is surging in the U.S. — and outbreaks can cost millions

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Measles outbreaks aren't just a public health threat. They can also be a financial burden on the U.S. — and the cost could only deepen as the once-eliminated disease spreads more widely.

The U.S. is expected to lose its official measles elimination status following a review by the Pan American Health Organization this week, after cases ratcheted to recent highs this year.

More than 5,100 measles cases have been reported nationwide in 2026 as of early November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. Eight deaths associated with the disease have been confirmed by states so far in 2026: six in Pennsylvania, one in New York and one in Wisconsin.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The tool also estimates that 2025 measles outbreaks in the U.S. — which involved 2,289 confirmed cases nationwide – carried an economic cost of more than $37 million.

The costs extend beyond treating people who become sick, accounting for the resources health departments spend investigating and containing outbreaks, as well as wages lost when people have to miss work. The tool uses more recent, state-specific cost and financing data than an initial study by the researchers, which relied on historical outbreak costs.

The estimates from the tool are not a definitive accounting of what the outbreaks actually cost. But it illustrates the hefty bill tied to the spread of a disease that can be prevented through vaccination, and the potential economic consequences of falling childhood immunization rates. CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases.

"We're starting to see a whole bunch of people who decided not to get vaccinated as kids or have never gotten exposed to measles because they benefited from herd immunity, who now make it into adulthood and risk potentially getting quite seriously ill and requiring very significant medical expenses, or dying at the hospital," Bryan Patenaude, a health economist at the International Vaccine Access Center at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, who helped develop the tool, said in an interview.

Measles outbreaks can trigger a costly public health response, even when many people who are exposed never become sick.

When a case is identified, public health officials must investigate the infection, identify and notify people who may have been exposed, coordinate with hospitals and schools, and sometimes organize vaccination campaigns to prevent further spread. Those efforts can divert staff and resources from other public health priorities.

Costs could differ heavily depending on the state.

Johns Hopkins' data includes national averages alongside state-specific inputs, including inpatient hospital costs and wages from government data or trusted health policy sources. In New York, for example, the average inpatient hospital stay is roughly $4,200 a day, while the minimum wage is $16 per hour.

The tool estimates that the 48 cases reported by New York in 2025 carried an estimated $1.1 million in costs, including nearly $749,000 in public health responses, $333,000 in productivity losses and roughly $24,500 in direct medical expenses.

Meanwhile, the 803 cases in Texas, the epicenter of the largest U.S. measles outbreak in 2025, carried estimated costs of nearly $13 million, according to the tool. That includes $9 million in public health costs, $4 million in productivity losses and around $288,000 in direct medical expenses.

The tool builds on a study published by Patenaude and other researchers in August, which examined the costs of measles outbreaks in the U.S. The researchers reviewed published studies and government reports to compile historical estimates of medical expenses and public health response costs across 18 states.

The paper estimated that outbreaks carried a historical average cost of nearly $59,000 per case, just from the public health response. The researchers also estimated how costs scale with outbreak size, separating fixed up-front response expenses from the additional costs associated with each new case.

"A lot of this is incurred up front, and that's because of the human labor that's involved in contact tracing and all of the different actors that sort of need to be mobilized to deal with the fact that there's an outbreak," Patenaude told CNBC. "It's a largely labor-driven cost."

That model estimated roughly $298,000 in fixed costs and about $16,300 for each additional case. But it relied on historical data, including a particularly large 2019 outbreak in New York City, where contact-tracing labor costs were high. The researchers also excluded productivity losses from that model because most of the historical studies they reviewed did not consistently account for them.

The economic burden of measles extends beyond the costs of treating infected patients and organizing a public health response.

People who are exposed to measles may have to quarantine, even if they never develop symptoms. Parents can also be forced to miss work to care for sick or exposed children, while workers who contract the disease may lose wages during their recovery.

A 2025 study by Joey Mattingly, a pharmacist and health economist at the University of Utah, used published epidemiological and cost data to model the potential economic burden of a hypothetical 1,500-case outbreak. His analysis included direct medical expenses, public health spending and lost productivity, and examined how the estimates changed under different assumptions, including hospitalization rates.

The study estimated that the hypothetical outbreak could cost about $90 million, including roughly $26 million in productivity losses.

Those indirect costs can add up quickly because measles is highly contagious and a single infected person can expose dozens of others. Those contacts may need to quarantine or miss work while health officials determine whether they have been infected, creating costs even when they never develop the disease.

Mattingly estimated that each person who is quarantined could amount to roughly $300 in lost productivity alone.

If a single measles case exposes 40 to 50 people who then need to quarantine, that could translate into an estimated $12,000 to $15,000 in lost productivity, even if none of those contacts become sick.

That ripple effect is one reason Mattingly argues that the economic costs of outbreaks can quickly outweigh the investment required to vaccinate people against measles.

"Even if you don't get the infection, the contact costs you," he said. "And so it's just showing that there's this multiplication or exponential growth on the economic side that happens."

The financial burden can also fall unevenly across communities. While the costs of responding to an outbreak may be shared among local, state and federal agencies, Mattingly said the impact is often felt directly by local health departments.

"It's not like your health department is sitting around waiting for measles outbreaks," he said. "They're working, doing all these other things, and imagine having to just drop what you're doing because there's been a huge outbreak in the community."

Patenaude said the findings could help state and local health departments better anticipate the resources needed to contain outbreaks — particularly as measles cases continue to rise.

"It's not super expensive to treat and deal with a single case of measles compared to a single case of cancer," he said. "But if you have these outbreaks popping up and you're having to constantly divert resources that could be spent elsewhere by state and local health departments on measles, then you're going to wind up spending a lot of money very quickly."

Winter weather can be devastating to outdoor furniture, appliances, and structures, which is why many people store as much as possible in a covered shed or garage throughout the cold season. Even though not everything can be neatly tucked away, such as your deck, walkways, and gates, you can take steps to prepare them for harsh winter conditions before snow starts to fall.

Outdoor winterization projects that involve painting, staining, or sealing help surfaces create a durable barrier against freeze-thaw cycles, moisture intrusion, and corrosive deicing salts. It’s important to complete these protective jobs before the first hard freeze so you can prevent deep structural damage during the winter months.

Leaving wood decks, fences, or patio furniture bare during winter allows moisture from snow and ice to seep into the wood pores. When trapped water freezes, it expands, causing the wood to split, warp, and rot. A fresh coat of waterproof sealant or oil-based stain acts as a hydrophobic barrier. Water pools on top of the sealed surface rather than penetrating the wood grain.

Before applying a compatible exterior stain or water-repellent finish, clean the surface, remove failing finish, and allow the wood to dry sufficiently. For decking and steps, choose a winterization product specifically approved for horizontal surfaces and foot traffic.

Marc Sharkey, owner of Dynamic Painting Inc, says that “the finish reduces water absorption from rain and melting snow. Repeated wetting and drying can contribute to checking, splitting, and warping; prolonged dampness also creates conditions for decay.

“Pay particular attention to exposed board ends and areas where the old finish has worn away.” He also warns: “A new coating cannot repair rotten wood.” Given that Sharkey’s business is based in Alberta, he has experience dealing with some of the harshest winters in North America.

This oil-based formula provides corrosion resistance and weather protection for outdoor metal surfaces . Learn more in our buyer’s guide.

Winter preparation is important to safeguard your home. Chipped paint on steel or iron railings, furniture, and gates exposes the raw metal to oxygen and winter humidity, rapidly accelerating the oxidation process. Road salt or salt-based sidewalk ice melts further expedite this corrosion, eating through the metal structures. Applying a coat of exterior, rust-resistant enamel or oil-based paint seals out moisture and oxygen, halting the chemical reaction that creates rust.

It’s a budget-friendly winterization project that also forms a slick, chemical-resistant barrier against salt splash-back. Before applying a coating, remove loose rust and flaking paint and clean away dirt and salt. Then apply an appropriate rust-inhibiting primer and exterior metal finish. Check compatibility carefully: steel, aluminum, and galvanized metal can require different preparation and primers.

“An intact coating limits exposure to moisture and oxygen, helping slow corrosion. Left exposed, rust can spread beneath surrounding paint, causing more peeling and pitting. Over time, severe corrosion can weaken components. Painting over loose rust does not solve the underlying problem,” explains Sharkey.

Moisture from ice and snow can cause significant damage to concrete because it is a highly porous material. When snow melts on walkways, driveways, or patios, the concrete absorbs the water. Subsequent freezing temperatures cause that internal water to expand, which leads to surface flaking, scaling, and deep cracking.

Unlike surface coatings, penetrating sealers chemically bond inside concrete pores. They create an invisible, breathable hydrophobic barrier that prevents liquid water from sinking into the stone, while still allowing internal vapor to escape.

Sharkey notes that “for compatible, absorbent concrete steps, walkways, or patios, a penetrating sealer rated for exterior concrete and freeze-thaw exposure can reduce water absorption. Confirm compatibility with any existing treatment and follow the preparation instructions.”

Reducing water and deicing-salt penetration helps protect against winter deterioration. However, just keep in mind that a sealer is preventive maintenance; it does not repair cracks, failing concrete, or drainage problems.

Google DeepMind’s AlphaGenome 2 Predicts Cell-State Variant Effects

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google DeepMind announced AlphaGenome 2 on November 11, 2026, a deep-learning model that predicts how DNA variants affect gene regulation in individual cell types and states. Trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles, it is available for non-commercial research and in private preview for commercial research.

The release addresses a blind spot in tissue-wide genomic models: a variant can matter only in a specialized cell or in a cell activated by disease, leaving its signal diluted in a tissue average. AlphaGenome 2 instead conditions each prediction on a selected tissue, cell type or cell state, including conditions generated from a researcher’s own single-cell experiment.

The earlier AlphaGenome 1 predicted molecular activity across broad tissues, while AlphaGenome Atlas offered predictions for all 9 billion possible single-letter changes in the human genome. AlphaGenome 2 is a separate model release, not an extension of that precomputed atlas.

AlphaGenome 1 generated fixed outputs for bulk tissues and cell lines simultaneously from a one-million-letter DNA sequence. AlphaGenome 2 evaluates one condition at a time and supplies that condition throughout the network, allowing it to represent how the same DNA segment is read differently across cell types. Its readouts include gene expression, RNA splicing and chromatin accessibility, with CpG DNA methylation added to the original model’s capabilities.

The technical paper describes training on human and mouse bulk and single-cell data. Researchers can supply single-cell RNA-seq data, a snapshot of gene activity, from a new experiment, and a cell encoder turns it into a condition without further fine-tuning. That allows predictions for datasets and cell states the model has not seen before.

The same design can fill in missing experimental readouts. Given gene-expression data alone, AlphaGenome 2 can predict unmeasured properties such as chromatin accessibility—whether stretches of DNA are open to regulation—helping researchers prioritize subsequent experiments. The trade-off is computational: every condition requires a separate prediction, making the model more intensive to run than fixed-track multitask models.

DeepMind’s paper reports that on TenK10K, a single-cell immune dataset spanning 28 cell types, AlphaGenome 2 recovered 30% of putatively causal expression-linked variants at 90% precision, compared with 16% for AlphaGenome 1 fine-tuned on single-cell data. It recovered 43% of variant effect directions at 90% accuracy, versus 23%. On genome regions excluded from training, the model also explained a median 34% of within-tissue, cell-type-specific gene-expression variance, against 23% for the same fine-tuned predecessor. These evaluations measure regulatory differences between related cells, the problem the conditional architecture is designed to address.

DeepMind also reports two research examples of cell-state-specific analysis. In an inflammatory bowel disease analysis, AlphaGenome 2 flagged rs2836882 from a seven-variant credible set, linked it to ETS2 approximately 290 kilobases away and localized the predicted effect to gut monocytes and macrophages, reproducing a mechanism previously established experimentally. Using single-cell data from a COVID-19 mRNA vaccine study that was new to the model, it distinguished vaccine-activated T cells from resting T cells and predicted a stronger effect for a variant associated with severe COVID-19 in the activated state. Together, the examples illustrate how a variant’s predicted effect can depend on both cell type and current state.

DeepMind precomputed two resources for researchers who do not want to run large-scale comparisons themselves. One maps enhancer–gene links across 640 primary single-cell reference conditions and 39,323 protein-coding and long non-coding RNA genes; enhancers are regulatory DNA segments that can act far from their target genes. Another scores more than 6.8 million unique common and trait-associated single-letter variants across 510 human conditions to nominate possible causal variants, target genes, effect directions and relevant cell states.

At launch, AlphaGenome 2 and expression-conditioning Colab tutorials were available through the AlphaGenome API for non-commercial research, with commercial research in private preview. The official documentation says non-commercial API use is free and query rates vary with demand. DeepMind also plans AlphaGenome 2 Skills for Google Antigravity, its agentic development platform. Genomics PLC CEO Peter Donnelly said he looked forward to incorporating AlphaGenome 2 into the company’s Mystra platform and described sequence-prediction models as tools for moving from association signals to information drug-development programs need.

The paper cautions that even at high precision, AlphaGenome 2 recovers only a minority of the expression-linked and disease-associated variants that statistical analyses nominate as likely causal. It can underestimate effects outside accessible chromatin, at very distant regulatory elements or through mechanisms acting after transcription. Transient, stimulated and disease states remain underrepresented in training and the precomputed variant panel, and fine-grained neuronal subpopulations remain difficult to distinguish.

Those condition representations come from observational, steady-state profiles and therefore capture correlations rather than causal relationships. It remains uncertain whether the model can predict responses to gene knockouts, overexpression or dynamic cell-state transitions. DeepMind restricts both AlphaGenome versions to research use; neither has been validated or approved for clinical or diagnostic purposes.

The central consequence is a more targeted route from a disease association to a candidate variant, target gene and cell state. AlphaGenome 2 can narrow that search across hundreds of conditions, but its incomplete variant recovery and correlational view of cell states mean the resulting hypotheses still depend on experimental follow-up.
