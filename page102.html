Switching GLP-1 medications or undergoing bariatric surgery may help people maintain greater weight loss than stopping treatment entirely, according to a new study presented at ObesityWeek and published in eClinicalMedicine on November 14.

The Cleveland Clinic study compared five strategies for managing weight after treatment with GLP-1 medications, analyzing data from 20,486 adults with obesity or type 2 diabetes.

People who stop taking GLP-1 medications often regain weight. Researchers examined whether continuing treatment, switching medications, or undergoing bariatric surgery could help limit weight regain or support further weight loss.

The findings showed that people who underwent bariatric surgery or switched between semaglutide (Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) experienced the greatest overall weight loss.

The study participants had an average age of 56.7 years, an average weight of 109 kg (240.3 lbs), and an average body mass index (BMI) of 38. All had been taking either semaglutide or tirzepatide for at least 90 days.

After 2 years, those who underwent bariatric surgery saw an average loss of 28.2% of their body weight or about 31.5 kg (69.4 lbs).

“We increasingly view medications and surgery as complementary tools rather than competing ones,” said Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“For patients with severe obesity (typically a BMI of 35 or higher, or 30+ with metabolic conditions like type 2 diabetes), bariatric surgery remains the most durable, cost-effective intervention available,” he added. “Using a GLP-1 beforehand can safely reduce liver volume and lower surgical risk, while surgery provides the permanent anatomical and hormonal reset needed to maintain that weight loss long-term without relying indefinitely on weekly injections.”

During the same period, those who switched between semaglutide and tirzepatide lost 16% of their body weight, or about 18 kg (39.6 lbs).

Participants who switched from semaglutide to tirzepatide lost 3 kg (6.6 lbs) more than those who continued taking semaglutide. However, participants already taking tirzepatide lost 4.5 kg (9.9 lbs) more by continuing treatment rather than switching to semaglutide.

A Florida federal court has tossed Tampa General Hospital's lawsuit against Eli Lilly over the 340B drug discount program.

Florida's Middle District court dismissed the case on a motion from the drugmaker on Wednesday. If Tampa General Hospital wants to file an amended complaint, it must do so within 30 days.

The hospital initially sued the pharmaceutical company over a policy it implemented that requires providers to submit claims-level data on pharmacy and medical dispensing if they participate in 340B. Lilly said it would pull back discounts for providers that did not comply with the requirement and has done so for several organizations, drawing ire from the industry.

Tampa General said the decision is "unconscionable and immoral" in the initial complaint, filed in July.

However, the district court argued that Supreme Court precedent holds that organizations like hospitals do not have the private right to sue if they have been charged beyond the threshold, based on a 2011 decision. SCOTUS said that the right to sue was not outlined by Congress, so it is not an avenue available to these organizations.

"The Court made clear that there is simply no private remedy to superintend the pricing and provision of these discounted drugs," per the order.

"To permit this suit would be to encourage piecemeal litigation and defeat the [Supreme] Court’s insistence on maintaining the Congressional plan 'harmoniously and on a uniform, nationwide basis,'" according to the document.

Eli Lilly cheered the decision in a statement.

"Today’s decision is an important step toward a more transparent 340B program," a Lilly spokesperson said. "Lilly’s claims data requirement asks hospitals for basic information—information they already give insurers every day—to prevent unlawful duplicate discounts."

"Decades of HRSA guidance and two federal appeals courts confirm that Lilly can lawfully require this information," the drugmaker said. "More than 3,200 hospitals and clinics already share it with Lilly, and we hope Tampa General Hospital will join them."

Tampa General Hospital has sued Eli Lilly and Company over the drugmaker’s “unconscionable and immoral” rescindment of discounted pricing under the 340B program.

The complaint, filed last week for the U.S. District Court in the Middle District of Florida, is early legal fallout from a policy Lilly implemented earlier this year that requires providers participating in the federal subsidy program to submit claims-level data for all pharmacy and medical dispensations. The company threatened to pull the discounts for those that didn’t comply and made good on its threat for a subset of hospitals last month, sparking outrage among provider groups.

Tampa General, a nearly 1,000-bed disproportionate share hospital, confirmed in the legal complaint that it was among those cut off. It is now spending 25% to 50% more on average for Lilly products, “many of which it must purchase because of Lilly’s status as the market’s only supplier of a given drug product.”

Tampa General’s lawsuit frequently describes Lilly in such terms, framing the drugmaker as holding significant monopoly power and wielding it in violation of the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act. It also often draws contrast to the 340B program’s intent to support the margins of safety-net hospitals providing care to vulnerable patients, against Lilly’s current status as the largest pharmaceutical company in the world by market cap.

A spokesperson for Lilly, in a statement given to Fierce Healthcare, described the lawsuit as “just another part of the broader hospital campaign to fight transparency and hide the fraud, waste and abuse that plague the 340B program.”

Tampa General asked the court to order an immediate end to Lilly’s policy and to award damages for the higher prices to which the hospital was subjected. It contends Lilly’s alleged failure to comply with 340B pricing requirements and decision to continue marketing its products as 340B-discounted are unfair trade practices under the Florida statute.

“The most valuable pharmaceutical company in the world is engaged in a campaign to transfer dollars earmarked for charitable purposes into its own pocket,” the complaint reads. “The direct and inevitable result of Lilly’s actions is reduced healthcare for poor people. Its actions shock the conscience and are self-evidently unconscionable.”

Tampa General, similar to the hospital associations that have protested Lilly’s policy, contends that the claims data it requires imposes substantial administrative burden on 340B providers. Even “hospitals who have caved to Lilly’s new demands” have effectively had the prices for their covered outpatient drugs increased due to those administrative costs, it wrote in the complaint.

Lilly and other drugmakers who have signaled similar plans have said they need claims data to spot instances in which a drug purchase is improperly granted multiple discounts under overlapping programs. The company has also disputed that the burden of its policy is as substantial as hospitals say.

“The claims data Lilly requires hospitals to share is information they already collect and send to insurers regularly,” the Lilly spokesperson said of the lawsuit’s claims. “Refusing to share that same data with Lilly shows that hospitals are more concerned with hiding rampant abuse in the program than improving its integrity. Lilly will keep working to ensure this program serves vulnerable patients instead of hospitals and their for-profit partners.”

The 340B program has proven to be a magnet for industry and policy conflict, particularly in recent years as its scale has ballooned. The divide is most evident in the ongoing clash over a pilot rebate program that the Trump administration attempted to launch at the top of the year but was forced to restart after the hospital industry’s legal pushback.

340B providers also received a shock this past week when the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services floated a significant reduction in its 340B reimbursement to 33.4% below average sales price in its annual proposed rule on hospital outpatient payments.

The NSW Planning Portal began public exhibition of the environmental impact statement (EIS) for Stockland Development’s proposed Aldington Road Data Centre on November 19, 2026. The two-building Kemps Creek campus has a stated maximum power consumption of 444 MW, with submissions accepted until December 17, 2026.

The State Significant Development remains under assessment, so exhibition is not authorization to build. Stockland’s October 19, 2026 operational update lists Kemps Creek as 50% Stockland-owned and targeted at AI/hyperscaler customers, giving the planning review direct relevance to its AI infrastructure strategy.

The amended Planning Secretary’s Environmental Assessment Requirements (SEARs), issued September 15, 2026, set out up to 360 MW of IT power consumption, data hall buildings as tall as 36.4 meters, ancillary offices, backup power and diesel storage, and a substation with distribution capacity of up to 1,000 megavolt-amperes. The IT figure is distinct from the portal’s campus-wide maximum, separating computing equipment from supporting infrastructure whose demand the EIS must also quantify.

On its project page, Stockland locates the proposal on industrial-zoned land in Western Sydney’s Mamre Road Precinct and says it is designed around air-cooling technology that does not rely on water for cooling. A March 2026 endorsement by the NSW Investment Delivery Authority recognized the project as private-sector investment in critical sectors including digital infrastructure.

Efficiency and energy supply form part of the approval test. The EIS must demonstrate the development’s ability to achieve a Power Usage Effectiveness score of 1.3 or less, equivalent to a five-star or better NABERS Data Centre Energy rating, and address state targets for Water Usage Effectiveness. It must also commit to matching operational electricity demand with NSW-based renewable energy supply at all times. These are assessment requirements, not measurements from an operating facility.

Backup-power and cooling systems must be modeled during unplanned outages, testing and maintenance. Noise and air-quality assessments must include cumulative effects from other proposed and approved data centers nearby, alongside separate work on traffic, water, biodiversity and utility infrastructure.

Stockland’s operational update describes its data center opportunity as a partnership with global operator EdgeConneX and reports approximately 450 MW of power secured across the platform, with four additional sites identified from its existing logistics pipeline. The secured-power figure is subject to final design, documentation and customer negotiations; it is platform-wide rather than a Kemps Creek capacity figure.

That distinction matters because the planning documents measure different things: secured power across Stockland’s portfolio, IT load at Kemps Creek, and the proposed campus’s maximum overall consumption. Keeping those measurements separate shows both the scale of the individual proposal and the earlier commercial and technical condition of the wider platform.

The Wetherill Park Data Centre record is at the Prepare SEARs stage and describes a single four-storey building operating 24/7 with approximately 238 MW of total capacity and 170 MW of IT load. The works also include demolition and site establishment, an on-site substation and a transmission line. Stockland’s update identifies that site as 100% Stockland-owned and targeted at the same AI/hyperscaler customer category.

A separate portal record for 192 Narellan Road in Campbelltown is at the Prepare EIS stage. It describes construction and 24/7 operation of a 100 MW data center with a maximum building height of 30 meters. Both it and Wetherill Park remain earlier in the planning sequence than Kemps Creek’s exhibition.

For Stockland, Kemps Creek is the immediate test of whether a campus targeted at AI and hyperscaler customers can satisfy NSW’s efficiency, renewable-energy and cumulative-impact requirements. Public submissions and the remaining assessment steps precede any construction decision; the company anticipates a determination in 2027, an expectation rather than a fixed approval date.

Indiana’s state park system offers a limited draw hunt that allows residents to target the abundant whitetails on park land that doesn’t get much hunting pressure. There are about 25 parks in the popular program, with two firearms hunts allowed on most of them. Each hunt runs just two days.

“I put in for the early two-day hunt on November 16 and 17 at a Northeast Indiana state park and got drawn for it,” Mike Miltroka tells Outdoor Life. “Trail cameras are not allowed on park [land]. But you can scout before hunt days. I scouted the park on Sunday, the day before the first day of hunting on November 16.”

Miltroka found a likely spot and, well before daybreak on Monday, he was in a tree saddle with his Ruger American Generation II chambered in 7mm PRC.

“It was cool and a little windy, but I was seeing deer soon after daybreak,” says Miltroka, a 38-year-old long-haul truck driver from Farmland. “I spotted a good buck far out, but the cover was too thick to shoot.”

As the morning wore on, Miltroka got a chance to take two does, and he dropped them both with his Ruger. He toted both does to his truck using a deer cart, since motorized vehicles are not allowed off-road.

The spot was already paying off, and Miltroka had a friend — who had seen a giant deer using the area before the hunt — to thank for tipping him off about it.

“I’d never seen the deer previously, but a buddy showed me a video [he’d taken] of the buck a month earlier while visiting the park … It was a place no one else was hunting, and I checked to make sure it was okay to hunt there – and it was.”

On the second day of the hunt, Miltroka chose a different tree. He climbed into a big walnut closer to the cover where he’d seen the buck on Monday. But unlike the day before, Miltroka didn’t see much except a coyote. Then, around 8:30, things changed.

“I heard something raking trees behind me, and I didn’t know what it was. I kept trying to see what was making all that racket, then I saw him out about 70 yards,” he says. “I knew it was a good shooter buck, and when I got him in my scope I squeezed the trigger, fired, and the deer took off running.”

Miltroka jacked another round into the chamber, but as soon as he picked up the running buck in his scope again, the deer went down.

It wasn’t until Miltroka studied the dead buck through his scope that he realized how immense the deer was.

“I started shaking, and yelling,” he says. “I got buck fever after it was over … I called a friend of mine and was so excited he asked if I was tied tight to my tree, and I said yes. I was shaking so much I might have fallen out of it.”

Once he was steady enough, Miltroka climbed down and walked up on the deer.

“I was in complete shock,” he says. “I’ve only taken about six bucks, and none even have the size of this one. I was screaming with joy.”

He filled out a transport tag for his buck, which filled his limit of three deer for the park hunt – only one of which can be a buck. Then he wheeled the buck to his vehicle, loaded it, and drove to park headquarters to check it in.

The buck’s field dressed weight was 181 pounds. The 22-point nontypical was green scored by an official Boone & Crockett measurer the day after Miltroka shot it. It gross scored 227 inches, with a net score of 197 6/8s inches. It has an outside antler spread of nearly 25 inches, and one main beam of the rack measures over 27 inches.

He will have the buck scored again after the required 60-day drying period for B&C and will enter the buck into B&C’s nontypical record category. He’ll also submit his buck to the Hoosier Record Buck Program.

“The park hunts are well run and there are plenty of deer,” says Miltroka. “I saw lots of deer being checked in during the hunts and some nice bucks.

The report also frames the current downturn as the shallowest bear market for bitcoin to date. Sygnum puts the drawdown at around 54%, compared with declines of more than 70% in previous cycles, and says digital assets enter 2027 with lower structural volatility and stronger fundamentals.

The outlook arrives as bitcoin trades near $63,000 after six straight weekly losses and a drop below its 200-week moving average. Sygnum’s case is that the market has changed since earlier cycles, not that the slide is over.

Sygnum describes a more mature market. Volatility is increasingly driven by broader dollar liquidity and market structure, it said, and institutions have added new channels through ETFs, leveraged trading and crypto-backed credit. By the end of October, stablecoin supply stood near $298 billion and tokenized real-world assets exceeded $39 billion.

Perplexity Publishes Guide to Managed RAG for Enterprise AI

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Perplexity published a guide on November 19, 2026, explaining how companies can outsource retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), the process that gives enterprise AI systems access to information beyond a model’s training parameters, through a managed service.

The guide calls this approach RAG as a service, or RaaS, and applies the term to arrangements where outside providers handle most or all RAG components and processes. Responsibility for vector databases, embedding models and retrievers moves to the provider, which can also manage ingestion, storage, security and continuing optimization. Depending on the package, the vendor supplies the language model and user interface or connects its retrieval service to the customer’s existing AI system.

Three deployment models frame the choice. Local RAG puts ingestion, retrieval and storage on the user’s own servers, potentially alongside locally run embedding and language models. A self-built cloud system uses APIs to reach hosted databases and third-party models, but internal administrators manage the connections and orchestration. With RaaS, the provider manages all or most of the system, and the enterprise supplies access to its database or cloud storage.

In the guide’s knowledge-management example, the provider receives knowledge-base access through an API. After cleaning the text, it turns passages into vector embeddings and places them in a vector database on its own servers. The provider also adds newly available documentation and connects its retriever API to the customer’s orchestrator, the software that coordinates user inputs and sends augmented prompts to the language model.

When a user queries the company chatbot, the orchestrator passes the query text to the vendor, which embeds it and searches the database. The orchestrator adds the returned information to the input context and sends it to the language model, whose answer is then delivered through the chatbot. The guide says supplying information absent from training parameters improves the accuracy and completeness of generated outputs.

The principal applications are internal knowledge management, customer support, sales assistance, customer-data access and customer-facing product chatbots. Internal assistants can search documentation stored in services such as Google Drive, SharePoint and Confluence. Sales systems can draw on support tickets, competitor research and call recordings, while customer-data applications can support subscription management, returns and changes to personal details. Product chatbots can also connect to backend databases to answer discovery and technical questions.

Perplexity lists faster deployment, reduced maintenance, scalability, automatic content synchronization and enterprise-platform integration as advantages of managed RAG, while noting that service and package-tier limits still apply. Its build-versus-buy framework centers on cost, scale and complexity. Self-hosting can suit clearly bounded usage and may avoid token and storage charges when every component runs on internal servers, although hardware and maintenance can offset those savings.

Advanced architectures can add multimodal and hybrid retrieval, along with reranking, and the guide says specialized vendors typically offer the most economical access to that expertise. Because most RAG systems already use some vendor services, the decision centers on how much of the overall system a provider will build and manage.

The guide divides providers into general AI platforms with built-in RAG and managed backend infrastructure vendors, presenting the former as ready-to-use products with integrations and the latter as options for tailored setups. Its seven-provider editorial selection comprises Perplexity, AWS, OpenAI, Microsoft Azure, Pinecone, LlamaIndex and Vectara.

Perplexity Enterprise appears in that selection as a suite of agentic tools with internal and project-level knowledge bases, web search, access to multiple generative models, a proprietary embedding model, external-data integrations, security and employee permissions.

On its separate Enterprise page, Perplexity says more than 50,000 organizations trust Perplexity Enterprise and describes Computer as drawing on 20 advanced models, with the broader platform connecting company files and tools for internal knowledge, research and complex projects.

The same page states that Perplexity does not train its large language models on enterprise customer data. Listed controls include SOC 2 Type II certification, configurable file retention, user management, SSO and SCIM, and audit logs for tracking user activity.

Companies can also split the approach, building straightforward systems themselves and using a provider for advanced applications. The practical consequence is that outsourcing need not be enterprise-wide: each deployment can place a different share of retrieval complexity, expense and maintenance with the provider, while the enterprise retains responsibility for whatever it keeps internal.
