10 Crime Shows With the Greatest Villain Performances, Ranked

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

One of the greatest strengths of crime television is its ability to skew moral compasses and deliver fascinating, complex characters that challenge our understanding of humanity. This is true of detective drama and organized crime suspense, and it is particularly true of the many great villains that populate these series, whether they are psychotic serial killers defined by their sinister presence or oddly charismatic crooks who are as charming as they are chilling.

It goes without saying that the performances of such characters are instrumental in determining their quality. Everything from the subtleties of facial expressions to the rhythm of line delivery, and to the physicality they are presented with, is all key choices actors can make in defining the brand of villainy their character exudes. Whether viewers hold a wicked love for them or dread every second of screen time they receive, these crime series find astonishing highlights in the form of their biggest and baddest antagonists.

Despite only appearing in the third season of Boardwalk Empire, Gyp Rosetti (Bobby Cannavale) stands as one of the Prohibition-era gangster series’ most piercing and unforgettable characters. Within a story that often feels methodical and pragmatic, Rosetti injects a tense volatility into every single scene he appears in, which is a testament to the astonishing writing of the character and Cannavale’s note-perfect performance of such an explosive character.

Whereas so many of the other adversaries Nucky Thompson (Steve Buscemi) encounters attempt to outsmart him through business maneuvers or political plotting, Rosetti relies on ferocious, bloodthirsty brutality and unwavering intent. His destructive, overwhelming personality is only made all the more commanding by the character’s sensitivity to perceived slights, especially as Cannavale imbues every second of screen time he gets with the simmering suspense of a ticking time bomb. Whether he’s erupting into a fit of rage or hurling oddly elegant insults, Cannavale makes Rosetti one of the most intimidating and unpleasant TV antagonists of all time.

As a grim descent into the criminal underbelly of Gotham City, The Penguin marks one of HBO’s more compelling productions of the decade so far, a rich mixture of comic-book style and gangland brutality. Colin Farrell’s lead performance is a driving force of this, but the miniseries benefits from its vast array of striking characters, with Cristin Milioti’s turn as criminal heiress Sofia Falcone being the noteworthy standout as she blends an erratic, unpredictable ruthlessness with underlying vulnerability and sympathetic sorrow.

Milioti worked closely with both movement coach Julia Crockett and the production’s costuming department to perfect Sofia’s physicality. Through every movement, she embodies the volatility of her explosive aspirations fighting to break free from her past of repression and institutionalization. In isolation, she is exceptional — the fourth episode’s focus on her time in Arkham Asylum was instrumental in her Emmy Award win — but the crackling, high-octane tension she brings to scenes with Farrell is her true triumph, crafting a complex yet purposeful obstacle to Cobb’s own ambitions of controlling organized crime in Gotham.

Purely as a superhero villain, Wilson Fisk (Vincent D’Onofrio), otherwise known as Kingpin, is a perfect foil to Daredevil (Charlie Cox), with his brutish strength challenging the hero’s more precise and calculating fighting style. However, it isn’t the imposing physicality that makes Fisk such a great antagonist, but rather the depth, nuance, and tragedy D’Onofrio realizes in the character in addition to his intimidating frame.

He plays the part with a certain childlike vulnerability, a stiff social awkwardness that is almost endearing in tandem with Fisk’s sophistication and his love for Vanessa (Ayelet Zurer). His stammering, soft-spoken nature only makes his volatile rage all the more terrifying, grounding the character in his insecurities that can give way to a destructive wrath at any moment. D’Onofrio’s ability to navigate such a polarizing dichotomy is astonishing, bringing both the physical gravitas and the timid personality together to make for one of the best mob boss bad guys in the medium’s history.

It may be difficult to separate hero from villain in a series like The Sopranos. Everyone is bad. But some are worse than others, as Tony Soprano’s (James Gandolfini) rogues' gallery perfectly illustrates. Amid a collection of menacing mobsters, Joe Pantoliano’s Ralphie Cifaretto is a striking standout, with the duality Pantoliano brings to the role being a significant reason for this.

The character is both charming and reprehensible. He has an air of charisma and quick-witted comedy that creates some of the more memorable and wickedly hilarious scenes in the series, but his volatile nature also underlines some of the more disturbing moments as well. His abrupt, sadistic murder of Tracee (Ariel Kiley) is a frightfully disgusting scene that tends to linger in the minds of viewers, one only made more chilling by the way Pantoliano pivots straight back to Ralphie’s more cheerful demeanor almost immediately after. Even within Tony’s crew, he is a disconcerting, unpredictable presence. That he made such a visceral impression despite featuring in just 21 of The Sopranos’ 86 episodes is a testament to Pantoliano’s performance.

At its best, Killing Eve presents a mixture of high-octane spy thrills and international crime drama that is nothing short of addictive. Its two lead actresses are instrumental in creating this gripping atmosphere of anxiety and awe, with Jodie Comer utterly outstanding as the brutal yet brilliant international assassin, Villanelle. While the character is initially presented as a ferocious, psychotic killer, she gradually evolves as her complex cat-and-mouse obsession with MI5 agent Eve Polastri (Sandra Oh) grows increasingly intense and intimate.

Comer is brilliant at exploring the depths of Villanelle while still presenting her as a ruthless, unpredictable villain. Her ability to switch between accents, dialects, and even languages as Villanelle navigates the world of international crime is captivating, making for a frightfully capable antagonist who exudes a sense of magnetism with her mixture of callous practicality and sadistic ire. Even slight facial twitches become transfixing, whether she’s conveying boredom, curiosity, or menace. The end result is a bold villain laced with theatricality and intrigue who is able to be profoundly complex and absolutely sinister at once.

Justified becomes more than just a standard police procedural series off the back of its quirks and eccentricities. The setting, the style, and the tone all play a pivotal part in this, as do the characters themselves, with few being as defining as the overarching antagonist, Boyd Crowder (Walton Goggins). Crowder was originally set to die in the pilot episode, but Goggins’ infectious screen presence saw the writers extend his arc across the six-season stretch, with Boyd going from being a white supremacist to a born-again preacher with a penchant for organized crime.

The theatricality Goggins imbues in the part is transfixing. Lines aren’t simply recited; they are danced with, performed in the truest sense of the word. What could so easily have been a typical redneck villain is instead a captivating cacophony of Southern charm, intellectual curiosity, and treacherous, underlying vulnerability. He is the epitome of a villain you can’t help but love, a grandiose persona of mayhem whose chemistry with Timothy Olyphant’s U.S. Marshal Raylan Givens is an essential pillar of Justified’s arresting allure.

Sometimes, in great detective drama, the only thing more satisfying than seeing the hero solve the case is seeing them meet their match. Sherlock operates on this idea magnificently. Introduced in the Season 1 finale, Andrew Scott’s Jim Moriarty is the perfect foil to Sherlock’s (Benedict Cumberbatch) analytical expertise, and one that perfectly fits into the series’ modernizing take on Arthur Conan Doyle’s classic sleuth stories.

Instead of moustache-twirling vaudevillian villainy, Moriarty is a cold and calculating criminal mastermind who pushes Holmes to his limit and beyond. The theatricality and energized fun that Scott brings to the role is a huge factor as to why the character is so unforgettable, but his ruthless cunning is what establishes him in the viewer’s mind in the first place. Both within the context of the story—being the perfect adversary to Sherlock—and on his own merits—his mesmerizing mixture of twisted humor, arresting charisma, and genuine menace—Jim Moriarty is a true icon of television antagonism.

There are few who would dispute the notion that Dexter’s fourth season is its strongest. It benefits from unrelenting high-stakes tension, a number of devastating plot twists, and the impeccable performance of John Lithgow as the overarching antagonist, Arthur Mitchell. Also known as The Trinity Killer, Mitchell presents a chilling duality between suburban normality and murderous rage, and it’s a divide that Lithgow excels at exploring and blurring as his cat-and-mouse game with Dexter (Michael C. Hall) escalates throughout the season.

However, Mitchell isn’t presented merely as being a sadistic serial killer hiding in the cozy comfort of suburbia, but is rather dissected as a fascinating, often tragic man governed by an overbearing God-complex, festering trauma, and a deeply disturbed inner child. It is undeniably terrifying, but there is an acute pathos that viewers are compelled to feel for him as well. Complimented by his seismic impact on Dexter’s personal life, Arthur Mitchell is both a devastating villain and a bitterly complex soul, with Lithgow’s deft portrayal of him making for one of the best antagonists in crime television.

It is impossible to overstate the achievement of what Mads Mikkelsen achieved with his performance throughout Hannibal. Not only did he have to epitomize true, calculating evil in a series loaded with frightful antagonists, but he also had to inherit the role from Anthony Hopkins’ iconic, Academy Award-winning turn in The Silence of the Lambs. He excels with that same sense of refined, sophisticated menace, but he also finds room to impart his own nuances to the character.

Through his manipulative working relationship with traumatized FBI criminal profiler Will Graham (Hugh Dancy), Lecter connives to garner control while still satiating his own homicidal impulses. Mikkelsen approaches the character as a fallen angel, a devilish villain alien to human morality cloaked in a veneer of sharp professionalism. The only glimpses behind the façade, particularly early in the series, are subtle facial twitches and shifts in posture. It’s eerily graceful, completely engaging, and one of the best television performances of the 2010s.

Breaking Bad has no shortage of great villains, from the savagely unpredictable Tuco (Raymond Cruz) to the sadistic Todd (Jesse Plemons), but it is impossible to look beyond Giancarlo Esposito’s Gustavo Fring as being the series’ greatest antagonist. In contrast to the show’s many explosive and eccentric antagonists, Fring is an image of cold poise, a terrifying entity of restraint and discipline harboring a cunning, murderous mind beneath the surface of a fast-food restaurant chain owner.

With Esposito’s tight and tense performance capable of injecting both comedy and chilling suspense into scenes, Fring’s lack of theatricality becomes a spectacle in itself. This quiet nature is imbued with tremendous power courtesy of Esposito’s rhythmic yet calm line delivery and his icily still presence, both of which present him as a figure of ruthless efficiency. Whether Walter White (Bryan Cranston) is working under Fring or waging a deadly war of wits against him, the cartel drug lord exudes a frightful yet transfixing air of lethal composure that makes him the greatest crime TV villain of all time as well as one of the best characters the medium has ever seen.

Most of its recent decline has been caused by a turbulent market, which probably won't be improving much in the very near term.

By Alex Carchidi – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:03AM EST

With Zcash (ZEC -7.27%) falling to $922 during its first big drop below $1,000 since September, it's natural for investors to wonder whether it's time to buy the dip, especially considering that the coin is still up 73% this year so far.

Price action alone doesn't make for a good investment thesis, so let's take a closer look at this privacy coin and determine whether it's worth buying right now, despite the turbulence and uncertainty in the crypto market and the world.

Zcash's recent decline from its highs is to be expected, given the bumps in the road it has faced.

For instance, on Oct. 29, hackers stole $6.3 billion from Binance, the largest crypto exchange, marking the largest-ever crypto theft and worsening the climate of fear that has been intensifying since a spate of other crypto hacks earlier in the year. Similarly, when October's Consumer Price Index (CPI) data came in above market forecasts, it sparked a flight from risky assets like crypto amid expectations that the Federal Reserve will hike interest rates at its next meeting in December. Separately, investors pulled $46 million from Grayscale's Zcash exchange-traded fund (ETF) in October, followed by another $15 million in early November.

None of those issues were caused by anything related to Zcash itself. Its main investment thesis, namely that investors will continue to seek to conceal their transactions and account balances from prying eyes, is still valid.

Now, let's turn to whether it makes sense to buy this coin right now.

Zcash just launched its latest network upgrade, NU7, which makes the chain significantly faster. It cuts the target block time, the gap between each batch of its confirmed transactions, from 75 seconds to 25 seconds. It also sequesters 60% of the network's transaction fees into a reserve that will disburse its funds to pay miners starting in around 2031. Those changes are well and good, but they don't imply any need to rush with a purchase.

One interesting factor that might make it a better buy is the spread of privacy in crypto. On Nov. 10, Helius, a Solana (SOL -4.23%) infrastructure company, launched a privacy protocol for use on Solana. Its CEO signaled the design was inspired by Zcash's. This imitation validates Zcash, but it also means more competition in the future.

Overall, I believe in Zcash's long-term investment potential, but the market is a bit too fragile for me to buy. A coin that lost over 41% in just eight weeks can do it again, and I do not want to be taking risks like that in the current environment of uncertainty.

But for those with a very high risk tolerance and a five-year-plus holding horizon, not to mention already having a diversified portfolio, buying a small amount of Zcash right now could still pay off.

Why Serie A players are wearing red paint on their faces in Week 12 fixtures

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Serie A players and coaches will be donning a splash red paint across their cheeks during the 12th round of league fixtures coming up this weekend to raise awareness for the ‘Un Rosso alla Violenza (‘a red card to violence’) campaign.

This will be the 10th time that Serie A has participated in the Rosso alla Violenza campaign, which is aimed to raise awareness around domestic violence towards women.

A number of gestures to raise awareness for the campaign are planned for the 12th round of Serie A fixtures: Players, coaches and officials will don the red face paint, and for the first time, supporters in the stands have been encouraged to participate as well.

Serie A captains will also wear a symbolic armband during Week 12. The hashtag #unrossoallaviolenza and several adverts and graphics will be shown in the stadiums and will be broadcast on TV ahead of kick-off.

“Serie A has been at the forefront of the fight against violence towards women for 10 years, and coincide with the international day for the elimination of violence against women during the 12th round of league fixtures, we will renew our determination and commitment to combating all forms of abuse and especially towards women,” said Lega Serie A President Ezio Simonelli.

Marco Chiesara, President of WeWorld, who organise the campaign, added: “Violence against women is not a private matter and does not only concern those who suffer from it. It is a collective responsibility. With this campaign, we want to reach a wide audience, particularly men, and invite them to question their own role in combating it.”

Rummenigge says “a decision will have to be made” over his Musiala if he fails to show signs of improvement, but admitted that the former Chelsea academy product’s health issues would make a sale difficult.

Fortnite players are coming up with some incredibly inventive ways to make the Pirate Shop Access grind a little easier. Endlessly opening chests gets tedious, fast, and the community wants to scratch off as many lottery tickets as it can.

One pretty busted way of doing that was ramming an SUV into a geyser and jumping out to infinitely glide, essentially giving players flight. That might not sound useful, but you could fly into and destroy entire buildings, opening hundreds of chests in minutes.

Epic Games quickly caught wind of what players were doing and, earlier this morning, disabled geysers. Considering the next Treasure Hours is today, with boosted token odds, it's no wonder the team worked so quickly to shut this one down.

This comes just days after the Respawn Rumble exploit. For whatever reason, rewards — including the Pirate Shop Access tokens — were enabled, and it was clear this was unintentional because they popped up with greyed-out chest icons and the name, 'PH' (placeholder).

Since you would respawn infinitely in Respawn Rumble, players took advantage of this open window to rush the storm and open however many chests they could. Later that day, Epic rolled out a fix.

Don't get the RNG twisted, though. Opening 137,500 chests doesn't guarantee you a token. There's a 0.00073% chance of finding the Pirate Shop Access, and the odds reset on every single chest. The reason players are finding inventive ways to open as many chests as possible is simply to roll the dice more, but your odds are in the toilet no matter how many you open.

Still, as far as Pirate Shop Access grinds go, flying like Superman into every building, boat, and pirate cove is one of the more creative.

Fortnite players are already getting fed up hunting for Pirate Shop tokens, and are calling on Epic Games to disclose the odds.

If you haven't given up and moved onto GTA 6 yet, the next Treasure Hours window is later today at 11am PT (7pm GMT), with 1.5x boosted odds (meaning you have a 1 in 91,667 chance of finding a token, or a 0.00109% chance). You won't be able to open 500 chests in minutes with the SUV-geyser trick, but if you come back at 6pm PT (2am GMT), the odds will be doubled.

A new positioning technology could make autonomous vehicles and robotaxis more reliable at recharging themselves.

Researchers in Japan have developed a new technology that could enable electric vehicles (EVs) to charge "simply by parking".

Wireless charging is still relatively nascent in the EV market, but scientists hope that integrating more wireless charging devices into roads and parking spaces will move the EV industry away from bulky plug-in infrastructure. It uses the same wireless power transfer technology found in modern smartphones and consumer electronics: a transmitter coil inside in a ground pad generates an oscillating magnetic field, which induces an electric current in a receiver coil underneath the vehicle. This current recharges the EV's battery cells.

However, small misalignments between the two coils can affect how quickly and efficiently the battery recharges. This new system uses short-range radio signals to determine the position of the vehicle's magnetic charging coil relative to the charging device in the ground, including its distance and angle. This helps the EV and its driver get into the optimal position for efficient charging.

The technology could be particularly useful for driverless "robotaxis" and other autonomous vehicles that need to be able to recharge quickly and efficiently without human assistance, the researchers said. They detailed their findings in a study published Aug. 26 in the IEEE Open Journal of Vehicular Technology.

"We hope this research will lead to a future where EVs charge simply by parking, much like smartphones placed on wireless chargers," Ryotaro Hada, first author of the study and a master's student at the University of Osaka's Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, said in a statement.

Existing methods for aligning an EV's charging coil with a wireless charging pad use magnetic sensors or cameras. Magnetic systems work best when the coil is close to the pad, while cameras can struggle in rain, poor lighting or when dirt obscures the pad, the researchers explained.

This new system uses four ultra-wideband radio devices mounted around the vehicle's charging coil to communicate with four antennas around the charging pad.

By measuring how long radio waves take to travel between the ultra-wideband devices and the antennas, as well as detecting tiny differences in how the signals arrive at each of the vehicle's sensors, the system can pinpoint the position and angle of the EV's charging coil relative to the ground pad.

The same technology is used in some digital car keys. They use short radio pulses to detect when the driver is close by, allowing the driver to unlock their vehicle without requiring a physical key.

In laboratory experiments using a scaled-down vehicle model, the system worked out where the vehicle's charging coil was to within 0.09 inches (2.32 millimeters) of its actual position and which direction it was facing to within 0.25 degrees. Correcting for distortions in the radio signals reduced the positioning error by around fivefold, the team said.

The researchers noted that the system still needs to be tested outdoors, on full-size EVs and under real charging conditions.

For quite awhile I have firmly believed that smart glasses are the next device that will evolve the future of computing, much like the iPhone heralded the next 20 years of tech progression. And this is despite the current "pervert glasses" backlash.

I am fascinated by the sudden influx of AR and XR glasses that offer immersive environments and while also looking "normal." I've already tried Meta VR Glasses and Snap Specs, but neither are actually available for everyone yet. The Google, Qualcomm and Xreal collaboration Aura glasses are.

All three glasses could be considered VR-lite, but I think they're about finding a use between AI glasses and wearing a full headset.

In that way, the Auras are the escaped experiment worth investigating. Following the Samsung Galaxy XR headset, these are just the second device to run Google's Android XR platform natively.

This first stab at an XR headset that I think most people will want is impressive and a great example of what Android XR could be, but it also means you're the guinea pig with all that implies.

Right when the Aura launched on November 17, I put together some early impressions of the glassese. For the most part, my thoughts haven't changed. But after using the glasses more, some plusses and minuses have solidified.

I am not an advocate for VR as it currently works with bulky, isolating headsets. However, I can still be impressed virtual reality experiences.

The Xreal Aura provides that with 6DoF spatial computing. The two temple cameras and the nose bridge camera enable pinch gesture controls and you can always use the puck as trackpad, which I defaulted to more often that not.

The pinch controls work well. Occasionally the hitbox was a hair off but I didn't notice lag when I adjusted. I will note that in darker environments, it didn't always work, which isn't totally surprising but something to know.

Out of the box, the Auras come with a variety of mixed-reality experiences, from a VR tour of the Galapagos, to some games and a 360 version of Google Maps that is a delight to explore.

VR enthusiasts will find the 70-degree field of view limiting but I wasn't bothered by it. Watching movies feels like you're in a theater for yourself, and extending my desktop workspace was seamless via the wired accessory cable (or wireless for some devices).

These might be the best displays Xreal has made to date. I tested the refreshed Xreal One Pro Plus a couple of weeks ago and thought it was an improvement, and now the Aura is taking it even further.

Xreal lists the resolution of the Aura's as 1,920 x 1,200 per eye (1200p). It makes for sharp images, especially in games and media. It feels premium.

The color gamut isn't robust and the Aura doesn't offer HDR like the One Pro Plus, but if you don't need that level of display detail, these look good. Plus, with 1200 nits of brightness, the display can handle a number of settings. And the electrochromic dimming gets pretty dark, meaning you should feel more immersed.

Personally, I have never had great luck reading text as is on AR glasses, and while the Aura's are better, you may find text blurry. Fortunately, you can zoom in, which helps.

One advantage the Xreal Aura glasses have over Meta's VR Glasses and the Snap Specs is the built-in Google ecosystem. Snap and Meta have both promised that they're building up their developer connections to create more apps for their glasses, but Google's slew of in-house designs and third-party apps is already robust.

On the productivity side, you've got the entire Google suite from Docs and Drive to your calendar and Gmail. Plus, there's the Google Play Store with normal phone games and a small but growing number of XR-specific apps.

Moving forward, as Android XR grows, this will be what sets the Aura and future Android XR devices apart from Snap and Meta.

Here's the rub: you don't really need XR glasses yet. Xreal's own lineup of AR glasses are already great for productivity, gaming and watching content by connecting to your laptop, mini PC, phone or gaming device.

Because of that, the flaws do stand out a bit more.

If we're thinking of the Aura as VR-lite, especially compared to the Meta VR Glasses, then the biggest missing component for the Xreal Aura glasses is games.

Yes, I did try Demeo, which was fun, and HoloChess, which felt like the Star Wars version of chess. But from what I could tell, there aren't many native XR/VR games on the Play Store, while the Meta Quest Store has hundreds, and Meta promised at least 75 launch titles specifically designed for the VR glasses next year.

You can play 2D games on the Play Store, which I did with some games I already have on my account. Xreal is selling its own first-party controller made with GameSir, but you can use any Bluetooth controller. Again, you can connect other Xreal glasses to your phone, laptop, or gaming device with a controller as well.

It's not clear how long it'll take native Android XR experiences to catch up to Meta, but for now, Meta has the lead.

Xreal rates the battery life of the Aura puck between three and four hours. I found that to be pretty accurate. In my testing watching an hour-long YouTube video and then playing some games for about 20 minutes knocked the battery life from 100% to about 80%.

I debated putting this as a flaw because I'm not sure if you want to keep these glasses on for more than three hours. Perhaps that is the sweet spot. Still, for anything longer, you'll need to recharge.

That said, heat becomes a concern. The puck can get warm, but it does include a fan to dissipate heat. Surprisingly, the glasses can get quite warm, especially in the area that runs across your forehead.

General use seemed okay, but at one point I accidentally turned on screen recording and I could feel the glasses heating up. It never got laptop-panini levels of hot, but as the tech ages, I foresee this being a problem.

The Xreal Auras have just three cameras. Two on the temples and one on the nose bridge. As mentioned, I find the hand tracking pretty decent and pinch gestures work well.

However, I've also worn the Meta VR Glasses, which have 6 external cameras and four internal ones. Fundamentally, Xreal adding seven more cameras would change the design of the glasses, which you can see in the ski-goggle look of Meta's forthcoming platform.

Still, eye tracking makes navigating the XR space better. And compared to Meta, where you can leave your hand on the table or in your lap and still use gesture controls, the three-camera Aura setup is more limiting.

The pushback is the puck trackpad, which I used most frequently. I am comfortable with trackpads and mice, and the puck is a nice midway point.

The puck is a mini-PC with glasses attached, but that doesn't make navigation the best without accessories or the trackpad.

If Xreal's next iteration can add eye tracking or even a couple more external cameras, the already good hand tracking would be even better.

I find the Xreal Aura glasses fascinating, and when its rivals finally launch, I think a new space in the tech world will open up that is more than just pervert glasses and bulky headsets. Perhaps I've given these glasses more grace than future versions will deserve, but for a first go in this space, I'm excited by the potential.

For many, I'm not sure the $1,000+ price tag is cheap enough to dive in, but for the curious and the early adopters, this is a device of potential. It hints at what mixed reality wearables could be.

When I use the Aura, I get what it's supposed to be. It's the taste of a Vision Pro or Quest headset in a lighter, more comfortable package. Xreal has set the bar; it'll be up to everyone to see if they can raise it.

Mercury is about to receive a robotic visitor from Earth.

The BepiColombo mission — a joint effort of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) — will arrive in orbit around Mercury today (Nov. 21), more than eight years after leaving its home planet.

The big moment will come after an orbit-insertion burn, which is scheduled to begin today at 1:24 p.m. EST (1824 GMT) and last for 37 minutes.

BepiColombo consists of two main spacecraft: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (MMO), also known as Mio.

The duo launched — along with a power and propulsion element called the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) — aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana in October 2018, beginning a long and looping trip through the inner solar system.

Mercury is a difficult planet to reach because it's so close to the sun. Spacecraft approaching the solar system's innermost planet are accelerated to high velocities by the immense gravitational pull of our star and must shed a great deal of kinetic energy to be captured by Mercury's much weaker tug.

So the BepiColombo team designed a trajectory that featured one flyby of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury over the course of about seven years. That flight time was increased by a year, however, after the mission suffered an issue with its solar panels, which reduced the power available for the MTM's electric thrusters.

Today's orbital insertion is the latest milestone in BepiColombo's Mercury arrival phase, which is expected to take about six months in total.

Arrival began on Sept. 3, when the MTM released MPO and Mio. The two orbiters remain connected today; they won't separate until Dec. 9 or thereabouts.

Shortly thereafter, Mio will settle into its final orbit, a highly elliptical path that will take it over Mercury's poles. MPO is also destined for a polar orbit, though a much less elliptical one than Mio's; the ESA probe will conduct a series of engine burns before finally getting there on March 10, 2027.

BepiColombo won't start doing science work until April 6, 2027. The two probes will gather complementary data from their two different vantage points, shedding a great deal of light on a mysterious world.

"The mission will study all aspects of Mercury, from the structure and dynamics of its magnetosphere and how it interacts with the solar wind, to its internal structure with its large iron core, and the origin of the planet’s magnetic field," ESA officials wrote in a BepiColombo mission description.

"The data will enable scientists to understand more about the origin and evolution of a planet located close to its parent star, and a better understanding of the overall evolution of our solar system," they added.
