An Ancient Virus May Hold the Secret to What Makes the Human Brain Different

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Could a virus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago help explain how the human brain became different?

Researchers at Lund University have discovered that ancient viral DNA embedded in the chimpanzee genome silences a gene involved in early human brain development. Their study, published in Science Advances, found that the gene, LINC00662, remains active in human brain organoids, where it helps support the development of nerve cells and the connections between them.

The finding offers a way to explore how humans and chimpanzees developed different biological traits despite sharing much of their genetic makeup. Evolution can change how existing genes are used, as well as which genes a species carries.

“The virus we investigated specifically affected chimpanzees. Yet humans and chimpanzees have largely the same genetic makeup. That is why it is important to understand the differences in how genes are regulated. Our findings suggest that one gene, LINC00662, plays a significant role in early human brain development,” says Johan Jakobsson, professor of molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The genetic remnant belongs to PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus. Its DNA became embedded in the chimpanzee genome during an ancient infection and has been inherited ever since. The researchers found copies of the viral sequence at numerous locations throughout chimpanzee DNA.

Those sequences are systematically shut down through methylation, a chemical process that helps regulate gene activity. At the LINC00662 location, the inherited viral insertion also keeps the neighboring gene silent.

To examine this difference during brain development, the team grew brain organoids from human and chimpanzee stem cells. These miniature, three-dimensional structures resemble brain tissue and reproduce some features of its early development.

“The gene was strongly expressed in the human organoids, but not at all in the chimpanzee organoids,” says Patricia Gerdes, a postdoctoral researcher in molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The researchers then tested whether the viral sequence caused the shutdown. Using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing tool, they removed it from chimpanzee cells. LINC00662 became active again.

“This shows that it is in fact the viral insertion that is responsible for the gene being switched off in chimpanzees,” says Patricia Gerdes.

A separate experiment explored the gene’s role in humans. When the team used CRISPR to silence LINC00662 in human brain organoids, genes involved in nerve cell development and the formation of connections between those cells became less active.

For Jakobsson, the results show how an infection can leave a lasting genetic change across a population, while helping explain how gene regulation can protect the brain or contribute to damage. The team will next investigate whether other viral remnants influenced brain evolution and whether such sequences are linked to brain diseases.

InfoQ Homepage News Cloudflare Makes Basin Serverless Analytics Generally Available

Nov 10, 2026 2 min read

Cloudflare has announced the general availability of Basin, a serverless analytics platform built on Apache Iceberg and R2 Object Storage. The managed service supports open data formats and integrates with tools such as DuckDB, Snowflake, and Apache Spark, with no data egress fees.

Introduced in preview as "Cloudflare Data Platform" just over a year ago, Basin combines data ingestion and SQL-based transformation, managed table maintenance, and distributed SQL queries, allowing developers to analyze large datasets without managing infrastructure. Marc Selwan, senior product manager at Cloudflare, and Micah Wylde, principal engineer at Cloudflare, explain why eliminating egress costs is central to the design:

A large driving force in the development and adoption of Basin is the continued growth we are seeing in the open Apache Iceberg ecosystem. (...) With Basin, you can read and write your data using any Iceberg-compatible engine, including PyIceberg, DuckDB, Snowflake, and Apache Spark. That kind of data portability is only possible with free egress, which allows developers to access their data in Cloudflare from the wide variety of tools in the ecosystem, regardless of region or cloud.

The platform combines three services: Basin Pipelines, formerly Cloudflare Pipelines, ingests events from Workers, HTTP endpoints, and Logpush, applies SQL transformations, and writes data to Apache Iceberg tables or R2; Basin Catalog manages Iceberg metadata and automates table maintenance; and Basin SQL provides a serverless, distributed SQL engine for querying Iceberg tables.

Basin aims to make analytics more accessible to smaller teams by running data ingestion, preparation, and queries on Cloudflare’s global network. This reduces the need to manage server clusters or move data between services, lowering the operational overhead of building and maintaining analytics pipelines. Selwan and Wylde explain the renaming:

Why Basin? A basin is where rivers from many sources come together to a single point. We felt that Basin perfectly captures how the platform is used: Pipelines brings data into Basin Catalog while Basin SQL makes it instantly queryable.

While some practitioners describe Basin as "open analytics infrastructure without the warehouse cathedral," Ramin Muradov, lead data analyst at PashaPay , comments:

I don't think it will knock Snowflake off its spot, at least not anytime soon. Cloudflare itself says Basin isn't a replacement for every warehouse workload. But there's a big layer of the market made up of teams whose data is too small to justify dedicated infrastructure and too big to fit in Excel. They now have an option.

Cloudflare is not the only cloud provider targeting unified analytics: as separately reported on InfoQ, AWS recently acquired DuckLabs and integrated DuckDB into Aurora PostgreSQL, aiming to simplify analytics across transactional and object storage data, while Google repositioned over the years BigQuery as the "single, unified AI-ready data platform."

Since its preview, Basin has increased ingestion capacity to 3 GB/s per stream, expanded SQL capabilities, improved Iceberg table maintenance, and added Logpush integration and Terraform support. Planned features, with no committed release dates, include real-time processing capabilities, support for the latest Iceberg specification, adaptive table-maintenance, and additional controls for data sovereignty and access management.

Cloudflare's Basin tutorial explains how to ingest events with Basin Pipelines, store them in Iceberg tables managed by Basin Catalog, and query them using Basin SQL.

Expert Tips for Scouting, Scattering, and Tagging Fall Turkeys

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Fall turkey hunting requires matching your strategy to the specific flock type you're pursuing. Adult gobblers, hen-poult family groups, and second-year jakes all behave and vocalize differently. The spring yelp that pulled in a gobbler won't work in October. Identify the birds first, then dial in your calls and approach accordingly.

Turkeys typically only receive passing mentions when folks discuss their favorite fall pursuits. And with spring being a distant memory and other seasons in full swing, that’s understandable. And unfortunate. Fall turkeys offer a sporting experience unlike any other outdoors adventure. And with more than 40 states offering autumn turkey seasons—some of which open in September and others that run deep into winter—opportunities abound.

But the fall game plays out somewhat differently than the familiar spring ritual. Here’s a quick look at the types of turkeys you’ll likely encounter in the autumn woods and some tips on how to bring one home.

Many hunters kill fall turkeys incidentally while pursuing deer or small game, sometimes encountering flocks at harvested crop fields or mast-rich wooded areas. There’s nothing wrong with that, but true students of the autumn game dig far deeper into turkey behavior and learn to interact with each type of fall bird.

During fall, turkeys are in different modes than in spring, as they’re no longer concerned with breeding. Food and the struggle for survival dominate their existence. They typically fall into several distinct flock types: adult gobblers, hens without poults, and hens with broods of poults. Several subcategories also exist: Male birds in their second autumn—those that were jakes the previous spring—group together. These are sometimes called jake-and-a-halfs. Also, hen-poult groups often combine into larger family flocks, called crèches. The various groups sometimes intermingle, especially at prime feeding areas or large common roost sites. Often, however, you’ll encounter them separately.

As with spring, calling can be critical in autumn for finding turkeys and getting them close. However, turkeys—especially hens and poults—can be much more vocal in fall, and their lexicon expands to include sounds many spring hunters don’t hear much. Roost sites that attract family flocks can sound like a din of turkey chatter at dawn. Young turkeys often utter kee-kees (a series of three to four ascending whistles) and kee-kee runs, which are whistling series followed by a crude yelp or two. The vocalizations might sound slightly different depending on whether it’s uttered by a jake or jenny. Brood hens use long, pleading strings of assembly yelps to gather their offspring after being separated. Gobblers are typically less vocal in fall, communicating via slow, raspy gobbler yelps and popping clucks. Still, it’s not uncommon to hear them gobble at times.

Because the various types of turkeys act and vocalize differently in fall, general hunting approaches can fall flat. For example, the straightforward hen yelps that made a gobbler respond in April will likely be met with indifference from male turkeys in October. To boost your chances, you must know the kind of bird you’re hunting and then fine-tune your strategy.

Generally, fall hunts fall under two broad categories: 1) Hunters who call in an attempt to make turkeys come to them. 2) Hunters, or specially trained turkey dogs, who scatter groups of turkeys, then set up and try to call the birds back. Knowing the type of turkey you’re after is especially critical during scatter scenarios.

“You need to be the turkey that calls to you,” said Marlin Watkins, a noted Ohio call maker and expert fall turkey hunter. “A young gobbler is not coming to hen sounds. You need to be that bird, whether it’s a jenny hen, young gobbler, or adult hen. And if you produce those sounds, you will be far more successful.”

When his dogs scatter a flock, Watkins immediately tries to determine the group’s composition—hens, gobblers, or a family flock—as that will dictate his calling approach. “If I’ve scattered turkeys, I start with jenny hen kees and kee-kee runs, provided it’s a family flock,” he said. “As soon as first bird answers, I mimic that bird. A young gobbler is not coming to a jenny hen normally, so if a young gobbler kees and gobbler yelps, that’s what I do, even to the point of going note for note. As the bird approaches, I soften the calling, but I do not quit, or they will walk away. They can hear other birds calling that I can’t.”

Depending on the flock type, reassembling turkeys might present a shot opportunity within 20 or 30 minutes. However, adult hens and toms often take longer to get back together, so Watkins uses a different approach after flushing gangs of gobblers or jake-and-a-halfs. “We usually wait 30 minutes or more and start with gobbler yelping and clucking,” he said. “If we get no response in an hour or more, we will mix in gobbling. We start slow, only making a call or two every 10 or more minutes. After no response for that hour or more, we get a little more aggressive.”

That patient yet observant mindset can pay off, as it’s often unclear how birds will react. “I’ve broken gobblers one day and sat three hours, heard one cluck and then shot him,” Watkins said. “The very next day, I’ve broken a different flock, and they started gobbling in an hour and gobbled nonstop until we killed one. You just never know what they will do.”

When hunting alone, or in situations when you can’t scatter turkey flocks, calling strategies become more opportunistic. Brett Ladeau, a Vermont hunter and contest caller, often starts fall quests with a basic tactic. “I typically approach a fall hunt by finding roost sites and try to pattern either a family flock or mixed flock,” he said. “To locate birds, I scout much like I do in spring. I like to be close to birds while they are still on the roost, so finding roost areas is an important tactic I use to find fall birds. I scout for roosting areas and mark them on my mapping app for future hunts. If possible, I also like to watch or hear birds fly to the roost in the evening so I know where they will be in the morning.”

If a roost hunt doesn’t pan out, Ladeau tries to locate turkeys via calling, often taking a mobile approach. “I use my spring technique of riding from spot to spot where I know there are birds, and once I find a few spots that hold birds, I will cold-call at a few well-known turkey haunts,” he said.

And as with hunting scattered groups, Ladeau tries to match his calling to the vocalizations of responsive birds. “If I’m hunting a family group or mixed group, I will use the kee-kee a lot, along with assembly yelps and other calls,” he said. “If I’m hunting a mixed flock or a group of male birds, I will gobble- and jake-yelp to try to entice them into range. My setup is to try to get as close to the birds as possible and then start my calling sequence. If I’m close to a roost, I might not call much, especially if I know their routine, but if not, I call pretty aggressively.”

Get to know the flocks of autumn this year, and fine-tune your calling to bring them into shooting range. Ducks and big bucks can wait a bit as you enjoy the sights and sounds of the fall turkey woods.

House votes to effectively end the impeachment effort against Pete Hegseth

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth takes questions from reporters during a news conference at the Pentagon on Nov. 10, 2026. Chip Somodevilla/Getty Images hide caption

The House has voted 211 to 204 to refer the articles of impeachment filed against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to committee, effectively killing the impeachment effort spearheaded by Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky.

Tuesday's vote short-circuits any potential floor vote on the articles of impeachment, and virtually guarantees the measure will not be taken up before the current session of Congress expires.

The vote provided a surprise reprieve for President Trump's embattled Defense Secretary, who was facing an impeachment vote by the full House as early as Wednesday.

Massie reintroduced the articles of impeachment on Monday, accusing Hegseth of high crimes and misdemeanors in connection to the war with Iran and U.S. strikes against Iran and on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean Sea and Eastern Pacific Ocean.

Massie's impeachment resolution sought to oust Hegseth over the capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro without congressional authorization, and the effort to punish Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Ariz., for a video telling service members they can refuse illegal orders.

Hegseth defended himself against the impeachment effort during a rare press conference at the Pentagon on Tuesday, criticizing Massie as a "disgruntled and defeated congressman."

"I stand behind every one of these operations," Hegseth said. "I was proud to execute them, and I would do it again tomorrow."

On Monday, Trump expressed support for Hegseth during a wide-ranging, impromptu press briefing at the White House.

"I'll stand by him," Trump said. "He won two wars. He was in charge. We all won."

The impeachment measure was Massie's second attempt against Hegseth. He first sought to bring the impeachment resolution to the floor before the midterm elections, prompting Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., to call an early recess to avoid the vote. That caused the resolution to expire, which is why Massie reintroduced the resolution again this week.

While the impeachment push may be quelled for now, Hegseth's troubles on Capitol Hill are not over. Democrats have promised vigorous investigations into Hegseth's Pentagon once they assume the majority in January.

Whether they will mount another impeachment effort remains unclear, but in an interview with NPR last week, Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to be the next Speaker of the House, said simply of Hegseth, "He's got to go."
