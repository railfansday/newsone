FAA investigators zeroed in on a routine network upgrade at a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, where a copper wire line was being upgraded to fiber optic cable by the contractor L3Harris. A new router that came online during the upgrade may have overwhelmed the older equipment, investigators say.

“Either through contractor error or a faulty piece of equipment, that router that was being replaced as we switched from copper to fiber, it started flooding other routers in our air traffic control system,” Duffy said in an interview Sunday.

This sent a barrage of data through the FAA’s internal communication system — about 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds – that air traffic controllers use to help guide pilots through the country’s airspace. When that new router was turned on, agency officials noticed the same issues at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, which had undergone similar work weeks before, Duffy said. These industrial-grade devices, called switches, are much larger than home Wi-Fi routers and built to withstand harsh conditions. While the agency pointed to the single router in Arkansas as the cause of Friday’s issues, it says it disabled 60 similar units as a precaution.

FAA Administrator Brian Bedford said Saturday that the agency is in the process of replacing around 2,000 Cisco routers with 600 Juniper routers. Could any one of those routine equipment upgrades suddenly overload the FAA’s communications system?

“What surprised me most is the redundancy part for this type of national infrastructure,” Ilia Rabinovich, vice president at the cybersecurity firm Sygnia, told CNET. “This is one of the first things we discuss with clients in terms of preparedness – having backups, having redundancy connectivity, having different connections that you can migrate to different places.”

“What they did starting in September at Baltimore-Washington is at a very low level, for about 60 days. On Oct. 13, more than 25 lawmakers sent a letter to the FAA asking it to pause the rollout of the tool, citing a lack of transparency and training for air traffic controllers.

“We have heard from several sources that frontline aviation safety workers were notified of the SMART tool rollout mere days in advance or not at all, giving them no time to learn how the system works, how it is incorporated into their workflow, and what oversight and accountability mechanisms maintain aviation safety standards,” the letter says.

Unlocking Stable On-Policy RL on TPU with Bitwise-Zero Train–Inference Mismatch

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models is notoriously sensitive to numerical instability: even tiny discrepancies between rollout generation and gradient updates can destabilize training or stall convergence. In LLM RL, the model serves as the policy, π(action | previous tokens), and true on-policy learning requires every action in an update to be generated and evaluated by that exact same policy. While a synchronous loop ensures each rollout batch is scored using the same weights that generated it, a critical numerical requirement remains: the sampler and trainer must evaluate that policy identically.

In practice, production RL stacks run sampling and training through separate, specialized execution paths. The sampler chooses each action token during incremental decoding and records its behavior logprob. The trainer evaluates the same token in batched forward-backward passes to compute its current logprob and gradients. Under the same weights, these differing execution paths can still assign different logprobs to the same token and context. The training schedule is on-policy, but the numerical execution has introduced a hidden off-policy gap that compounds over multi-turn trajectories.

Our zero train–inference mismatch (Zero-TIM) implementation, developed in Tunix and verified at multi-host scale across 64 TPU v5p chips (DP8×TP8), closes this numerical gap. At the same weights, the sampler and trainer assign bitwise-identical logprobs to the same action and token history—bringing state-of-the-art, true on-policy stability to TPU RL training.

A reduction is a tree of floating-point additions. Floating-point addition is not associative, so changing the tree changes where rounding occurs. The effect is easier to expose in bfloat16, which retains less precision than float32.

The same issue appears in model reductions: matrix products accumulate partial sums, normalization computes statistics, and tensor-parallel shards combine partial outputs. In our original stack, sampler decode and trainer forward used different token-array shapes and forward implementations. JAX specializes computations to their input shapes, and the training forward also runs inside a differentiated program.

Across these paths, reduction order and intermediate precision were not consistently aligned. Floating-point rounding turned those arithmetic differences into different logprob bits. The weights and model equations were identical; the arithmetic was not.

The contract is simple: across sampler decode, serving prefill, and trainer forward, all three paths must produce bitwise-identical logprobs for every sampled action—comparing raw floating-point bytes under the same rollout weights, token history, and sampling settings, with zero rounding or tolerance.

Why three paths instead of two? In a decoupled RL stack, a direct sampler–trainer disagreement shows that a numerical gap exists, but not where it enters. Introducing serving prefill as a middle checkpoint turns alignment into a systematic two-step diagnostic that any RL stack can adopt:

Sampler decode vs. prefill verifies that the serving engine agrees with itself between incremental token generation and full-sequence re-scoring, while prefill vs. trainer verifies that the serving and training engines agree across compilation and differentiation boundaries.

Achieving Zero-TIM does not require merging the sampler and trainer into a single monolithic engine. Instead, we use a generalizable four-part recipe that bridges separate serving and training pipelines across multi-host TPU meshes: locking within-shard kernel accumulation, ordering cross-shard reductions, standardizing the logprob scoring tail, and wrapping serving kernels with custom differentiation rules.

Inside each tensor-parallel rank, batch-invariant Pallas kernels control accumulation order and intermediate precision. Projection kernels traverse fixed contraction blocks in order, keeping the reduction blocking independent of token-row count. Partial products accumulate in float32, with explicit output casts.

The next tile continues from the float32 accumulator, not the cast output. This construction covers the attention and MLP projections and the LM head. Dedicated kernels fix RMSNorm reductions and SwiGLU’s intermediate casts. The existing Pallas paged-attention forward is retained.

For row-parallel projections, each tensor-parallel rank produces a partial output. We separate communication from addition: ppermute transports the partials, then every rank adds them in the same source-rank order.

The reduction order is explicit in the program rather than selected by a collective implementation.

Alignment must extend beyond model logits to the probability of the sampled action. Decode, prefill, and trainer apply matching sampling transforms, use the same fixed Pallas log-softmax construction, and select the recorded action token.

Prefill and trainer score the action generated during rollout rather than choosing another action. We also preserve sampled token IDs across environment turns so that every path evaluates the same token history.

The trainer reuses the serving prefill computation. Custom vector–Jacobian products retain the serving kernels for forward evaluation and provide their backward rules. The matmul example shows this separation:

The differentiable replica mirrors the kernel’s accumulation and casts, but is used only to construct the pullback. Forward values still come from the serving Pallas kernel.

Training places that forward inside a differentiated JAX program. We pass --xla_allow_excess_precision=false through XLA_FLAGS to the compiler process, constraining excess intermediate precision in the operations surrounding the Pallas kernels.

Long trajectories are evaluated in fixed-size chunks, carrying the paged KV cache forward. All action logprobs feed the complete train-batch RL loss. The reverse pass propagates gradients through those cache connections and combines parameter-gradient contributions before the optimizer update.

Across the AI systems community, eliminating train–inference mismatch has rapidly become a state-of-the-art requirement for stabilizing large-scale RL convergence. Recent open-source efforts—including vLLM with TorchTitan and RadixArk/LMSys Miles—have demonstrated how batch-invariant kernels and bitwise numerical alignment prevent off-policy drift during policy optimization.

Our work delivers an open-source, modular JAX/XLA and Pallas implementation of this contract for TPU in Tunix. Verified across a multi-host 64 TPU v5p (DP8×TP8) mesh, these kernel and collective patterns provide a reusable blueprint that teams can adopt to achieve bit-exact on-policy RL across distributed JAX/TPU stacks.

Agents are sessions, not requests: size GKE node pools for AI agent sessions

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your company wants to move its coding agents from developer laptops to a shared GKE cluster, for persistence, scale, and governance. Each of those agent sessions stays open all day but works only part of it, and how you run them decides how much of your node bill pays for waiting.

By the end of this article, you will know why an agent session needs a different runtime than a web request, how Agent Substrate and AX run sessions on GKE, and how to identify cost optimization opportunities in your GKE node pools. This article assumes you know Kubernetes Pods and node pools. If not, check out the GKE overview.

A web request lasts milliseconds. The server answers it, keeps nothing, and serves the next one, so Kubernetes scales a web app by its traffic: more requests, more Pods.

An agent session works another way. A coding agent's workspace can stay open for the developer's whole workday: it keeps its memory and its files between prompts, and it works only after a prompt arrives.

How much of that day does the agent really work? Much less than it waits. In a study of GitHub Copilot agent traces, Banruo Liu and colleagues found that in a typical session with several prompts, the agent waits for the user about 80% of the time. A turn, one prompt and the agent's whole response to it, takes 396.3 seconds of agent work on average.

Put those numbers on one developer's day. With 2 prompts an hour over an 8 hour workday, which is our assumption, the agent works 22% of the day and waits for the other 78%. If each session holds a machine, you pay for that 78%.

Teams usually pick one of two answers, and both work well for web apps.

The first is to give each session its own Pod. The session keeps its state, but its Pod holds 1 vCPU and 1 GiB all day. For example, a c3-standard-4 node fits 3 such Pods after GKE's reservations, so in our cost model 100 coding agents need 34 nodes and about USD 1,288 a month, for agents that work 22% of the time.

The second is to scale the Pod to zero between prompts. The node is free, and files on a persistent volume survive, but the process and its memory do not, so the next prompt starts a new agent process.

Based on that, you want a third option: keep the session, and free the machine while the session waits.

Agent Substrate and AX are built for that: the developer's session stays open, and the sandbox that serves it gives its machine back while the session waits. Together they cover the parts of an agentic platform that are the same for every team: the control plane, the runtime, and the sandbox. Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. AX answers what the agent is.

Following the CNCF donation of gVisor, Agent Substrate, and OpenRL in November 2026, you have the flexibility to run this open source, agentic platform across any environment. Agent Substrate 1.0 is generally available on GKE.

One distinction helps from here on. The session is the multi turn interaction between the developer and the agent, with its conversation history. The compute that serves it is an isolated sandbox: an actor in Agent Substrate, or a Task in AX. Parking acts on the sandbox, and the session stays open.

Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. It separates the stateful agent from the compute it runs on. An actor is the sandbox for one agent, and it holds the agent's running processes, files, and identity. A worker is a reusable sandbox drawn from a warm pool of Kubernetes Pods, and one worker hosts several actors.

An agent runs code that a model wrote, so anything in its environment can leak through a prompt injection or a bad command, API keys included. Gateways control the actor's inbound and outbound traffic and add credentials on the way out, based on the actor's identity, so the agent never holds the keys.

SuspendActor snapshots an idle actor to object storage and frees its worker, and the next request restores the actor on any warm worker. Using this approach, we can achieve higher density by multiplexing more agents to run on the same amount of compute.

AX answers what the agent is and what it knows. You declare a Task, which says what runs (image, command, and limits), and a Workspace, which says what the agent knows (repositories and files). AX runs each Task as an actor. ax suspend parks the task and keeps its /workspace. Today AX asks for a volume snapshot, so the files come back on resume and the agent's processes start fresh (runner guide).

AX passes suspend, resume, and delete to Agent Substrate. The AX roadmap adds budgets, policies, and gateways, which limit what an agent may do, and a record of what each task did.

Two details decide the bill before you size anything.

First, you decide when a sandbox parks. Agent Substrate does not suspend an idle actor by itself (#424), so your agent harness, or the service in front of it, calls the suspend API after an idle timeout. Until then, an idle actor keeps its worker. When idle actors hold every worker, a request for another actor waits up to 5 seconds by default, then fails with HTTP 503 (Agent Substrate docs).

Second, you decide how much CPU each sandbox is promised. An actor is sized by its CPU and memory limits, and each worker books those limits against the capacity you declare for its pool (API guide). Our cost model sets that capacity to what the node really has, so every actor keeps its full limit. You can declare more capacity than the node has, and then you overcommit CPU at the pool instead of in each Pod.

Our cost model prices parking for the same 100 coding agents, at Google Cloud list prices in us-central1.

With a 2 minute idle timeout, each session holds a worker 29% of its open time. A warm pool sized for bursts (the 99th percentile of sessions holding a worker at once, if sessions act independently, plus 15% headroom) needs 15 nodes. Add one node that runs around the clock for the Agent Substrate control plane and its PostgreSQL database, and a job that scales the pool to zero after hours:

Parking saves 40% of the node bill. The pool is full about once in 3,500 turns, so a prompt rarely meets that 503. In an hour at 2.5 prompts instead of 2, the same 15 nodes are full for 3% of turns, so size the pool for your busiest hour.

Keep in mind that parking pays most when your agents wait the most. At 2 prompts an hour, the saving is 40%. At 4 prompts an hour, the agent works 44% of the hour, the pool grows to 27 nodes, and the saving is 4%. Around 4.5 prompts an hour, one Pod per session becomes the cheaper option, so measure your prompt rate first.

Also, the pool size is yours to set. The autoscaling option in the GKE install guide follows worker Pods, not suspended actors, so scale the pool to zero after hours with a scheduled job: a pool that runs all 730 hours of the month costs about twice the one Pod per session bill.

Finally, the fleet size matters. The Agent Substrate control plane costs the same for 10 sessions or 1,000, so parking pays from about 37 coding sessions, or 13 for an assumed chat assistant that works about a minute an hour.

Overcommit changes the comparison. If your Pods request less CPU than their limit, Kubernetes places them by their requests. A Pod that requests 0.25 vCPU with a 1 vCPU limit packs 12 to a node, so the same 100 coding agents fit on 9 nodes, fewer than our parked pool. Agent Substrate can overcommit too, through the capacity you declare on the worker pool, and our cost model does not price that case.

Overcommit has the same cost on both sides: agents share CPU and slow down when their neighbors are busy, during builds and tests. Compare like with like before you decide to park: Pods and actors with the same CPU guarantee.

Back to the shared cluster. Agents are sessions, not requests: size the warm pool for the sessions that hold a worker, not the sessions that are open.

We would park long lived sessions that wait most of the hour, and keep one Pod per session when developers prompt more than about 4 times an hour. Size the warm pool for working time plus a short idle timeout at your busiest hour, and compare with your current Pods at the same CPU guarantee before you park.

Reminder Backpressure: Scheduling Email, SMS, and Push Through Rate-Limited Workers

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A SaaS user reminder scheduling API should store mutable property-management intent in a due table, then feed email, SMS, and push jobs into a rate-limited queue; cron alone can wake work, but it cannot prove that a tenant received anything.

Short answer: for user reminders sent through a rate-limited pool, keep future intent in a due table, claim it with a short lease, and enqueue only ready work. Let workers deliver email, SMS, or push with an idempotency key. This gives the best delivery evidence without pretending that cron or a queue can prove receipt.

The recommendation is deliberately boring. Use the database for mutable calendar intent and a queue for backpressure. A lease makes crashed claims recoverable; a stable delivery key makes repeats harmless at the application boundary. Then record every attempt separately from the reminder itself.

Start by naming the guarantees. A reminder API accepts intent. A scheduler decides when that intent becomes eligible. A queue moves eligible work. A channel adapter attempts delivery. Those are four different promises, and folding them into one scheduled flag makes operations look cleaner than they are.

For a property manager, a lease-expiry notice may be edited after a tenant renews, while a maintenance-window alert may fan out to hundreds of residents. The former needs cheap cancellation and a clear current state. The latter needs controlled throughput. A future row handles edits well; a ready queue absorbs the burst without asking the database sweeper to wait behind slow channel calls.

Use at-least-once processing as the working assumption. A worker can finish the external call and lose its acknowledgement, so the same job may return. Exactly-once delivery is not a useful API promise here — the scheduler does not control the channel provider or the recipient's device. What it can control is a stable key such as propertyId:reminderId:channel:revision, plus an atomic record showing whether that revision has already been accepted for delivery.

This distinction is small on a diagram and huge at 09:00, when every building's daily reminder becomes due. Don't make the scheduler call providers directly. It should claim, enqueue, and get out.

The state machine needs more precision than pending and sent. I use names such as scheduled, claimed, ready, attempting, accepted, retryable, and terminal. Accepted means the channel adapter accepted the request; it does not mean a human read it. Keep provider receipts or later delivery events as separate evidence.

A claim should carry a lease token and expiry. The dispatcher selects due rows, changes them from scheduled to claimed in the same database transaction, and commits before publishing ready jobs. After publication it records ready. If it stops between commit and publish, a reconciler can reclaim expired leases. If it publishes and stops before recording ready, the queue may contain a duplicate. The delivery key handles that branch. This is why the hybrid has two recovery mechanisms: leases repair missing queue messages, while idempotency absorbs repeated ones. Neither substitutes for the other. Dead-letter queues become useful after a message crosses the retry threshold: they isolate repeatedly failing work for inspection or redrive rather than cycling it forever, but they are not an archive and they don't explain the failure by themselves. Store structured attempt data — reminder ID, revision, channel, attempt number, eligibility time, claim token, adapter outcome, and correlation ID — while keeping message content and tenant contact data out of logs.

One sharp edge: preserve ordering only where the product requires it. A cancellation or corrected notice must not be overtaken by an older revision. Unrelated tenants do not need a global order, which would turn one slow job into a building-wide traffic jam. Compare revisions at delivery time and discard superseded work as a successful no-op.

The clock needs an explicit policy too. Store an absolute instant for dispatch and retain the user's time zone plus the rule that produced it. Recalculate future rows when the user edits that rule. Cron is good at recurring activation, but its traditional schedule describes recurring wall-clock times; it is not a per-recipient delivery ledger.

Keep the scheduling API small. Config bloat usually starts when transport settings, retry policy, tenant time zones, and provider credentials all leak into one request. The caller should submit reminder intent. Operations policy belongs behind the boundary.

The values 200 and 30_000 are sample controls, not universal defaults. Benchmark them against the database, queue, and strictest channel allowance. I care about four measurements: claim latency, oldest-ready-job age, attempts per accepted delivery, and terminal outcomes by channel. Throughput alone can look healthy while one tenant's reminder sits behind a hot property.

Workers should take capacity from a channel-specific limiter before calling the adapter. A shared pool with separate email, SMS, and push budgets can use spare concurrency without letting a burst on one channel consume every slot. On a retryable outcome, add jittered delay and preserve the same delivery key. On a terminal outcome, record it once and stop. The exact classification depends on the adapter contract; I'm not sure a generic library can choose it correctly without application-specific policy. Tests should pin that contract.

Test the ugly boundaries with a fake clock and deterministic adapters: a crash after claim, a crash after publish, two dispatchers racing for the same row, a revision change while a job waits, exhausted channel capacity, and redrive from the dead-letter queue. Run a load test with a deliberately tiny allowance — for example, five concurrent sends — and check oldest-job age, not just average throughput. Fast averages hide starvation.

Stick with a cron sweep when the reminder set is small, a few minutes of timing drift is acceptable, and the team already operates a transactional database. It has the shortest time-to-first-call and the least infrastructure. The catch is that scanning, claiming, retrying, and deduplicating are still your job; cron only starts the sweep.

Choose delayed messages as the source of truth when reminders are short-lived, rarely edited, and the broker's supported delay and retention behavior match the product requirement. This can remove the dispatcher and its due-row index. It is not suitable when users frequently reschedule far-future reminders or when product support needs a simple query for every pending notice. Your mileage may vary because broker semantics differ; verify maximum delay, retention, redrive, ordering, and cancellation before committing.

The hybrid is also a poor fit for a tiny service with one daily digest and no burst problem. Two stages mean two dashboards, a reconciler, and more deployment surface. Start with the cron sweep there. Move to the hybrid when worker saturation, edit volume, or recovery evidence becomes a measured problem.

No option makes multi-channel delivery exactly once. Pick the smallest design that keeps intent recoverable, constrains the worker pool, and leaves evidence an operator can trust.
