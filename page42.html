Multi-Turn Agentic Reinforcement Learning with Tunix and Gemma 4 on Google TPUs

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In our previous posts - Easy FunctionGemma finetuning with Tunix on Google TPUs and Autonomous LLM post-training with Tunix on TPUs - we showed how Google Tunix streamlines Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and single-turn Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on Cloud TPUs. Real-world AI agents, however, rarely finish their job in a single turn. They have to reason over partial observations, invoke tools, observe environment feedback, and adapt their strategy across multiple steps. So in this post, we will walk through how to train a multi-turn, tool-using LLM agent using Tunix's agentic reinforcement learning pipeline on Cloud TPU v6e-8. You can find the complete code in this GitHub repository.

We will revisit the Plane Strike board game from an earlier TensorFlow/JAX reinforcement learning blog, replacing the custom MLP policy with Gemma 4 E2B in thinking mode on a single Cloud TPU v6e-8 host. Plane Strike is played on a 6x6 grid (A1 to F6) where one 8-cell plane is hidden in one of 4 orientations. Each turn, the agent sees the result of its previous strike and the raw visible board (. untried, X hit, O miss), thinks through candidate placements, and calls the strike(cell="...") tool to strike one cell. The game ends when all 8 plane cells are hit, or after 23 strikes.

GPS, Timezone and IP Are Three Different Things: Testing Geo Behaviour on Real Phones

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 19 • Originally published at robotactions.com

Short answer: you set your test phone's GPS to Paris, and your checkout still shows prices in Canadian dollars. That is because GPS is only one of the signals that say where a phone is. The server it talks to reads its IP address. The app reads its clock. The app store reads the account. If you want to see what a user in Paris sees, those signals have to agree on one city, and each one is set in a different place. This post goes through each signal, what it controls, how to set it on a real Android phone or iPhone, and how to check that nothing leaked.

Two more signals are worth knowing because they cannot be changed by any of the above:

Most "it works in testing but not for users in Brazil" bugs come from testing one signal and assuming the others followed.

GPS and API responses can be faked from inside your own test setup: you mock the location, or you intercept the response from your geo-lookup service and return a different country. That covers anything your app decides.

It does not cover decisions made before your code runs:

To test those, the traffic has to actually leave from that country. That means a proxy.

Use this whether you test on your own phones or in a device cloud.

In both cases the proxy applies to that Wi-Fi network only. Mobile data still goes out directly.

If your provider gives you a SOCKS5 proxy, prefer the socks5h:// form where your tool accepts it. With socks5h, DNS lookups are done by the proxy, in the target country. With plain socks5, the phone or your tool resolves hostnames locally first, which can send it to a CDN node near you, not near the exit IP, and quietly undo the test.

A phone's proxy setting is a request, not a rule. Apps and system services that open their own connections can ignore it:

So "the browser shows a New York IP" is not proof that your app's traffic left from New York. If the behaviour you are testing comes from one of these paths, check that path specifically: watch the connections the phone opens while you use the app, and look for any that went straight to the internet.

RobotActions runs real Android and iOS devices in our cloud, and we added proxy routing for this kind of test. You bring your own HTTP(S) or SOCKS5 proxy from any provider and pay them directly. We do not sell proxies. Paste it once in Settings, Proxies (in any of the usual formats, including a provider's host:port:user:pass line or a whole curl -x command), click Test to see the exit IP and city, and save it. The password is stored encrypted and never shown again. On a device, Location & Network applies it, checks it before the phone changes, and can move the GPS and timezone to the proxy's exit city in one click. On Android the exit IP is also read back from inside the phone. Releasing the device removes the proxy and puts GPS and timezone back. Proxy routing is on the paid plans.

AI agents get the same thing as MCP tools:

On iPhone, fullDevice: true is opt-in and sends every app and system service through your proxy, not just the ones that honour the proxy setting. While it is on, UDP traffic such as calls and some games stops. ios_proxy_leak_check watches for 5 to 60 seconds while you use the app and lists the connections that went around the proxy. Both proxy and timezone are cleared automatically when the device is released.

Your Card Game's Turns Need a State Machine, Not Callbacks

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I built a small deckbuilder for a devchallenge jam, and like every first-time card game dev, I started with the thing that feels natural in Godot: signals and callbacks. card_played fires, which triggers on_card_played() on three other systems, one of which emits mana_spent, which triggers a UI update and also checks win conditions. It worked great for about two weeks. Then I added triggered abilities ("whenever a creature dies, draw a card") and the whole thing turned into a debugging nightmare where I genuinely could not tell you what order things happened in.

This post is about the rewrite: modeling turn structure, phases, and triggered abilities as an explicit finite-state machine (FSM), and why that unlocked undo/replay almost for free.

The core issue with signal-driven turn logic isn't that signals are bad — it's that turn order becomes implicit and distributed. Nobody owns "what happens next." Every system that connects to card_played is independently deciding when it's allowed to react, and Godot's signal dispatch order depends on connection order, which depends on scene tree instantiation order, which depends on... nothing you want to be debugging at 2am.

Concretely, this breaks down in three ways once your game grows past trivial complexity:

The fix is to stop treating turn progression as "things that happen when events fire" and start treating it as a graph of named states with explicit, enumerable transitions.

I modeled the turn as a strict phase sequence, with each phase being a distinct state object rather than a flag:

gdscript enum Phase { UNTAP, UPKEEP, DRAW, MAIN, COMBAT, MAIN_2, END, CLEANUP, }

class_name TurnStateMachine extends RefCounted

var current_phase: Phase = Phase.UNTAP var transition_table := { Phase.UNTAP: Phase.UPKEEP, Phase.UPKEEP: Phase.DRAW, Phase.DRAW: Phase.MAIN, Phase.MAIN: Phase.COMBAT, Phase.COMBAT: Phase.MAIN_2, Phase.MAIN_2: Phase.END, Phase.END: Phase.CLEANUP, Phase.CLEANUP: Phase.UNTAP, }

signal phase_entered(phase: Phase) signal phase_exited(phase: Phase)

func advance() -> void: phase_exited.emit(current_phase) current_phase = transition_table[current_phase] phase_entered.emit(current_phase)

This alone doesn't look that different from what people already do. The real difference is the rule I enforced everywhere else in the codebase: nothing is allowed to mutate game state outside of a phase transition or an explicit action resolution. No system is allowed to "just react" whenever it feels like it. If something wants to happen, it has to go through the machine.

The thing that actually broke my original signal-based design was triggered abilities. "Whenever a creature dies, draw a card" sounds simple until three creatures die simultaneously from one board wipe, and two of those deaths also trigger abilities that kill other creatures.

The fix: triggers don't execute immediately. They get pushed onto a resolution queue, and the state machine has a dedicated RESOLVING_TRIGGERS state that drains the queue one at a time, re-checking for new triggers after each resolution (this mirrors how Magic: The Gathering's actual rules engine handles the stack, and for good reason — it's a solved problem).

gdscript var trigger_queue: Array[TriggeredAbility] = []

func queue_trigger(ability: TriggeredAbility) -> void: trigger_queue.append(ability) if current_phase != Phase.RESOLVING_TRIGGERS: _enter_resolution_state()

func _enter_resolution_state() -> void: var previous_phase := current_phase current_phase = Phase.RESOLVING_TRIGGERS while not trigger_queue.is_empty(): var ability := trigger_queue.pop_front() ability.resolve() # resolving may queue MORE triggers — loop keeps draining current_phase = previous_phase

This single change killed an entire category of bugs. Because resolution is a loop inside one state rather than a chain of emitted signals, there's no ambiguity about ordering, and I can log the queue at any point to see exactly what's pending.

Once every state mutation happens through a small set of commands rather than ad-hoc function calls, undo and replay stop being special features and become a natural consequence of the architecture. I used a lightweight command pattern where every action produces a reversible record:

gdscript class_name GameCommand extends RefCounted

func apply(state: GameState) -> void: push_error("override me")

func revert(state: GameState) -> void: push_error("override me")

class DrawCardCommand extends GameCommand: var player_id: int var drawn_card_id: String

The turn engine keeps a flat history list. "Undo" is just revert() on the last command. Replay for the jam's leaderboard verification is just replaying the command list from an empty GameState and checking the hash at the end matches. I didn't design the command log for replay specifically — it fell out of needing undo during the "take back a misclick" phase of playtesting, and the replay feature was maybe 40 lines of glue on top.

To be fair about the cost: this architecture is more boilerplate upfront. Every single action — drawing a card, dealing damage, tapping a permanent — needs its own command class instead of a quick one-liner in a signal handler. For a jam with a 72-hour timer, that felt expensive on day one.

It also makes animation sequencing trickier, because visual feedback wants to happen during state changes, not strictly before or after. I ended up having commands emit a visual_hint payload that the presentation layer consumes asynchronously, decoupled from the actual state mutation timing — the state machine doesn't wait on animations, ever.

And if your game genuinely has no triggered abilities, no undo requirement, and a fixed linear turn order, you may not need any of this. Signals are fine for a tic-tac-toe clone. The complexity is earned, not default.

If you've shipped a card or board game with any kind of "stack" of triggered effects — how did you handle simultaneous triggers and ordering? Did you go full FSM, or find a lighter-weight trick that worked for your scope? I'd genuinely like to know what breaks first in other people's designs, because mine was ordering, and I suspect that's not universal.

This guide adds web search to OpenClaw with the AgentSearch provider plugin, @agentsearchhq/openclaw-web-search. It works right away with no API key, no signup and no wallet: up to 25 searches a day per IP. If you need more, give it a small wallet and each search costs $0.003 in USDC on Base over x402, with no daily cap.

Running Multiple Trials per Task for AI Agent Evaluation

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Once an agent completes a task with your product, the next question is how consistently it can do it again. Even with the same instructions, tools, and documentation, it may take a different route, spend longer on the work, or get stuck on something it previously completed easily.

When developers rely on agents to use your product, unpredictable behavior can interrupt their work and make the experience harder to trust. Repeated trials help you find where the workflow breaks down and test whether clearer documentation, better tools, or more useful error messages help them complete tasks more consistently.

Running the same task multiple times helps you measure how consistently an agent succeeds and uncover failures a single run might miss. Each execution, or trial, uses a defined agent configuration and starts independently, with an empty environment and no conversation history or intermediate work carried over from another trial. When comparing product versions, this repetition also helps you assess whether a change improved performance or the results simply varied between runs.

Agent behavior can vary even under apparently deterministic settings. In the 2026 study On Randomness in Agentic Evals, researchers collected 60,000 coding-agent trajectories on SWE-bench Verified. Across ten complete evaluation runs of each configuration, scores varied by 2.2 to 6 percentage points from the lowest to the highest run. Variation persisted even when the temperature of the underlying LLMs was set to zero. For engineers depending on agents to get work done, those inconsistencies can become a recurring source of friction.

Multiple trials also give you more behavior to inspect. A task that passes four times and fails once offers a useful opportunity to compare approaches. Did the agent find different documentation? Choose another tool? Misinterpret a response? Reviewing those differences can point toward changes you can test.

The ranges below offer a starting point for choosing trials per task. You’ll generally need more trials when results change from run to run, you’re looking for a small improvement, or you’re trying to catch a failure that rarely happens.

Suggested Trial Counts by Evaluation Goal

Even major AI labs use different trial counts. OpenAI averaged four attempts per task on SWE-bench Verified, while Anthropic averaged 25 trials. For your evaluation, the useful count depends on how much the results vary and how small an improvement you want to detect. Research on coding agents shows that smaller improvements can require substantially more repetition. Start with a pilot to understand that variability, then use it to plan the trials your comparison needs.

The smaller the improvement you want to detect, the more trials you’ll generally need to distinguish it from normal variation. For example, this coding-agent study estimated that, at its median observed variability, detecting a two-percentage-point improvement would require approximately nine complete benchmark runs per system. Detecting one point would require 36. Those estimates assumed 80% statistical power and a 5% significance threshold, with 500 tasks in each complete run.

There are three important metrics worth understanding when evaluating agents: pass@1, pass@k, and pass^k. The first two grew out of code generation research, where tests could check whether a system produced a working solution in one attempt or among several candidates. OpenAI’s 2021 HumanEval paper refined how to estimate these probabilities.

As evaluations expanded to agents handling everyday workflows, consistency became another important question. The 2024 τ-bench paper introduced pass^k to measure the probability that all k independent trials succeed, averaged across tasks. Together, these metrics describe success on a single attempt, success within multiple attempts, and consistency across repeated runs.

Results from the 2024 τ-bench retail evaluation show why it helps to measure success across repeated trials. GPT-4o’s function-calling agent achieved 61.2% average success on a single attempt, measured by pass@1. Its pass^8 score fell below 25%, meaning that, averaged across tasks, the probability of succeeding in all eight independent trials was less than 25%.

Agent evaluations take time and compute, so choosing between more tasks and more trials comes down to what you need to learn. Add tasks when important workflows or conditions are missing. Run more trials when you need stronger evidence about how consistently agents handle the cases you already cover.

Choosing between more tasks and more trials

Repeated trials help you understand how consistently an agent handles a task, but the benefit of repetition has limits. Once you have a clearer picture of that behavior, testing more representative tasks, rather than more trials of a smaller set of tasks, may tell you more about performance across your product. Use your initial results to decide where uncertainty remains. If you’re trying to detect a small improvement, you may need more tasks, more trials, or both.

Use repeated trials to assess how reliably agents complete the workflows your product supports. Inspect outcomes and traces, identify where behavior varies, and test whether changes to tools, documentation, or error messages address those problems. Balance repetition with representative task coverage so your results support conclusions about both individual workflows and the broader product experience.

Em gets quizzed about his classic hits by his alter ego.

Eminem joined forces with Spotify to celebrate the Detroit legend becoming part of the streaming giant’s Billions Club with an episode from the popular series, which arrived on Wednesday (Nov. 18).

In typical Em fashion, he put his own creative spin on the visual, drawing inspiration from his animated The Slim Shady Show from 2000.

In the episode, Eminem appears at the Spotify office surrounded by Billions Club accolades, and then he’s fittingly served a dish of his mom’s spaghetti in the “Lose Yourself” plaque.

The “Not Afraid” rapper gets annoyed when sauce spills on his new Air Jordan 4s, and then an old friend taking on his Slim Shady persona greets him from the center of the pasta dish.

Em is then pulled into an animated world and takes a trip down memory lane while getting quizzed on his classic hits that have joined the Billions Club over the years, including “Not Afraid,” “Stan,” “Lose Yourself,” “Love the Way You Lie” with Rihanna, “Godzilla” featuring Juice WRLD, “Mockingbird,” “Superman,” “Till I Collapse,” “Rap God” and Akon’s “Smack That.”

The episode closes with Em returning to the real world and threatening to kill his Slim Shady alter ego, who’s hiding out in the spaghetti, a callback to his 2024 album, The Death of Slim Shady (Coup de Grâce), which arrived in July 2024 and topped the Billboard 200.

Eminem has reached the summit of the Billboard Hot 100 on five separate occasions in his decorated career. He reached No. 1 with both of his Rihanna collaborations “Love the Way You Lie” and “The Monster,” as well as “Not Afraid,” “Lose Yourself” and “Crack a Bottle,” the last track with his close friends Dr. Dre and 50 Cent.

50 actually revealed in October that Em is working on a new album, which caused the Detroit rapper to pull out of contributing to the Street Fighter soundtrack. Fif told the Associated Press at the film’s premiere: “Em, he put a couple pieces together for it and decided because he got closer to his album, he kinda fell back on that because he’s preparing his presentation for the next album.”
