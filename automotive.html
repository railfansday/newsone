New hypothesis explains why the right side of the human brain controls the left side of the body

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The brain's crossed wiring runs through every vertebrate group, and two neuroscientists trace it to the eyes of the first fishes.

A stroke on the right side of the brain weakens the left arm and leg. The brain is cross-wired that way, and for more than a century scientists have assumed there was an evolutionary reason for it.

Two neuroscientists have traced the crossing through every major group of living vertebrates. They now propose that evolution may never have selected it for the jobs it does.

On their reading, the crossed wiring may simply be inherited from the way the first fishes’ eyes were wired to their brains, some 500 million years ago.

Martin Wessendorf, an associate professor emeritus of neuroscience at the University of Minnesota, and Daniel Wang, a researcher in the University’s Department of Neuroscience, wrote the review.

It is a hypothesis built from more than a century of published anatomy rather than a new experiment. However, the authors wrote that it may be testable in fossils.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wang said the question reaches beyond his own field.

“This’ll be relevant to anyone who wants to know more about why brain injury on one side of the head often leads to loss of function on the opposite side of the body,” he said.

The crossing begins at the eye. The lens flips the image, so whatever sits to your right lands on the left side of each retina. The nerve fibers carrying the right half of the view then sort themselves at a junction behind the eyes and run to the left side of the brain.

One place that information goes is a structure in the midbrain called the optic tectum. It holds a map of the opposite half of visual space, and it fires fast turning and escape movements.

Every vertebrate examined appears to have one, lampreys and hagfish included, along with the crossing junction behind the eyes.

Living jawless fish carry both, so the authors conclude that the last common ancestor of all vertebrates probably did too. That puts the first crossed wiring in the Cambrian period, among jawless fishes that lived roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

Anatomists call crossed wiring contralateral organization, and its age is what struck Wang.

“What surprised me the most was likely just how old the contralateral organization of the optic tectum is for vertebrates – spanning even to the Cambrian,” he told Earth.com.

Body senses crossed next. Two pathways carry touch, pain, temperature and body position from the spinal cord up to the brain, and both cross the midline in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

The one for light touch and body position is missing in fishes, so the authors trace it to the last common ancestor of the four-limbed animals.

Movement pathways cross too. One runs from the optic tectum down to the spinal cord and turns the body toward whatever just appeared at the edge of vision.

Lampreys and bony fish appear to have no such pathway. Sharks have one that mostly stays on its own side, and rays and lungfish have a crossed one.

Reptiles appear to have lost that pathway. A second crossed pathway, from a cluster of midbrain cells called the red nucleus, is thought to help control limbs.

True mammals appeared about 165 million years ago. By then, those crossed senses and movement pathways had already existed for almost 200 million years.

The pathway that lets you move one finger at a time is the special case. It runs from the cerebral cortex, the six-layered outer sheet found only in mammals, down to the spinal cord, and it crosses in the lower brainstem.

In most mammals, it ends where touch signals enter the cord and adjusts those signals. Few mammals besides primates, raccoons and kinkajous have a version that reaches the nerve cells that drive muscles.

So the circuit that gives humans fine control of their hands appears to be a sensory circuit put to a new use. Wang said that was the finding that surprised his co-author most.

“This demonstrates that circuitry that’s involved in one function can, with just a change or two, become a function that’s entirely different.”

The crossing isn’t universal in this pathway. In goats and elephants, it runs down both sides of the spinal cord, and in hedgehogs and moles it mostly stays on its own side. Even in humans, an estimated 10 to 25 percent of its fibers don’t cross.

Wang pointed to the goats and elephants, “so there’s a decent amount of diversity there,” he said. The authors read that variety as a sign that natural selection wasn’t strongly favoring the crossing in this pathway.

Isaac Newton proposed in 1704 that the junction behind the eyes sorts nerve fibers by visual field.

Ramón y Cajal, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on the structure of the nervous system, went further in an 1898 paper. Once the visual map crossed, he proposed, it would pay for the maps of hearing, touch and movement to match it.

In computer models published in 2014, an animal controlled by the opposite side of its brain was likelier to survive a blow to one side of the head or body.

At least two other research groups have blamed a twist in the body plan, dating to the split between vertebrates and their invertebrate ancestors. That argument goes back to an 1822 dissection of a crayfish.

The authors see a problem with the twist. The visual system was probably crossed in the first vertebrates, but the pathways for touch, limb control and finger control didn’t exist yet.

So the authors doubt that a single ancient twist can explain them.

The authors’ own answer starts with the optic tectum. Its map of the opposite half of visual space sits on top. Beneath it, in register, sit maps of touch and hearing from the same half, and at the bottom sit the cells that fire movements.

Wessendorf and Wang propose that this stack biased the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later toward crossing.

When the cortex evolved, most of the pathways carrying signals into it were already crossed. A cortex wired to its own side would have needed extra crossings to collect vision, touch and hearing from the far side, or a rewiring of every older circuit.

The authors argue that either change would have carried a “wiring cost,” leaving mammals that were less energy-efficient, less intelligent, or both.

Not everything crosses. Smell and taste mostly stay on the same side, and the authors note that neither sense maps space the way vision, hearing and touch do.

The cerebellum, which coordinates movement, works the same side of the body it sits on.

“We propose that contralateral organization didn’t necessarily evolve as an adaptation, but because evolution is an engineer that builds with the foundation and materials it has at hand,” Wang and Wessendorf wrote.

The authors list what would test the idea. Their first prediction is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates had both the crossing junction and an optic tectum.

Cambrian fossils of jawless fish exist, and a fossilized brain cast from one could show whether both were there.

Wang said the finding that would complicate the idea is an early fossil vertebrate whose optic tectum handled vision on the same side rather than the opposite one.

The authors also predict that crossed pathways from the forebrain to the spinal cord will prove widespread in birds. Unfortunately, relatively few bird species have been examined.

Crocodilians appear not to have been checked, and the authors predict they have one too.

Hearing is the one sense the authors treat with caution. It appears to have evolved partly on its own in different lines.

What remains unknown is whether any Cambrian fish skull preserves enough of its brain to show the crossing.

Until one does, the idea rests on living anatomy. The next checks are in the fossil record, and in the birds and crocodilians whose spinal pathways have yet to be mapped.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Neuroanatomy.

The Ford Mustang GTD 1901 Takes Inspiration From a 125-Year-Old Race Car

The Blue Oval is celebrating a century and a quarter of racing history with a special-edition Mustang GTD.

Henry Ford was racing cars before the Mustang, GT40, or even the Ford Motor Company itself existed. Back in 1901, he won his first race behind the wheel of a two-cylinder race car nicknamed “Sweepstakes,” a car that produced just 26 horsepower. 125 years later, Ford has unveiled the 815-horsepower 2028 Mustang GTD 1901—a special edition inspired by the car that helped launch Henry Ford's automotive empire.

The Mustang GTD 1901 has a 789-horsepower advantage over the Sweepstakes car, but the design inspiration is clear. The Mustang GTD 1901 features a distinctive Royal Red exterior and a wide, off-center black racing stripe featuring a bright red centerline. The color combination reverses the original race car's black body and deep red chassis components. Additional diagonal graphics and exposed carbon-fiber elements, including the front splitter, rocker panels, rear diffuser, and wing, complete the exterior look.

Have a seat inside the Mustang GTD 1901, and you immediately realize this is not an ordinary GTD—that is, if there is even such a thing as an ordinary GTD. The first thing you notice are the Mahogany Red leather seats with Race Red stitching. Keep looking, and you’ll see there are also matching red accents on the seatbelts, while the steering wheel receives Mahogany Red faux suede.

The Performance Package is standard equipment on the Mustang GTD 1901. Options include the magnesium wheels, which reduce the unsprung weight by 50.3 pounds. If you want to spice up the interior, you can order the optional titanium trim package, which gets you redesigned titanium shifter paddles that have been lengthened for the 2028 model year. The titanium trim in the GTD, by the way? It comes from retired F-22 fighter jets.

Ford has not announced how many Mustang GTD 1901’s will be built, or how much they will cost. But we wouldn’t count on seeing too many at your local track day.

A lot has changed since Henry Ford won his first race; horsepower figures, aerodynamics, and suspension technology have come a long way. But the desire of the company that bears his name to prove itself on the racetrack apparently hasn't changed much at all, and that’s something we’re certainly thankful for.

Chevy, GMC, and Cadillac EV Owners Can Now Plug And Charge At Electrify America

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

The ability to simply plug in at a fast-charging station and start charging within seconds was once a luxury once reserved for Tesla drivers at Superchargers. However, with the advent of Plug & Charge, this seamless operation has come to many other brands, including General Motors. GM announced Plug & Charge support back in 2022, starting with EVGo. Now, Plug & Charge is all in one space via GM's Energy Pass App, with Ionna and ChargePoint added to the Plug & Charge list in 2026. Today, it confirmed support for the largest CCS fast-charging network in the country: Electrify America.

The update comes to CCS-equipped General Motors EVs, with NACS-equipped EVs to follow in early 2027.

To use Plug & Charge with Electrify America (or any other charging network that has a Plug & Charge agreement with GM), the driver must be registered with GM’s Energy Pass in the MyChevrolet, MyGMC, or MyCadillac app. The Energy Pass links your EV with a default payment type, allowing charging stations to start the charging event and bill the driver directly without navigating any other app. The driver doesn’t have to do anything but plug in; there’s no need to use a third-party app or fiddle around with a frustrating interface on the charger itself.

The function works with Ionna, EVGo, and ChargePoint, and now Electrify America. The Energy Pass section of the apps not only controls payment, but serves as a one-stop-shop by showing past charging history and letting you locate and navigate to charging stations. Tesla Supercharging Plug & Charge was announced in summer 2026, but it has not quite yet come to the Energy Pass app yet. It should arrive in 2027.

In order to get more people behind the wheel of EVs, industry leaders have stressed that the ease and quality of charging stops must improve. This goes beyond just making sure there are enough charging stations available and making sure they’re in working order. From start to finish, the charging experience should be easy and painless. Saving time and making things as seamless as possible should help make the charging experience nearly frictionless, which is a good thing for EV adoption.

“Bringing that experience to GM EV drivers is another important step forward. It removes friction from the charging experience and lets customers focus on what matters: plugging in, charging, and getting back on the road.” Robert Barrosa, president and CEO of Electrify America, said in a press release.

GM says that on average, the flow of power should start within 45 seconds of plugging in. That is significantly quicker and easier than navigating through a series of menus and apps to start a charging event without Plug & Charge.

Importantly, the addition of Electrify America brings 6,200 chargers that are capable of speeds of up to 350 kW. Some of GM’s largest EVs, like the Chevrolet Silverado EV or Cadillac Escalade IQ, advertise their charging times when connected these ultra-fast charger.

Participation in GM Energy Pass is optional. If the driver doesn’t want to use it, they are free to use the existing charging station apps to charge the vehicle. But it should be easier to use Energy Pass, at least at Electrify America, EVGo, and Ionna stations.

BMW Sets 2 New Nürburgring Records With Help from Pirelli

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

A revised semi-slick tire helped the carmaker knock down the compact car and touring car lap records at the Nordschleife.

Pirelli’s P Zero Trofeo RS is one of its most extreme performance tires, one capable of helping performance cars extract the most out of their potential. And that’s especially true when the company works with automakers directly—as the Italian rubbermaker did with BMW, developed a bespoke version specifically for the BMW M range. The new semi-slick tire remains street legal, but doesn’t sacrifice at-the-limit grip. And it helped BMW set two new ‘Ring lap records with its BMW M2 with the M Performance Track Kit and BMW M3 CS Touring.

The BMW M2 with the M Performance Track Kit managed a lap time of 7:25.068minutes. This lowered the Nürburgring Nordschleife compact car record, previously held by the M2 CS, by 0.5 seconds. The long-roof BMW M3, however, made more significant history: BMW’s M3 CS Touring lapped the 12.9-mile racetrack in 7:29.49 minutes, setting a new record for the fastest touring car ever by shaving off more than five seconds. The previous touring car lap record of 7:35.06 was held by the standard BMW M3 Touring. A combination of increased horsepower, stronger brakes, and custom rubber—as well as BMW M Development engineer Jörg Weidinger behind the wheel—pushed the wagon to success.

“The bespoke development program is the result of close collaboration between Pirelli and BMW M’s R&D teams. Thanks to advanced virtual development techniques, the two teams were able to adapt the tire’s characteristics to the different requirements from the earliest stages of the project, in line with Pirelli’s Perfect Fit approach, which aims to create products tailored to each specific model or application,” the tire maker said in a statement.

Pirelli initially tested the tires alongside BMW at the Nürburgring Nordschleife, and the tires were also later tested at the Nardò test circuit in Italy. BMW and Pirelli have collaborated on over 30 bespoke tire projects across the P Zero family over the years, and like many others, a keen eye can tell which ones are made for Bimmers; the manufacturer-specific tires feature a BMW star marking, as a nod to the integrated development process.

Extra-Fluffy Cinnamon Rolls

📅 Dec 22, 2022  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

At first glance, these homemade cinnamon rolls have all the classics: buttery, golden-brown dough, a gooey cinnamon-and-brown-sugar filling, and a generous swoop of cream cheese frosting. But a couple of smart moves make them especially fluffy, with a tender crumb that stays soft well into the next day (assuming there are any left).

One is tangzhong, a Chinese technique that cooks a small portion of flour and milk into a thick paste before it’s added to the dough. The precooked starch helps the dough hold on to more moisture, resulting in pillowy, supple cinnamon rolls.

The other is brown butter, which gives the filling a nutty, toasty edge. The tangy cream cheese frosting balances all that richness; add the optional lemon zest for extra brightness.

Can I make these cinnamon rolls the night before?

Yes. You can refrigerate the dough after its first rise or assemble the rolls in their baking pans and refrigerate them before the second rise. Cover tightly with plastic wrap to prevent the dough from drying out. The next day, let the chilled dough sit at room temperature for about an hour before proceeding with the recipe, making sure the shaped rolls have doubled in volume before baking.

This is a soft, moisture-rich dough, but it should become smooth and elastic as you knead it. For the most consistent results, weigh your flour rather than measuring by volume. Also, make sure the tangzhong has thickened into a paste before adding it to the dough: You should be able to see the tracks of your whisk across the bottom of the saucepan. Avoid adding excessive extra flour, which can make the finished rolls dense.

Can I make these cinnamon rolls without a stand mixer?

Yes, though kneading this soft, enriched dough by hand takes considerable time and effort. Several readers have reported success doing so. If using a stand mixer, keep an eye on the motor during the extended kneading time. If it begins to strain or overheat, stop the machine and finish kneading by hand.

Can I use active dry yeast instead of instant yeast?

This recipe calls for instant yeast, which can be mixed directly into the dough. Active dry yeast generally requires activation in warm liquid and may affect rising times, so we recommend sticking with instant yeast for the recipe as written. Whisk ¾ cup whole milk and ¼ cup (31 g) all-purpose flour in a medium saucepan until smooth. Place saucepan over medium heat and cook, whisking constantly, until mixture is thickened and whisk lines are visible on bottom of pan, about 2 minutes. Scrape tangzhong into the bowl of a stand mixer and let cool.

Add 1 large egg, one ¼-oz. envelope instant yeast (about 2¼ tsp.), ⅓ cup (67 g) granulated sugar, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt,  and remaining 1½ cups whole milk to tangzhong and whisk to combine. Add remaining 5½ cups (688 g) all-purpose flour. Fit bowl onto stand mixer; fit mixer with dough hook attachment. Mix on low speed until combined, about 1 minute. Increase speed to medium-low and mix, scraping down sides of bowl occasionally, until dough is smooth and elastic, 18–24 minutes. Pinch off a golf-ball-size piece of dough and stretch between your fingers as thin as possible without breaking. If you can see light through the dough, it’s ready. If not, continue mixing a few more minutes, then try again.

Add ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter, room temperature, cut into pieces, to dough and mix on medium speed until incorporated, about 2 minutes. Continue mixing until dough pulls away cleanly from sides of bowl and is no longer tacky to the touch, 5–7 minutes longer. Remove bowl from mixer and cover with a damp kitchen towel. Let sit in a warm spot until doubled in volume, 60–90 minutes.

While the dough is rising, cook ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter in a small saucepan over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until butter foams and milk solids turn dark amber, about 4 minutes. Transfer to a heatproof bowl and let cool slightly. Add 1 cup (packed; 200 g) dark brown sugar, 2 Tbsp. ground cinnamon, 2 Tbsp. heavy cream, and ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt to butter and mix to combine.

Butter two 9"-diameter cake pans. Punch down dough and transfer to a lightly floured surface. Stretch dough into a rough rectangle; if dough feels stiff, let sit 5–10 minutes to allow gluten to relax. Arrange dough with short side facing you and roll out to a 24x13" rectangle. Spread filling over dough in an even layer.

Starting at edge closest to you, tightly roll up dough into a log; roll log seam side down. Using a sharp serrated knife and long sawing motions, trim ends of log; discard. Using serrated knife or, preferably, unflavored dental floss, cut log in half crosswise, then cut each half into 6 equal rounds. (If using dental floss, slide floss underneath dough, then cross over top and pull ends through.) Nestle 6 rounds, cut side down, in each prepared cake pan. Cover with damp kitchen towels and let sit in a warm spot until doubled in volume, about 30–50 minutes.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 350°. Bake rolls until golden on top and puffed (don’t be alarmed if they rise dramatically in the center), 25–30 minutes.

Meanwhile, using an electric mixer on medium speed or a whisk, beat 4 Tbsp. unsalted butter, room temperature, and 8 oz. cream cheese, room temperature, in a large bowl, scraping down sides of bowl as needed, until combined. Add 1¼ cups (138 g) powdered sugar, 1 Tbsp. heavy cream, 1 tsp. lemon zest (if using), and ¼ tsp. Diamond Crystal or Morton kosher salt and beat until mixture is smooth and sugar is incorporated. Taste and beat in more powdered sugar (up to ¾ cup) if desired. Thin with more cream as needed, a teaspoonful at a time, until frosting is thick but spreadable.

Let cinnamon rolls cool in pan 5 minutes; spread frosting over.
