'The Rings of Power' Season 2 Recap: What To Remember Before Prime Video's Epic Fantasy Series Returns

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Prime Video's The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power is one of streaming's most eventful fantasy series, exploring parts of Middle-earth that fans haven't seen before with an action-packed account of the Second Age. Now that Season 3 promises to pick up after a five-year time jump, refreshing your memory on the story will prove crucial for The Rings of Power's imminent return. Below, we recap the biggest twists, major events, and key characters that are all poised to set up the show's darkest installment yet.

The Rings of Power Season 2 begins with the mysterious wizard known as the Stranger (Daniel Weyman) and his Harfoot companions, Nori (Markella Kavenagh) and Poppy (Megan Richards), traveling to Rhûn to discover the truth about his identity. With the Dark Wizard (Ciarán Hinds) and his people in pursuit, the Stranger is forced to use his unpredictable magic, which separates the trio. Nori and Poppy encounter the Stoors, another Hobbit-like group, but their leader, Gundabale (Tanya Moodie), isn't exactly friendly. However, they discover their people share a history, as a caravan of Stoors once left in search of an idealized place to live and became the nomadic Harfoots.

While looking for his friends, the Stranger encounters Tom Bombadil (Rory Kinnear), who becomes his eccentric mentor and later predicts the Stranger will have to stop both the Dark Wizard and Sauron (Charlie Vickers). When Tom Bombadil asks the Stranger to choose between his destiny and his friends, the Stranger picks the latter and confronts the Dark Wizard, who destroys the Stoors' village in the conflict. In the aftermath, the Stranger finds the staff from his dreams, proving he passed the test by choosing friendship over power. Nori and Poppy decide to help the Stoors, who start calling the Stranger "Grand Elf" — which eventually becomes the name Gandalf, revealing his identity.

In the wake of King Tar-Palantir's (Ken Blackburn) death, Númenor is facing a crisis. Míriel (Cynthia Addai-Robinson) is Queen Regent, but Pharazôn (Trystan Gravelle) is plotting to replace her, and even Elendil's (Lloyd Owen) daughter, Eärien (Ema Horvath), is among his allies. During Míriel's coronation, Eärien reveals the existence of the palantír Míriel has been consulting, and Pharazôn seizes power. Believing Elendil's recent vision in the palantír hints at inevitable destruction, Míriel orders Elendil not to fight back.

However, Pharazôn targets the Faithful, and his son, Kemen (Leon Wadham), kills Valandil (Alex Tarrant) in a temple and places the blame on Elendil's shoulders. Elendil is put on trial, which forces him to face the Sea Worm. However, Míriel takes his place, and though her survival proves her innocence, Pharazôn accuses her of allying with Sauron, seizing control of the throne. Míriel tells Elendil to leave without her and protect the sword Narsil, and Elendil flees the city, just as he had seen in the palantír.

Elsewhere, Isildur (Maxim Baldry) has survived Mount Doom's eruption in Season 1, unbeknownst to his family, and has been captured by the spider Shelob. His horse, Beric, saves him, and they encounter Estrid (Nia Towle), who is looking for her betrothed. She leads Isildur to Pelargir, an old outpost where the Low Men are staying. The two only survive the journey thanks to Arondir (Ismael Cruz Córdova), who is still grieving Bronwyn (Nazanin Boniadi), the human woman he loved who died between seasons.

Prime Video's fantasy prequel premieres in November.

When Bronwyn's son, Theo (Tyroe Muhafidin), is taken while confronting Wild Men, Arondir realizes Estrid is a part of that same group. Their pursuit of Theo leads them to a pair of Ents. Angered by the Uruk's destruction of the forest, the Ents initially want to attack, but Arondir promises to help and frees the prisoners, which include Theo and Hagen (Gabriel Akuwudike), Estrid's betrothed, complicating her and Isildur's romance. Later, Kemen brings the Númenoreans back to Pelargir. While he allows Isildur to return, he refuses to let him take possession of Estrid. Even worse, he forces the Low Men to harvest timber from the trees, endangering Pelargir's peace with the Ents.

One of Season 2's main storylines follows Sauron and the creation of the infamous rings of power. Though he suffers a brief setback in Season 1 when Galadriel (Morfydd Clark) learns the truth about him, he continues his plan in Season 2 by manipulating Celebrimbor (Charles Edwards) into creating more rings. When Celebrimbor turns away Sauron's Halbrand guise because of Galadriel's warnings, Sauron transforms himself into the Elf Annatar, a messenger from the Valar. With this identity, Sauron convinces Celebrimbor to make seven rings for the Dwarves and nine for Men, despite Celebrimbor's reluctance. Slowly, Sauron gains more power within Eregion, hiding the imminent attack from the Elves. When Celebrimbor ultimately sees through Sauron's illusions, his people don't believe his insistence that Sauron isn't who he claims to be.

On the Elves' side of the story, High King Gil-galad (Benjamin Walker) loses trust in Galadriel after she was taken in by Sauron, while Elrond (Robert Aramayo) opposes using the rings. He seeks out Círdan (Ben Daniels) the Shipwright for help, but Círdan supports using the rings to restore the Elves' fading and ultimately ends up wearing one alongside Galadriel and Gil-galad. Galadriel's ring gives her foreboding visions, and she decides to visit Celebrimbor and verify his well-being. Gil-galad insists Elrond lead the quest to Eregion, but the path is impeded. Elrond defies the advice of Galadriel's ring and takes them through the Barrow-downs, where the Barrow-wights attack. Though they barely make it out alive, the Elves then discover an army outside Eregion, led by Adar (Sam Hazeldine). Galadriel willingly surrenders herself to Adar as his prisoner so Elrond can warn Gil-galad about Adar's invasion.

Season 1 leaves Durin IV (Owain Arthur) and his father, King Durin III (Peter Mullan), at odds, and Season 2 explores the building rift between them as Durin's wife, Disa (Sophia Nomvete), tries to make peace. An earthquake destroys Khazad-dûm's lighting system, making food scarce, and the Dwarves struggle to find a safe place to continue digging into the mountain. When Celebrimbor gifts King Durin a ring of power, it seemingly solves this problem, despite Durin's protests, but the king grows obsessed and greedy.

Disa's discovery of a mysterious threat in the caves beneath Khazad-dûm pushes Durin to a breaking point, and he publicly rebels against his father to stop the miners. However, when Elrond asks for help in the upcoming battle, Durin leaves his post to rally the army, and King Durin digs toward the danger, uncovering the Balrog. Durin's protests finally get through to his father, who leaves behind his ring and sacrifices himself to protect his son. This leaves Khazad-dûm in chaos, especially as Durin's brother gains support to oppose him in becoming the new king.

Via flashback, The Rings of Power establishes the power struggle between Sauron and Adar. When the two ultimately clash, Adar tries to kill Sauron and believes he has succeeded. Sauron changes his form, leading to his Season 1 arc, but in Season 2, Adar serves as a major threat to Sauron's plans. When Adar leads his Uruks to Eregion and takes Galadriel prisoner, he argues that Sauron is a mutual enemy, and they could destroy him by combining Morgoth's crown and Galadriel's ring. Neither Galadriel nor Elrond falls for Adar's words, but Elrond does give Galadriel the means to escape before going to battle. Galadriel teams up with Arondir, who has been following Adar's forces, and convinces him to fight with the Elves. Meanwhile, Celebrimbor briefly escapes Sauron, passing several rings of power to Galadriel before sacrificing himself in a confrontation with the Dark Lord.

Unfortunately, the Elves' plan is impacted when the Dwarves fail to arrive at Eregion. Although Adar attacks Elrond for Galadriel's ring, he returns it to her in exchange for her help against Sauron. During the battle, Adar's obsession turns the Uruk against him, and they ally with Sauron instead before killing Adar. Galadriel and Sauron face off, and Galadriel is wounded by Morgoth's crown but successfully escapes with her ring. The Dwarves ultimately arrive and put an end to the battle as the Elves evacuate the destroyed city. Gil-galad, Arondir, and Elrond find Galadriel and, at Elrond's suggestion, use the ring to heal her. In Season 2's final moments, the Elves flee to a protected sanctuary that will eventually become Rivendell. With Sauron now leading his own army, the Elves resolve to fight him, setting up the war that will engulf Middle-earth by the time The Rings of Power returns.

Inflation Hotter Than Economist Expectations, Raising Odds of 2027 Rate Hikes

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Inflation picked up speed in October, as the latest Consumer Price Index (CPI) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) accelerated faster than predictions from economists.

The October CPI saw the all-items index grow 0.4%, a slight decrease from last month’s 0.6% rise, but “hotter than expected,” according to Navy Federal Chief Economist Heather Long. Original forecasts were at 0.2% to 0.3% increases, as noted by Realtor.com® Senior Economist Jake Krimmel.

Inflation’s jump this month was due to increases in shelter inflation (up 0.3%), contributing to over 30% of the increase. Food inflation also advanced in October by 0.5%. Energy inflation, which decelerated in October, still saw a rise of 0.7%.

This month’s data does not include annual inflation numbers as the BLS noted it did not collect data in October 2025 due to the government being shut down at the time. As a result, it cannot calculate 12-month percent changes.

With the data available, Long said October’s increase “implies about a ~4% inflation rate for the past year.”

The index for all items less food and energy—core inflation—grew by 0.3% in October, the same rate of growth it saw in both September and August. Indexes that contributed to increase include household furnishings and operations, recreation, medical care, motor vehicle insurance and education.

Long said this month’s data “underscores why so many Americans voted the way they did in the mid-term elections,” speaking of the “blue wave” that overtook the elections last week.

“The same supply-side and geopolitical pressures are still at work, with energy playing an important role and tariff-driven goods inflation not going away,” added Krimmel. “These are the costs most salient to consumers and voters, as we saw, yet the Fed has less direct control over them.”

He noted that for the Fed outlook, a “December hike now looks almost certain, with odds jumping to 99% this morning.”

“Additional hikes in early 2027 look more in play as well, with the 10-year Treasury up 9 basis points just 25 minutes after the CPI report release,” Krimmel continued.

Looking at housing, Krimmel said that near-term there could be “upward pressure on mortgage rates again” due to rising Treasury yields and the potential of a rate hike from the Fed.

Giving and Receiving: Adoption attorney often on road donates building to legal services group for children

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Lawyers sometimes take unusual paths to the profession, but adoption lawyer Robert Noone’s was inspired by men of the cloth. After attending St. Mary’s Seminary in Baltimore, he was helping an order of Jesuits assist the residents of Appalachia with community organizing and voting issues. The priests encouraged Noone to enter law school instead of the priesthood because they thought he could better advocate for social change as an attorney.

To support himself through West Virginia University College of Law, he played guitar in bars. After graduating in 1983, he did labor law and personal injury work at Crandall & Pyles, and by 1989 he decided to open his own firm, Robert Noone Legal Services, where he represented children as a guardian ad litem in abuse and neglect cases and as a juvenile defense attorney with cases that ranged from truant kids to kids who kill. He also handled some adoption cases in West Virginia. He now focuses on adoption services in West Virginia and Alabama.

In 2024, he signed over the deed to his 5,000 square-foot building in Logan, West Virginia, and renovated the building for its new tenant. and this month, ChildLaw Services Inc.—a statewide nonprofit law firm that exclusively works with children by offering life skills and educational programs and legal help for juvenile defense, guardianships and other services—moved in. The nonprofit law firm named the building the Noone ChildLaw Center for Children and Teens.

ChildLaw helped Noone with his work over the years, and he later became a “virtual lawyer”—performing almost everything online and meeting people by video, at their homes or inside local fast-food restaurants. He plans to continue adoption work without an office.

“He has always had a love and heart for children, so we’ve teamed up over the years on projects. He’s been a good friend,” says Monica Holliday, ChildLaw Services’ executive director.

Where you really considering becoming a priest?

Yeah, let's not say that loud. I'd been in a minor seminary, then a college seminary and then getting ready to go into theology. The Catholic education back in the liberal days, they tell you to question everything. And I think I questioned myself right out of a religion. And I went to work with the Jesuits.

Why after making partner at Crandall & Pyles did you decide to start your own firm?

I had a judge tell me: “Bob, I've watched you for a while. It's time for you to, you know, venture out on your own.” And that gave me confidence. And, you know, a lot of encouragement from friends. And what I would not want to write down, though, in a labor law firm, I was probably paid less than my secretary.

Is it true you wear the hats of both an airplane pilot and the Rev. Bob Noone, officiating marriages, including same-sex weddings?

I would meet people at airports and perform them there, but also I did a lot just in my office. I wanted people to feel that it was not just a “drive-by” wedding. Or “fly-by,” as the case may be. I always made them tell me their love story, and I would shape the vows from what I learned. That was fun when the South was so resistant to change. … Now acceptance of LGBTQ has gotten better here.

You worked as a guardian ad litem during your career and then decided to just focus on the legal work around adoptions. Why?

You get to that point. Where you hear your last sad story: that mother who wouldn't see her kid because, oh, she forgot to go pee in a jar. And I hit that point a few years back, and I decided I'm just going to help put kids in homes.

You are a music lover and have been in many bands as a performer. But you have a story about a young lady who was skipping school who also, you discovered, had some hidden musical talent.

This would have been around 2009. I was doing a lot of juvenile work then, and she was truant. I said, “What do you do for fun?” And she was big. She quit going to school because she was so overweight. And she said, "Well, I love to sing." I’m thinking she would name Pink or Gwen Stefani or one of these people. She goes, “Aretha Franklin.” Well, she did great. But the real kicker was, I said, “If you go to school”—this was in like April—"the next two months without missing, you'll get to sing with my band, Daddy Rabbit."

How many adoptions have you helped with over the years?

When I count all those I supervise—I have two lawyers now that work with me—probably a little over 2,000.

In West Virginia last week, there's one point the three of us may be in four or five different counties in one day. People thought after 43 years, I'm giving a building away, I'm retiring. Hell to the no! I'm only 69, people. There's a lot of music left to play, cases to argue.

What kinds of challenges do you help navigate for people wanting to adopt?

In West Virginia, we do our same-sex adoptions, but we also do adoptions for people that aren't married. You know, just to clarify, because [the law] says a single parent can adopt or a married couple—husband and wife—but it doesn't say a single parent. And Alabama, in fact, passed a law that specifically forbids two single people from adopting. Go figure that out.

11 Classic Italian Dishes That Belong On Your Thanksgiving Table

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Italian food is inherently celebratory. The cuisine embodies togetherness, featuring giant loaves of semolina bread, big salads, and overflowing family-style platters that snake their way around the table until there's a little bit of everything on everyone's plate. And while we didn't all necessarily grow up eating meatballs, gnocchi, and lasagna, these meals have slowly become a fundamental part of the American cooking canon. Italian food isn't necessarily easy to prepare, but its familiarity and simple elegance make the dishes feel accessible, even if they're difficult to master.

Italian food is also so well suited for the holiday table because of its adaptability. Regardless of your culinary background or self-identified food heritage, most classic Italian dishes can be reconfigured to fit any cuisine or occasion, including Thanksgiving. When you think about it, the two are a natural fit. Italian food is hearty, cozy, and comforting, with plenty of creamy carbohydrates and hearty main courses. The same goes for your typical Thanksgiving fare, allowing this list to feature recipe ideas that blend the two. Think pumpkin-stuffed ravioli, turkey lasagna, and risotto topped with roasted butternut squash. These interesting mashups will undoubtedly bring some extra excitement (and cheese) to your upcoming holiday menu.

A whole roast turkey is one of the most divisive holiday dishes. Done correctly, it's succulent, juicy, and full of bold poultry flavor; prepared improperly, you're left with pounds of bland, boring meat. But you don't need to cook a 10-pound bird to get turkey on the Thanksgiving table. These slow-cooker Italian turkey and zucchini meatballs use classic Italian cooking methods and ingredients to bring Thanksgiving flair to the holiday table. Meatballs are typically viewed as casual fare, but with the right cooking techniques and seasonings, they can be just as delicious as anything.

This recipe calls for a mixture of ground turkey meat and shredded zucchini. Why? The zucchini helps the meatballs retain moisture and adds some savory vegetal notes to the meat. These two ingredients are paired with a supporting cast of garlic, egg, breadcrumbs, and Pecorino Romano cheese to round out the flavor and give the meatballs a firmer texture. More importantly, the slow cooking keeps the meatballs submerged in a homemade tomato sauce, trapping moisture and keeping that notoriously dry turkey meat nice and luscious. This technique not only makes perfectly prepared meatballs, but also lets you tend to Grandma and Cousin Peter without worrying about something else overcooking.

This rich turkey tomato Bolognese is another creative way to add turkey to your Thanksgiving feast without cooking, carving, and roasting a massive piece of poultry. Bolognese is essentially a slow-simmered Italian sauce made with tomatoes and ground meat. As it cooks, the ingredients meld together, creating a thick, stew-like consistency that's greater than the sum of its parts. Although it is typically made with ground beef or pork, you can easily substitute turkey without losing any of the dish's heartiness or texture. The aromatics give it a sweet undertone, and the white wine helps deglaze the pan, capturing those brown bits of concentrated flavor.

But what sets this recipe apart is another ingredient synonymous with an authentic Bolognese yet largely omitted in jarred varieties: milk. Dairy may seem like a strange addition, but it gives the sauce a smooth, rich texture and helps tenderize the meat. Equally important to building your Bolognese sauce is pairing it with the correct pasta. Spaghetti is the most common but not the best option (at least according to Ina Garten). Instead, choose something flatter with more surface area, like tagliatelle or pappardelle. If you're feeling tubular, go with a shape with a wider diameter, like rigatoni, so the sauce can get trapped inside.

Sausage has rightfully earned its place on the Thanksgiving table, so much so that you can find it in everything from stuffing to cornbread casseroles to white gravy. But depending on which style you choose, the meat can easily overpower or oversalt a dish. When preparing this zucchini involtini, it's best to use an uncooked, Italian-style sausage. Unlike those popular, pre-cooked, vacuum-sealed chicken sausages, a quality Italian sausage comes raw and in a natural casing; otherwise, you can't sufficiently break up, brown, and mix the meat with other ingredients.

This appetizer requires some preparation, but the final result is well worth the effort. The most laborious task is slicing the zucchini into long, thin planks and piping them full of the sausage and ricotta filling. After you prepare the rolls, the rest of the dish comes together quickly. Just top each one with shredded cheese, and bake them together in a thin layer of sauce. The chew of the sausage paired with the sweet tomato sauce and creamy ricotta makes for an unforgettable appetizer that tastes just as impressive as it looks. If you don't want to make your tomato sauce from scratch, you can easily substitute your favorite jarred marinara.

Agnolotti started in the Piedmont region of northern Italy as a humble, pinched pasta that farmers would make to use up leftover bits of meat. Unlike ravioli, which are perfectly square, agnolotti are thin and rectangular, more like a standard bedroom pillow rather than a throw. This agnolotti di zucca is the perfect seasonal pasta, especially if you're in the mood to make fresh dough.

And while many Thanksgiving centerpieces feature a cornucopia of squash and gourds, this recipe actually includes them in the dish, specifically in the agnolotti filling, which is made with a mixture of ricotta cheese and roasted delicata squash. If you don't want to deal with chopping and de-seeding, a pumpkin purée will also work, but the flavor may fall short without those natural caramel notes you get from freshly roasted squash. The fresh pasta is an excellent foil, the dough chewy and toothsome as it cradles the delicate filling. After the agnolotti are stuffed and boiled, they're finished in a pan with butter and sage for a bite that's all things autumnal. Obviously, this dish isn't exactly light fare, so serve it in place of your more classic holiday carbohydrates.

Speaking of ravioli, you can't have a list of classic Italian dishes without mentioning this instantly recognizable stuffed pasta. Moving beyond squash, this homemade ravioli recipe focuses on the other orange gourd: pumpkin. But we're not talking about the canned stuff; this dish gets its unique flavor from freshly roasted sugar pumpkin. Does it require more prep work? Yes. You have to remove the seeds and stringy pulp, roast the pumpkin, and scoop out the flesh, but these extra steps add a bright earthiness you just can't recreate with canned pumpkin.

Unlike most pasta fillings, which are salty, savory, and creamy, this recipe goes in another direction, pairing pulverized almonds with ground nutmeg, ricotta cheese, and, of course, roasted pumpkin. The finished ravioli are coated in a traditional butter-sage sauce, furthering that autumn feel. Again, these require homemade dough, but it's fairly forgiving if you roll it out to the proper thickness — slightly less than 1 millimeter thick. Cut the dough in circles, and fold over the filling to make pretty little half moons, a nice, festive homage to the fall harvest.

The Thanksgiving table is usually covered with the classics: stuffing, cornbread, turkey, and sweet potato casserole. But this predictability can easily veer into monotony. Change is difficult, but it can be a good thing, especially for your palate. Reinventing your holiday menu isn't so much about eliminating as it is about balancing. Holiday food is notoriously rich and sweet; adding a dish that's salty, spicy, and earthy can help bring some relief.

These spicy sausage stuffed mushrooms deliver all of the above. A stuffed mushroom is a festive appetizer, but if you use super-sized portobellos, they quickly become worthy of a main course. The stuffing is a mixture of spicy sausage, breadcrumbs, egg, and grated cheese. If you want to sacrifice a mushroom (or stems), you can dice it up and add it in there too. Once the filling is sautéed, all you need to do is clean and stuff your mushrooms, then bake until everything is cooked through. Antarctic blue whales survived near extinction, but can they fully recover?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Antarctic blue whales are slowly recovering from whaling, but their population remains around 2% of its former size.

Several whale populations that were hunted hard in the 20th century have already climbed back to their old numbers. Antarctic blue whales, the largest blue whales on Earth, are not one of them.

Their numbers are rising again, but a new estimate suggests there are still only about 2% as many as there were before whaling began.

Since the last recorded catch in 1973, the population has grown nearly tenfold, from about 418 whales to about 4,018 in 2024.

The team got those numbers from a computer model rather than a head count, so each one comes with a lot of uncertainty.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington’s School of Marine and Environmental Affairs, led the work with colleagues in the US, Australia, Japan and South Africa.

It’s the first full assessment of these whales in more than 20 years.

“No whale was hunted quite to the extent that Antarctic blue whales were hunted,” Rand said.

In 1904, whalers killed 11 Antarctic blue whales. The hunt grew so fast that by 1930 they were taking 30,405 in a single year.

Breeding couldn’t keep up with a hunt on that scale. In many seasons, whalers removed between 10% and 40% of the whole population.

By the time the last recorded catch occurred in 1973, whalers had killed a total of 347,662 Antarctic blue whales.

The team estimates that about 206,000 Antarctic blue whales lived in the Southern Ocean before whaling, and that only about 418, roughly 0.2%, were left by 1973.

Even a ban didn’t stop the killing right away. The International Whaling Commission agreed to end the commercial catch of these whales in 1963, but illegal Soviet whaling carried on for about another decade.

Counting whales spread around an entire continent is hard, so the team pulled together three kinds of information, starting with the old catch records.

The second came from ship surveys, including three full trips around Antarctica between 1978 and 2004 that recorded every blue whale the crews saw. Japanese ships also surveyed part of the same waters from 1989 to 2008.

The third came from photographs, since each whale can be recognized from pictures of its sides.

By tracking how often the same whales reappeared in photos from 2003 to 2019, the team estimated about 2,889 in Antarctic waters for the 2018 to 2019 season.

The researchers then combined all three in one computer model. It estimated how many whales were alive in every year from 1904 to 2024.

The model’s best estimate for 2024 is 4,018 whales.

The estimated range runs from about 1,900 to 8,400 whales, and even the high end would be less than 5% of the population before whaling.

So the whales are coming back, but from an extremely low starting point.

The team estimates the population is growing by about 4.5% a year, slower than the 7.3% a year reported in the last assessment, published in 2004.

Co-author Trevor Branch of the University of Washington’s School of Aquatic and Fishery Sciences led that study.

The team traced the difference to updated Japanese survey numbers and the new photo-based count.

“I think this is relatively realistic for the kind of population growth we would expect from them,” Rand said.

If growth continues along the model’s path, Antarctic blue whales wouldn’t reach even 60% of their old numbers until around 2102.

The authors note that this date depends on how many whales lived there before whaling, a number that is itself uncertain.

Other whales have recovered much faster. Blue whales off western North America, gray whales in the eastern North Pacific and North Pacific humpbacks have all returned to or passed their pre-whaling numbers in the past decade.

But most of those populations weren’t pushed down nearly as far. Growth that starts from a few hundred animals takes a long time to add up.

“I think it’s probably an open question whether they will make it back to pre-whaling levels,” Rand added.

Antarctic blue whales feed mainly on Antarctic krill, small shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean. That food supply may have changed.

Large baleen whales dive deep, eat several tons of krill a day and excrete iron-rich waste near the surface.

Scientists think that waste feeds the tiny ocean plants krill depend on, so losing millions of whales may have meant less krill.

Blue whales returning now will likely face more competition for that krill. Humpback, fin and Antarctic minke whales eat it too, and they may be recovering faster.

Krill fishing is projected to grow, and earlier research has linked shrinking sea ice and warmer water to fewer krill.

The experience of southern right whales shows how recovery can stall.

Several of their populations are having fewer calves, with longer gaps between births, even though they’re still well below their pre-whaling size.

If the Southern Ocean can now support fewer blue whales than it once did, the authors wrote, the old population size may no longer be a realistic target.

The new numbers are probably on the low side. Ship surveys assume observers spot every whale directly in the ship’s path, but blue whales are often underwater, and some likely get missed.

Studies of other blue whale populations suggest those misses could make the survey estimates 4% to 25% too low.

The photo count assumes whales mix randomly around Antarctica each year. In fact, they return to the same ocean basin 54% to 64% of the time, compared with about 33% if they moved at random.

Both problems point toward more whales and faster growth than the model shows. Neither would change the main finding, which is that the population is growing but still far from recovered.

Keeping track is also getting harder, because full ship surveys around Antarctica have largely stopped.

Underwater microphones can pick up blue whale songs, but scientists still can’t turn the number of calls into a count of whales.

“Antarctica is a big place, and it’s not just one country’s ocean,” Rand said. “We really need more monitoring and continued monitoring to figure out what is going to happen to this species in the future.”

The authors called for more ship surveys, more photographs and more listening.

Without that work, researchers won’t be able to tell whether the recovery holds up as the Southern Ocean changes.

The full study was published in the journal Endangered Species Research.

Scientists figured out how to use engineered bacteria to dissolve rocks faster and capture carbon dioxide

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Engineered ocean bacteria kept rust off olivine sand and sped up rock weathering, a slow process that pulls carbon dioxide into seawater.

As rock slowly wears away, it releases compounds that trap carbon dioxide and take it out of the atmosphere. The trouble is speed. Left alone, the process takes thousands of years, partly because iron in the rock turns to rust and coats the surface.

Now, a team led by Harvard researchers has engineered a common ocean bacterium to keep that rust from building up. In tanks of real harbor water, the microbes made crushed rock dissolve about three times faster than plain seawater did.

That’s a big jump for a process that has looked too slow to matter. So far the researchers have tested it only in lab reactors and a few glass tanks, not in the open ocean.

Neil Dalvie, a synthetic biology fellow in systems biology at Harvard Medical School, led the study with colleagues at the Wyss Institute and the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability.

Dalvie told Earth.com he wanted to highlight the interdisciplinary nature of the work.

“We learned about alkalinity and mineral measurements from geochemists in the rock weathering industry,” he said. “Very few people have both the skillsets for molecular biology, microbial engineering, and mineral processing.”

“Some people describe rock weathering as a silver bullet for climate change,” said Dalvie. “The problem is that it’s too slow.”

The team worked with olivine, a common mineral, in the form of sand. When olivine dissolves in seawater, it gives off magnesium, silicon and iron.

The magnesium is the useful part. It makes seawater more alkaline, and alkaline seawater takes up carbon dioxide from the air. This is held as bicarbonate, a form of carbon dissolved in water.

Because magnesium stays in the ocean for a very long time, the authors count that carbon as stored for good.

Iron is the problem. It doesn’t stay dissolved. It forms a layer of rust on the rock, and that layer slows any further dissolving.

The rust is one reason that natural rock weathering takes so long. Spreading crushed rock on farm fields or coastlines helps, but the authors note that the carbon captured that way has been hard to measure.

Many microbes already have a way to deal with rust. When they run low on iron, they release molecules that grab iron and make it dissolve in water. Biologists call them siderophores.

Once a microbe has enough iron, though, it stops making these molecules. And a microbe doesn’t need much.

The team tested this with Alteromonas macleodii, a marine bacterium that makes its own siderophore. The researchers ran 16 small lab reactors with different amounts of olivine sand and different flow rates.

The bacteria made large amounts of the molecule only when there was little rock and the water moved fast.

With more rock, the bacteria had iron to spare and quit. The authors concluded that the bacterium’s natural settings rule out steady siderophore production in a tank big enough for industrial use.

So the team rewired the bacterium. They replaced the genetic switch that turns siderophore production on only when iron runs short with one that keeps it on all the time.

Dalvie said the change itself was fairly easy. Showing that it worked was the hard part.

“The bacteria keep growing, the cells keep making siderophores, the rock keeps dissolving – it’s very hard to measure all these things at the same time,” Dalvie said.

In the small reactors, the engineered strain produced more than 100 times as much of the siderophore as the natural strain. Olivine dissolved about 2.6 times faster with the engineered cells than in reactors with no cells at all, while the natural strain made no clear difference.

Then came a bigger test. The researchers placed more than 8.8 pounds (4 kilograms) of construction-grade olivine sand in the bottom of 3-gallon (11-liter) glass fish tanks.

Unfiltered seawater from Boston Harbor flowed through each tank at about 1.6 quarts (1.5 liters) a day. Some tanks got a slow drip of nutrients, and two of those also got a fresh dose of the engineered bacteria once a week.

In those two tanks, magnesium came off the rock 3.1 times faster. The water also became more alkaline, which the team calculated as about 0.018 ounce (0.50 grams) of carbon dioxide removed from the air each day.

That’s about half the weight of a paper clip. The amount is small, but it was measured directly in the water rather than estimated from a model.

In earlier small reactors fed raw seawater, the engineered strain held steady for about a week before its numbers fell. That’s why the pilot tanks got a fresh dose weekly.

The strain’s staying power surprised the team. “We were actually surprised that our originally engineered strains were able to survive and perform for over a week in unfiltered seawater,” Dalvie said.

“We think that biofilms may help protect the bacteria from growth competition and viruses in the seawater.”

Bacteria have to eat, and making their food releases carbon dioxide too. So the team ran a life-cycle analysis, an accounting of the emissions from building and running a full-size version.

Sugar failed on both counts. Making it emits too much, and in the lab, feeding it to the bacteria left the water less alkaline, likely because of acidic byproducts. The researchers switched to acetate, a simple food that some companies now make from carbon dioxide itself.

Ordinary bacteria failed the accounting too. Feeding wild microbes enough to keep them hungry for iron would release far more carbon dioxide than the faster weathering captured.

With engineered bacteria and a carbon-neutral acetate feed, the model predicted 74 percent more net carbon removal than a tank of rock and seawater alone. The researchers assumed the 2.6-fold speedup from the lab would hold at that scale.

“We’ve found a way that it could work and, maybe more importantly, now we have a model to calculate what it would look like to implement this at scale,” he said.

Four of the authors, Dalvie among them, have filed a patent application on strains of bacteria engineered for siderophore production that keep rust off rock.

The pilot tanks didn’t stay fast. After about three weeks of feeding, dissolving and carbon uptake dropped in every tank.

Slimy films of microbes had built up on the glass and on top of the sand, especially in the tanks that got the engineered bacteria.

The films appeared to keep the water from mixing, and the researchers suspect they blocked the rock. A thin crust had also formed, and most of the dissolving probably happened in the top 0.4 inch (1 centimeter) of sand.

The authors suggest that breaking up that top layer from time to time could keep the rock working, but they haven’t tested it. Dalvie and his colleagues say the next steps are larger experiments, better nutrient mixes and more efficient bacteria.

Dalvie said a real coastline or mine would need “some sort of enclosure or tank” to make enough of the iron-grabbing molecules. Such a tank “would resemble existing wastewater processes,” he said.

The bigger hurdle, he said, is the food for the bacteria. “Green feedstocks like this need to be available at large scales,” Dalvie said, referring to the acetate made from carbon dioxide and electricity.

The study did not analyze the economics of capturing carbon dioxide. “The price of carbon needs to be high enough to justify a new biochemical process,” he stressed.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Biotechnology.

Homemade Dog Treats With Applesauce and Cheese

📅 Apr 28, 2025  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

These homemade dog treats are crunchy, cheesy, and enthusiastically approved by my little curmudgeon. The recipe began with a dog biscuit from the December 2005 issue of Gourmet. While the generous quantities of butter, brown sugar, and salt in the original gave me pause, I used it as inspiration for these cracker-like treats, swapping in ingredients I’d discussed with my vet and knew my pup would love. I’m hoping yours will too.

Whole wheat flour provides structure plus fiber, while brown rice flour helps the biscuits bake up crisp. Old-fashioned rolled oats give the treats a pleasantly nubby texture. The dough comes together with an egg and unsweetened applesauce. Be sure to check the applesauce label for added sweeteners, especially xylitol, which is toxic to dogs.

I like Gouda for its aroma—it drives my dog into a frenzy—but sharp cheddar or Swiss will work too. Curly parsley and mint lend a fresh herbal note and mild breath freshening, but the real secret ingredient is a little anise seed. Sometimes called “dog nip,” anise has a scent that some dogs find irresistible. Fennel seed makes a fine substitute.

Can I make these homemade dog treats with pumpkin?

Absolutely. For a fall-inspired variation, use plain canned pumpkin purée instead of applesauce, oat flour instead of brown rice flour, sage instead of parsley, and sharp cheddar for the cheese. (Honestly, add a little salt and you could serve these on a Thanksgiving charcuterie board.) For your dog, though, skip the salt and be sure to use 100% pumpkin purée, not pumpkin pie filling, which contains added sugar and spices.

IF USING A FOOD PROCESSOR: Pulse 1 cup (113 g) whole wheat flour, ¾ cup (96 g) brown rice flour, ¾ cup (76 g) old-fashioned rolled oats, 1 tsp. baking powder, ½ tsp. ground fennel seed, and ½ tsp. ground ginger in a food processor to combine (about three 1-second pulses). Add 1 cup (4 oz.) shredded Gouda or sharp cheddar, ¼ cup curly parsley leaves and tender stems, packed, and ¼ cup mint leaves, packed, and pulse to incorporate (two to three 1-second pulses). Add ¾ cup unsweetened applesauce and 1 large egg, and pulse until a coarse dough forms, about 20 seconds. (You can roll out dough right away, but it’s much easier to work with it if you let it chill first.) Divide dough into two rounds and wrap each in plastic, or place in separate resealable containers, and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes and up to 24 hours.

IF NOT USING A FOOD PROCESSOR: Whisk 1 cup (113 g) whole wheat flour, ¾ cup (96 g) brown rice flour, ¾ cup (76 g) old-fashioned rolled oats, 1 tsp. baking powder, ½ tsp. ground fennel seed, and ½ tsp. ground ginger in a large bowl to combine. Add 1 cup (4 oz.) shredded Gouda or sharp cheddar and toss to coat the cheese. Finely chop ¼ cup curly parsley leaves and tender stems, packed and ¼ cup mint leaves, packed; set aside. Create a well in center of flour mixture. Add ¾ cup unsweetened applesauce and 1 large egg to well and mix with a wooden spoon, bringing flour into center until combined. Fold in chopped herbs. (You can roll out dough right away, but it’s much easier to work with it if you let it chill first.) Divide dough into two rounds and wrap each in plastic, or place in separate resealable containers, and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes and up to 24 hours.

Bake dog treats, rotating baking sheets front to back and top to bottom halfway through, until cheese has bubbled up and treats have darkened slightly, 12–15 minutes.

Do Ahead: Dog treats can be baked, cooled, and stored at room temperature in an airtight container for up to 3 weeks or frozen for 3 months.
