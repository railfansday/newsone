Antarctic blue whales survived near extinction, but can they fully recover?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Antarctic blue whales are slowly recovering from whaling, but their population remains around 2% of its former size.

Several whale populations that were hunted hard in the 20th century have already climbed back to their old numbers. Antarctic blue whales, the largest blue whales on Earth, are not one of them.

Their numbers are rising again, but a new estimate suggests there are still only about 2% as many as there were before whaling began.

Since the last recorded catch in 1973, the population has grown nearly tenfold, from about 418 whales to about 4,018 in 2024.

The team got those numbers from a computer model rather than a head count, so each one comes with a lot of uncertainty.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington’s School of Marine and Environmental Affairs, led the work with colleagues in the US, Australia, Japan and South Africa.

It’s the first full assessment of these whales in more than 20 years.

“No whale was hunted quite to the extent that Antarctic blue whales were hunted,” Rand said.

In 1904, whalers killed 11 Antarctic blue whales. The hunt grew so fast that by 1930 they were taking 30,405 in a single year.

Breeding couldn’t keep up with a hunt on that scale. In many seasons, whalers removed between 10% and 40% of the whole population.

By the time the last recorded catch occurred in 1973, whalers had killed a total of 347,662 Antarctic blue whales.

The team estimates that about 206,000 Antarctic blue whales lived in the Southern Ocean before whaling, and that only about 418, roughly 0.2%, were left by 1973.

Even a ban didn’t stop the killing right away. The International Whaling Commission agreed to end the commercial catch of these whales in 1963, but illegal Soviet whaling carried on for about another decade.

Counting whales spread around an entire continent is hard, so the team pulled together three kinds of information, starting with the old catch records.

The second came from ship surveys, including three full trips around Antarctica between 1978 and 2004 that recorded every blue whale the crews saw. Japanese ships also surveyed part of the same waters from 1989 to 2008.

The third came from photographs, since each whale can be recognized from pictures of its sides.

By tracking how often the same whales reappeared in photos from 2003 to 2019, the team estimated about 2,889 in Antarctic waters for the 2018 to 2019 season.

The researchers then combined all three in one computer model. It estimated how many whales were alive in every year from 1904 to 2024.

The model’s best estimate for 2024 is 4,018 whales.

The estimated range runs from about 1,900 to 8,400 whales, and even the high end would be less than 5% of the population before whaling.

So the whales are coming back, but from an extremely low starting point.

The team estimates the population is growing by about 4.5% a year, slower than the 7.3% a year reported in the last assessment, published in 2004.

Co-author Trevor Branch of the University of Washington’s School of Aquatic and Fishery Sciences led that study.

The team traced the difference to updated Japanese survey numbers and the new photo-based count.

“I think this is relatively realistic for the kind of population growth we would expect from them,” Rand said.

If growth continues along the model’s path, Antarctic blue whales wouldn’t reach even 60% of their old numbers until around 2102.

The authors note that this date depends on how many whales lived there before whaling, a number that is itself uncertain.

Other whales have recovered much faster. Blue whales off western North America, gray whales in the eastern North Pacific and North Pacific humpbacks have all returned to or passed their pre-whaling numbers in the past decade.

But most of those populations weren’t pushed down nearly as far. Growth that starts from a few hundred animals takes a long time to add up.

“I think it’s probably an open question whether they will make it back to pre-whaling levels,” Rand added.

Antarctic blue whales feed mainly on Antarctic krill, small shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean. That food supply may have changed.

Large baleen whales dive deep, eat several tons of krill a day and excrete iron-rich waste near the surface.

Scientists think that waste feeds the tiny ocean plants krill depend on, so losing millions of whales may have meant less krill.

Blue whales returning now will likely face more competition for that krill. Humpback, fin and Antarctic minke whales eat it too, and they may be recovering faster.

Krill fishing is projected to grow, and earlier research has linked shrinking sea ice and warmer water to fewer krill.

The experience of southern right whales shows how recovery can stall.

Several of their populations are having fewer calves, with longer gaps between births, even though they’re still well below their pre-whaling size.

If the Southern Ocean can now support fewer blue whales than it once did, the authors wrote, the old population size may no longer be a realistic target.

The new numbers are probably on the low side. Ship surveys assume observers spot every whale directly in the ship’s path, but blue whales are often underwater, and some likely get missed.

Studies of other blue whale populations suggest those misses could make the survey estimates 4% to 25% too low.

The photo count assumes whales mix randomly around Antarctica each year. In fact, they return to the same ocean basin 54% to 64% of the time, compared with about 33% if they moved at random.

Both problems point toward more whales and faster growth than the model shows. Neither would change the main finding, which is that the population is growing but still far from recovered.

Keeping track is also getting harder, because full ship surveys around Antarctica have largely stopped.

Underwater microphones can pick up blue whale songs, but scientists still can’t turn the number of calls into a count of whales.

“Antarctica is a big place, and it’s not just one country’s ocean,” Rand said. “We really need more monitoring and continued monitoring to figure out what is going to happen to this species in the future.”

The authors called for more ship surveys, more photographs and more listening.

Without that work, researchers won’t be able to tell whether the recovery holds up as the Southern Ocean changes.

The full study was published in the journal Endangered Species Research.

Scientists figured out how to use engineered bacteria to dissolve rocks faster and capture carbon dioxide

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Engineered ocean bacteria kept rust off olivine sand and sped up rock weathering, a slow process that pulls carbon dioxide into seawater.

As rock slowly wears away, it releases compounds that trap carbon dioxide and take it out of the atmosphere. The trouble is speed. Left alone, the process takes thousands of years, partly because iron in the rock turns to rust and coats the surface.

Now, a team led by Harvard researchers has engineered a common ocean bacterium to keep that rust from building up. In tanks of real harbor water, the microbes made crushed rock dissolve about three times faster than plain seawater did.

That’s a big jump for a process that has looked too slow to matter. So far the researchers have tested it only in lab reactors and a few glass tanks, not in the open ocean.

Neil Dalvie, a synthetic biology fellow in systems biology at Harvard Medical School, led the study with colleagues at the Wyss Institute and the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability.

Dalvie told Earth.com he wanted to highlight the interdisciplinary nature of the work.

“We learned about alkalinity and mineral measurements from geochemists in the rock weathering industry,” he said. “Very few people have both the skillsets for molecular biology, microbial engineering, and mineral processing.”

“Some people describe rock weathering as a silver bullet for climate change,” said Dalvie. “The problem is that it’s too slow.”

The team worked with olivine, a common mineral, in the form of sand. When olivine dissolves in seawater, it gives off magnesium, silicon and iron.

The magnesium is the useful part. It makes seawater more alkaline, and alkaline seawater takes up carbon dioxide from the air. This is held as bicarbonate, a form of carbon dissolved in water.

Because magnesium stays in the ocean for a very long time, the authors count that carbon as stored for good.

Iron is the problem. It doesn’t stay dissolved. It forms a layer of rust on the rock, and that layer slows any further dissolving.

The rust is one reason that natural rock weathering takes so long. Spreading crushed rock on farm fields or coastlines helps, but the authors note that the carbon captured that way has been hard to measure.

Many microbes already have a way to deal with rust. When they run low on iron, they release molecules that grab iron and make it dissolve in water. Biologists call them siderophores.

Once a microbe has enough iron, though, it stops making these molecules. And a microbe doesn’t need much.

The team tested this with Alteromonas macleodii, a marine bacterium that makes its own siderophore. The researchers ran 16 small lab reactors with different amounts of olivine sand and different flow rates.

The bacteria made large amounts of the molecule only when there was little rock and the water moved fast.

With more rock, the bacteria had iron to spare and quit. The authors concluded that the bacterium’s natural settings rule out steady siderophore production in a tank big enough for industrial use.

So the team rewired the bacterium. They replaced the genetic switch that turns siderophore production on only when iron runs short with one that keeps it on all the time.

Dalvie said the change itself was fairly easy. Showing that it worked was the hard part.

“The bacteria keep growing, the cells keep making siderophores, the rock keeps dissolving – it’s very hard to measure all these things at the same time,” Dalvie said.

In the small reactors, the engineered strain produced more than 100 times as much of the siderophore as the natural strain. Olivine dissolved about 2.6 times faster with the engineered cells than in reactors with no cells at all, while the natural strain made no clear difference.

Then came a bigger test. The researchers placed more than 8.8 pounds (4 kilograms) of construction-grade olivine sand in the bottom of 3-gallon (11-liter) glass fish tanks.

Unfiltered seawater from Boston Harbor flowed through each tank at about 1.6 quarts (1.5 liters) a day. Some tanks got a slow drip of nutrients, and two of those also got a fresh dose of the engineered bacteria once a week.

In those two tanks, magnesium came off the rock 3.1 times faster. The water also became more alkaline, which the team calculated as about 0.018 ounce (0.50 grams) of carbon dioxide removed from the air each day.

That’s about half the weight of a paper clip. The amount is small, but it was measured directly in the water rather than estimated from a model.

In earlier small reactors fed raw seawater, the engineered strain held steady for about a week before its numbers fell. That’s why the pilot tanks got a fresh dose weekly.

The strain’s staying power surprised the team. “We were actually surprised that our originally engineered strains were able to survive and perform for over a week in unfiltered seawater,” Dalvie said.

“We think that biofilms may help protect the bacteria from growth competition and viruses in the seawater.”

Bacteria have to eat, and making their food releases carbon dioxide too. So the team ran a life-cycle analysis, an accounting of the emissions from building and running a full-size version.

Sugar failed on both counts. Making it emits too much, and in the lab, feeding it to the bacteria left the water less alkaline, likely because of acidic byproducts. The researchers switched to acetate, a simple food that some companies now make from carbon dioxide itself.

Ordinary bacteria failed the accounting too. Feeding wild microbes enough to keep them hungry for iron would release far more carbon dioxide than the faster weathering captured.

With engineered bacteria and a carbon-neutral acetate feed, the model predicted 74 percent more net carbon removal than a tank of rock and seawater alone. The researchers assumed the 2.6-fold speedup from the lab would hold at that scale.

“We’ve found a way that it could work and, maybe more importantly, now we have a model to calculate what it would look like to implement this at scale,” he said.

Four of the authors, Dalvie among them, have filed a patent application on strains of bacteria engineered for siderophore production that keep rust off rock.

The pilot tanks didn’t stay fast. After about three weeks of feeding, dissolving and carbon uptake dropped in every tank.

Slimy films of microbes had built up on the glass and on top of the sand, especially in the tanks that got the engineered bacteria.

The films appeared to keep the water from mixing, and the researchers suspect they blocked the rock. A thin crust had also formed, and most of the dissolving probably happened in the top 0.4 inch (1 centimeter) of sand.

The authors suggest that breaking up that top layer from time to time could keep the rock working, but they haven’t tested it. Dalvie and his colleagues say the next steps are larger experiments, better nutrient mixes and more efficient bacteria.

Dalvie said a real coastline or mine would need “some sort of enclosure or tank” to make enough of the iron-grabbing molecules. Such a tank “would resemble existing wastewater processes,” he said.

The bigger hurdle, he said, is the food for the bacteria. “Green feedstocks like this need to be available at large scales,” Dalvie said, referring to the acetate made from carbon dioxide and electricity.

The study did not analyze the economics of capturing carbon dioxide. “The price of carbon needs to be high enough to justify a new biochemical process,” he stressed.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Biotechnology.
