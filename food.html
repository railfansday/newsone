The 7 Most Common Mistakes Home Cooks Make When Cooking Mashed Potatoes

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The simplest Thanksgiving side deserves better.

In a traditional Thanksgiving meal, mashed potatoes are a seemingly simple side dish that does a lot of heavy lifting. They're the perfect vessel for a puddle of gravy, and they’re a creamy complement to everything else on your plate—yes, even your aunt's questionable green bean casserole.

Mashed potatoes prove you can transform even the humblest ingredients into something magical. And yet, we've all somehow still eaten bad ones. With a recipe this basic, even the smallest mistakes have nowhere to hide.

Thankfully, those mistakes are 100% avoidable. We rounded up the most common ways to mess up mashed potatoes so that yours turn out as delicious as possible this holiday season.

It doesn’t matter if you cut your potatoes into a fine dice or rough chunks. Whatever size you pick, stick with it. Making sure your potato pieces are roughly the same size will help them all cook at the same rate. If your knife work is haphazard, you may end up with some pieces that are perfectly cooked, others that are waterlogged and mushy, and some that are still firm in the center.

Like pasta, potatoes need a good amount of seasoning to really shine—which is why salting the cooking water is so important. You can be generous with the salt, since much of it stays in the water and ends up going down the drain. When you start seasoning early in the cooking process, it’s easier to build flavor and adjust as you go.

In the hustle of cooking all of your Thanksgiving dishes, you may be tempted to boil your potatoes ahead of time and mash them later in the day. But it’s always better to work with potatoes while they’re still hot and easier to mash. Waiting until they’re cool will likely require far more elbow grease—and you’ll still end up with uneven chunks.

A smooth, creamy mash is the height of decadence. To make sure the potatoes are lump-free, some people may reach for a blender—but this is one of the most consequential mistakes you can make. Potatoes are naturally starchy, and the more you work them, the more starch gets released from their cell walls. Breaking out the blender may be well-intentioned, but it can leave you with potatoes that are gummy and gluey. Instead, opt for the right tool, like a hand masher, food mill, or potato ricer.

Another important thing to consider is that potato starches continue absorbing moisture as mashed potatoes sit. They can soak up the milk you’ve added, leaving your potatoes dry and dense. Don’t be afraid to add more liquid—or at least keep some extra on hand to loosen up your mash before serving.

Anthony Bourdain famously pointed to generous amounts of salt and butter as part of what makes restaurant food taste so good. That’s especially true for mashed potatoes. You may be scared to add more than a light sprinkle of salt, but don’t forget to taste and adjust the seasoning before serving. A little extra salt can make all the difference for your Thanksgiving dinner.

Whether you’re feeding your immediate family or a small army, you should always make a big batch of mashed potatoes. This side is one of the most popular components of a Thanksgiving menu, so it never hurts to make extra. It’s also one of the most versatile dishes for repurposing leftovers. You can transform them into gnocchi, shepherd’s pie, or crispy fried croquettes. It may require more mashing in the moment, but your future self will thank you.

9 Most Overlooked Thanksgiving TV Episodes, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Come November, it seems like the same Thanksgiving episodes circulate on every streaming list. They're the Friends holiday flashbacks, the parade mishaps of Seinfeld, the tofurkey served by Mrs. Kim on Gilmore Girls. If you've seen one "best Thanksgiving TV" list, you've seen them all. And sure, those episodes earned their spot, but the holiday is a pressure cooker for good TV. One table, too much wine, friends and relatives who know exactly which buttons to push, and a meal no one can escape.

There are plenty of episodes out there celebrating the day spent stuffing faces and cringing at historical re-tellings of Pilgrims and Native Americans getting along. But for every episode that makes the annual rotation, there's one just as good that never gets its due. In fact, the Thanksgiving TV canon runs deeper than most people realize. These are the ones worth adding to your queue.

After nine seasons of burning bridges across Philadelphia, the It's Always Sunny gang decides to use Thanksgiving dinner to make peace with everyone they've wronged. The motivation isn't guilt — it's that they've run out of places to go. Dennis (Glenn Howerton) tallies up the bars, restaurants, and entire neighborhoods they've been banned from, and the only solution anyone can come up with is inviting the McPoyles, the Waitress (Mary Elizabeth Ellis), Liam (Jimmi Simpson), and other recurring casualties of the Gang's sociopathy to Mac (Rob Mac) and Dennis' apartment for a reconciliation feast. The dinner devolves almost immediately.

Every attempt at an apology turns into a new argument. The food fight starts early, and the episode ends with the Gang barricading their guests inside the apartment, drilling the door shut, and leaving while the place catches fire. It's the season finale, and it works as a perfect inversion of what a Thanksgiving episode is supposed to do. Where most shows use the holiday to bring people together, It's Always Sunny uses it to prove that some people are fundamentally incapable of change, and it's funnier for being honest about it.

Bob Belcher (H. Jon Benjamin) takes his Thanksgiving turkey extremely seriously, so when his carefully brined bird keeps ending up in the toilet, he treats it as a crime scene. The kids all have motives (mostly to mess with Bob), though Linda (John Roberts) is too cheerful to be innocent, and Gayle's (Megan Mullally) cats are lurking around with unclear intentions. Every time Bob replaces the turkey, it winds up back in the toilet. He goes through three birds before the episode is over.

The payoff is both absurd and oddly sweet: Bob has been sleepwalking and putting the turkey in the toilet himself because he's dreaming about potty-training a young Tina (Dan Mintz). It's the kind of reveal that could only work on Bob's Burgers, where the emotional logic is always a little sideways but completely sincere. The Belchers' Thanksgiving episodes are a tradition unto themselves at this point, but "Turkey in a Can" is one of the best, a holiday mystery that wraps up with Bob coming to terms with his kids growing up.

By Season 5, Ghosts has gotten really good at finding new ways to strand Sam (Rose McIver) between the living and the dead. "Planes, Shanes and Automobiles" sends her on a book tour road trip with ghost Pete (Richie Moriarty) riding along, and the two of them get on each other's nerves almost immediately. When an airline strike threatens to strand them and wreck their Thanksgiving plans, Pete channels his inner John Candy.

Back at Woodstone, the ghosts are fed up with Jay's (Utkarsh Ambudkar) inability to meet their demands while Sam is gone, so they recruit Kyle (Ben Feldman), the only other living person who can see them. Kyle immediately one-ups Jay at ghost-sitting, and the rivalry between them is a low-key highlight of the whole season. The episode ends with Kyle joining the Arondekars' first Thanksgiving at Woodstone, and the found-family energy of that final scene is the kind of thing Ghosts has always done well, even when the rest of the TV landscape has moved on from network sitcoms.

Five episodes in, Succession was still finding its legs, and then the Roys sat down for Thanksgiving dinner. Logan (Brian Cox) hosts at his penthouse, and the guest list alone is a disaster waiting to happen: his estranged brother Ewan (James Cromwell) shows up from Canada, Kendall (Jeremy Strong) is quietly building a vote of no confidence, Connor (Alan Ruck) brings Willa (Justine Lupe) and raises eyebrows across the table, and Tom (Matthew Macfayden) is starting to realize what he's actually signed up for. Everyone at this dinner has an agenda, and the holiday gives them just enough cover to pretend they don't.

The episode's title comes from a children's memory game the family plays after dinner, and it's during this game that Logan, showing signs of cognitive decline from his stroke, strikes his grandson Iverson (Quentin Morales). It's a moment that reframes everything that came before it. The rest of this list skews funny, messy, heartfelt. This one is none of those things, and it's here anyway, because Thanksgiving isn't always sweet. Some families weaponize the dinner table, and the Roys do it better than anyone on television.

This one aired on Thanksgiving night in 1999, and Sean Hayes absolutely demolished it. Jack's (Hayes) mom Judith (Veronica Cartwright) comes to Will (Eric McCormack) and Grace's (Debra Messing) apartment for the holiday, and Will and Grace are stunned to realize she has no idea her son is gay. Jack has been telling her that he and Grace used to date. Karen (Megan Mullally) jumps in, claiming to be Jack's current girlfriend, and the deception snowballs from there until Jack's friends push him to tell his mother the truth. For a show that wore its queerness proudly from the pilot, the fact that Jack had never come out to his own mother added real weight.

Hayes plays the scene where he finally tells her with the kind of vulnerability he didn't always get to show on the series, and it makes the comedy around it hit differently. The episode won the series a special Thanksgiving timeslot and helped cement Will & Grace's second season as the one that earned its Outstanding Comedy Emmy.

The Pearson family Thanksgiving episodes of This Is Us became a whole thing over six seasons, but the first one is where the template was set. Jack (Milo Ventimiglia) and Rebecca (Mandy Moore) are driving the kids to her parents' house when things go sideways, and they end up at a run-down motel called the Pinewood Lodge. The manager wears a pilgrim hat and calls himself "Pilgrim Rick." Jack, being Jack, puts on the hat, declares himself the new Pilgrim Rick, and turns a busted Thanksgiving into a family tradition that the Pearson kids carry into adulthood.

In the present-day timeline, that tradition is still going on, but the holiday takes a devastating turn when Randall (Sterling K. Brown) discovers that Rebecca knew about his biological father and kept it from him for his entire life. He confronts her during the family's yarn game, and the scene is a gut punch. The genius of "Pilgrim Rick" is how it holds both things at once: the warmth of a family building something out of nothing and the fractures that were always underneath.

When this episode aired in November 2001, Brad Pitt and Jennifer Aniston were married, which turned a solid Friends Thanksgiving into a full-on pop culture event. Pitt plays Will Colbert, a formerly overweight high school classmate of Ross (David Schwimmer) and Monica (Courteney Cox) who shows up to Thanksgiving dinner looking like, well, Brad Pitt. The twist: Will co-founded the "I Hate Rachel Green Club" with Ross back in the day, and he has not moved on.

The episode's best gag is the slow reveal that Ross' grudge against Rachel was organized enough to have its own club, its own membership, and its own rumor campaign. Although Joey (Matt LeBlanc) eating an entire turkey by himself while wearing Phoebe's (Lisa Kudrow) maternity pants is a great subplot. The real-life casting adds a weird meta layer that makes the whole thing funnier in retrospect, and it earned Pitt an Emmy nomination for Outstanding Guest Actor in a Comedy Series.

Modern Family spent eleven seasons proving that the funniest version of family dysfunction is the kind where everyone genuinely means well. Phil (Ty Burrell) dreams too hard, Claire (Julie Bowen) controls too much, Jay (Ed O'Neill) avoids feelings, Gloria (Sofía Vergara) pulls them out of him like teeth, and every week, the confessional interviews let them explain exactly why they were right before the episode proved them wrong. "Three Turkeys" takes all of that and "stuffs it" into one house in one afternoon.

Phil and Luke (Nolan Gould) take over Thanksgiving dinner. Claire lets them, and also secretly cooks a backup turkey, because trusting Phil with a major holiday meal is a bridge too far. Jay and Gloria, meanwhile, fake a trip to Colombia just to get out of going. It's the kind of episode where every character has a totally reasonable plan that falls apart the second it touches anyone else's totally reasonable plan. But the Thanksgiving setting makes everything tighter than a regular episode, because nobody can leave and dinner is on a deadline.

Casting Jamie Lee Curtis and Rob Reiner as Jess' (Zooey Deschanel) divorced parents was one of the best moves New Girl ever made, and this episode is their debut. Jess invites her mom Joan and dad Bob to the loft for Thanksgiving dinner, planning separate meals so they don’t have to see each other. When they both show up at the same time, Jess pivots into full Parent Trap mode, enlisting Nick (Jake Johnson) and Cece (Hannah Simone) to help reunite them. The highlight here is how the rest of the friend group is roped into Jess' childhood scheme.

Curtis and Johnson, basting a turkey together a bit too sexually, have an off-the-rails energy while Reiner and Deschanel share some quiet, grounded father-daughter scenes New Girl didn't always make time for. And then there's Schmidt (Max Greenfield) versus his cousin Big Schmidt (Rob Riggle), which turns Thanksgiving dinner into a physical competition nobody wanted. It's a crowded episode, but New Girl's second season was the show at its most confident, and "Parents" holds all of it together.

Avery Fisher of Delphi, Indiana, and his best friend since high school, Nathan Van Dokkenburg, put in for an Indiana parks draw hunt on the recommendation of a buddy. Both drew the two-day Nov. 16-17 firearms season.

Indiana has special limited-draw, short-term deer hunts on about 25 public parks around the state. The hunts help manage the burgeoning deer herds, and most allow sportsmen to take three deer per hunt, with a limit of one buck. The number of hunters drawn for each park varies, but about 150 hunters are typically allowed on a park for each two-day hunt.

Neither Fisher nor Van Dokkenburg had ever walked the park they drew to hunt, which they declined to name. But they spent a lot of time figuring out where they should be on the property well before the hunt.

“We studied a lot of Google Maps and onX to get an idea where we should hunt far back in the park well away from other hunters,” Fisher tells Outdoor Life. “It’s rolling hills with big creek drains and draws … I’ve hunted that kind of land previously, and I had a good idea where to go early that Nov. 16 opening morning.”

The hunters checked in when the park opened at 5 a.m. and were prepared for an all day sit with packs, gear, and food.

“We got far back into the park, about one and a half miles from the road,” says Fisher, 25, who works as a financial advisor. “We stayed on ridgetops, and about one mile in, Nathan and I split up.

Fisher went about another half mile, hiking by the light of his headlamp.

“In the dark I spotted a tree line that had a lot of buck rubs. Then I saw a doe, and I figured it was a pretty good place.”

He climbed about 30 feet off the ground and settled into his saddle with his coffee and snacks his favorite .44-cal Ruger, a gift from his father years ago.

It was cold early that morning, and he heard something in a nearby ravine around 7:45 a.m.

“I thought I saw a buck and put my binoculars on it, and it was one with a good spread and nice tines about 100 yards away.”

He raised his rifle and rested it against the tree for support while looking for the buck in his scope.

“But my breath kept fogging the scope,” he says. “I kept cleaning it with my gloves, and the deer kept getting closer … I tried not looking at his rack as he turned uphill and then I saw his sticker points as he was rubbing trees.”

Fisher found the shoulder in his scope and squeezed the trigger. The buck ran uphill past Fisher, wobbling as it went. When the buck stopped at 60 yards, Fisher shot again. The buck dropped.

Fisher texted Van Dokkenburg and told him he’d shot a nice buck, but to keep hunting because they were allowed to shoot up to three deer apiece.

“A group of does came in at about 9 a.m., and I shot one and it fell,” Fisher says. “I decided not to shoot any more deer that morning.

“We had to bone and pack out two deer like we were on a Western hunt. I figured that was enough for us to lug out of there that day.”

Van Dokkenburg saw nothing to shoot and met up with Fisher about 11 a.m., filming Fisher as he first walked up to his buck and realized how huge it was.

“I was speechless at the size of his rack,” says Fisher. “I knew he was big, but not a rack like that. I’m grateful and thankful to the Lord.”

Using a wheeled cart and backpacks, the two hunters hauled both deer back down to their vehicle.

“I think my pack with deer and gear weighed over 100 pounds, and I had to stop and offload more from my pack to the wheeled cart,” Fisher says.

They then drove to check in their deer with park personnel.

Fisher’s 13-point buck has been measured with an unofficial green score of 199 5/8 inches. It turned out Fisher’s second shot had been unnecessary, since his first had struck the heart.

“We saw some deer, including another far-off buck, but we didn’t shoot another deer that second day,” he says. “Nathan is drawn for another two-day park hunt later this year, so he still has a good chance of taking deer, maybe even a huge buck.”

Te Araroa: more muddy boogeymen

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In order to walk Te Araroa you must accept mud into your heart (and shoes and socks and blisters). You also must never trust roots for they are deceivers and false friends (slippery, so slippery). The dome is no exception to these rules. You will never escape the mud until you have completed the trail.

Dome day dawned and I was NERVOUS. I had been thinking about a zero for about a day and a half before that, but had kept pushing on because I wanted to stay with my group. I also made the biggest mistake of my life, not having a coffee.

The dome began with fields of mud, and our group split up pretty early into the day. It was the kind of terrain that we all prefer to navigate solo, it’s no fun when people are watching you slip and slide up and down a muddy hill, or you feel like you’re holding others up. I was feeling so rough mentally, and my hiker hunger had really started to kick in the night before, so I spent the morning mentally cataloguing all the food I had left and whether it would be enough to get me to the dairy in Puhoi. I was exhausted physically and mentally before the day even really began. But I kept going.

When I told people about walking Te Araroa I talked a lot about wanting to build resilience and the ability to keep going even when I really didn’t want to. Some part of me knew this was the first moment where I could start to learn that. So singing broken clips of songs to myself and sometimes even counting my steps, I trudged on.

I caught up with Sinèad at the end of the first big hill and descent section, and we walked the road section together. I fantasized about going to one of the open houses along the road and asking them for a coffee. I didn’t think they’d let my muddy hiker self in so I kept moving.

When we approached the next big, forested hill section of the day we found a fellow hiker, Xavier, eating half his lunch. I immediately sat down and ate a handful of every one of my accessible snacks. The others took off and I spent a further ten minutes talking myself into continuing. I knew that on the other side of this hill was a campsite where, if I really wanted to, I could stop for the night. I also knew that in less than a kilometre I’d hit the 500km mark.

I felt so sleepy, not just tired but burny-eyes-sleepy. I thought desperately about the single sachet of instant coffee deep in my food bag and sang “just keep swimming” to myself. By the time I was about ¾ through this hill I was once again mentally inventorying my meals, trying to decide if maybe I could break this section into two days.

Then I heard some girls ahead of me and like a puppy seeing its people again after a long work day, I perked up, thinking it was my friends. Unfortunately it was just two very clean-looking, white-sneakered day hikers.

Though they weren’t who I was hoping to see, the whiteness of their sneakers told me that from here, the trail would be more chill. I pushed on and eventually I could hear Sinèad’s accent floating through the trees. And there was our friend, Zoe, getting water from the stream! I crossed the stream and climbed up to the campground where I was delighted to find there was a picnic table so I wouldn’t have to sit on the ground.

I laid out my very wet tent to dry (still thinking about staying there), drank the last of my water, and filtered some into my mayo-turned cold soak jar to shake up my last instant coffee sachet. Sinèad said she watched me essentially reboot as soon as I started drinking the coffee.

It was 1:30 at this point, we’d been walking for six and a half hours, and I didn’t really have a sense of how much further there was to go. I decided to take a long lunch and to eat two wraps, food inventory be damned!

And then my saviours began to arrive. First was another set of day hikers, the older of whom helpfully told me I seemed very tired (I did not hold back when I agreed with him). And then Annabel, who I had been worried about because she normally walks at a similar pace to me and I hadn’t seen her all day.

After some lecturing about my choice to not wear boots, the day hikers gave me a bag of pumpkin seeds, dates and ANZAC biscuits. The snacks were enough to convince me to put my wet, muddy socks back on and walk the final five or six kilometres to the dome cafe.

Annabel and I climbed the third and final hill of the day which was actually suddenly easy terrain, and then descended a really well-graded, touristy track. I honestly felt like I was flying on that terrain and having Annabel around to chat to made it go really quickly. After eleven hours of walking (including the two hour lunch I took) we made it to camp. The dome cafe was an oasis, it had a bathtub and yes I did sit down in it. I pitched my tent and we all took turns having a hot shower and I think that place saved my life. My mashed potatoes have never tasted better. This was a big day of learning for me. Mostly that I can do it even when it’s a tough day.

I hope my work colleagues enjoy seeing my muddy sock water

But it would be disingenuous to choose anything else because it is what was mostly going through my mind when I wasn’t inventorying my food or dreaming of coffee.

The Tucson Restaurant Turning Cactus Into Fine Dining

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Traipsing through Tucson Mountain Park, a Midwestern blow-in might not identify much to eat. Plants armed with burs, barbs, and spines all but scream “do not touch.” For chef Aaron Lopez and June Chee, however, this prickly, dusty landscape stocks a pantry of possibility. The married co-owners of Ursa see the Arizona desert as a rich ground for foraging.

Gray, tangled brittlebush, which the average Tucsonan walks or drives by almost daily, can be pressure-cooked, caramelized until black, and baked into cake. Dusty, sage-colored salt bush, when hung to dry, takes on an intensely umami, aquatic saltiness like seaweed. The sweet buds of the Seussian-green palo verde tree become briny, like olives, when suffused with lye.

Don’t even get Lopez started on the dozens of cacti varieties that thrive here, which the restaurant sources from local grower Toadlet Farm and which Lopez harvests throughout the year. The restaurant processes the young and mature paddles, fruit and their seeds, buds, and flowers, which are then grilled, poached, cold-smoked, juiced, dehydrated, nixtamalized, or preserved into jam, wine, and vinegar.

There are hundreds of edible plants in the Sonoran Desert. Ursa strives to showcase their potential, one 10-course meal at a time.

“We treat Ursa as an art showroom, demonstrating to people what these plants can do, what sustainable means, and what was here before it got easy,” says Lopez. The idea was inspired in part by his Sonoran grandfather, Hector Lopez, who died in the spring. “[He] was the first person I ever heard say they’re proud to be from the desert,” the chef said.

Ursa began as a pop-up in El Centro, California, in 2023. Lopez and Chee moved to a historic brick building in downtown Tucson, re-opening their doors in October 2025. It’s a homecoming of sorts for the couple, who are both from the Sonoran Desert borderlands. They met in Hawaii, where Chee was working in clean energy and transportation. Lopez, meanwhile, spent roughly 18 years cooking in fine dining and modern progressive restaurants like Redbird and Orsa & Winston in LA, and Pai Honolulu. But he was longing for the desert.

After helping a friend open a restaurant and hosting a few popups in Austin, Lopez and Chee bought a desert foraging guide, packed up their two dogs and began driving around the Southwest. It made sense to settle in Tucson, which was recognized by UNESCO in 2015 as a Creative City of Gastronomy for its continuous 4,000-year agricultural history and deeply rooted Sonoran Desert food traditions.

“That’s a unique thing about being here, the connection from seed to plant to restaurant that we hadn’t been able to grasp before,” Chee says.

Consider a recent course that Ursa built around tepary beans, in which the ancient, nutty bean is refried and topped with h:al squash that’s been lactofermented. The squash’s leaves are fermented to make Japanese-style kosho and its flowers are pickled, dried, crushed with dried saltbush, then tossed with barrel cactus seeds for a desert furikake. It’s served with grilled coins of chorizo made from Lopez’s great-grandmother’s recipe and housemade flour tortillas, to all be washed down with an amazake drink made from fermented wheat and goat fat. Don’t get too attached, however. Riffs owing to finicky ingredient availability occur here almost daily.

Running a restaurant using only ingredients foraged and farmed from within about eight to 10 miles means abiding by pretty intense guardrails. The only wheat used is pima club, an ancient soft white variety cultivated for centuries by the Akimel O’odham (Pima) people. Ursa does not use cucumbers, radishes or citrus.

“We’re not trying to substitute anything, like, ‘How do we achieve cucumber? Let’s grab a cactus,’” Lopez says. “We have to throw everything out, really strip it down. Sometimes we have to let the ingredients be bitter or spicy. We have to put our egos aside and let the ingredient be the star.”

For staff who didn’t grow up or learn to cook here, the learning curve at Ursa can feel a little like alighting on a new planet, even if certain flora happen to resemble prickly weeds in their own backyards.

“We have these really cool moments in the restaurant where people are like, Oh! I have lamb’s quarter just growing in my yard!” Chee says of the spinach-like desert green that appears all over Ursa’s menu.

A young Aaron Lopez with his grandfather Hector Lopez.

Ursa’s approach isn’t always easy for customers to understand, so the couple came up with a more down-to-earth option. This fall, the restaurant debuted a four-course, family-style Lineage menu for $75, alongside the existing 10-course Harvest menu ($150). Not Ribeye: Bobby Flay Recommends This Steak Cut For The Best Tacos

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When taco night rolls around, you'll want to head to the butcher and grab some good meat. If you're making carne asada, your instinct might be to pick the ribeye. While it might be the gold standard of steak, tacos and ribeye are an inherent mismatch. Tacos are one of the most democratic foods out there, while ribeye is made for deep pockets and expensive tastes. Even Food Network's Bobby Flay recommends skipping it. Instead, he swaps the pricey cut for the seriously underrated skirt steak.

Ribeye is America's favorite steak thanks to its rich marbling and tender texture, but you'll rarely find it in taquerias. Beyond being pricey, ribeye's assets are its downfall in tacos. In an interview with Food and Wine, Flay revealed the reason why he often chooses to skip ribeye altogether: "[Ribeye] can be inconsistent because the fat doesn't always melt even when cooked properly." This is a real problem in tacos. Encased in a taco, you don't have the option to trim around a rubbery pocket of fat or leave it on the plate. Even if you trim the fat before adding the steak to your tacos, it's so unevenly distributed that you risk ending up with wildly inconsistent slices.

Unlike ribeye, skirt is cut from the plate primal, a hard-working area near the diaphragm. Rather than being streaked with generous ribbons of fat, it's comprised of long, coarse muscle fibers, which give it a chewier, more fibrous bite along with a bold, beef-forward flavor. Its thin profile may not win it fans at white tablecloth restaurants, but it's exactly why taquerias can't get enough of it. All that surface area compared to its thickness means skirt steak cooks quickly, soaks up marinades fast, and slices evenly, which is everything you want from taco meat.

There's no cut of beef more well-suited to carne asada than skirt. Literally translating to "grilled meat," the beef is usually marinated in a citrus and garlic blend, with the acidic ingredients helping to tenderize the surface of the meat. After being left to soak up the flavor for a couple of hours, it's then seared or grilled over an open flame or charcoal to impart a smoky, charred flavor (an air fryer also works if you can't grill). Because skirt is such a thin cut, it doesn't enjoy spending too much time over high heat; it only needs around two to four minutes per side. Skirt can be prone to drying out, so it would be wise to avoid cooking past a medium doneness.

How you cut skirt is just as important as how you cook it. Compared to other cuts, skirt has particularly long, dense muscle fibers, and if you slice them with the grain, you'll leave them intact, resulting in chewy, rubbery bites. Slicing against the grain shortens these fibers, turning them into more satisfying, bite-sized strands. To identify the grain, look closely at the surface of the meat to spot the direction of the muscle fibers running through the beef.

Although skirt is beloved by taquerias, that doesn't make it suited to all beef tacos. One popular style where we would skip skirt is birria, the slow-cooked, tender beef marinated in a chili-and-spice adobo served with a flavorful broth for dipping. The whole concept of birria revolves around slow-braising, requiring tough, collagen-rich cuts like chuck or beef cheek, which break down into tender, shreddable meat over several hours. While skirt does have some connective tissue, it's simply too thin and lean to survive hours of prolonged heat.

This 30-Mile-Long Canyonlands Art Gallery Might Be Utah’s Best Backpacking Trip

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When you think of ancient rock art, what comes to mind? Handprints? Stick figures? Maybe a long-limbed man holding something? For the most part, you’d be right. The petroglyphs and pictographs of the southwest are beautiful, but they aren’t known for their intricate details.

But there’s one canyon in Utah that blows those expectations out of the water.

Hidden deep inside Canyonlands National Park, Salt Creek Canyon holds some of the most impressive pictographs I’ve ever seen. Enormous human figures, elaborate patterns, and colorful paintings that have survived for hundreds of years. It’s an open-air art museum, and one that’s slowly weathering away.

And the museum doesn’t stop at rock art. There are Ancestral Puebloan granaries, settlers’ abandoned ranch buildings, and reminders of the conservationists who helped protect this landscape.

A walk through Salt Creek Canyon is a walk through history. Over two days and more than 30 miles, it became one of my biggest backpacking surprises of the year.

Trail: Salt Creek Trail + Angel Arch Detour Start: Cathedral Butte / Salt Creek Canyon Trailhead Finish: Cave Spring / Salt Creek Gate Distance: 30.78 miles (my GPS; mapped distance may differ) Elevation gain: 1,054 feet (my GPS) Type: Point-to-point, vehicle shuttle required Permits: Required for overnight trips Highlight: The All American Man pictograph

The trail begins high in the contested Bears Ears National Monument, in the shadow of Cathedral Butte. Typically, this is the sort of view you’d have to earn after hours of climbing. Instead, you start at the highest point, looking down into a canyon that stretches toward the horizon.

It’s mostly downhill from here, but don’t mistake that for easy. Thirty miles with a loaded backpack is still 30 miles, especially when much of it is through deep sand.

About two miles in, we crossed into Canyonlands National Park. My Gaia GPS map was littered with waypoints and “Don’t miss!” markers, the first of which were Kirk Spring and Kirk’s Cabin.

In an otherwise dusty landscape of sagebrush and juniper, a little oasis appeared. Reeds, cottonwoods, and bright green vegetation surrounded a small spring. We filtered some water and continued toward the cabin, an 1890s-era log home with a fieldstone chimney.

Outside the weathered log structure sat the wheels and axles of an old wagon. Inside, bear traps and antlers hung on the walls. It looked like something out of a history museum, only sandier, dustier, and considerably more authentic.

This was our first introduction to one of Salt Creek’s defining qualities: Layers of human history occupy nearly every corner of the canyon.

Long before ranchers arrived, Indigenous people—Ancestral Puebloans and Fremont, then Ute, Paiute, and Diné—lived, hunted, and farmed here. Later came European explorers, conservationists, and eventually backpackers like us. Different people, different centuries, but all drawn to the same remarkable place.

One of the biggest reasons was water. Unlike much of Canyonlands, Salt Creek supports a surprisingly lush corridor of vegetation, even in a dry year. We encountered several places to filter water, although sources became scarcer farther down the canyon. (Water here is seasonal and never guaranteed, so come prepared to carry plenty.)

Around mile six, a small spur trail led us beneath a sandstone overhang. Tucked into the alcove were several stone structures, likely granaries used by Ancestral Puebloan people to store their harvests. Just below them, dark-green vines spilled across the sand. They looked like squash plants, still thriving in the shadow of structures built centuries ago.

I’d come to Salt Creek expecting incredible rock art, but nothing prepared me for what we found around mile eight: The All American Man.

High inside a sandstone alcove stands a human-like figure, nearly life-size, painted in striking blue, white, and red. He appears to wear white boots, an elaborate headdress, and something resembling an American flag across his body.

For decades, the figure’s unusual appearance led some to question whether it was even ancient. Radiocarbon testing eventually confirmed its authenticity, suggesting it dates to around the 14th century. Standing below it, I found the whole thing almost impossible to believe. (Do yourself a favor: If you plan on hiking Salt Creek, don’t look up pictures of the All American Man beforehand. Let it surprise you.)

Not far beyond, we encountered another extraordinary panel near Four Faces Spring. Four enormous, intricately patterned faces stared out from beneath a sandstone alcove. Remnants of stone structures sat beneath them.

Who were these figures? Were they people, spirits, or something else entirely?

Their exact meaning remains uncertain, but the artwork survives, part of a cultural history that remains important to Indigenous communities today.

The farther we walked, the more the canyon changed.

Towering sandstone walls closed in around us. Willows, reeds, and cottonwoods transformed the dusty trail into something resembling a jungle. Small streams appeared beneath the vegetation.

Unfortunately, so did the mosquitoes. I’ve spent a lot of time hiking through the desert, but I’ve never encountered so many of the little suckers.

After miles of hiking, a soft rain finally brought some relief. A rainbow appeared against the red canyon walls just as we approached SC4, our reserved campsite for the evening.

At roughly mile 12, SC4 is the last of the four designated Salt Creek campsites when traveling north. It wasn’t quite halfway through our route, which meant we had an even longer second day ahead.

The next morning, we packed up and headed toward another of the canyon’s great attractions. Several miles off the main trail, at the end of a remote side canyon, stood Angel Arch.

Arches National Park may be famous for its sandstone formations, but Angel Arch can hold its own against just about anything you’ll find there.

Its massive sandstone opening rises roughly 135 feet high, with an unusual formation beside it resembling an angel leaning forward with folded wings. It’s an incredible sight, made even more impressive by the effort required to reach it. We were the only people there. In fact, we were the only hikers on the entire trail: We hadn’t seen a single other person or group. The detour added several miles to an already long day, but I’d do it again without hesitation.

Back on the main route, one more archaeological treasure awaited us: the Flying Carpet Panel. Like the All American Man and Four Faces, the artwork was enormous, with figures and intricate designs spread across the sandstone.

Then came the final miles. The beautiful canyon trail eventually gave way to a sandy four-wheel-drive road, where every step seemed to require twice the effort. After nearly 30 miles, walking through deep sand was just about the last thing my legs wanted to do.

Eventually, we reached a gate, a paved road, and the end of our journey.

As spectacular as the scenery was, what I’ll remember most about Salt Creek isn’t the canyon itself. It’s the people who called it home.

For centuries, Ancestral Puebloan communities farmed along the creek, stored food in cliffside granaries, and created artwork that still watches over the canyon today. Walking past these places, I couldn’t help thinking about how many people had traveled this same canyon before us. How many had looked up at those same cliffs, collected water from those same springs, or sheltered beneath those sandstone overhangs?

We spent two days here, carrying everything we needed on our backs. For others, this canyon was home. For a brief moment, we got to be part of its story too.

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

Before we jump into specific styles, however, it’s useful to give a quick rundown on some of the broad strokes of what makes a Japanese-style knife unique.

Many Japanese knives are designed for push cuts or pull-through slicing rather than the rocking chop of a heavy, Western chef’s knife. They tend to have a straighter edge than their Western counterparts, making them slightly less adept at speeding through a mince of herbs, but more precise in their cuts. Most Japanese-style knives also feature a sheep’s foot tip that curves down to the point, giving the user more control over fine-point work.

Japanese knives are often made with a harder steel than their Western counterparts. Harder steel helps a knife’s edge retain sharpness, which lets manufacturers use steeper sharpening angles for a finer edge. This helps most Japanese knives slice more delicately than Western knives, though harder steel can chip on material like ice or bone.

Traditional Japanese-style knives are only sharpened on one side, giving them a much finer edge than the 50/50 bevel of Western-style knives that are sharpened on both sides. Some modern Japanese knife makers have pivoted to a 50/50 style bevel for ease of sharpening; however, when you’re shopping, you’ll encounter plenty of knives with 70/30 bevel with the inside edge (the left edge for a right-handed knife) sharpened at a steeper angle. A 70/30 bevel is easier to maintain than a single-bevel but offers a sharper edge than a 50/50 bevel.

Many traditional Japanese kitchen knives feature a wa-style handle—an octagonal wood handle that envelops a hidden knife tang (the metal part of the knife that connects to the handle). Wa-style handles help keep knives lighter and nimbler, while many Western-style knives use full-tang construction, adding weight and heft to the blade. Many modern Japanese knifemakers, though, combine full-tang knife handles with Japanese blade shapes to create a hybrid style knife that’s both durable and adept at slicing.

The gyuto, or Japanese chef’s knife, originated in Japan in the early 1900s, when French cooking began to gain popularity in a rapidly modernizing country. This knife style took cues from the traditional French chef’s knife and combined them with elements of traditional Japanese knife-making. The resulting Japanese take on a chef’s knife was primarily designed for slicing meat, though it also features a curved edge that makes it easy to chop herbs. Compared to a Western chef’s knife, the gyuto has a straighter blade and a drop point tip with a lower overall profile. This is primarily designed so that the user can place the knife's tip on the cutting board and draw it through food, taking advantage of the gyuto’s sharp edge and thin blade for precision slicing. But while the gyuto excels at slicing meat, its general shape makes it an overall great pick to anchor your knife collection, and many home cooks opt for a gyuto as their daily driver over a traditional chef’s knife.

We’ve long been fans of the gyuto fan here at Bon Appétit, with MAC’s Professional Hollow Ground Chef’s Knife topping our chef’s knife review for years. For a wicked sharp, more traditional option, we really like the Kazan Arare by Shibata gyuto, which impressed us so much we had to give it its own review.

Increasingly common as a part of brands’ knife sets, the santoku knife is a relatively modern knife shape, first appearing in the 1940s or so. Nicknamed the “three virtues” knife, the santoku draws on the gyuto, nakiri, and deba to create a well-rounded utility knife that’s great for cutting meat, fish, and vegetables. Typically, these knives feature a pretty steep sheep’s foot tip for precision point work, a tall heel for push cuts, and just enough curve for pull-through slicing and rock-chopping. A santoku knife is great for well-rounded utility work—think cutting cheese and an apple for a snack, slicing leftover roasted chicken to make a sandwich, or dicing an onion for a curry.

Typically, we like a santoku knife that’s between six and seven inches, so it contrasts with the longer gyuto. Our favorite overall model is the MAC Professional Santoku Knife, but if you’d like a razor-sharp version with a traditional wa-style handle, we love the Kazan Ginsan Nashiji Santoku.

Nakiri knives have a straight edge and a sturdy spine, specifically designed for chopping vegetables. Sometimes referred to as a “vegetable cleaver,” the nakiri excels at push cuts, making it great for both matchsticking carrots and breaking down tough-skinned squash. Since most gyutos feature thin blades and fine edges, the nakiri also holds a spot in the Japanese knife lineup as a tougher, heavier knife for more demanding tasks. Traditional nakiri knives may also feature a blunt, rounded tip, which can be rotated against the cutting board to create a smooth, chopping motion.

The Tojiro DP Damascus Nakiri is by far our overall favorite tested nakiri knife for its extremely sharp edge and overall balance, but it’s also pricey. We also really like the Tojiro Classic nakiri, though it can have stock issues due to its popularity and price point.

The deba knife is a traditional Japanese knife designed for fish butchery. Unlike a Western fillet knife, which is designed to be flexible and nimble, the deba is a heavy, sturdy blade with a thick spine that gives the heel of the blade a cleaver-like feel. But a deba knife is also designed for precision slicing, and usually features a traditional single-bevel edge. That single-bevel edge allows the knife to come to a finer point than a double-bevel edge, and the sheep’s foot tip adds to the deba knife’s ability to portion out delicate fish fillets, as well.

We haven’t tested deba knives because they require a bit of knowledge and skill to use them for breaking down whole fish, but if you’re intrigued by the shape and want a cleaver-like knife for your kitchen, the Seki Mago Roku Deba from Kai is an affordable, solid option. Otherwise, we think most people would be better served looking to a nakiri or santoku knife.

Named for the French word “petite,” which means small, the petty knife is often a replica of the gyuto, just in a much, much smaller form. Most petty knives are around five inches long and feature a thin, lightly curved blade with a drop point tip and heel just tall enough to keep your fingers from bashing into the cutting board. Petty knives are great utility knives for small tasks, like dicing a shallot, slicing garlic, or slicing herbs, but they’re also long and nimble enough to be great slicers. Unless you do a lot of handheld turning and peeling, a petty knife is a great replacement for a paring knife in your lineup.

We found that both the MAC Professional Petty Knife and the Tojiro Classic Petty Knife were sharp enough to slice a steak while still being the perfect size to slice strawberries for your morning yogurt bowl.

Traditionally a fisherman’s knife used for all sorts of purposes on the boat, the funayuki is an old-school knife shape that predates the gyuto and santoku, and it likely helped inspire both shapes. The traditional funayuki is a shorter knife, around seven inches long, with a narrow, pointed tip and a tall heel. They are santoku-sized but gyuto-shaped. Unlike the deba knife, which was a heavy, single-bevel knife used for fish butchery, the funayuki was designed to be light and nimble so that fishermen could fillet their lunch while at sea. These days, most home cooks might pick up a funayuki as a secondary, shorter gyuto, or opt for this shape over a santoku as an all-purpose utility knife with a narrower tip.

Though it’s not as common in modern kitchens, one of our favorite Japanese chefs' knives is the Akiru-Saku Funayuki Gyuto, which is more traditionally shaped like a funayuki but is advertised as a gyuto since the two shapes are so similar.

The kiritsuke is a Japanese slicing knife with an angular “sword tip” that allows the user to make precision cuts. The initial design combined a long slicing shape and tall heel, making the kiritsuke a hybrid knife, similar to the santoku. For most people, the kiritsuke will be a version of an all-purpose chef’s knife that could take the place of a gyuto as a daily driver, with the big difference coming in the shape of the tip.

When reviewing chef’s knives, we found that the Shun Classic Kiritsuke was a great all-purpose chef’s knife with a unique tip that some people might prefer over a gyuto, since it curves upwards more like a Western chef’s knife and allows for more of a rock chop motion.

Boning and fillet knives are often unitaskers that don’t see much use unless you’re a fan of home butchery, but the Japanese honesuki is an even more specialized boning knife shape. Designed specifically for poultry, the honesuki is a steep, wedge-shaped knife with a very fine point and a tall heel. The goal is to have a knife that’s delicate enough to slip between a chicken joint while being sturdy enough to cut through cartilage. While the honesuki was designed for a specific purpose, we find that this shape is more versatile and can be swapped in for a petty knife for other small chopping and dicing tasks in ways that a curved fillet knife can’t.

We like the MAC Professional Honesuki as a great option that could tackle most boning-knife tasks while remaining sharp and versatile enough for utility work. If you find yourself breaking down a whole chicken while also wanting a strawberry-slicing knife, the honesuki could be a nonconventional multitasker.

Antarctic blue whales survived near extinction, but can they fully recover?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Antarctic blue whales are slowly recovering from whaling, but their population remains around 2% of its former size.

Several whale populations that were hunted hard in the 20th century have already climbed back to their old numbers. Antarctic blue whales, the largest blue whales on Earth, are not one of them.

Their numbers are rising again, but a new estimate suggests there are still only about 2% as many as there were before whaling began.

Since the last recorded catch in 1973, the population has grown nearly tenfold, from about 418 whales to about 4,018 in 2024.

The team got those numbers from a computer model rather than a head count, so each one comes with a lot of uncertainty.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington’s School of Marine and Environmental Affairs, led the work with colleagues in the US, Australia, Japan and South Africa.

It’s the first full assessment of these whales in more than 20 years.

“No whale was hunted quite to the extent that Antarctic blue whales were hunted,” Rand said.

In 1904, whalers killed 11 Antarctic blue whales. The hunt grew so fast that by 1930 they were taking 30,405 in a single year.

Breeding couldn’t keep up with a hunt on that scale. In many seasons, whalers removed between 10% and 40% of the whole population.

By the time the last recorded catch occurred in 1973, whalers had killed a total of 347,662 Antarctic blue whales.

The team estimates that about 206,000 Antarctic blue whales lived in the Southern Ocean before whaling, and that only about 418, roughly 0.2%, were left by 1973.

Even a ban didn’t stop the killing right away. The International Whaling Commission agreed to end the commercial catch of these whales in 1963, but illegal Soviet whaling carried on for about another decade.

Counting whales spread around an entire continent is hard, so the team pulled together three kinds of information, starting with the old catch records.

The second came from ship surveys, including three full trips around Antarctica between 1978 and 2004 that recorded every blue whale the crews saw. Japanese ships also surveyed part of the same waters from 1989 to 2008.

The third came from photographs, since each whale can be recognized from pictures of its sides.

By tracking how often the same whales reappeared in photos from 2003 to 2019, the team estimated about 2,889 in Antarctic waters for the 2018 to 2019 season.

The researchers then combined all three in one computer model. It estimated how many whales were alive in every year from 1904 to 2024.

The model’s best estimate for 2024 is 4,018 whales.

The estimated range runs from about 1,900 to 8,400 whales, and even the high end would be less than 5% of the population before whaling.

So the whales are coming back, but from an extremely low starting point.

The team estimates the population is growing by about 4.5% a year, slower than the 7.3% a year reported in the last assessment, published in 2004.

Co-author Trevor Branch of the University of Washington’s School of Aquatic and Fishery Sciences led that study.

The team traced the difference to updated Japanese survey numbers and the new photo-based count.

“I think this is relatively realistic for the kind of population growth we would expect from them,” Rand said.

If growth continues along the model’s path, Antarctic blue whales wouldn’t reach even 60% of their old numbers until around 2102.

The authors note that this date depends on how many whales lived there before whaling, a number that is itself uncertain.

Other whales have recovered much faster. Blue whales off western North America, gray whales in the eastern North Pacific and North Pacific humpbacks have all returned to or passed their pre-whaling numbers in the past decade.

But most of those populations weren’t pushed down nearly as far. Growth that starts from a few hundred animals takes a long time to add up.

“I think it’s probably an open question whether they will make it back to pre-whaling levels,” Rand added.

Antarctic blue whales feed mainly on Antarctic krill, small shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean. That food supply may have changed.

Large baleen whales dive deep, eat several tons of krill a day and excrete iron-rich waste near the surface.

Scientists think that waste feeds the tiny ocean plants krill depend on, so losing millions of whales may have meant less krill.

Blue whales returning now will likely face more competition for that krill. Humpback, fin and Antarctic minke whales eat it too, and they may be recovering faster.

Krill fishing is projected to grow, and earlier research has linked shrinking sea ice and warmer water to fewer krill.

The experience of southern right whales shows how recovery can stall.

Several of their populations are having fewer calves, with longer gaps between births, even though they’re still well below their pre-whaling size.

If the Southern Ocean can now support fewer blue whales than it once did, the authors wrote, the old population size may no longer be a realistic target.

The new numbers are probably on the low side. Ship surveys assume observers spot every whale directly in the ship’s path, but blue whales are often underwater, and some likely get missed.

Studies of other blue whale populations suggest those misses could make the survey estimates 4% to 25% too low.

The photo count assumes whales mix randomly around Antarctica each year. In fact, they return to the same ocean basin 54% to 64% of the time, compared with about 33% if they moved at random.

Both problems point toward more whales and faster growth than the model shows. Neither would change the main finding, which is that the population is growing but still far from recovered.

Keeping track is also getting harder, because full ship surveys around Antarctica have largely stopped.

Underwater microphones can pick up blue whale songs, but scientists still can’t turn the number of calls into a count of whales.

“Antarctica is a big place, and it’s not just one country’s ocean,” Rand said. “We really need more monitoring and continued monitoring to figure out what is going to happen to this species in the future.”

The authors called for more ship surveys, more photographs and more listening.

Without that work, researchers won’t be able to tell whether the recovery holds up as the Southern Ocean changes.

The full study was published in the journal Endangered Species Research.

Scientists figured out how to use engineered bacteria to dissolve rocks faster and capture carbon dioxide

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Engineered ocean bacteria kept rust off olivine sand and sped up rock weathering, a slow process that pulls carbon dioxide into seawater.

As rock slowly wears away, it releases compounds that trap carbon dioxide and take it out of the atmosphere. The trouble is speed. Left alone, the process takes thousands of years, partly because iron in the rock turns to rust and coats the surface.

Now, a team led by Harvard researchers has engineered a common ocean bacterium to keep that rust from building up. In tanks of real harbor water, the microbes made crushed rock dissolve about three times faster than plain seawater did.

That’s a big jump for a process that has looked too slow to matter. So far the researchers have tested it only in lab reactors and a few glass tanks, not in the open ocean.

Neil Dalvie, a synthetic biology fellow in systems biology at Harvard Medical School, led the study with colleagues at the Wyss Institute and the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability.

Dalvie told Earth.com he wanted to highlight the interdisciplinary nature of the work.

“We learned about alkalinity and mineral measurements from geochemists in the rock weathering industry,” he said. “Very few people have both the skillsets for molecular biology, microbial engineering, and mineral processing.”

“Some people describe rock weathering as a silver bullet for climate change,” said Dalvie. “The problem is that it’s too slow.”

The team worked with olivine, a common mineral, in the form of sand. When olivine dissolves in seawater, it gives off magnesium, silicon and iron.

The magnesium is the useful part. It makes seawater more alkaline, and alkaline seawater takes up carbon dioxide from the air. This is held as bicarbonate, a form of carbon dissolved in water.

Because magnesium stays in the ocean for a very long time, the authors count that carbon as stored for good.

Iron is the problem. It doesn’t stay dissolved. It forms a layer of rust on the rock, and that layer slows any further dissolving.

The rust is one reason that natural rock weathering takes so long. Spreading crushed rock on farm fields or coastlines helps, but the authors note that the carbon captured that way has been hard to measure.

Many microbes already have a way to deal with rust. When they run low on iron, they release molecules that grab iron and make it dissolve in water. Biologists call them siderophores.

Once a microbe has enough iron, though, it stops making these molecules. And a microbe doesn’t need much.

The team tested this with Alteromonas macleodii, a marine bacterium that makes its own siderophore. The researchers ran 16 small lab reactors with different amounts of olivine sand and different flow rates.

The bacteria made large amounts of the molecule only when there was little rock and the water moved fast.

With more rock, the bacteria had iron to spare and quit. The authors concluded that the bacterium’s natural settings rule out steady siderophore production in a tank big enough for industrial use.

So the team rewired the bacterium. They replaced the genetic switch that turns siderophore production on only when iron runs short with one that keeps it on all the time.

Dalvie said the change itself was fairly easy. Showing that it worked was the hard part.

“The bacteria keep growing, the cells keep making siderophores, the rock keeps dissolving – it’s very hard to measure all these things at the same time,” Dalvie said.

In the small reactors, the engineered strain produced more than 100 times as much of the siderophore as the natural strain. Olivine dissolved about 2.6 times faster with the engineered cells than in reactors with no cells at all, while the natural strain made no clear difference.

Then came a bigger test. The researchers placed more than 8.8 pounds (4 kilograms) of construction-grade olivine sand in the bottom of 3-gallon (11-liter) glass fish tanks.

Unfiltered seawater from Boston Harbor flowed through each tank at about 1.6 quarts (1.5 liters) a day. Some tanks got a slow drip of nutrients, and two of those also got a fresh dose of the engineered bacteria once a week.

In those two tanks, magnesium came off the rock 3.1 times faster. The water also became more alkaline, which the team calculated as about 0.018 ounce (0.50 grams) of carbon dioxide removed from the air each day.

That’s about half the weight of a paper clip. The amount is small, but it was measured directly in the water rather than estimated from a model.

In earlier small reactors fed raw seawater, the engineered strain held steady for about a week before its numbers fell. That’s why the pilot tanks got a fresh dose weekly.

The strain’s staying power surprised the team. “We were actually surprised that our originally engineered strains were able to survive and perform for over a week in unfiltered seawater,” Dalvie said.

“We think that biofilms may help protect the bacteria from growth competition and viruses in the seawater.”

Bacteria have to eat, and making their food releases carbon dioxide too. So the team ran a life-cycle analysis, an accounting of the emissions from building and running a full-size version.

Sugar failed on both counts. Making it emits too much, and in the lab, feeding it to the bacteria left the water less alkaline, likely because of acidic byproducts. The researchers switched to acetate, a simple food that some companies now make from carbon dioxide itself.

Ordinary bacteria failed the accounting too. Feeding wild microbes enough to keep them hungry for iron would release far more carbon dioxide than the faster weathering captured.

With engineered bacteria and a carbon-neutral acetate feed, the model predicted 74 percent more net carbon removal than a tank of rock and seawater alone. The researchers assumed the 2.6-fold speedup from the lab would hold at that scale.

“We’ve found a way that it could work and, maybe more importantly, now we have a model to calculate what it would look like to implement this at scale,” he said.

Four of the authors, Dalvie among them, have filed a patent application on strains of bacteria engineered for siderophore production that keep rust off rock.

The pilot tanks didn’t stay fast. After about three weeks of feeding, dissolving and carbon uptake dropped in every tank.

Slimy films of microbes had built up on the glass and on top of the sand, especially in the tanks that got the engineered bacteria.

The films appeared to keep the water from mixing, and the researchers suspect they blocked the rock. A thin crust had also formed, and most of the dissolving probably happened in the top 0.4 inch (1 centimeter) of sand.

The authors suggest that breaking up that top layer from time to time could keep the rock working, but they haven’t tested it. Dalvie and his colleagues say the next steps are larger experiments, better nutrient mixes and more efficient bacteria.

Dalvie said a real coastline or mine would need “some sort of enclosure or tank” to make enough of the iron-grabbing molecules. Such a tank “would resemble existing wastewater processes,” he said.

The bigger hurdle, he said, is the food for the bacteria. “Green feedstocks like this need to be available at large scales,” Dalvie said, referring to the acetate made from carbon dioxide and electricity.

The study did not analyze the economics of capturing carbon dioxide. “The price of carbon needs to be high enough to justify a new biochemical process,” he stressed.

Homemade Dog Treats With Applesauce and Cheese

These homemade dog treats are crunchy, cheesy, and enthusiastically approved by my little curmudgeon. The recipe began with a dog biscuit from the December 2005 issue of Gourmet. While the generous quantities of butter, brown sugar, and salt in the original gave me pause, I used it as inspiration for these cracker-like treats, swapping in ingredients I’d discussed with my vet and knew my pup would love. I’m hoping yours will too.

Whole wheat flour provides structure plus fiber, while brown rice flour helps the biscuits bake up crisp. Old-fashioned rolled oats give the treats a pleasantly nubby texture. The dough comes together with an egg and unsweetened applesauce. Be sure to check the applesauce label for added sweeteners, especially xylitol, which is toxic to dogs.

I like Gouda for its aroma—it drives my dog into a frenzy—but sharp cheddar or Swiss will work too. Curly parsley and mint lend a fresh herbal note and mild breath freshening, but the real secret ingredient is a little anise seed. Sometimes called “dog nip,” anise has a scent that some dogs find irresistible. Fennel seed makes a fine substitute.

Can I make these homemade dog treats with pumpkin?

Absolutely. For a fall-inspired variation, use plain canned pumpkin purée instead of applesauce, oat flour instead of brown rice flour, sage instead of parsley, and sharp cheddar for the cheese. (Honestly, add a little salt and you could serve these on a Thanksgiving charcuterie board.) For your dog, though, skip the salt and be sure to use 100% pumpkin purée, not pumpkin pie filling, which contains added sugar and spices.

Add 1 cup (4 oz.) shredded Gouda or sharp cheddar, ¼ cup curly parsley leaves and tender stems, packed, and ¼ cup mint leaves, packed, and pulse to incorporate (two to three 1-second pulses). Add ¾ cup unsweetened applesauce and 1 large egg, and pulse until a coarse dough forms, about 20 seconds. (You can roll out dough right away, but it’s much easier to work with it if you let it chill first.) Divide dough into two rounds and wrap each in plastic, or place in separate resealable containers, and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes and up to 24 hours.

Our classic pecan pie recipe has no chocolate chips, no booze, and no hard-to-find ingredients. Instead of focusing on embellishments, we focused on what matters most: the deep flavor of toasted pecans, a caramely custard, and a buttery, flaky pie crust.

Brown sugar and a spoonful of dark molasses add depth to the filling, while toasting the pecans before incorporating them makes their flavor stand out and their texture more robust. Blind-baking the crust—baking it before adding the filling—helps keep the bottom crisp beneath the gooey custard.

When the pie comes out of the oven, the edges should be set and slightly puffed, but the center should still wobble like Jell-O. It will continue to set as it cools, so resist slicing too soon. Serve it all on its own, or gild it with vanilla ice cream or whipped cream.

Can I make this pecan pie without corn syrup?

Yes. Corn syrup gives this classic pie its perfectly gooey texture, but there are alternatives. Brown rice syrup will add a toasty flavor and can be used in equal parts. Golden syrup, honey, and maple syrup are all too sweet and will change the pie's flavor and texture.
