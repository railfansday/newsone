Queen's Domain review

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Where to start with Queen's Domain? It's tempting to begin with King's Field; after all, FromSoftware's seminal first-person action-RPG is an explicit inspiration for developer Freshly Baked Games. But 40 hours later, it feels like so much more. It's equally tempting to invoke another FromSoftware game: Dark Souls. Queen's Domain owes an obvious debt to that game's celebrated level design: the dazzling interconnectedness of its vertiginous world. But that's not quite right either. The deeper you delve, the more there's something of the spirit of The Legend of Zelda, a touch of Metroidvania too. Until finally you concede that Queen's Domain, with its remarkable world of densely packed secrets, is very much its own.

All this begins with an island. You, Prince Beriik, are a castaway, washed ashore on Neasied. Your father, the king, has disappeared, and your search has brought you, somewhat circuitously, here. As you clamber to your feet and survey the island, it makes an immediately striking impression: all strange peaks and crooked spires; a cramped, chaotic mass of landmarks lost amid the dense undergrowth. Pyramids perch atop steep plateaus; a vast, moss-tinged mausoleum sprawls across the land to one side while the other is dominated by an imposing tower. It's a world made all the stranger by Queen's Domain's retro aesthetic. Here, shimmering low-res textures and low-poly shapes converge to create elaborate spaces and structures that feel slightly at odds with the lo-fi vibe. It's not quite old, not quite new; instead, something of a world lost outside of time. It makes for an enigmatic, disorientating landscape. And when paired with a soundtrack that delights in discordance, as it swings from gentle ambience to pulsing synths, it's a mood that refuses to resolve.

There is so much to say about Neasied. And so much I absolutely will not spoil. But very early impressions don't do it justice at all. As you take your first tentative steps onto the island, presented with a forking path that eventually winds back in on itself, it's easy to mistake Queen's Domain for a first-person Dark Souls - especially if, like me, you're only passingly familiar with King's Field. As in most Soulslikes, early progress is defined by a punishing inch-by-inch crawl from one brutal encounter to the next; where death - via chicken-cricket hybrids, ladybird-rats, and other wonderfully odd things - comes fast and frequently, respawning you back at the last Crystal Monument you visited. And because its wilfully old-school design eschews modern-day niceties such as a combat lock-on or dedicated roll button, there's a looseness to fighting that takes a while to click. But it does, slowly, click.

This is a game that demands you lean into its limitations, encourages you to find ways to make them work. At first, those Souls-honed instincts are likely to be your undoing as you naturally scramble for a dodge button that isn't there. But soon a different sort of pattern emerges: you realise that to avoid a ladybird attack, you simply need to step back - but not so far you don't have time to nip in and strike while your opponent takes a pause. A charging enemy might require a different approach - a well-timed backstep and swivelling sideways strafe to put you in the right place for a counter. And in this way, combat becomes a compelling sort of puzzle as you learn each of the 100+ enemies' little ticks - including rock, paper, scissors-style weaknesses to different weapon types - and figure out how to respond.

There's more to consider. Your melee damage output, for instance, is directly tied to your stamina bar: the more stamina you have, the harder you'll hit; too low and it's a waste of a swing. That forces you to choose your moments carefully, moving out to safety while your bar refills. And there's a charge attack, also linked to your stamina, that does more damage still. Already combat has a distinctively deliberate rhythm, but then come your first throwing knives. These are most immediately useful against enemies ordinarily out of reach - like the laser-spewing foliage dogging your journey early on - but they're equally valuable for luring individual enemies away from the throng. Throwing weapons take a while to return to your pouch, meaning they too need to be deployed with purposes. And while knives are just the start - you'll acquire an increasingly exotic selection of throwables including poison-tipped darts and homing blades - they all fundamentally work the same way.

And the interplay of these various warm-ups and cooldowns creates an increasingly engaging rhythm as you learn to juggle between your variously equipped weapons to maximise their damage potential. You might, for instance, fire off a one-two-three volley of homing darts, then use a magic lantern to toss out a massive fireball that bounces destructively around the room. And it's there, in the ensuing chaos, you'll charge up your blade, letting rip then returning to your darts again. Combat might encourage a methodical pace, but that doesn't mean you can't fight fast, and there are some fascinating synergies among the various weapons, many having their own wonderfully distinct mechanisms. There's a brawling gauntlet that lifts you clean off the ground, for instance, and a vampiric blade capable of stealing an enemy's life force. There are hammers, creatures, and an unexpected mid-game subclass, utilising a third rechargeable power source, that I am definitely not spoiling. Queen's Domain's 100 or so weapons create ample space for experimentation and customisation, and the further from its cautious beginnings you stray, the brisker, the more exhilarating its combat becomes. In my current, late-game form, I'm a whirl of blades and blasts and up-close attacks that feels anything but Soulslike.

Queen's Domain isn't just a nippier sort of game, though. It quickly reveals itself to be a friendlier, more accommodating one. Levelling, for instance, happens passively as you play, meaning every swing feels like it's contributing to your progress. Weapons level independently too, encouraging specialisation. There are no flasks, no accumulated currency you'll need to worry about dropping on death, no subsequent corpse runs. And progress can be saved at any time using the nearest Crystal Monument, meaning that when you respawn, you won't have to grind through the same opponents again. Enemies do eventually reappear of their own accord, so you can't exploit the save system indefinitely. But unlike Souls-style bonfires, Crystal Monuments only repopulate the world when you explicitly tell them to - when you Rest to clear status effects and restore health - giving you considerably more agency. You can decide when to push forward, when to retreat and lock in your progress, and when to reap the risky benefits of a rest and reset. Together, it makes for a breezier, less punishing, more consistently rewarding riff on the traditional Souslike, while never losing that I-can-do-this just-one-more-turn allure.

But as immensely enjoyable as Queen's Domain's combat is, the real draw here, unquestionably, is the world. Neasied is extraordinary. Right from the start, as you pick yourself off the sand and survey the landscape, its enigmatic huddle of peculiar landmarks isn't just intriguing, it's inviting. These odd pyramids, these curious plateaus practically demand to be explored. But how? The answer comes perhaps an hour in when, fancily clad skeleton boss successfully slain, you get your hands on the Holy Sword Endu. It's an immediate boon, far more lethal than the rubbish rusty blade you've been wielding so far. And its secondary attack - a long-range jab that propels you forward - is handy too. It's also your key to unlocking Neasied.

Endu's violent forward momentum can, you see, fling you across previous uncrossable gaps, gradually carrying you further and further as more upgrades reveal themselves. Suddenly, Queen's Domain takes flight, quite literally, becoming something closer to an exploratory sandbox as it adopts the spirited acrobatics of a platform game. And as you experiment with your newfound freedom, the more paths you explore, the island begins to take on a dizzying new form. Neasied, when viewed from up high, reveals itself to be a vastly intricate traversal puzzle. That peculiar structure in the distant ledge? If you can find an appropriate ledge to fling yourself from, it's now yours for the investigating - and from there, one, two, three more tantalising branches are revealed. Neasied is an astonishing piece of design: a ludicrous, elaborate tangle of hidden pathways and secrets, forever folding in and around itself, that's crafted to invite curiosity and then constantly reward.

Freshly Baked Games always feels one step ahead. I can't recall a single moment where my exploratory What-ifs - What if I throw myself this way? What if I do a falling mid-air turn? What if I toss myself down this hole? - didn't bear fruit. Sometimes your prize is a steaming platter of boon-granting food, sometimes it's another bit of increasingly exotic weaponry. Perhaps you'll find a strange NPC with a favour to ask; fish eggs and mysterious skulls; a shopkeeper with a thing for ducks (who, in a typically lovely touch, festoons his store with the goods you're willing to sell), and so many mysterious new puzzle threads to pull. As you fling and probe and push on forward, that thing you've been carrying around for hours - or, conversely, that thing you saw hours ago - suddenly finds new purpose. There's a definite touch of Metroidvania as old areas reveal new truths the further you go. And because there's so little handholding, so little explicit direction, every discovery feels like your own.

But just as you think you're starting to get a handle on Queen's Domain's shape, along comes another surprise: dungeons as vastly intricate as the world outside. You'll navigate a labyrinthine of waterlogged caverns and angry fish where progress involves puzzling past ancient mechanisms. Later, an arcane tower where gargoyles prowl. Here, the convoluted upward route takes circuitous laps among the endless bookshelves, spilling out the windows onto teetering precipices with alarming sea views. Then down, however briefly, to eerie chambers below. Suddenly, the mood is less Soulslike, more Zelda, and your expectations shift again. And still new discoveries lead to new paths and fresh enigmas.

And it keeps on going. As Freshly Baked Games gradually peels back Neasied's layers, it constantly finds new ways to awe: yet more surprises, yet more secrets, your exploratory digressions earning still more intriguing rewards. There comes a point where you think surely you must have seen everything, explored its deepest caverns and most mysterious tombs, but down you go again. And somewhere around the midpoint, Queen's Domain takes a truly audacious turn. There is a - let's call it a tonal lurch for fear of spoilers - that signals another shift. Suddenly, there are new toys to play with, an autumnal landscape so complex in its demands, it genuinely made my brain pulse. And then it's over. Until it's not.

There are, it turns out, secrets beneath the secrets; a still more intricate web of interconnected threads spun throughout this already fascinating, impossibly complex world. The Neasied that welcomes you really is just the tip of it all. Exactly when you'll come to this realisation depends how thorough you are in the earlier hours - how willing you are to explore its darker corners and brave its more punishing realms - but for me the before-and-after managed to divide my 40-ish hour playthrough neatly in two. You reawaken with a sense of a quest unfulfilled, a hint that more is required. And so, in lieu of more explicit direction, you set out again, probe deeper, explore further, and slowly the vaguest shape of a new objective - a succession of tiny threads seemingly all leading in the same direction - begins to emerge, tucked along uncharted paths, perhaps, or in suspicious corners, or in item descriptions that suddenly feels like clues.

It's here in this second half - something of a dark mirror to the first, in classic Zelda style - that Queen's Domain takes on a more esoteric air; its web of distantly scattered enigmas requiring real dedication to to unfurl. Why this pot on a stick? Why this book? How do these unlit lanterns relate to this thing, and that thing, and this other thing on the other side of the world?

It's clever stuff, even if navigating its late-game threads can occasionally feel just a little too much, a little too abstruse, and you'll potentially face a lot of needle-in-a-haystack backtracking if you haven't been taking judicious notes as you go. Do I have any other quibbles? There are perhaps too few boss encounters that feel like genuine events. And while combat generally feels fair and balanced, serving as a propulsive drumbeat to the broader exploratory rhythms, there are a few late-game all-the-enemies-at-once-style difficulty spikes that go just a bit too far. But then another 'What if?' and the world responds, and those frustrations melt away.

I remember a time in my youth - back before dataminers and FAQs - when video games felt unknowable. Endless enigmas built from creativity and code. It felt like anything could be hiding in there if only you knew where to look, and it was easy to let your imagination run wild. Queen's Domain is so full of secrets, so full of mystery and wonder, it feels like the sort of game my enthusiastic child-brain might have dreamed up all those years ago. 40 hours in, I've seen an ending, but it feels like I'm still far from through. So many mysteries still linger, so many things remain on my checklist of speculative to-dos. Out in the real-world, I've got a Zelda and a GTA awaiting. But it says everything about Queen's Domain - this brilliant, vast, deep enigma; this absurdly audacious thing - that I'm still not yet ready to let go.

But sometimes you already have a box running nginx for example.com, with TLS sorted, an auth layer in front of your other admin pages, and no appetite for yet another subdomain and certificate to look after.

Coldtill, the self-hosted crypto payment gateway I work on, has supported running under a path like /coldtill since v1.2.1 in October. This post is the setup in full, the two mistakes that break it, and the reason I'd still tell most people to use a subdomain. That last part is at the end, and it matters.

Three details are worth slowing down for.

No trailing slash on proxy_pass. In nginx, proxy_pass http://127.0.0.1:8080; passes the request URI through unchanged, while proxy_pass http://127.0.0.1:8080/; replaces the matched /coldtill/ with /. That one character is mistake number one from the list above. A rewrite that strips the prefix does the same damage.

One value in X-Forwarded-For. It's tempting to use $proxy_add_x_forwarded_for out of habit. The docs deliberately set it to $remote_addr, the single address nginx saw. Coldtill only trusts the rightmost X-Forwarded-For hop, and by default its Caddy only believes forwarded headers from private address ranges, which is where nginx connects from. That matters because the rate limits work per client IP: login is limited to 10 attempts a minute per IP and per email, and the merchant API to 600 requests a minute per IP. If every request looks like it comes from 127.0.0.1, your customers and your attackers share one bucket.

If something else sits in front of nginx, such as a CDN, $remote_addr will be the CDN's address, not the visitor's. Fix that in nginx with its real IP module before it gets passed on, so the one value you forward is the real one.

client_max_body_size 4m. The Caddy config in Coldtill's docs caps request bodies at 4 MB too, so this just keeps the two layers agreeing on the limit.

Your customers need the checkout, and your shop's server needs the merchant API. Everything else under the prefix is the dashboard. Here's what has to be reachable without your admin login:

Miss /api/checkout/ and the checkout page will load but can't fetch the payment it's meant to show. Miss /branding/ and the customer sees a checkout without your logo. Both look like bugs in the gateway when they're actually in the proxy config.

This is the main reason to run under a path at all: you already protect /admin or similar with something, and you want the payment dashboard behind the same thing. Protect the whole prefix, then carve out the open paths with a regex location:

This works because of how nginx picks a location. It finds the longest matching prefix location, then checks regex locations in order, and a matching regex wins unless the prefix location was marked ^~. So don't add ^~ to the /coldtill/ block, or the open paths will quietly start demanding a login and your checkout will break for every customer.

Your layer sits in front of Coldtill's own, not instead of it. Login still needs the password and the two-factor code, and anything that moves money still asks for a fresh code.

Two things the docs spell out, and that are easy to break with a "helpful" proxy setting.

Every dashboard request that changes something must be JSON and carry an Origin header whose host matches the site URL's host. So keep proxy_set_header Host $host;, and don't let nginx rewrite Origin. If saving a setting in the dashboard fails while everything else works, check this first.

What limits the damage is the design underneath. A two-factor code is still needed before payouts, API keys and other sensitive actions. API payouts only go to address-book entries.
