Is Broadcom a Buy After Falling 9% in One Day?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The recent drop in Broadcom stock does not align with its fundamentals.

Broadcom (AVGO +0.74%) dropped by 9% as artificial intelligence (AI) fears continue to compound. Financing risks and questions about the ROI on AI investments dominated October, and midterm results are the new key item in November.

The growth stock was once approaching $500 and is now under $300 at the time of this writing. However, this drop does not correlate with the status of Broadcom's fundamentals. The dip looks like a good buying opportunity for long-term investors.

It's always good to look at a company's recent earnings results and guidance before making a decision regarding its stock. In this case, Broadcom delivered superb results.

Revenue jumped by 48% year-over-year, with AI semiconductor revenue doing the heavy lifting. That part of the business was up by 143% year-over-year and represented almost half of total sales.

Furthermore, Broadcom guided for AI semiconductor revenue to more than triple in its fiscal 2026 third quarter, which the company will report in early December. The momentum is not limited to 2026. The chipmaker is on record anticipating $115 billion in AI semiconductor sales in fiscal 2027, and that number is expected to double to $230 billion in its fiscal 2028.

Broadcom also expects more than $30 in earnings per share (EPS) in its fiscal 2028. That implies a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio below 10 at current levels. The fact that Broadcom guided for fiscal 2028 shows that chip sales are still heating up.

Democrat victories in the midterm do not mean that the AI buildout is canceled. Regulations may get stricter, and future tax breaks may be less generous, but it's extremely unlikely that Democrats order a complete halt for the entire industry.

The AI buildout isn't just producing data centers. It's also creating jobs, and data center developers are investing directly into communities.

Even if Democrats clamp down on data centers harder than expected, especially in Texas, tech giants have been investing in international data centers for years. For example, Alphabet recently announced a $15 billion investment in a Malaysian data center.

Hyperscalers have been more than happy to buy AI chips, including the ones that Broadcom creates. While Nvidia is the undisputed leader in the GPU industry, Broadcom has established itself as the firm leader in the Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) industry.

Whether large data centers are primarily built in the U.S. or international countries in the future remains anyone's guess, but all of those facilities will need chips. Tech spending isn't going down and continues to trend upward. Broadcom remains well-positioned in the AI boom despite the ongoing correction.

Matteo Thun designs Torre Velasca interiors using "building itself as the starting point"

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Alberto Strada (unless stated otherwise)

Italian architect Matteo Thun looked to the original materials and colours of Milan’s iconic Torre Velasca when creating its new interiors, which feature pink hues inspired by the tower’s dusky facade.

Thun has designed 72 flats in the upper floors of the tower, ranging from suites to duplex penthouses. He approached the interior design of the skyscraper, built in the 1950s by the architecture studio BBPR, with a focus on reinterpretation.

“We allowed the masters and the building itself to speak, imagining a way of living within it that would carry the building a little further into the future, adding a new chapter to its history,” Thun told Dezeen.

“We did not seek contrast: our design was informed directly by the materials and colours of Torre Velasca, and we chose not to intervene where we felt the architecture had already achieved a sense of completeness.”

His studio preserved original details, including doors, window frames, loggias and tiles in the bathrooms and kitchens. These were then juxtaposed with modern furniture and evergreen design classics.

“The interiors bring together new furniture designed by the studio and design references spanning the 1950s to the 1980s, including iconic pieces developed in new colourways in collaboration with historic Milanese companies,” Thun said.

“With a deep appreciation for craftsmanship and a strong attention to detail, these designs share an ability to evolve without losing their identity.”

The colour palette, too, was inspired by the original hues of the Torre Velasca.

In the living rooms, walls are covered in custom-made velvet wall coverings by textile company Dedar in a pale pink colour that resembles the pink plaster on Torre Velasca’s facade.

“The building itself was our starting point,” Thun said.

“The pink plaster, developed and patented specifically for the tower, together with the blue and green floor tiles found both inside and in the loggias, formed the basis of our colour palette,” he continued.

“We developed different gradations of these tones, opting for darker shades of green and blue to define the entrance areas and bedrooms. “

As well as the apartments, Thun also designed the interior of the reception, lobby and the corridors on the ground floor.

And while this was an interior project, Thun says it ended up becoming “a lesson in architecture” for his studio.

“Working with a building as distinctive as Torre Velasca challenged us to think and work first and foremost as architects,” Thun said.

“The challenge was not to reinvent the building, but to find ways to make its original character feel relevant today, while remaining true to BBPR’s vision,” he continued.

“In this sense, the project became a process of discovery, with the building itself serving as our greatest teacher. It was, in many ways, a lesson in architecture for us.”

Thun has previously designed the interior of the boutique hotel The Julius Prague and “hotel-like” rooms for a German hospital.

TL;DR: Leap, described by Solana Labs as a mobile trading app for tokenised stocks, ETFs, metals, currencies and crypto, launched in early-access beta on iOS on November 5. The app brings several asset categories into one interface, but each token’s legal rights and user eligibility still need separate examination.

Solana’s tokenised-asset ambitions are moving into another consumer-facing interface. In a November 5 announcement distributed by Solana Labs, Leap said it had launched an early-access iOS beta app for trading multiple kinds of assets represented onchain.

The offering brings tokenised stocks and exchange-traded funds alongside metals, currencies and cryptocurrencies, with the stated goal of providing market context, clearer execution and detailed profit-and-loss tracking. Leap says it was built by the team behind Dialect.

Putting assets in the same app can make switching between markets easier for users, but the underlying instruments may remain quite different. A conventional listed share, a tokenised entitlement to a share, a cryptocurrency and a claim linked to a commodity can involve different legal rights, custodians and market hours.

The announcement should therefore be read as an application launch, not proof that every listed security has been made directly transferable to every wallet or that all asset classes operate under identical rules.

NewsBTC examined the NYSE’s planned tokenised securities infrastructure, highlighting the importance of transfer agents, corporate actions and investor rights. Those questions still matter when trading takes place through a polished phone screen rather than an institutional desk.

Leap’s November 5 release describes early access on iOS, not unrestricted public availability across all devices and countries. Beta launches allow developers to test user experience and market integrations, but they also mean functionality, supported regions and service rules can change.

The company highlights informed trading, market information and precise P&L presentation. Those features can help users understand orders, yet the accuracy of a dashboard depends on price feeds, cost-basis tracking and the availability of underlying markets. A clear interface is a benefit only if it communicates the risks, fees and settlement terms faithfully.

Liquidity also deserves attention. A tokenised equity can be technically transferable on a blockchain while the market for it remains thin. Bid-ask spreads, redemption arrangements and the ability to exit positions can differ from the traditional market in the underlying asset.

The wider Solana ecosystem has been expanding its settlement and tokenised-market infrastructure. NewsBTC’s reporting on Solana’s delivery-versus-payment tool examines the institutional side of this effort. Leap is attempting to bring related activity to a consumer trading experience.

That does not by itself guarantee meaningful network revenue or token demand. App adoption should be measured through active users, actual trades, repeat usage and transparent liquidity conditions, rather than the breadth of assets named in a launch release.

The next useful disclosures will be supported jurisdictions, precise asset rights, custody arrangements, fee schedules, trading hours and the move from beta to wider distribution. Leap has put a new interface in users’ hands. The larger test is whether the financial products behind that interface are as clear and dependable as the app aims to be.

As COP31 Antalya kicks off, can world move beyond big promises?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Droughts, floods and extreme heat are upping the pressure on delegates at the UN climate talks in Antalya, Turkey. Can countries move beyond pledges to concrete action?

With its pulsing music, flashy images and barrage of special effects, the key message in Antalya's marketing video is clear: Turkey, the host of the 31st edition of the UN climate talks, is cool, stylish, modern — and eco-conscious.

Both President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and his wife, Emine, make an appearance, with Turkey's first lady calling climate change "one of the most visible manifestations of the burden human activity has placed on our planet."

From November 9-20, delegates from nearly every country in the world are coming together in the Mediterranean city of Antalya to chart a way out of the climate crisis. Turkey is the official host, and Australia will lead the negotiations. But the issues they'll confront during those 12 days are far more serious than the showy marketing campaign make them out to be.

Severe drought, extreme heat, wildfires and record-low water levels in Europe's main waterways — summer across the continent was dominated by climate emergencies.

And in late August, a series of flash floods triggered by a rock-ice avalanche in Nepal and Tibet washed away entire villages, killing more than 1,400 people and leaving thousands missing. Climate experts have linked the disaster to melting ice and a warming climate.

Globally, carbon emissions are still rising: the UN has concluded that it is no longer possible to keep global temperature rise to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels, the goal agreed at COP21 in 2015.

The global transition away from polluting fossil fuels like coal, oil and gas will be at the center of attention in Antalya — once again. To date, there's been no consensus on whether and how to transition rapidly away from this main source of global warming.

Despite the setbacks, the world has made some progress thanks to the 2015 Paris Agreement.

David Victor, a climate expert at the University of California San Diego, pointed out that before then, the planet was hurtling toward an average temperature rise of between 3-3.5 C.

If current climate commitments are fully implemented, the latest UN projections show a rise of up to 2.6 C. It's a significant step forward, even though warming of that magnitude will still have worldwide consequences.

"There's no question that we could be doing more," said Victor, though he cautioned against pinning too much on this year's summit.

All agreements at COP need to be unanimous, meaning individual states can quash any sweeping resolutions. Added to that challenge are the ongoing wars, trade conflicts and other economic problems swirling around this year's negotiations.

"It's like a giant Christmas tree in which everybody's hung their different ornaments, and the tree is groaning and more ornaments are being put on it," said Victor. "We have to look at it for what it is: It's a place where people talk about stuff."

Instead of waiting for all COP member states to come to an agreement, Victor sees a better chance for change in what he called "clubs" — smaller alliances of governments and companies working together to advance actual solutions, whether it be in aviation, shipping or electromobility.

"The Paris process is important. It has to go forward," he told DW. "But all the work is going to be done in clubs."

An example of this approach was last April's first ever International Conference on Transitioning Away from Fossil Fuels in Santa Marta, Colombia.

Colombia and the Netherlands invited representatives from 57 countries and key sectors to discuss ways to move the world away from coal, oil and gas, after an agreement on a similar plan failed at the 2025 climate summit due to opposition from several oil-producing states.

With an eye to the ongoing trade conflicts and energy crisis, hosts Turkey and Australia have included green industrialization and the clean energy transition among their aims. One goal is to increase the share of electricity in global energy consumption to 35% by 2035, up from around 20% today.

A recent analysis by the International Energy Agency found accelerating electrification could cut fuel-importing countries' energy bills by more than $400 billion (€357 billion) by 2035, compared to 2025 levels. The idea could appeal to regions dependent on fossil fuel imports — among them Germany and the European Union.

Ahead of COP31, German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider said Germany and the EU wanted to spark new momentum in the "global phaseout of fossil fuels."

It's wouldn't just be good for the climate, but a chance for many countries to boost their economies and reduce dependence on energy imports, added Schneider in a statement.

Oil-producing states like Saudi Arabia, however, aren't interested in deals that will undermine their business model. At recent UN climate conferences, they've repeatedly blocked any final agreements including phaseout plans.

Even China, despite its massive expansion of wind and solar energy over the last 20 years, could be against the idea. The economic superpower traditionally rejects any outside demands, proposals or interference concerning its national decisions on energy policy.

Every country in the world is affected by climate change, with loss and damage calculated in the hundreds of billions from heat, drought and storms. Worst hit are vulnerable countries in the Global South and island nations, who are at risk of disappearing as sea levels rise.

That's where money comes into play, with developing countries calling for more support to protect their citizens from the harms of global warming. At the 2024 UN climate talks in Baku, Azerbaijan, industrialized nations agreed to free up at least $300 billion annually in climate financing by 2035.

But where exactly all those funds will come from remains hotly debated. At the same time, several major developed countries — most notably, the US — have cut their development aid.

The German government only paid out €4.7 billion from its 2025 budget for international climate financing, down from €6.1 billion in 2024.

"The level of financial support for developing countries today is far from what's needed," said Jan Kowalzig, a climate expert at Oxfam Germany.

Breaking financial promises like this comes at the expense of losing the trust of developing countries and, as a result, the diplomatic power, on which Germany and other industrialized nations rely to shape policy to fight climate change.

Cosima Cassel, who focuses on climate diplomacy at the independent think tank E3G in Berlin, believes Turkey's push to turbocharge electrification during an oil crisis could be successful.

For Cassel, a successful outcome at what are expected to be difficult negotiations in Antalya would reveal actual political will behind the grand agreements.

When it comes to electrification, that means taking the expansion of electricity grids and battery storage capacity seriously — not to mention removing the barriers to further electrification.
