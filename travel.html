NOAA Just Tweaked Its Winter Forecast. Here’s What’s Changed for Skiers.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) has some encouraging news for skiers hoping for a major rebound after last winter’s dismal snow season, particularly in California and the Southwest.

The agency’s Climate Prediction Center released its latest winter outlook on Thursday, Nov. 19, updating its forecast for the months ahead. The biggest development? The odds of above-average precipitation have increased across California and the Desert Southwest, offering a more promising outlook for a couple of the ski regions that have struggled with snow in recent years.

The new forecast tweaks a few things from its previous iterations. Here’s a cheat sheet, but be sure to read on for more specifics and a regional breakdown:

Finally, NOAA says that the new precipitation forecasts are more reliable than their temperature forecasts this winter, which they attribute to the strong El Niño and an active southern storm track. In other words, there will be moisture, but there’s no guarantee it’ll come down as snow.

The latest outlook favors above-average precipitation across the southern United States, stretching from southwestern Oregon and California through the Desert Southwest and into the southern Plains and Southeast.

For ski country, California and the Southwest stand out. NOAA increased the probabilities of above-average precipitation in both regions compared with its previous outlook. Southern California and southwestern Arizona now have greater than a 60 percent chance of finishing the December-through-February period wetter than average.

That could be welcome news for resorts in the Sierra Nevada, including Mammoth and the Lake Tahoe region, as well as Arizona Snowbowl and New Mexico’s Taos Ski Valley and Ski Santa Fe. The forecast also offers some encouragement for southern Colorado, including Purgatory, Wolf Creek, and Telluride, although it’s far less clear for the Front Range and Northern Rockies.

It’s worth noting here that NOAA is forecasting total precipitation, not snowfall, per se. Whether that moisture falls as snow or rain will depend on temperatures, snow levels, and where individual storms track. Still, a wetter-than-average winter would be a step in the right direction.

NOAA continues to favor below-average precipitation across parts of the northern Rockies. The region also faces an increased likelihood of higher than average temperatures, a combo that’s not music to the ears of resorts in Montana, Idaho, and Wyoming. The Pacific Northwest also faces odds of warmer-than-average temperatures, particularly across Washington and Oregon.

Elsewhere, the outlook favors wetter-than-average conditions across much of the East Coast and Southeast. However, warmer temperatures could complicate snowfall prospects, particularly at lower-elevation ski areas.

This is the latest chapter in a winter forecast that skiers have been watching closely for months. Earlier this summer, NOAA’s increasingly confident projections of a potentially historic El Niño raised hopes for a snowy rebound across parts of the West following last season’s exceptionally poor conditions.

In July, SKI spoke with meteorologists and ski-travel experts about what the developing climate pattern could mean for the upcoming season. The early consensus pointed toward the Southwest, particularly southern Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, and California, as regions with favorable odds for an active storm track. Much of that remains the same.

But there were plenty of question marks, and there still are, namely what will happen in central Colorado and Utah. NOAA’s earlier outlook showed average precipitation for those destinations, and the latest forecast is slightly more encouraging. We still recommend choosing higher elevation ski areas to pad your odds for snow rather than rain.

NOAA’s next seasonal outlook is scheduled for December 17. Until then, skiers will have to keep watching the shorter-range forecasts and hope the promising signals translate into the kind of winter the West desperately needs.

Is Early-Season Skiing Worth It? Here’s the Case for Going.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

There’s a lot to love about skiing: being outdoors, pushing yourself to improve, finding your flow, and being part of ski culture. But too often, the things that make skiing great get overshadowed by cost and crowds. It’s hard to find your flow when the run is crowded, and even the best ski day is hard to enjoy when lodging, lift tickets, and lunch leave you financially stretched.

The solution is early-season skiing. Few people book a ski trip in November or early December, and even local skiers tend to be unenthused. But I think they’re missing out. In fact, early season is one of my favorite times to ski. Early season turns have led to breakthroughs in my technique, and it’s one of the only times when the mountain feels small enough to meet up with friends.

Plus, everything that makes skiing great still stands: you’re outside, you’re improving, you’re dipping into your flow state, and you’re more a part of ski culture than if you were to come for President’s Weekend like everyone else.

And the things that make skiing less appealing are non-issues.

The cost of an early ski season trip can be half or even a third of what you’ll pay just a few weeks later. In Jackson Hole, for example, if you visit in early December, rather than Christmas week, you’ll pay $85 for a lift ticket instead of $340. Similarly, a room at the ultra-luxe slopeside Hotel Terra Jackson Hole increases by $600 a night based on whether you stay in early or late December. The same goes for big ski weeks like spring break and Presidents’ Day—and the weeks in between aren’t much cheaper.

And then there’s the fact that when you’re not skiing at the same time as everyone else, every aspect of the day (or your entire trip) becomes more enjoyable. You’ll get in more skiing thanks to shorter (or nonexistent) lift lines, and you don’t have to worry about running into someone on the way down. And when you aren’t one of 10 people waiting in line for rental gear or a table at the restaurant, you’ll get warmer, more personalized service. (Plus, in early season, everyone is fresh and excited for the season ahead, not tired and over it.)

When the mountain is fully open, and everyone’s ski legs are firing, the pressure to go big is real. It can be difficult and dangerous to try to keep up, especially if you’re new to skiing or it’s your first (or only) ski trip of the year.

But in the early season, everyone is still getting their ski legs under them, and the skiing is more about fine-tuning your technique and rebuilding muscle. There’s nothing showy about it, and most people leave their egos at home.

The biggest argument against early season skiing is the lack of snow. Indeed, in the early season, the snow hasn’t had time to accumulate. Often, only the lower parts of the mountain are open, and sometimes only thanks to snowmaking. It’s not the time to do a hike-to, and you’ll rarely find any powder, so show up with your expectations (and ego) in check.

But if you’re willing to rethink what a ski day should look like, you can enjoy everything that makes skiing great while avoiding its biggest drawbacks.

james turrell fills new york gallery with floating cubes and glowing walls of light

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

the legendary light artist brings floating cubes and luminous walls to almine rech's tribeca gallery in new york.

James Turrell has filled Almine Rech’s Tribeca gallery in New York with luminous forms that seem to extend from the walls and hover in midair. Titled Something Old, Something New, Something Borrowed, Something Blue, the exhibition brings together over fifteen works, and even includes new installations that revisit some of the artist’s earliest experiments with light and perception.

A rectangular opening glows violet against a darkened wall. Elsewhere, a luminous cube appears to float where two walls meet, with its sharp edges suggesting a solid object suspended in space. The room itself becomes difficult to read as the light alters viewers’ sense of depth and distance.

The show is on view through January 30th, 2027, and marks Turrell’s first presentation with Almine Rech in the United States. It arrives nearly four decades after their first collaboration in Paris, and returns to ideas that have occupied the artist since the 1960s.

Among the new works is the installation Shallow Space, part of a series in which James Turrell uses illuminated apertures to create unexpected depth within a wall. At first, the luminous surface can appear completely flat, like a field of color. As the viewer’s eyes adjust, the opening reveals an actual space beyond the wall, although its dimensions remain difficult to judge.

In Raemar II (2026), a glowing rectangular form casts violet light across the surrounding surfaces. Color spreads beyond its edges and gradually dissolves into the room. From a distance, the work resembles an illuminated painting, although its relationship to the wall is far more ambiguous.

The exhibition also introduces new pieces from the artist‘s Glassworks series, which uses precisely programmed LEDs behind etched glass. Colors change gradually across these luminous surfaces, and the apparent depth of each work shifts with them. Some passages of light appear dense and opaque, while others seem to recede far beyond the physical dimensions of the piece.

Another new installation, Afrum Violet (2026), returns to James Turrell’s Cross Corner projections. A sharply defined violet form occupies the intersection of two walls, where the geometry of the room helps create the illusion of a three-dimensional cube. The surrounding surfaces absorb a softer wash of purple, while the central form retains its remarkably precise edges.

Turrell began experimenting with these projections in 1966 at the former Mendota Hotel in Ocean Park, California, where he converted the building into a studio. By directing light onto the walls at specific angles, he discovered that a simple projection could appear to have weight and volume. A shape formed by light could become surprisingly convincing as a physical presence.

Six decades later, the strange effect remains. From one spot, the illuminated planes read as a solid cube. Move through the room, and the relationship between the shape and its architectural setting begins to change. The installation asks viewers to spend time with something as familiar as a corner and reconsider exactly what they are seeing.

Premier League clubs have voted to protect the organisation's bigwigs from any individual legal action taken by Manchester City's sponsors.

Instead, the clubs - including City themselves - will foot any legal bills incurred by a potential corporate defamation suit.

Daily Mail Sport understands there was widespread support from clubs to insulate Premier League chief executive Richard Masters and the four other league directors.

The vote passed with 18 clubs in favour, and City and Nottingham Forest against.

It is understood Forest opted to vote that way as they felt they had not been given sufficient information or enough time to consider the proposal and its implications, as opposed to representing a show of support for City.

The matter was the first item on the agenda at the Premier League shareholders' meeting in the Savoy Hotel on Thursday and was addressed quickly with limited discussion.

Clubs had a paper for the proposed rule change before the meeting and there was significant support to extend the legal protection to individuals on the league's board.

This marks the first time since the Premier League’s inception that insurance arrangements for its board members have been reviewed.

Your smart home hub placement is more important than you'd think

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Whether it fits your home's aesthetic or not, that hub may need to be front and center.

A robust smart home system makes it easy to control the many devices keeping your household in the 21st century, but there are many ways that system can fail. Like any gadget, smart home devices are prone to issues that have no clear cause and can be frustrating to diagnose. Among those potential causes is something you may never guess: placement. As the center of your setup, capable of receiving signals from all directions, you'd think you can place your smart home hub anywhere, but it's not as simple as that.

Many hubs feature controls for all of your connected devices, so it makes sense that you'd keep them in a central spot. But if you control your devices from separate apps or just don't need to make manual adjustments often, it can be tempting to hide the hub out of sight. But since it needs to send and receive signals from all your home's devices, it's important to ensure they aren't blocked or being impeded.

While not all smart home devices run on Wi-Fi, it's helpful to treat the hub the way you would a Wi-Fi router. The most important part of placing a smart home hub like Anker's MindBase is making sure signals aren't being physically obstructed. Wi-Fi and other forms of wireless communication can be blocked by walls, furniture and doors, so try to keep as clear a path as possible between the hub and the devices communicating with it. Because you're likely controlling devices throughout your home, some will inevitably have a weaker connection than others, but placing the hub in a central place will help minimize problems. If your hub has visible antennas, try to also point them toward your smart home devices.

Hubs can also experience interference from other communication devices, like Wi-Fi routers. It's impossible to completely eliminate this kind of interference, especially if you're living somewhere with a lot of Wi-Fi traffic, like an apartment building. You should place your hub close enough to a router so that it's still receiving a strong signal, but they should never be right next to each other to avoid interference. Modern routers have settings that allow them to change to less crowded frequencies, and turning that on or setting your router and hub to different bands can help cut down on congestion as well. Other electronics like phones and microwaves can also interfere with smart home hubs, and the only solution here is to keep them away from each other.

If you're limited on placement options, you can create a linked network for Zigbee or ZWave connections to travel to the hub. Keep in mind that the signal they use has a fairly short range. Because these devices form a mesh network, each device repeats the signal it receives, letting you form a chain of devices through your home. That means that the signal from your hub only needs to reach one other device to be effective, but the signal does get weaker across multiple devices. So, even for a network that branches across devices, the closer to the hub a device is, the better.

Tips for Hunting Mature Bucks Over Bait (Plus the Ethics of If You Should)

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 9, 2026 4:16 PM EST

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

During the 2023 deer season, I shot my biggest buck ever: a Kentucky main-frame 12-pointer with a couple extra points that scored 168 6/8 inches. And I shot that big sum-buck over a yellow pile of ear and persimmon-flavored shelled corn. I even dropped a protein block in the middle and seasoned it with scent for good measure.

And as I write this, I can hear blood boiling and I can see the angry comments coming. Hunting whitetails over bait is still a controversial topic across much of deer country. It is, of course, illegal in many states in the Midwest, Northeast, and West. And even in places where baiting is legal, some argue that hunters should avoid using it.

The National Deer Association published an article explaining how baiting deer can spread disease: “By design, baiting and supplemental feeding concentrate deer and other wildlife species for the purposes of hunting, trapping, viewing, or simply increasing nutrition available to the animals. Some argue baiting and feeding have positive attributes, but no one can disagree they have numerous negative implications to a deer herd, including altering animal behavior, degrading the habitat near bait and feed sites, increasing the susceptibility to predation, and greatly enhancing the opportunity for disease transmission.”

The NDA’s formal position on baiting deer is this: “The NDA opposes the expansion of baiting where not currently legal. The NDA will not work to repeal baiting where currently legal, except where CWD (or other known diseases) is present. The NDA supports the use of baiting by wildlife professionals conducting scientific research. The NDA supports continued research on the effects on baiting in deer management programs.”

Baiting is legal and very popular in many Southern states. All hell broke loose when Kansas regulators began discussing the potential of banning deer baiting. Hunting deer in Texas is synonymous with hunting over a feeder. But still, many folks don’t understand how big bucks relate to bait piles.

Two comments I always hear when the bait debate pops up go something like: “Oh, you bait, bro? Must be easy killing those big bucks.” Or, “Bait won’t kill big, mature bucks, Jack.”

I scoff at both of those statements. Neither are true, and it’s far easier to screw up on a mature buck with a poor baiting strategy than it is to fill a tag with one. Like other tactics, baiting is just another tool in the arsenal. It isn’t a cheat code.

I know because I’ve been baiting Kentucky whitetails most of my hunting career, and it took a long time before I started experiencing success on mature deer. Here is how to bait deer and, more specifically, how to bait a monster buck—plus some considerations if you’re on the fence about legal baiting.

The early season is my favorite time to bait a monster buck. Hunting pressure is low. There’s plenty of cover for concealment. Deer are on predictable bed-to-feed lines of movement. There’s no better time to catch a buck off-guard over a bait pile.

Target warm-season bedding areas, such as north-facing slopes or low-lying areas near water that offer a high stem count. Then, determine what destination food source deer are targeting, such as green soybeans, fresh-cut corn, or dropping white oak acorns. Place the bait between the bedroom and dinner table.

Don’t overlook the pre-rut. This is a great time to bait a monster buck. Bucks are moving more but aren’t running wild just yet. So, capitalize on that buck’s rising testosterone levels. Often that leads to increased daylight travel.

As with the early season, it’s still all about bed-to-feed patterns. But now, deer are likely settling into their fall bedding areas and food sources. So, find these locations (which vary greatly by region and habitat type) and plant bait stations along lines of travel.

Once the rut hits, you might get a buck to hit a bait pile. But most of the time, a rutting buck is more interested in scent-checking does that have been through the buffet line. Because of this, bait stations are still effective during the rut, but you must hunt them differently.

Usually, I prefer to place bait piles on the downwind sides of doe bedding areas. Or locate the bait between two doe bedding areas. Bucks like to travel these locations anyway, so it’s a no-brainer game plan.

Once the rut is over, and the late season arrives, it’s all about the food again. But still, focus on late-season bedding areas. Think solar bedding (south-facing slopes) and thermal cover (dense stands of coniferous trees). Also, consider what deer are eating now, including remaining waste grain, green plots, remaining acorns, but most importantly, woody browse.

Once again, the best bet is placing bait piles between bedding areas and destination food sources. Use these as staging areas to intercept bucks during daylight. At this point, they aren’t likely to reach destination food sources before dark. But if your bait site is located close enough to bedding, they might hit that corn pile during legal shooting hours.

No matter the time of year, there are certain tactics that apply to virtually every baiting scenario. Of course, you have to locate a big buck before bait will help you. No volume of bait will bring in a monster buck if he doesn’t already live there. But once you’ve got a big buck located, follow these tips.

Some people say deer hunting over bait isn’t ethical. But here’s the thing: Some hunting ethics are situational. I’m not talking about taking poor shots or breaking hunting laws. Those ethics are clearly black and white.

For me, baiting deer is more of a gray area. First off, if it’s legal, it’s legal. When analyzing the differences of state-to-state guidelines on baiting, you’ll see regional discrepancies just like you will with crossbow regulations, the use of deer urine lures, and season structures. So if your area allows baiting, then it’s likely that agency wildlife managers for that area have decided baiting is not detrimental to the deer and deer hunting.

Second, it’s important to realize that it’s not like you can just throw out bait at any random spot in the woods and start shooting trophy bucks. It’s far from a canned hunt. If you live in an area that permits baiting, understand that bait has several different applications. Where legal, it can be used to help you attract deer, hold deer, feed supplemental nutrition, take inventory of deer, and more.

As for effectiveness, people commonly say bait stations are either fool-proof or that they turn bucks nocturnal. In my experience, neither are true.

Whitetails are smart, especially around bait piles. They learn to approach bait piles downwind and they pick up on hunters’ scent in the area. Yes, they will often hit bait at night. But no buck is 100 percent nocturnal—that’s a myth.

If you suddenly start seeing a target buck avoiding bait during daylight, it’s likely because you screwed up and disrupted that deer. Or, he started bedding elsewhere. Or, he started targeting a different destination food source. Or, maybe he’s just being a wild, unpredictable deer.

The point is, hunting over bait is still deer hunting. Anything can happen, and most of the time the big buck wins. But if you understand how to bait deer and you execute a well-crafted game plan, then baiting just might lead to the biggest whitetail of your life.

Street takeovers are a menace. Heck, the disruptive crime has jurisdictions like Compton installing new medians to prevent takeovers. This, however, isn't that kind of takeover. Twice a year, thousands of snakes take over a small road in Illinois. It's the sort of thing that would be enough to make Indiana Jones ditch his motorcycle, drop his whip, and head for the hills. Now, before you conjure mental images of "Raiders of the Lost Ark" and a writhing tangle of venomous vipers, this is all part of nature. Though there are still thousands of slithery critters on the so-called "Snake Road," a few of which are venomous.

It's enough to shut down the road that runs through part of the Shawnee National Forest in southern Illinois. Every year, the little road closes to vehicle traffic due to the influx of scaly travelers. Not once, but twice every trip around the sun. In fact, it's the only known road in the United States to shut down in observance of a reptile migration. The shutdown isn't meant to protect the national forest's human visitors, either – it's to make sure the road-going snakes can complete their journey unharmed.

Shawnee National Forest is a biodiverse landscape spanning 289,000 acres between the Mississippi River and the Ohio River. It's home to around 35 migratory species of snakes and 23 species of migrating amphibians. Twice a year, those critters make the trek between the national forest's limestone bluffs and the LaRue Swamp. In the spring, the reptiles and amphibians travel from the bluffs to the swamp. In the fall, the ectothermic excursionists do the trip in reverse order, returning to the bluffs from the LaRue Swamp. Be it a slither, crawl, or hop, these reptiles and amphibians are on a mission.

The adventure takes the animals right across Forest Service Road 345, also known as LaRue Road. "We call it Snake Road," Forest Service Enforcement Officer Anderson Harris told CBS Evening News. It's a fitting name, too. The Forest Service closes the road because of snake, reptile, and amphibian migrations from March 15 to May 15 and September 1 to October 30 every year. "That protects animals from being incidentally killed by vehicles running over them," Officer Harris added. "It's the only road in the U.S., to my knowledge, that we close for migrations of the amphibians and reptiles."

At first, locals weren't so pleased with the idea of closing the road for four months of the year. Some of the more cruel motorists made a game of killing as many snakes as they could on the road. Eventually, they came around to the idea of closing the road. Best to leave the reptilian travelers in peace, even if you're curious.

Before you ask, yes, you can go check out the cold-blooded crossers for yourself. While the road is closed to cars, it's open to visitors on foot. That said, you might want to pay close attention to where you step. Of the snakes that migrate through Shawnee National Forest's Snake Road, at least three are venomous. For starters, there's the timber rattlesnake, a pit viper with a maraca for a tail. There's also the cottonmouth, also known as the water moccasin. Finally, the eastern copperhead, which can average up to three feet in length.

Again, you might want to steer clear of Snake Road if you have a bad case of ophidiophobia. If herpetoculture is your hobby of choice, it might be worth checking out. That said, collecting the snakes as they cross the road is against the law. Of course, if you're looking for something viper-esque, a clean Ram SRT-10 might be a charming enough snake. Frankly, you'd probably rather a series of snakes choose to shut down a little service road in Illinois than find a giant snake nestled in your engine bay. That is, unless Steve Irwin is your kindred spirit or something. If you're more like Indiana Jones, you'd be more likely to curse, "Why did it have to be snakes?"

These Are the 13 Tactics Expert Whitetail Hunters Actually Use to Tag Mature Bucks

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 4, 2026 5:13 PM EST

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

The best deer hunters in the nation (known and unknown) all share a common thread. They are students of deer and the woods, and they never stop learning. As such, they continually experiment with new and advanced deer hunting tactics.

In order to learn some of these secrets (and to share them with you), I called a handful of the best deer hunters I know. Here’s how they find success season after season.

Mark Drury of Drury Outdoors has bagged more big deer than most. His approach is proven, and the process he uses includes saving and analyzing a lot of trail camera data, especially historical information. He has kept every single trail cam pic of a racked buck since 2007.

“Save trail monitoring pictures in an organized fashion for easy review in future years,” Drury says. “Bucks are incredibly habitual from year to year. Study your pics year-round to formulate patterns with individual bucks or areas. [Over time you’ll build] a database that helps lead to patterns in future years, as crop rotations, weather, and moon in future seasons mirror conditions that occurred in the past. It takes a lot of time to assemble and study but saves time in future seasons.”

Grant Woods of Growing Deer TV is one of the most renowned deer scientists in the country. He seems to get inside the mind of whitetails and understands what they do and why they do it. To that point, he stresses making hunting decisions based on what deer do, and not what we think they should do.

“An issue I’ve seen is hunters making setup and tactical decisions based on human thoughts,” Woods says. “The hunters I know that have repeated success filling tags in all types of conditions and habitats always think like a deer. They consider what deer need that day, and what will deer avoid.”

As Paul Beasley, co-host of “Canada in the Rough” TV, says, hunters are creatures of habit. “The longer we hunt a piece of ground, the more established our hunting setups and entry/access/travel points become,” he says. “We tend to rely more on what has worked before, and while deer in general also tend to gravitate to the same trails, food sources, and pinch points, it has been my experience that mature bucks need to be treated as individuals and hunted accordingly.

“The buck I shot this year helps illustrate my point. We have hunted this same 100-acre property for over 10 years, and have always taken deer off of it in the same 50-yard-by-80-yard area. Throughout the spring and into summer, this buck was working that area perfectly, but as hunting season approached, his behavior changed. His appearances on camera began to fall off. So, I scrambled to put up more cameras and found that he hadn’t gone anywhere. He was just using different parts of the property.”

Beasley killed that buck on the second afternoon of the season. He arrowed from a setup that his crew had never harvested a buck from before.

“It was through paying attention to this individual buck’s behavior, and not just doing the same old thing that has always worked, that allowed me to put my tag on him,” Beasley says.

Oftentimes deer hunters do things without thinking. We react, rather than making decisions based on logic. HeadHunters TV’s Nate Hosie agrees, and says we should hunt smarter and not harder.

“Growing up, I used to think the more I was out there the better my chances,” he says. “It’s not completely wrong, but as I’ve grown as a hunter, I’ve learned to pick my moments for when to strike. Wind, temperature, time of year, trail cam intel, [and more] now go into my hunt. The less I’m in and out, and the higher probability my attempts are, the more success I find.”

Rick White is a longtime staple in the outdoor industry and he suggests that hunters go all or nothing with scent management.

“One of the biggest things I try to instill in people is always going to the woods as scent-free as possible,” he says. “Always play the wind. Never intentionally hunt a stand on a bad wind. But also know that deer don’t always come in from where we want them to. So, if you’re not going to follow every step with scent elimination, then save your money.”

It sounds like rudimentary advice, but even experienced hunters make blunders that lead to being seen, heard, and smelled by deer. It’s something we must continually keep at the forefront of our mind. Will this entry route cause bedded deer to see me? Will this exit route blow scent into a bedding area?

Bone Collector Co-Host Nick Mundt knows deer hunting as well as anyone. As a former deer hunting guide and current big deer killer, he stresses the importance of going undetected when getting in and out of your hunting spots.

“Never think the wind will just work,” he says. “Always know that they’ll bust you if the wind is wrong. It dictates every sit.”

Aaron Gaines of Ani-Logics advises hunters to kill a target buck as early as possible. The early season and pre-rut offer opportunities to hunt consistent bucks.

“This is when he’s on a pattern and most predictable,” Gaines says. “To do this, I utilize most recent information from cameras. I’m very disciplined on hunting the right wind. If he’s daylighting, and I’ve got the right wind, things usually work out well.”

During the rut, whitetail bucks seem to travel via loops. That’s not hearsay or some hokey hack from your grandaddy’s hunting journal. That’s straight from a deer biologist. More specifically, former CEO of the Quality Deer Management Association and current HuntStand biologist, Brian Murphy.

“The biggest piece of advice I’ve learned over the years is to develop a plan and then stick with it long enough for it to have a reasonable chance of success,” Murphy says. “In other words, informed optimism. The hunters who have done their homework and keep a positive attitude, often after multiple dismal outings, are ultimately the most successful. Knowing from research that bucks often take a few days to complete loops within their home range, it’s not surprising that it takes persistence to increase the odds you will get an opportunity.”

Nathan Mrnak, COO of Base Camp Leasing, is a diehard deer hunter. He spends many days in the field each year. He stresses that it’s OK to move off your original plan and make a move.

“Picking out a stand location can be one of the toughest decisions while setting up on a mature whitetail,” he says. “I have had many first sits that ended with my target buck just out of bow range. Seeing his exact route provided the last bit of intel to moving a set just 50 yards to capitalize the next sit. This has worked time and time again in big timber.”

The most basic element of deer hunting is hunting deer where they are. To do that, you need current information.

Own the Season’s Art Helin knows this well. “Hunt where the bucks want to be during the correct phase of the season,” he says. “Food sources during early season, transition areas during the pre-rut (between field and bedding and staging areas), funnels and ridge edges during the rut (all day or mid-day sits especially), and back to food sources late season.”

During the rut, a lot of deer hunters choose to do all-day sits. Whitetail Properties Land Specialist Ben Harshyne does, too. However, he changes things up.

“I grew up hunting all-day sits in one stand,” he says. “Over time, I learned that deep timber and thick cover are action-packed in the morning and afternoon, yet often go cold by evening. In the afternoon, shift focus to hunting cover and edges near food sources or along travel lines leading to food. Not necessarily the actual food edge, but nearby. Does bed near food and still want to feed — and the bucks know it. Few stands are productive all day, so fine-tune your late-day move to target cover that relates to food.”

Big buck killer Jared Mills has arrowed a lot of big whitetails. He says don’t be afraid to be aggressive, but do it at the right time.

“I think most guys tend to be too conservative when hunting a target buck, staying on the fringes and only getting out when the conditions are perfect,” he says. “That’s fine if you have the entire season to hunt, and there’s little chance of him getting killed by a neighbor, but that’s a very rare scenario for most of us. While the windows of opportunity to encounter a target buck are already small enough, we make them even smaller by not getting in there after him. Be smart with your hunting, of course, but don’t sit around waiting for the absolute perfect time to move in, otherwise, you’ll likely miss a chance.”

Finally, just keep hunting. You won’t always kill a deer on opening day. It might just be closing day. So, keep hunting, and don’t give up.

“Have patience and persistence,” Helin concluded. “The entire season can change, and it can all happen in a short minute. Stay with it, and never give up, but also never get lazy. Always follow the same regimen from day No. 1 until the end.”

Thailand: Two Uyghurs at Risk of Return to China

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Detainees stand behind cell bars at the police immigration detention center in Bangkok, Thailand, January 21, 2019. © 2019 Sakchai Lalit/AP Photo

(Bangkok) – Thai authorities should not forcibly return two Uyghur men to China, where they face grave risks of torture and other serious abuses, Human Rights Watch said today. The two men, whose names are not public for security reasons, were transferred from prison to immigration detention in Bangkok after receiving a royal pardon, apparently in preparation for deportation.

“Previous Thai governments have kept Uyghurs in inhumane detention for years and then complied with Beijing’s demands to send them to China, where they were forcibly disappeared,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul should end forced returns to China and allow the two Uyghur men to travel to a safe third country.”

The two Uyghur men have been detained by Thai authorities since 2014, when one of them was only 14 years old. They were serving a prison sentence for offenses related to an escape attempt from immigration detention in January 2020. On October 21, 2026, the authorities transferred them from Bangkok Central Prison to the Bangkok Immigration Detention Center Suan Phlu after they had served most of their seven-year sentence. Thai Foreign Ministry officials told a parliamentary committee that in August the Chinese embassy in Bangkok demanded their return to China.

Under Thai immigration law and regulations, the two Uyghur men will be deported to China unless they object to the deportation and are resettled to a third country. Lawyers representing them told Human Rights Watch that they have already informed Thai authorities that the two do not consent to be sent to China. Thai immigration authorities have repeatedly refused to allow the United Nations refugee agency (UNHCR) access to the men to determine their refugee status.

The Thai government is obligated to respect the international law principle of nonrefoulement, barring anyone’s forcible return to a place where they would face a genuine risk of persecution, torture or other ill-treatment, or a threat to their life or other comparable serious human rights violations. Refoulement is prohibited by the UN Convention Against Torture, to which Thailand is a party, as well as customary international law. Thailand’s Prevention and Suppression of Torture and Enforced Disappearance Act prohibits deportation when there is a substantial risk of torture or ill-treatment.

In March 2014, Thai police in Songkhla province, near the Malaysia border, arrested about 220 Uyghur men, women, and children, charged them with immigration violations, and soon after transferred them to an immigration detention facility in Bangkok. In several separate incidents around the same period, the authorities arrested dozens of other Uyghurs and placed them in immigration detention facilities around the country. In July 2015, about 170 of the Uyghur women and children detained in Songkhla were released to Turkey. A week later, however, the Thai authorities forcibly transferred over 100 Uyghur men to Chinese authorities, who flew them to China.

On February 27, 2025, Thai authorities took 40 Uyghur men from immigration detention and forcibly returned them to China. Upon the men’s return, the Thai authorities participated in a Chinese government-organized propaganda trip to show some of these men with their families. Despite promises by the Thai and Chinese authorities, there has been no credible monitoring of those Uyghurs since their return.

UN human rights experts issued a statement on the one-year anniversary of the deportation and said the families of returnees “have received no communication, no confirmation of the place of detention or whereabouts, and no indication as to whether their loved ones are even alive.”

Following the deportation, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk said in February 2025 that it was “a clear violation of international human rights laws and standards,” and urged Thai authorities to “ensure there are no further deportations and the remaining members of the group, including potential refugees and asylum-seekers, being held in Thailand are fully protected in accordance with their obligations under international law.”

Uyghurs are Turkic-speaking Muslims, most of whom live in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in China’s northwest. Over the past 10 years, China’s repression of Uyghurs has increased significantly as the authorities have intensified a widespread and systematic campaign of human rights abuses against them, several amounting to crimes against humanity.

The authorities have subjected the population to arbitrary detention, unjust imprisonment, mass surveillance, forced labor, cultural and religious persecution, and severe movement restrictions. Uyghurs considered to have left China illegally are, if forcibly returned, viewed with intense suspicion and subject to arbitrary detention, interrogation, torture, and other cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment.

“Prior to the UN Human Rights Council’s review of Thailand’s human rights record on November 9, governments should press Thailand to immediately end its abusive treatment of Uyghurs,” Pearson said. “The Thai authorities should be protecting Uyghurs fleeing persecution, not jailing them, and certainly not returning them to harm’s way.”
