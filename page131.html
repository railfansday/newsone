After spending more than a decade in the English game, former Arsenal defender Kieran Gibbs decided he was up for a new challenge in 2021.

This saw the 10-time England international head across the Atlantic, where he signed for Inter Miami, in a move that saw him experience and embrace an entirely different footballing culture.

For Gibbs, the opportunity to work with David Beckham and Phil Neville would prove to be a key factor in his decision to up sticks, as was the chance to play a part in a league that was clearly growing.

After spending his whole career in English football, Gibbs was keen to try something new, as he recalls the circumstances that lead to his decision to move across the Atlantic.

“I felt like I wanted to try something new and different,” Gibbs tells FourFourTwo. “Once I spoke to David Beckham and Phil Neville, the game in the US and how much it’s growing appealed to me. There’s a lot of opportunities here. I’d always wanted to play in a different country.”

Gibbs would enjoy two seasons playing in Miami before hanging up his boots in 2022, but remains settled in Florida to this day.

“I’m glad that I picked here because it feels like home now,” he continues. “I go back to the UK when I can, but I’m very settled here. “I’m very grateful that I got to play football in another country and experience a different league.”

And while Gibbs clearly has no regrets over this move, he concedes there were initial challenges when he made the move.

“I knew it was going to be a physical game,” he admits. “People mentioned the travel, but I didn’t think that was going to be quite so difficult.

“The heat in Miami is tough as well, when you’re playing through the summer. When I moved here in July, I struggled to adapt to that.”

Gibbs also remains excited about the future of the MLS, believing that last summer’s World Cup will help the sport to continue to grow in the United States.

“It’s a growing league and there’s a lot more to come from MLS over the coming years. The World Cup this year could be a springboard for the future.

“The game has come a long way since the last World Cup here in 1994, when MLS hadn’t even been founded, so it’s still a very young sport. That’s why I’m excited to be here. I want to see how we can keep transforming the game.”

The three-headed Kaiju's origins aren't just space-based; one of the largest structures in the universe shares its name.

Godzilla goes toe-to-toe with long-time foe King Ghidorah in Godzilla Minus Zero, which opened in theaters on Friday (Nov. 6). Though the three-headed golden Kaiju might seem like a natural native of Monster Island, King Ghidorah actually emerged from the depths of space.

However, it isn't just the origins of King Ghidorah — also known as Monster Zero in the Godzilla movie Invasion of Astro-Monster (1965) — that are this beast's only cosmic connection. One of the largest structures in the universe, the King Ghidorah Supercluster, was named in honor of Godzilla's primary antagonist.

King Ghidorah is a three-headed, twin-tailed golden dragon from the stars, first introduced in the fifth Godzilla movie, Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster (1964).

In this first appearance, part of the Shōwa era (1964–1975) of Godzilla history, Ghidorah ravaged Venus, wiping out its population 5,000 years before arriving on Earth encased within a highly magnetic meteorite. Ghidorah was bested this first time by Godzilla, Rodan, and Mothra.

Undeterred, Ghidorah returned for a rematch just a year later in Invasion of Astro Monster (1965), after being discovered supposedly terrorizing the inhabitants of Planet X, a world found beyond Jupiter. In this movie, it is revealed that Ghidorah is less a king and more a pawn, under the control of calculating Xiliens as part of a plan to subjugate Earth. This will become a theme.

Freed from that control, Ghidorah returns to deep space, once again falling under the thrall of an alien race in 1968's Destroy All Monsters.This movie sees Ghidorah hold its own against a who's who of kaiju, eventually succumbing to the combined might of Godzilla, Minilla, Mothra, Rodan, Manda, Gorosaurus, Anguirus, Kumonga, Baragon, and Varan.

Even with three gravity-beam-blasting heads, that is hardly fair!

Ghidorah returned with some backup for a final time in the Shōwa era in 1975's Godzilla vs. Gigan. This time, Ghidorah and his tag-team partner Gigan (who resembled a giant one-eyed space chicken) were under the control of cockroach-like Nebulans. Getting mind-controlled three times is just embarrassing!

Fortunately, the Shōwa era drew to a close in 1975 before Ghidorah was forced into servitude for a fourth time.

Ghidorah returned in the rebooted Godzilla timeline of the Heisei era (1991–1998) in 1991's Godzilla Vs. King Ghidorah, but its cosmic origins are gone. The Heisei Ghidorah begins life as three genetically engineered creatures called Dorats in the future before being morphed into a three-headed form via nuclear exposure.

Ghidorah was given a spiritual origin in the Millennium era's Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack (2001), actually serving as a protagonist, helping Mothra in halting Godzilla's rampage.

Ghidorah is once again an extraterrestrial in Universal Pictures' MonsterVerse movie Godzilla: King of the Monsters (2019), being described as having "fallen from the stars" in ancient times before rising to challenge Godzilla.

Now King Ghidorah is back once again in Godzilla Minus Zero, maintaining his extraterrestrial origin story and once again challenging Godzilla to an epic kaiju battle. Those are some of the fictional versions of this golden beast, but what does it look like in reality?

Arguably the most impressive feat of King Ghidorah has been to make itself such an important part of pop culture that astronomers took note. The King Ghidorah supercluster is a collection of galaxies located around 6.7 billion light-years from Earth. Covering an area of the sky equivalent to 19 full moons, as seen from our vantage point, this cosmic Kaiju was discovered in late 2022 by Japanese astronomers using data from the Subaru Telescope.

The King Ghidorah supercluster is comprised of at least 19 individual associated galaxy clusters. It stretches out for an incredible 1.3 billion light-years, meaning the entire Milky Way could fit across it around 12,300 times. That makes it the largest supercluster ever discovered.

To put into context just how monstrous the King Ghidorah supercluster is, consider the fact that it has a mass equivalent to 10,000,000,000,000,000 suns, 10 times as massive as the Milky Way's home supercluster, Laniakea. Even Godzilla may think twice before attempting to tussle with that particular King Ghidorah!

Almost exactly a decade ago, the media industry was in the middle of a video revolution — or so it thought. In the mid-2010s, publications poured money into burgeoning social media video efforts, chasing the promise of reaching larger audiences and opening new lines of revenue. Print publications and digital news outlets alike hired teams, pumped out content, and entrusted platforms like Facebook with their reach and distribution.

That pivot-to-video slowly revealed itself to be a lot of smoke and mirrors when it became clear that Facebook had inflated video views. Newsrooms cut writers and editors in a move to video — and then cut those video teams when they realized the pivot had not paid off. For many in the media industry, social video brings to mind layoffs and instability rather than the promised abundance.

A “pivot” feels inadequate to describe the current emphasis on social video, not just in newsrooms but in society writ large. Social media companies have reorganized their feeds to emphasize video, spurred by the massive growth of TikTok during the pandemic. But the media industry is still playing catch-up, and in some cases it seems to have been slow to jump in (perhaps cautious given past experience). It took years for some publishers to start incorporating vertical video on their websites, apps, and stories, for example, and it takes coordination and intention to get individual reporters in front of a camera to talk about their work. In the meantime, YouTube has become the most popular streamer, commanding nearly 16 percent of TV viewing, according to Nielsen. Twenty percent of Americans regularly get news from TikTok, and that number jumps to 39 percent for people 18 to 29 years old, Pew found.

Viewers favor the stripped-down selfie-style video dispatches from personalities they trust, even if those creators are not themselves reporting and writing the news. Compared with a decade ago, the barrier to entry and cost of making video content has plummeted: Anyone with a smartphone can become a video creator, no fancy equipment or editing needed. Social video is fast, personality-driven, casual, and, at times, unfiltered — the exact opposite from what news outlets are used to. A second pivot-to-video is underway, and the media industry once again wants in.

Video is unavoidable on social media, if not an expected part of how people and institutions show up online. TikTok’s influence is felt everywhere: on competitor platforms like Meta’s Reels and YouTube Shorts, of course, but also in unexpected places, like LinkedIn. There are no signs of it slowing down, either: The time spent watching Reels on Facebook was at an all-time high in Q3. Early tests in other countries suggest Meta is toying with the idea of dropping users directly into full-screen video upon opening the Instagram and Facebook apps. The appetite for video seems insatiable, and if news organizations won’t lean in, armies of content creators will be happy to scoop up the audiences.

Some news outlets were relatively early to the short-form social video boom. The Washington Post, for example, found notable success on TikTok before many peers had even created dedicated accounts on the platform. (Dave Jorgenson, the face of the Post’s social video effort, left the paper last year. He now makes short and longform videos under Local News International — perhaps a testament to the trust that viewers place in individual creators.)

Beyond having a short-form video presence, some traditional news outlets are leaning into formats and styles popularized in the vertical video era. CNN partnered with Jubilee, the popular channel that produces debate-me-style videos on hot-button political topics. The CNN town hall with Texas Senate candidate James Talarico was conducted in the Jubilee style. The New York Times, which for years has produced polished documentary-style videos, recently introduced the vertical video series “The Call.” Unlike the Times’ more buttoned-up videos, the point of “The Call” is to create a sense of intimacy, like you’re getting updates from a friend: The show’s host ostensibly makes a video call to a journalist reporting on the ground to ask them about what they’re witnessing.

News outlets are also directly hiring or working with content creators. Some outlets like The Washington Post have spun up so-called creator networks, where they partner with influencers to produce content independent from the newsroom. A new program supported by Poynter and Microsoft pairs content creators with a handful of news outlets so they can create content tailored for social media.

And then, of course, there are the clips. Podcasts became videos, which became bite-sized clips of podcasts formatted for vertical feeds. TV shows became web shows, which gave rise to microdramas. News articles — even major investigations — are now adapted for vertical video audiences. Individual reporters (myself included) make talking-head clips explaining our reporting. Earlier this year, I called this phenomenon “the clippening”: the reformatting of all media to be legible in a vertical video feed, even if the original work is neither vertical nor video. It’s hard to fault the people and institutions putting resources into social video — audiences are consuming it — but it’s also feeling a lot like the past.

One of the misguided beliefs of pivot-to-video 1.0 was that traction, views, or engagement on social media platforms would be enough to offset (and ideally surpass) the cost of making the content. For many, that ended up not being true, with flawed metrics, of course, being part of the reason: Virality feels nice, but monetizing that attention isn’t always straightforward. These days, social media companies have largely coalesced around the “views” measurement meaning the number of times a post was served up on somebody’s feed, whether they watched the whole thing or swiped away immediately. How many people who watch a clip of a podcast go on to listen to the full episode? Are the people watching my TikToks about my stories actually going to The Verge to read it, or buying a subscription to support my work? You can’t eat views.

The hope is that we will find new audiences where they’re already spending time. There are millions of people discussing news that journalists are uncovering — it’s important that we reach them, build trust, and be part of the conversation. What’s certain is that if reporters and news outlets don’t, a whole slew of creators will be there to take their place.

Just as we’re getting used to short form, the ground is once again shifting under our feet. Not content simply being social media, platforms are looking to be a destination for longer form, higher production quality entertainment. Tech companies know that people watch videos on their phones, but they would also really like users to watch on their televisions, too.

This spring, YouTube’s pitch to content creators (and advertisers) was to make exclusive shows for the platform. YouTube is dominating the TV streaming wars, but other platforms like Instagram are pushing in that direction, too: Its recently announced “Features” effort allows for 90-minute horizontal video uploads, and it seems to be specifically angling for television viewing. (Unsurprisingly, there are a lot of questions from creators about what they get in return for posting longer content.) Some news outlets are already thinking ahead and eyeing longform. The BBC struck a deal with YouTube in early 2026 to create special programming specifically for the platform, then followed it up by announcing a 24/7 YouTube livestream in an effort to reach young people.

Short form isn’t going anywhere, at least right now. But these efforts to move into longer, more resource-intensive video content could be a harbinger of what’s to come — and what could soon be asked of news publishers and influencers alike. News media have had a complicated relationship with tech platforms. On one hand, it’s where audiences are, and platforms allow publishers to reach those people. On the other hand, tech companies signal to outlets (and creators) what kind of content they should prioritize, and everyone has to scramble to meet the new demand. Just as soon as journalists start getting comfortable with front-facing selfie videos, we may be facing a whole new beast: videos that are horizontal and longer, with a higher production value and higher barrier to entry that we typically associate with TV and streaming.

The US military said a vessel trying to leave an Iranian port was hit due to the naval blockade on the country. On its part, Iran said three "non compliant" oil tankers caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz.

The United States struck a commercial ship attempting to breach its naval blockade on Iran, the US military said Sunday.

According to the US Central Command (CENTCOM), the cargo vessel "tried to depart regional waters after visiting an Iranian port," before its stern was struck by "precision munitions" shot from a US military aircraft.

In a video posted by CENTCOM, an explosion can be seen on the M/V Huda's stern as a result of the shooting, with the ship's civilian crew ignoring "repeated warnings."

The authorities in the UAE said 22 people had been rescued due to a fire on a vessel in the Gulf of Oman.

The US naval blockade on Iran, intended to pressure the country into lifting its own blockade on the Strait of Hormuz, has been in force for months.

Iran's own blockade of the waterway, through which a fifth of the world's oil and gas supplies flowed before the war started in late February, saw energy prices spike around the world.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said three "non-compliant" oil tankers caught fire while going through the Strait. Tehran has regularly attacked vessels going through the Strait of Hormuz without its approval.

Elsewhere in the Middle East, the escalation between Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen and Saudi Arabia continued to rage.

Saudi Arabia said it had intercepted incoming missiles shot in the direction of the capital, Riyadh, on Sunday.

How do porta potties work?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 8, 2026 8:03 AM EST

We’ve all used them. Whether at festivals, campgrounds, construction sites, or even weddings, porta potties are an unavoidable—and often underappreciated—part of modern life. They’re the unsung heroes of moments when bathrooms are few and far between, faithfully answering nature’s call, one flushless visit at a time.

But how do porta potties work, exactly? They’re so commonplace—and so universally dreaded—that most people try to get in, do their business, and get out as quickly as possible, without giving much thought to what’s happening. But plug your nose, lift the lid, and brave a peek inside, and you’ll find a simple yet elegant system designed to reliably meet one of humanity’s most basic needs.

Conventional toilets rely on a combination of water, pressure, and gravity to do their job. When you flush, water rushes from the tank into the bowl, pushing waste down through a curved pipe called a trap and into the plumbing system.

From there, the wastewater travels through sewer pipes to a treatment plant or, in some instances, a septic system. A small amount of water remains behind in the trap, creating a seal that prevents sewer gases from wafting back into the bathroom.

Once the process is complete, the tank refills with clean water from the plumbing system, typically from a municipal water supply or private well, and the porcelain throne is ready for its next user.

Portable toilets—also known as portaloos, portajohns, and a variety of other nicknames—use water, too. But that’s about where the similarities end. “There’s no plumbing, and it really doesn’t smell good,” says Walt Terry, director of operations for Metro Services, a family-owned portable toilet and dumpster rental company in Little Rock, Arkansas. “It’s not the most pleasant thing in the world.”

Modern porta potties come in a variety of shapes and sizes, from swanky multi-stall trailers to no-frills cubicles. The standard plastic model you see at events has a relatively simple design, one that traces its roots to George Hardig. Hardig filed a late-1960s patent for porta potties. Later he became a co-founder of PolyJohn Enterprises, still one of the leading portable toilet manufacturers today.

Made of large, rigid plastic panels, Hardig’s invention was designed to be weather- and insect-proof, simple to clean and maintain, and lightweight and easy to move. His model was cylindrical and featured a removable toilet tank, while today’s are usually rectangular and have the holding tank built-in. But other than that, not much has changed in porta potty design since Hardig first developed his portable toilet “cabana” nearly six decades ago.

These tall boxes have a large empty cavity at the back, topped by a toilet seat. When users do their business, waste simply drops down into the large, roomy hole beneath.

“It’s just a big void space,” says Katie Gossett, office manager for Tidy Tim’s, Inc., a portable restroom and septic service company her dad started more than 30 years ago in central Ohio.

“The holding tanks can vary a bit in size, but your average is like 25 to 30 gallons.”

The holding tank is filled with some amount of water, typically between five and 10 gallons, as well as a special, dark-blue chemical cocktail that serves numerous purposes. It helps keep the funky odors under control and starts to break down the waste and toilet paper, while also hiding the tank’s contents. The color is also a key indicator of a porta potty’s freshness.

“It will actually turn green once it’s not working anymore—when it gets there, it’s not a good sight,” says Terry. “The chemical goes in there and does as much as it can, until it can’t.”

There’s another easily overlooked component that helps keep porta potties from getting too stinky: the vent pipe. The next time you use a portable toilet, look up at the roof. You’ll spot a small pipe protruding from the back corner, discreetly drawing foul gases out of the holding tank and venting them into the air.

Some models also have built-in urinals, which simply funnel urine into the holding tank for those who prefer to remain standing when they pee.

When it’s time to service a porta potty, specially trained employees from the rental company arrive with a powerful vacuum truck. They use a hose to suck out the tank’s liquids and solids, transferring the waste into the truck for disposal.

Many vacuum trucks also have a separate compartment filled with clean water, which staffers then use to wash out the porta potty’s entire interior—the tank, as well as the walls, ceiling, and floors.

Sanitizing spray and a quick wipedown with a microfiber towel finishes the job—which is a lot less gross than you might imagine. “Our guys know what they’re doing,” says Gossett. “I’m not going to say accidents don’t happen, but most of the time, when they get [back to the office], they are exactly the way they left. They very rarely get dirty.”

Mammograms May Offer Early Insight Into Cardiovascular Disease

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Mammograms are widely used to help detect breast cancer, but they may also offer insight into heart health.

The routine X-ray screening may identify breast arterial calcification (BAC), which is the presence of calcium deposits within the walls of the small arteries in the breast.

On a mammogram, BAC is commonly identified as thin, white, parallel lines that give a “tram-track” appearance.

While BAC is harmless to the breasts, it is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease. A 2022 study found women with BAC were 51% more likely to develop heart disease or experience a stroke than those without it.

Laxmi Mehta, MD, a cardiologist at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said BAC is associated with cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol. Additionally, BAC has been connected to:

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

How to Scrape Instagram Comments With an API (curl + Python to CSV)

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Getting all the comments from a public Instagram post sounds like an afternoon of work. It usually is not. I build SocialHolmes, a social data API, so I deal with this every day. Here is why it is awkward, which options you really have, and a small script that saves any public post's comments to CSV.

1. The official Instagram Graph API. If you want comments on your own business or creator account, use it. It is the right tool: free, supported, stable. The catch is that it only returns comments on accounts connected to your app. It cannot fetch comments on someone else's public post, which is what most research, competitor and giveaway use cases need.

2. DIY scraping (Playwright/Selenium, or replaying the GraphQL calls). You get full control and no per-row fee, but you own the whole maintenance burden: login sessions, proxies, selector fixes, retries. Fine for a one-off; painful as a pipeline.

3. A paid scraping API. Someone else maintains the scraper and you call an endpoint. You trade money for time. I use SocialHolmes for the rest of this post (disclosure: I build it), but the pattern of start a run, poll, read pages is common to most of these services.

Grab the post's shortcode: the part after /p/ in the URL. In instagram.com/p/C1a2B3cDEfG/ it is C1a2B3cDEfG.

Sign up for an API key (1,000 free results, no card), then export it:

You get back 202 Accepted and a run object with an id and a status. limit is the most comments you want. It is also the most you can be billed.

Statuses are queued, running, succeeded, empty, failed and cancelled. Comments come back as JSON, up to 100 per page; use offset to page.

If you do not want to write code at all, the dashboard has a form (Instagram, Comments, paste the shortcode) and a Download CSV button.

If you use Claude or another MCP client, SocialHolmes exposes an MCP server, so you can skip the script and ask "get the first 200 comments on instagram.com/p/C1a2B3cDEfG". It runs the same endpoint and costs the same.

Comments are public, but a stored record of who said what is personal data under GDPR and similar laws. Stick to public data, respect rate limits, think about whether you really need usernames (the text and timestamp are often enough), and check the law and Instagram's terms for your jurisdiction and use case. Why Your Personal Knowledge Base Should Be Local-First and AI-Friendly

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We've become incredibly good at collecting information.

Articles, bookmarks, screenshots, PDFs, research papers, code snippets, ideas, and random thoughts. There's always something worth saving.

But there's a problem: most of this information ends up scattered across different applications.

Your notes are in one app, bookmarks in another, documents in cloud storage, and screenshots somewhere in your file system.

Over time, managing information becomes almost as difficult as finding it.

I think the next generation of personal knowledge management tools should approach this problem differently.

They should be local-first, built around portable data formats, and accessible to AI agents.

Most productivity applications provide a convenient experience by managing your information behind the scenes.

You create notes, organize folders, add tags, and everything works.

Depending on the application, exporting your information might mean downloading archives, converting proprietary formats, or losing relationships between documents.

Your information technically belongs to you, but accessing it outside the application can be difficult.

This creates unnecessary dependency on a single software provider.

A personal knowledge base should be something you can maintain independently of the application used to access it.

Local-first software prioritizes keeping data on your own device while allowing applications to provide a convenient interface for working with it.

Instead of treating the cloud as the only place where your information exists, your local device becomes an important part of the architecture.

Local-first doesn't automatically mean every application is private or secure. Implementation matters.

But the underlying philosophy is valuable: your data should not become inaccessible simply because an application changes its business model or disappears.

Markdown is one of the simplest ways to make information portable.

A Markdown file is essentially a plain-text document with lightweight formatting conventions.

You can open this file in a text editor, code editor, Markdown application, or process it programmatically.

Portability: Your notes aren't locked into a proprietary format.

Longevity: Plain text is easy to preserve and migrate.

Interoperability: Many applications and development tools support Markdown.

Automation: Scripts and AI agents can read and modify Markdown files.

This makes Markdown particularly interesting for people who want more control over their personal knowledge base.

AI agents are changing how we interact with software.

Previously, we manually searched through documents, copied information, and organized everything ourselves.

Now, agents can perform many of these tasks.

But there's a fundamental requirement: agents need access to your information.

Consider a workspace where your notes, research, and resources are stored in accessible files.

You don't necessarily need an AI chatbot built into every productivity application.

Sometimes, making your data accessible through a filesystem or CLI is enough to let external AI tools work with it.

This is an interesting design direction: instead of building another isolated AI experience, make existing software interoperable with the agents people already use.

A lot of knowledge management applications are built around documents, folders, and hierarchical organization.

That works well when you're writing structured notes.

But research and brainstorming aren't always linear activities.

Instead of organizing everything into a hierarchy, you can arrange resources spatially.

Related information can live together. Different concepts can form clusters. Your workspace can evolve as your understanding develops.

One feature I'm particularly interested in is its agent CLI.

It allows AI agents to work with the Toxvas workspace through its files, making the workspace more accessible to external tools.

It's about reducing friction between collecting information, understanding it, connecting ideas, and actually using what we've learned.

Local-first architecture, portable formats like Markdown, visual workspaces, and AI interoperability are interesting pieces of that direction.

Your knowledge base should be an asset you own, not another collection of data trapped inside an application.

Building Orquest at SharkByte: Turning Career Planning Into an AI-Powered Game 🦈🎮

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This weekend, my team and I participated in SharkByte Hackathon, where we challenged ourselves to build something that could actually help students.

One problem we kept thinking about was career planning. As students, we're constantly making decisions about classes, degrees, and our future, but sometimes we don't even know what career we want to pursue.

What if figuring out your future felt more like playing a game?

That's how Orquest — A Game for Your Future! was born.

Orquest is an AI-powered career discovery game that helps students explore their interests, discover possible careers, and build personalized educational pathways.

Instead of starting with a degree, we wanted students to discover the jobs that interest them first.

The idea is to make career planning something students can actively explore instead of just reading a list of recommendations.

We built Orquest using React, JavaScript, Vite, Python, and FastAPI.

We integrated Google Gemini API for career guidance and used GitHub to manage our team's work.

We also experimented with Swarms API and Prune API, successfully testing a three-agent Swarms workflow and collecting a verified signed Prune report.

For deployment, we used Docker and Render.

During development, we worked with OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Base44 to help us build, debug, and improve different parts of the application.

One of the hardest parts wasn't writing the code. It was making everything work together.

At one point, our frontend couldn't communicate with our backend because of a CORS issue.

Then we spent a lot of time trying to get Gemini working. Our API requests kept failing until we discovered a problem with the JSON schema we were sending.

We also had to manage different GitHub branches, integrate our teammates' work, and deal with AI development tool limits. When one of our teammates ran out of Codex credits, he switched to Base44 to continue improving the UI.

And of course, we were doing all of this while trying to stay within a $0 out-of-pocket budget.

Eventually, we got Orquest deployed publicly, and seeing Gemini generate a real career response through our website was one of the best moments of the hackathon.

This hackathon taught me that software development is much more than knowing how to program.

You need to understand how different systems connect, how to investigate errors, how to communicate with your team, and how to make decisions when time is running out.

I also learned a lot about REST APIs, AI integrations, frontend/backend communication, testing, and deployment.

One of my biggest takeaways was that getting something to work is exciting, but understanding why it works is even more valuable.

Orquest is still growing. We want to improve the user experience, expand our verified educational pathways, and eventually let students save their career progress permanently.

This weekend showed me how much a team can accomplish when everyone brings different skills and ideas together.

I'm grateful for the experience and everything we learned along the way.
