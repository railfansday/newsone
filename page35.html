FHFA raises conforming loan limit to $855,400 in 2027

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

FHFA will increase the 2027 baseline conforming loan limit by 2.71% to $855,400 for one-unit properties, with a $1,283,100 ceiling in high-cost areas and higher caps in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands.   AI Summary

The baseline conforming loan limit will rise 2.71% in 2027 to $855,400 for one-unit properties in most parts of the country, according to the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will be authorized to buy loans up to that balance in the vast majority of U.S. counties.

The $855,400 cap is an increase of $22,650 from 2026 and reflects slower home price growth tied to a weaker housing market and higher-for-longer mortgage rates. The annual adjustment was 3.25% last year and above 5% in each of the prior two years.

Under the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 (HERA), the national “ceiling” for high-cost areas is set at 150% of the baseline. For 2027, that ceiling will be $1,283,100 for one-unit properties in markets where 115% of the local median home value exceeds the baseline limit. Conforming caps first surpassed $1 million in these high-cost areas in 2022.

Special statutory provisions apply in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands. In these areas, the 2027 baseline loan limit for one-unit properties will be $1,283,100, while the ceiling will be $1,924,650.

“Due to rising home values, the CLL will be higher in all but 24 U.S. counties or county equivalents,” the FHFA said in a statement.

Each November, FHFA publishes new conforming loan limits for the coming year. Increases resumed in 2016, after home prices returned to pre-Great Recession levels, a condition required by HERA. The statute also prevents the ceiling from declining in years when home prices fall; the cap only moves higher when national prices increase year over year.

This year’s adjustment is tied to FHFA’s third-quarter 2026 House Price Index (HPI), which tracks changes in average U.S. home values over the prior four quarters. Nominal, seasonally adjusted, expanded-data HPI showed house prices rose 2.72% on average between the third quarters of 2025 and 2026, essentially matching the 2.71% rise in the baseline conforming loan limit.

The new limits expand the amount of mortgage debt that can qualify for conventional financing backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Loans above FHFA’s limits are classified as jumbos, a segment still dominated by banks and portfolio lenders that set their own credit standards and capital requirements.

Mortgage lenders once again anticipated FHFA’s announcement and raised their internal conforming loan limits ahead of the official release to compete for volume. But they were conservative.

Rocket Mortgage was an early mover, lifting its conforming loan limit to $845,000 for single-family homes in 48 states in September. CrossCountry Mortgage adopted the same level.

Pennymac set its internal limit at $850,000, while United Wholesale Mortgage increased its one-unit cap for conventional and Department of Veterans Affairs loans to $847,440.

Leash Biosciences Raises $11M to Build Scientific AI Training Worlds

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Leash Biosciences announced an $11 million financing on November 24, 2026 to build scientific AI training environments from wet-lab measurements. Innovation Endeavors invested alongside Insight Partners, backing Leash’s effort to give AI agents reusable experience from physical experiments.

Innovation Endeavors reported that Leash’s collection has reached 6.3 million molecules, 44 billion experimental measurements and more than 1,200 proteins. The investor’s thesis is that AI for medicine development needs more and better data, not simply larger models and more compute, and that Leash’s measured datasets can address models’ difficulty generalizing beyond their training data.

The financing follows Leash’s October 14, 2026 introduction of Minos, a framework that turns premeasured protein–small-molecule binding experiments into replayable worlds. An agent selects experiments within a constrained budget, receives outcomes derived from wet-lab measurements rather than human or model judgments, updates its hypotheses and chooses again. The physical work is paid for during construction, allowing subsequent agent runs to reuse the measurements at computational speed.

An accompanying working draft calls this property counterfactual density: researchers can compare an agent’s choices with the measured outcomes of alternatives it skipped. Historical datasets contain only experiments their original creators chose to run, while conventional lab-in-the-loop systems must physically run additional experiments to obtain new outcomes.

In the main cold-start task, an agent must find the strongest binder it can among roughly 1 million or 6 million molecules, depending on the library, with no starting hits and a budget of 100 molecule tests submitted in batches of up to five. This is a simplified structure-activity relationship campaign, meaning the agent uses measured responses to chemical changes to guide subsequent choices.

Leash tested 58 combinations of chemical library, model and inference effort. The draft reports substantial variation in performance: greater inference effort generally helped within model families, but spending alone did not determine success. Many configurations outperformed random search.

To test whether agents were using chemistry, researchers scrambled the molecular structures associated with building-block identities while preserving those identities and the measured outcomes. Performance dropped sharply in both libraries, which Leash interpreted as evidence that agents were using chemistry information rather than relying solely on structure-blind local search.

Leash also examined 4,602 independent structure-activity-relationship trajectories and identified recurring failure modes: stopping before the experimental budget was exhausted, repeatedly testing the same molecule and weaker models persevering in unproductive chemical neighborhoods. Stronger models more often abandoned those neighborhoods, showing how the environments can expose decision processes rather than just endpoint scores.

The STRELKA release contains Minos worlds for 34 protein targets screened against AMA020, packaged for Harbor, together with narrations from that trajectory corpus. The official STRELKA page asks visitors to request access.

The experiments remain narrower than full medicinal chemistry. They concern binding enrichment against single protein targets, not the broader optimization of potency, selectivity and drug-like properties required in development campaigns. Agents are also restricted to molecules already screened unless Leash orders or synthesizes and tests new ones.

The financing backs a data infrastructure approach in which the value lies in reusable physical experience, not just the binding candidates a screen might produce. Leash’s working draft leaves open whether experience acquired in Minos improves scientific decision-making in previously unseen physical worlds, a central qualification for its potential use in reinforcement learning and other post-training.

Why Is Everyone So Obsessed With Protein?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Welcome to Gut Check, our column dedicated to the complex, ever-evolving relationship between food and our bodies. Whether you’re curious about mindful eating or want to understand what makes picky eaters picky, read on and let award-winning journalist Betsy Andrews answer all your burning questions.

I recently shed 20 pounds for my health, and I’m keeping it off by avoiding simple carbohydrates—pasta, pastries, white rice, most breads—and exercising with weights and resistance bands. Carbs make you feel full, at least temporarily, and without them, especially when I’m hungry after a workout, I’m turning to an even more filling macronutrient: protein. I’m 63, and my wife is 60. We’ve heard that dietary protein is key to wellness in aging. But, after recent blood work, she is worrying about “bad,” or LDL, cholesterol. All this leaves me pondering the types and amounts of proteins I should be cooking that won’t increase her cholesterol levels or my waistline but will make for satisfying, healthy meals.

In my current obsession with protein, I’m not alone. Americans are bingeing on the stuff: Social media is glutted with influencers promoting protein powders and heavy meat consumption; supermarket shelves have filled with packaged snacks advertising excess protein; and restaurants are overhauling menus to feed the needs of protein-hungry diners. Even the new USDA dietary guidelines encourage protein uptake. At its extreme, it’s a trend known as protein-maxxing, and everyone from the Kardashians to the American Medical Association is talking about it.

Our bodies need protein to function. “Protein is made of amino acids. When we digest protein, we release those amino acids and use them to build and maintain our own proteins: muscle, liver, heart, lungs, and even some bone,” explains kinesiology professor Stuart Phillips, director of the Physical Activity Centre of Excellence (PACE) at Ontario’s McMaster University in Canada. “Protein supplies materials for enzymes, antibodies, transport proteins, some hormones, and the repair of tissues and organs.”

Of the 20 amino acids in our bodies, nine are essential, meaning we can’t create them ourselves. We must get them from eating protein, and we need to keep up protein consumption, because our bodies deplete it as we digest it. “The simple takeaway is that protein builds you from the inside out. It is foundational,” says dietitian Daphene Altema-Johnson, senior program officer at the Johns Hopkins Center for a Livable Future. “But unlike fat, your body doesn’t have a dedicated storage system for protein, so you need a steady supply from food.”

In a culture built on body image, protein is also touted for its body-sculpting and weight-loss benefits. “You use protein to help build and maintain muscle mass and also recover from a workout,” explains Zach Rocheleau, the creator of the protein-rich recipes that comprise The Flexible Dieting Lifestyle. “It’s also the most satiating macronutrient. Getting the satiety effects of protein and thus feeling full, so eating less calories, is the driver that goes into losing weight.”

When influencers like Rocheleau claim that the protein they’re eating has helped them shape their physique, they aren’t wrong. Research does show that if you eat more protein it assists (to a point) in building lean muscle mass during resistance training. And studies have established the relationship between protein consumption, satiation, and weight loss. “The hype has come from real physiological needs,” says Dr. Megan Rossi, founder of The Gut Health Doctor, “but it’s more of an aesthetic effect, where people associate protein with big muscles and think the more protein they eat, the bulkier they’ll get.”

Scarfing down cheeseburgers and looking great doing it sounds good, but eating too much protein can put stress on your body, especially the kidneys. When you eat protein, Altema-Johnson explains, “The nitrogen part of it is stripped off and converted to urea, which is filtered out by your kidneys. Lots of nitrogen waste leads to a chronically higher kidney workload.”

For most people, this shouldn’t be much to worry about: Though he notes that this isn’t an endorsement of long-term protein bingeing, Phillips’ meta-analysis found no negative effect of higher protein consumption on kidney function in healthy adults. But if your kidneys aren’t in tip-top shape, stressing them can contribute to high blood pressure and other heart conditions. And studies show that consume excess animal-based proteins, in particular, can increase the risk of kidney stones.

There’s another potential problem: Your small intestine can only digest so much protein, so in high amounts, some of it ends up in your colon, where it can produce potentially harmful by-products. “Excess protein, like poorly absorbed egg whites and red meat, can ferment and produce toxic metabolites, which are associated with heart disease,” says Rossi. The solution is to eat fiber as well. But when you’re protein-maxxing, other nutrients, including fiber, can fall to the wayside.

Finally, though protein-maxxing is often touted as a way to slim down, the opposite can be true, too. “Your body has a ceiling for how much protein you can use,” says Altema-Johnson. Excess protein gets converted into glucose or fat for energy. Calories are energy units. You only need so many of them, and if you’re “chasing a protein number instead of eating until full,” as Altema-Johnson puts it, studies show that the extra calories can lead to weight gain.

Some protein-enriched packaged foods harbor sneaky calories. According to Rocheleau—who, by the way, eschews the phrase “protein-maxxing” in his work—“You would think that somebody putting out a high-protein product would take calories from carbs and fats and replace them with protein, but generally they keep the product the same but add protein, so you’re getting a lot more calories.” Plus, the marketing of protein-added snacks actually creates an insidious halo effect. “If you slap ‘high protein’ on a package, there’s the perception that it is automatically healthy, which can lead to overconsumption,” says Altema-Johnson. And while dairy- or plant-based protein products, including powdered supplements, can be a convenient way to support higher training needs to fill a genuine gap in the diet, Phillips’ research shows that they’re not a substitute for exercise itself and that protein intake had diminishing gains over a certain amount.

So what is the optimum amount of protein we should consume? Guidelines differ. Like the USDA previously, Canadian guidelines suggest a daily protein intake of .8 grams per kilogram of body weight. (To convert your body weight from pounds to kilograms, just divide it by 2.2.) For me, at 77 kilograms, 62 grams of protein per day is a good target, according to these guidelines, which comes out to a little over 8 ounces of roast chicken to meet my goals for the day. U.K. guidelines recommend slightly less, at .75 grams per kilogram, while the new USDA guidelines have been cranked up to 1.2 to 1.6 grams. At that serving goal, I can scarf down more than a pound of roast bird a day, and in theory, that’s what experts want me to do. For active and older adults, Phillips likes the new USDA targets, because we lose muscle mass as we age, and we start to need more protein to help retain it.

But not all protein is created equal. With my partner’s concern over cholesterol, maybe a skin-on bird dripping fat or a well-marbled steak seared in butter, both high in saturated fat, isn’t optimal for us. “This is where individualization matters,” says Altema-Johnson. “The goal is not zero red meat, but smaller proportions, moderation, and smarter cuts.” Experts like her, and plenty of studies, will warn you away from fattier and, especially, processed meats. “Unprocessed steak, ground beef, even pork chops have a modest health-associated risk,” Altema-Johnson says. “But bacon, sausage, deli meat, hot dogs—if you’re eating those constantly, you’re at higher risk for heart disease, colorectal cancer, and other health issues.”

Unlike animal proteins, not all plants contain every amino acid that our bodies need. But you can combine various vegetable proteins to meet those goals. Phillips and his colleagues found that young vegan and omnivorous men eating around 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day achieved similar muscle and strength gains with resistance training. “Grains tend to be relatively low in lysine. Legumes help address that limitation, while grains complement legumes’ amino acid profiles,” he says. “Beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, edamame, eggs, milk, yogurt, nuts, and seeds can all contribute. Soy is a particularly useful plant protein.” You don’t have to mix and match them at every meal, but a diverse plant-based diet can bring the protein you need.

The biggest takeaway is that you can’t just protein-max in the absence of overall healthy lifestyle choices. One of those is a balanced diet. According to Altema-Johnson, we should “individualize, moderate and balance. It’s important to have a diverse plate. If you’re focusing on one type of protein, especially meat, you’re missing out on all the good yummies a body needs.”

Chief among those yummies is fiber. Rossi says that recent studies support the idea that increasing protein can have benefits for weight-lifting and even aging, “but you need to increase fiber to balance it so your microbiome has more to eat than just excess protein. We need to swing the fiber flag.” Another recent study showed that 93 percent of Americans get enough protein, but other research reveals the opposite for fiber: Just 7 percent of us eat an adequate amount. That’s not good. A review of more than 5,000 studies found that a diet low in red meat but high in fiber-rich whole grains, vegetables, and nuts lowered our risk of death due to heart disease. “For gut health,” Phillips says, we should “ask what your protein habit is pushing off the plate.”

The other important lifestyle choice is exercise. “You can’t just expect to be sitting on the couch and eating protein and having these huge gains. Physical activity and resistance training bring far bigger results,” Rossi notes. That’s why I’ve ultimately been committed to moving more: Preventing muscle loss is insurance for my future. “Without enough protein and—most importantly, in my opinion—resistance exercise, this loss speeds up significantly,” says Altema-Johnson. “It affects strength, ability, balance, and fall risk. Muscle loss is directly tied to independence. It’s the difference between carrying your own groceries at 80 or needing help.”

Of course, I’m getting a late start. “You do not wake up at 65 with a new protein requirement. Start paying attention in midlife (over 40), and pair the protein with strength training, as that is the big lever to promote health, not protein,” notes Phillips. Well, I’m not 65 yet, but I did almost hit that age without addressing these health needs. Now I know that I need to exercise, and to support the training, I need a bit more protein, but not piles of it, and it has to come from a variety of sources. Perhaps most importantly, I’ll continue to take whatever the trend-setters are pushing on the socials with a grain of salt. As Phillips puts it, “Protein is a nutrient, not an identity. The goal is enough to support health, not as much as you can eat.”

Specifically, the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE)’s analysis showed that an estimated 7.8% of Medicaid state-directed payments reached rural hospitals in FY2025, or $11.5 billion out of $148.3 billion. Scaling from that baseline in line with H.R. 1’s multi-year payment rate phasedown, with some cumulative adjustments, showed that total nominal cumulative spending on rural state-directed payments would decline by 42.9%, or $81.6 billion, from 2026 through 2035 under the bill. This rate is again slightly higher than the CMS Office of the Actuary’s 40.8% estimate for all affected hospitals, which ASPE said is the result of the higher average dollar weighted payment rates rural hospitals currently receive, “which means that rural providers have more room to reduce rates to meet the new limits of 100% to 110% of Medicare.”

The ASPE report’s bottom line, however, is that $75.2 billion of the $81.6 billion reduction rural hospitals face, or 92.1%, reflects projected increases over the FY2025 baseline of $11.5 billion. “Counting every dollar of projected savings as a cut below existing funding gives the public the wrong picture.
