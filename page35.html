Britain’s coastal floods have a surprising connection to distant skies

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and Arctic help explain where extreme storm surges strike Britain’s coast and northwestern Europe.

Storm surges look like local disasters, but which stretch of Britain’s coast floods can often be traced to air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Those far-off patterns help decide whether northern or southern coasts take the high water.

A surge is hard to forecast, because wind, low air pressure, and the shape of the coast all play a part at once.

Understanding why surges hit certain places, such as East Anglia in eastern England, could give coastal towns earlier flood warnings.

Lisanne Blok of the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the British Antarctic Survey.

Her team studied more than 70 years of sea levels around northwestern Europe.

A computer model rebuilt those levels day by day from 1950 onward, using past weather data rather than tide gauge readings.

The team first removed the tides and the normal seasonal rise and fall of the sea. Then they used machine learning to sort the coastline into regions that tend to get extreme surges on the same days.

They ended up with six regions across the seas of northwestern Europe, plus eight smaller ones inside the North Sea.

“If we can identify conditions emerging in, for example, the Atlantic that might result in a storm surge in East Anglia, then we can pinpoint parts of the coast that should prepare for flooding,” said Blok.

Big, repeating pressure patterns over the ocean are what scientists call climate modes.

“Computer models used to forecast risk of coastal flooding don’t always account for large-scale climate modes, instead focusing on localized weather variables,” she said. “But what our findings tell us is that climate modes are really key.”

A storm surge builds when strong winds and low air pressure push a bulge of seawater toward land.

Sometimes it rises high enough to spill over flood defenses. If that happens at high tide, with big waves on top, the sea can reach extreme heights and flood the coast.

Over January 31 and February 1, 1953, a deep low-pressure system moved down the North Sea and pushed a huge mass of water along Britain’s east coast.

It arrived with a spring tide, the extra-high tide that comes around a new or full moon, and sea levels rose about 10 feet (3 meters) above predicted levels.

Floods swept through parts of Britain, the Netherlands, and Belgium.

Another surge struck Britain’s east coast on December 5 and 6, 2013, with water levels in some places higher than during the 1953 flood, but modern defenses prevented a disaster on that scale.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel shape, tightest between East Anglia and the Netherlands, pushes water toward land. Its shallow floor, roughly 30 to 490 feet (10 to 150 meters) deep, makes the surge taller.

On the worst 1% of days in the rebuilt records, surges added at least 22 inches (57 centimeters) to the North Sea’s water level, more than in any other region.

“The storm surges are very dynamic and complex in this region,” said Blok. “There is also heightened risk, because lots of people live along the low-lying coastal regions bordering the North Sea.”

Next, the team compared each region’s surges with those big pressure patterns.

“We can link regional storm surge extremes to very distinct climate patterns,” Blok said. “I didn’t expect such clear relationships, especially across the large distances involved.”

The strongest links were to pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere, including the North Atlantic Oscillation, a seesaw in air pressure between the regions around Iceland and the Azores.

When Iceland’s pressure is low compared with the Azores, the jet stream, a fast wind high up, moves north, and surges become more likely on northern and central coasts.

“As the storms shift to the north, they also deliver high seas to more northerly coasts,” said Blok.

When pressure over Iceland is unusually high instead, the pressure difference with the Azores shrinks.

The jet stream slows and bends south. Storms then take southern routes, bringing high seas to southern coasts.

Storms behind extreme surges off southwest England and west of Ireland tended to start near Newfoundland, off eastern Canada, thousands of miles away.

Storms behind surges farther north, in the seas toward Norway, more often began off Greenland or in the central North Atlantic.

Those southwestern surges were also more common in autumn, while the other regions saw most of theirs in winter.

Ali Mashayek, an associate professor in Cambridge’s Department of Earth Sciences, was the study’s senior author.

“Conditions in the Arctic have a big impact on UK weather,” he said. “That’s something we’ve known for a while, although we haven’t been able to link it to storm surge distribution before.”

Mashayek said Arctic warming will weaken the pressure difference that powers the jet stream, and “that means it is likely we will see a shift in UK storm patterns, including where storm surges occur, in future.”

But he added that “this is something we are still trying to understand.”

It’s time to view local weather extremes in a global context, Mashayek said. “We now have more evidence that the extreme weather in our backyard may be driven by what happens in very distant parts of the world.”

“This speaks to the broader issue that climate change and the extremes it induces are a truly global problem that need global solutions.”

The surge records left out waves and long-term sea level rise. The team also didn’t check whether the links change from season to season.

Next, the researchers want to run the same analysis on future climate projections, to see whether the surge regions shift as the planet warms.

The full study was published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

Flash memory maker YMTC is pushing ahead with plans for a Shanghai IPO as soon as early 2027

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Chinese NAND flash maker plans to raise 33 billion yuan ($4.9 billion) in the offering, according to Bloomberg

Yangtze Memory Technologies Co., China's biggest maker of NAND flash memory, is aiming to go public on the Shanghai Stock Exchange as soon as the first quarter of 2027, according to Bloomberg.

The Wuhan-based company, known as YMTC, plans to list on the exchange's tech-focused Star board, and that effort remains in progress, according to Bloomberg. YMTC advanced to the following phase in September, with plans to raise 33 billion yuan ($4.9 billion). The Shanghai Stock Exchange put its initial set of questions to the company in early September, which marked the formal start of the review.

The IPO had been paused as of last week, according to Reuters, which based its report on an internal company memo and on sources with knowledge of the situation.

China's stocks regulator held a meeting with investment banks, where it informally told them to halt IPOs, according to the Financial Times, which cited three unidentified people. Big stake sales by existing shareholders are also on temporary hold until markets rebound.

The pause is not a “one-size-fits-all” measure, according to Bloomberg, which cited a Cailian report quoting a senior investment banker. The banker said that easing the flow of new shares a bit suits what the market currently needs and should not be taken as IPOs being shut down entirely.

Dealmaking has slowed after a wave of fundraising that ran through the end of summer, according to Bloomberg. That wave included CXMT’s 66.6 billion yuan ($9.9 billion) offering in July. YMTC's progress may calm worries over swings in the market and the prospect that Beijing will delay listings for several of its biggest companies.

YMTC filed for a Shanghai IPO in August to raise $4.9 billion. Its prospectus outlined plans to issue new stock equal to 10% to 12% of its post-offering share capital, with an overallotment option of up to 15%. The company said it would use the proceeds to upgrade production lines and develop next-generation storage products, and it said it expected to list in 2027.

YMTC ranks third among NAND memory makers by shipments, behind Samsung Electronics Co. and SK Hynix, according to Bloomberg, which cited Counterpoint Research data. YMTC passed Kioxia Holdings and Micron $MU Technology to reach that position.

Trump wanted little Kei trucks for America, Toyota makes it happen

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Does this herald the start of a small car revolution, or is it a meaningless distraction?

The idea that Americans should be offered a greater choice of smaller cars has support from strange bedfellows indeed. After all, who could forget President Trump—usually one to take a maximalist approach to any problem—announcing last year that he wanted the Department of Transportation to make Japan’s diminutive “Kei” cars legal here in the US.

Ars’ attempts to get DoT to tell us more about the potential influx of little vehicles have mostly fallen on deaf ears. But this week, there has been movement on the topic, with Toyota announcing that it will bring back the Scion brand with the KEI-01.

Here in the US, the preponderance of humongous SUVs is a consequence largely of government policies, specifically corporate average fuel economy standards that made it less profitable to sell smaller, efficient cars. Japan’s Kei cars are what happens if the government instead restricts the size, engine capacity, and power output.

A Kei car must fit within the following dimensions: 11.2 feet (3.4 m) long, 4.9 feet (1.5 m) wide, and 6.6 feet (2 m) tall, with an engine displacement of no more than 0.66 L and an output of 63 hp (47 kW) or less. That’s a far cry from the more than 16 feet (4.9 m) of a full-size pickup truck, but it makes sense for a country where you’re not allowed to buy a car unless you have somewhere to park it, with those spaces being at an absolute premium.

Being affordable to buy and economical to run sure doesn’t hurt either, and last year, Japan bought almost 1.7 million Kei cars, out of total sales of 4.6 million vehicles. Depending on where you live in the US, it can be varying degrees of difficult to import a Kei car, at least until now.

Toyota killed off its Scion sub-brand more than a decade ago, ending the haggle-free approach and cars aimed at younger buyers in favor of selling more Toyotas. But now it’s back as “Scion by Toyota,” which the automaker will apparently use to experiment with less traditional ideas. But forget about a cool-but-boxy EV in the mold of the Honda N-One or Nissan Sakura.

“The future of mobility will not be defined by one vehicle, one technology, or one way of thinking,” said Simon Humphries, Toyota’s chief branding officer and head of design. “Scion by Toyota gives us a place to challenge conventional thinking, explore new possibilities, and create mobility in new ways. The KEI-01, SxS, and BKE concepts each represent a different possibility—and together, they show what can happen when curiosity leads the way.”

Before anyone gets too excited, the KEI-01 will be classified as a “low-speed vehicle,” which means it can’t go any faster than 25 mph (40 km/h). So if you were hoping to use one of these but need to drive on anything but surface streets, be prepared to get honked at, a lot, and not in a good way. In fact, unless you’re buying a fleet, Toyota won’t sell you a KEI-01 at all, at least not to begin with.

Oh, and it’s only available as right-hand drive, since that’s the side of the road Japan drives on. But it does feature all-wheel drive, so if you own a ranch—or perhaps a whole load of golf courses—the utility becomes a little more obvious. Toyota says the KEI-01 is suited to small business owners in urban environments, farmers and ranchers, organizations that have campuses that need fleets of facility vehicles, delivery drivers, and outdoor enthusiasts. The trucks will be shipped to Toyota’s assembly plant in Indiana as knock-down kits, adding 50 jobs to the plant in mid-2027.

Low-speed vehicles have been street-legal in parts of the US for many years now. In my neighborhood in Washington, DC, Circuit operates a low-speed EV shuttle on demand, and gas-powered golf carts are very popular in retirement communities. Pricing for the KEI-01 hasn’t been announced, but speculation runs from as little as $10,000 to as much as $28,000.

CFTC is warning Kalshi it could face charges over contract filing failures

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Kalshi calls the issue a procedural error involving a handful of contracts, many of which were not live, and says it is addressing it

Kalshi has received a warning from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission that it could face charges over how it filed new event contracts with the agency, according to Reuters, which cited Politico. The CFTC’s notification suggests charges may be imminent.

The CFTC is examining whether Kalshi neglected to notify it about event contracts the company planned to list, Kalshi confirmed to CNBC. The company said the agency singled out contracts tied to weather, and that it is cooperating with the regulator to resolve the issue. Kalshi did not say when the investigation began.

"The notice is about a procedural error involving the submission of a handful of contracts, many of which weren't live," Elisabeth Diana, head of communications at Kalshi, said, according to CNBC. Kalshi declined to comment to CNBC on possible charges.

Reuters reported that the probe covers dozens of new products, while Kalshi described it as involving a handful of contracts.

The CFTC had not responded to requests for comment from Reuters or CNBC when they published.

Prediction market platforms may launch event contracts on their own certification, with no need for advance CFTC sign-off, provided they follow the Commodity Exchange Act, according to CNBC. That process allows new contracts to be introduced faster.

Back in August, the CFTC directed Kalshi to remove sports-related mention markets, which let traders wager on the words individuals use during a broadcast or speech, while the agency studied that contract type, according to CNBC.

I’m thinking about applying to Yale, Columbia, and Hunter to do my MFA. I want to have the option to teach, make better art, and expand my community. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope could capture images 30% sharper than — and remain operational for twice as long as — initially expected. Researchers say these "remarkable" developments could greatly enhance the volume and quality of the data they will analyze.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is nearing its final destination more than 1 million miles (1.6 million kilometers) from Earth, where it will soon scour the universe for a host of cosmological treasures, including an estimated 100,000 new alien worlds.

But even before its mission truly begins, recent developments suggest the state-of-the-art spacecraft could take better images than — and remain operational for more than twice as long as — initially expected, further delighting already-excited astronomers.

"It is fantastic to learn that Roman is not just reaching its design goals but exceeding them," Eamonn Kerins, an astronomer at the University of Manchester in the U.K. who will help lead one of Roman's major surveys, told Live Science.

Roman is NASA's latest flagship space telescope, following in the footsteps of iconic observatories like the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). The $4 billion machine ‪—‬ which is over 42 feet (12.7 meters) long and weighs around the same as a fully grown Tyrannosaurus rex ‪—‬ successfully launched into space on Aug. 30, more than a decade after scientists started building it.

The telescope is now en route to Lagrange point 2 (L2), a fixed point relative to our planet where Earth's gravity and the sun's gravity cancel each other out. There, Roman will join several other important spacecraft, including JWST and the European Space Agency's Euclid telescope.

Roman was launched into space on board one of SpaceX's Falcon heavy rockets. It lifted off Aug. 30 from NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

During Roman's journey to L2, which is currently around 85% complete, mission scientists have remotely switched on the telescope's various instruments to test whether they function as expected. So far, this "commissioning process" has gone off without a hitch, and by the time Roman arrives in less than two weeks, it should be ready to snap its first in-depth images of the cosmos.

Researchers were already pumped to see what Roman will be able to do once it is in place and fully switched on. However, that excitement has further intensified in recent weeks, thanks to two postlaunch developments.

First, a series of near-perfect rocket burns, executed during and shortly after launch, saved more than a decade's worth of fuel that can now be used to extend Roman's mission. Second, after remotely adjusting the telescope's mirror, researchers discovered it should be capable of snapping images with around 30% greater detail than engineers predicted.

The team that designed, launched and remotely commissioned the orbital observatory has "done an amazing job" and "their work has ensured that Roman is more than up to the task," Kerins said.

Shortly after Roman arrived in space on board a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket that lifted off from NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida, mission scientists conducted two major burns, or engine firings, which gave the spacecraft enough momentum to escape Earth's orbit and make the journey to L2.

Roman is around the same size as a small truck and was packed into the nose cone of the Falcon Heavy rocket prior to launch.

Thanks to a combination of "exquisite planning by our orbital dynamics team" and "brilliant execution by the operations team," these burns were so precise that each one saved up to four years' worth of fuel, Jamie Dunn, director of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, said in a Sept. 14 statement.

On top of this, Roman ended up being much lighter than initially planned, which allowed engineers to pack another four years' worth of fuel into the spacecraft before launch. "Since Roman's [weight] was lower than we budgeted for, we were able to fill the propellant tanks to their capacity rather than only filling them as much as we needed," Alison Rao, the Roman propulsion lead at NASA Goddard, said in the statement.

In total, these savings could give Roman an additional 12 years of fuel, which could more than double the mission's planned 10-year lifespan. That means Roman could still be capturing images of the cosmos in 2048.

Given that Roman can collect data up to 1,000 times faster than Hubble, this extended lifespan will greatly enhance the amount of data the new space telescope will be able to capture. "With a 20-year Roman lifetime, we could, in theory, do over 20,000 years of Hubble survey work," Kerins said. "That's a lot of science!"

Roman is now on its way to L2, where the gravity of Earth and the sun cancel out, allowing it to remain in one place without expending much fuel.

This prolonged lifespan will let astronomers compare images of specific objects decades apart — such as expanding nebulas or colliding galaxies — allowing them to detect changes they otherwise might have missed.

It may also let them study objects or phenomena that we haven't even discovered yet, which is particularly exciting, Kerins noted. "The real show-stopper results will be new things we just aren't even thinking about right now," he said. "The possibilities are truly breathtaking."

There is also the chance that Roman's mission could be extended even further, because engineers often find clever ways of making active spacecraft last longer. For example, Hubble is more than two decades past its initial mission parameters, and the recently deceased Swift Observatory lasted 10 times longer than initially predicted before falling back to Earth last week.

Roman's secret weapon is arguably its Wide Field Instrument (WFI), made up of a 288-megapixel camera attached to a 7.9-foot (2.4 m) mirror, which will capture ultra-detailed images with a field of view roughly 100 times larger than Hubble's.

Roman's WFI consists of a 288-megapixel camera attached to a 7.9-foot (2.4 m) mirror. It will be capable of capturing images with a field of view 100 times greater than Hubble.

We have already seen the first WFI images — a series of blurry, green dots captured during a test of the instrument's 18 different light detectors ( see below). However, those photos were nothing like what WFI will capture in the future; those images will be so detailed that you would need dozens of high-definition TVs to properly display a single snap in full resolution, Kerins said.

After Roman completes the initial WFI commissioning phase, those images will be around 30% sharper than designers predicted. "All of the images from Roman's cosmic surveys will be even sharper than we expected," Alden Jurling, a senior optical engineer who works on Roman at NASA Goddard, said in an Oct. 14 statement. "It's a great outcome."

The improvements were first noticed after the WFI's car-sized mirror was realigned by just one-sixteenth the diameter of a human hair, demonstrating just how intricate the commissioning process has been. "Calibrating an instrument is like tuning a piano or guitar before a concert," Maxime Rizzo, a WFI systems engineer at NASA Goddard, said in the statement. "You adjust everything until it's just how you want it and then leave the settings alone so it performs consistently while you use it."

NASA released Roman's first photos on Sept. 15. Each small square represents one of the telescope's light detectors, and each blurry green spot is a distant star. (This is just a test image and does not represent how the telescope's proper photos will look.)

The improved image quality could really come to the fore during Roman's hunt for potentially habitable exoplanets, which is what Live Science's readers are most excited about for this mission.

Kerins, who is leading the Exoplanet Demographics Working Group for the Transits in the Roman Exoplanets Survey, is particularly excited about this development, but he said it does not necessarily guarantee that Roman will find more exoplanets than initially predicted.

The rest of Roman's commissioning phase is also proceeding as planned.

Roman's coronagraph instrument, which will block out the light from distant stars to allow WFI to spot alien worlds, has passed its main commissioning phase and is working as intended, according to a NASA blog post.

Roman has completed around 85% of its journey to L2 and should arrive at its final destination in the next few weeks. (The timeline in this image is a screenshot from Roman's commissioning process website, captured on Nov. 11.)

Meanwhile, a global network of ground stations, which will receive up to 1.4 terabytes of data from Roman every day, has established contact with the spacecraft and is receiving signals as expected, another blog post revealed.

All of these early wins raise hopes that Roman can have a long and successful career, and are a testament to the years of work that went into designing the spacecraft and planning the mission.

"What we're seeing now with the actual optical performance and estimated lifetime of Roman is just how good that design process has been," Kerins said. "It's a remarkable achievement. I can't wait to see what Roman is going to give us."

Actually, Robert Pattinson Would Make a Perfect James Bond

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

My colleagues and editors are sick of hearing me say it, but I believe it with my whole chest: The actor playing James Bond should never be more famous than James Bond. There’s something about Bond—his cultural footprint, and the dangerous yet seductive life he leads in his stories—that makes him too big to be a footnote in a great actor’s resume. James Bond creates leading men; James Bond is not for leading men to create.

So you have to know why it’s not easy for me to say this either: Robert Pattinson would be fantastic, and exactly the new James Bond we’re looking for.

You’ve probably heard by now that Amazon, which acquired full creative control over all things Bond from longtime owners Eon Productions and the Broccoli family, has been searching for the next 007 in a movie to be directed by Denis Villeneuve.

What was once an arena of speculation has exploded into a circus, with different names being bandied about at once. Namely: Callum Turner, Josh O’Connor, and Jack Lowden. (There is obviously a specific type of white man they’re going for here.) Just when it seemed as if the new Bond would finally be unveiled, there came a rush of two really big names suddenly in the running. Among them is Pattinson, reported by The Hollywood Reporter on November 6, along with Nicholas Hoult. Pattinson secretly auditioned for the role during a break in filming for The Batman: Part II, which halted filming in October when Matt Reeves attended to a personal matter.

No shade to Hoult or any of the other actors, but Pattinson is now the most interesting candidate despite a whiff of overexposure this year. Between The Drama in the spring, The Odyssey in the summer, Primetime in the fall, and soon Dune: Part Three in the dead of winter, 2026 has been shaping up into a banner year for Pattinson. Not since Twilight has the man been so prolific on our screens.

But even before the year began, Pattinson had long ago outgrown his image of a tween heartthrob. He’s a prodigious talent capable of daring choices and executing on them in a way you can’t ever expect. (Who else would say "cock" like this?) His popular line from The Odyssey—“Somebody get these beggars out of here!”—didn’t become a meme on its own. Pattinson made “Beggars” sound like a slur, and in the same breath, his nervous blinking and quivering smile cemented Antinous as an unconfident weasel whose puffed-out chest was, in fact, hot air. Unlike previous Batmans, who often played the Caped Crusader like a knight in matte black armor, Pattinson’s interpretation was/is that of an antisocial weirdo everyone stares at like a freak. Which he is.

Like Batman, Bond has also been a role that people misunderstand, sometimes even by the men who play him. (It is not my biases talking here, I really do think Daniel Craig is the only actor to ever nail the nuances and pathology of the character.) Bond is stylish and can say the right thing at the right time, be it to enemies or lovers. But he’s not a dashing or noble hero. He’s a brute in Brunello Cucinelli. A thug with a $9,000 Omega. He literally kills for a living—and had to kill to get that right—and his attachment to his job is downright destabilizing.

Recall in Skyfall, after Judi Dench’s M orders Moneypenny to shoot, Bond copes with endless drinking, womanizing, and gambling. That might sound like a fun weekend in Vegas, but in the context of Bond, his fallout from discovering his expendability led him to a really, really dark path. It is not normal to drink shots with a scorpion on your hand, nor is it healthy to be up in the morning reaching for booze from behind the bar. Bond might live a life many of us men dream of, but it comes at a severe cost. I don’t know what kind of Bond movie Villeneuve has in mind, but if the creative team is lining up like this, we’re in for something special.

You can’t exactly guesstimate what kind of 007 Pattinson might be based on his previous roles. There is truly little common ground between Batman and Bond. But one thing is for sure: if Pattinson gets the part, we are in for a goddamn show.

Can Creativity Make You Happier?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

The game show Family Feud pits two families against each other in a race to find the most popular answers to silly questions like “Name something you might find in a bathtub.” If you guessed “water” and 50 of the 100 people who had been surveyed had also chosen that answer, you would get those points. If you answered “arachnid,” you might get zero points. Even though many children learned the "Itsy-Bitsy Spider" song growing up, "arachnid" would probably not be one of the most-popular answers.

Creative, unconventional, divergent thinkers might be at a disadvantage on Family Feud. Yet research demonstrates that generating creative, original thought and activity can make you happier (Tan, et al. 2021).

Moderate stress, like what you might feel when taking a test, can improve our convergent thinking—the type of thinking that's required when we want to find the right answer to a question or a problem. However, stress can stifle divergent, out-of-the-box, creative problem-solving. Divergent thinking involves generating multiple novel ideas from multiple perspectives and/or combinations of perspectives (Guo et al., 2024). When we feel looser, freer, and more relaxed, we can access our creative mind more readily.

Creativity can lower stress by offering outlets for strong emotions. Poets, authors, songwriters, dancers, and other artists find that strong emotions can fuel creativity as they strive to transform pain into beauty. Psychotherapists often recommend creative writing, journaling, and other artistic outlets as a therapeutic intervention. Creativity can increase well-being, providing opportunities to feel more fully alive and joyful (Tan, et al. 2021).

Every month, a group of friends gathers at our home for a musical jam session. We improvise songs, switch instruments, and often end by drumming on percussion instruments. This shared creativity strengthens our friendship and our sense of playfulness. Engaging in creative arts with others reduces loneliness and supports social connection, an essential requirement for happiness (Perkins, et al. 2021).

Many young people have formed deep friendships from Dungeons and Dragons, Magic: The Gathering, and similar role-playing games that require creative imagination, divergent thinking, and sometimes even elaborate costume creations.

Studies show that group-based arts and creativity programs can improve the health, well-being, and quality of life of older people. These activities strengthen social ties, encourage an active imagination, and promote physical activity, all essential for a healthy life (Sowerby, et al. 2026).

When I suggest to clients that they might improve their happiness by finding a creative outlet, I often hear, “But I’m not a creative person.” I usually ask them a few questions like, “Have you ever substituted a different ingredient in a recipe when you didn’t have the right ingredients? Or have you ever had to try different ways to teach your child how to tie shoelaces?” Typically, people can think of several times in the recent past when they have been creative.

Author, psychotherapist, and writing coach Marni Freedman writes in her delightful new book, Artistic Badassery, “The latest research on creativity from both a psychological and a neuroscientific perspective shows that creativity is not an inborn gift or a fixed trait, but rather a set of skills that can be developed through practice, effort, and the right conditions.”

Like any mental muscle, creativity needs exercise to develop. If you’re just learning to play guitar, you may not yet have enough musical vocabulary to improvise. Yet you can write a song after learning only three chords. All you need is an idea and a melody. It can be fun to create something original, even if it wouldn’t pass critical muster. Try making something just for the fun of it, without criticizing the outcome. Let yourself play in the sandbox of creativity. It might make you happier.

I think there should be a Family Feud game for divergent thinkers. You would try to pick the most novel answers to the questions asked, and the person who picks logical items that no one else chooses wins. Here’s a fun question to start: “What items might be found in a national ballroom?”

When Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva gave his victory speech in late October, he took one hand and held it aloft in tribute to “our general,” as his supporters cheered the “warrior of the Brazilian people.” The title was fittingly bestowed on André Janones, the 42-year-old congressman who vigorously mobilized almost 11 million voters as part of Lula’s “digital army,” marking the first time since Brazil’s return to democracy four decades ago that the runner-up surged ahead in the second round of a presidential election. It was a remarkable turnaround that will to be studied by election watchers for years to come.

In one of the most consequential elections in Brazilian history, some three-quarters of the population were getting their news from social media, and Meta—the owner of the most prominent platforms—was supportive of Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of the now-imprisoned former president. Lula’s campaign was allegedly thwarted online on Meta’s platforms. United Nations experts described as interruptions of “one side’s” social media accounts, curtailed audience engagement, and choked user feeds.

Users reported that Meta’s artificial intelligence tools refused to generate campaign posters for Lula, whose name would temporarily not appear in searches, while Bolsonaro’s profile was recommended. Leaked details revealed that Meta’s employees confirmed internal manipulations tilting the digital field in Bolsonaro’s favor, benefiting his conservative political movement. Noting its failures to stem disinformation in Brazil’s last elections, the company’s own oversight board issued its concern over “recurring enforcement failures in Meta’s election integrity efforts.”

What overcame the algorithm was Janones’s “Lula’s Army” WhatsApp channel, which swelled into the millions as the second round approached. Voters were mobilized as “soldiers,” enthusiastically showing their support through storms of emojis, and they were then dispatched on “missions” to mobilize more people. This effort helped mobilize more than 10 million supporters for Lula in the second round.

Unlike Meta’s other platforms such as Instagram and Facebook, WhatsApp is less susceptible to algorithmic control. Human beings, not algorithms, share what they want to share. Lula’s campaign recalled the original promise of technology as a force to connect, inform, and mobilize. As India’s Cockroach movement and Serbia’s student movement have recently demonstrated, digital tools can be powerfully used to help people reclaim their voices offline.

Brazil’s presidential election underscores not only the power of people to bring change, but also the danger that digital chokepoints, created by the power of a single tech company, can shape who gets heard and who doesn’t. Brazil’s is not a solitary case of Meta’s platforms influencing the result of a major election, or of voters mounting a countereffort. Asked for comment, Meta referred to a statement claiming that any impediments were not deliberate, but rather the result of technical glitches, and perhaps negligence. They also highlighted a Nov. 11 statement from Brazil’s electoral authority that it found no “deliberate action to skew the election.”

But these claims fail to reckon with the company’s own announcements that it is discarding fact-checkers and loosening controls on recommended political content. What’s more, at the same time that Brazil’s Supreme Court was regulating social media platforms, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerburg said he was working with the Trump administration to “push back” against “censorship,” disdainfully referring to Latin American “secret courts” that can order companies to take down content in the name of moderation and integrity.

Whether or not this interference was intentional, what matters is that it was possible. A single company can now stand between citizens and the representatives that they should freely elect, controlling who gets to be seen or who can be heard. The concentration of such power in the hands of so few becomes a chokepoint that can threaten democracy, even democracy itself as a political system.

In response, it will not be enough to add layers of regulation or ask Meta and other tech companies to do better. When these avenues have been pursued, they encounter hard limits. In India, Meta’s moderation of hate speech has been scrutinized; in Myanmar and Sri Lanka, rights groups accused Facebook of inflaming crimes against the Rohingya and other Muslim minorities; in Ethiopia, its algorithms amplified ethnic violence.

Two years ago, the European Commission opened still ongoing formal proceedings against Meta’s platforms under the Digital Services Act, and in the United States in recent months, it was subject to billion-dollar penalties for failing to address child safety and other problems around content moderation. In every case, enforcement has been slow, inadequate, ineffective, or too opaque to track or evaluate.

The infrastructure needed to regulate Big Tech is politically compromised. In the United States, Brazil, and other large markets, the industry runs a vast policy operation that runs deep into legislatures, think tanks, and lobbyists. There is also a regulation trap: The same politicians who we count on to regulate technology are the ones who depend on the tech companies to campaign, communicate, and fundraise.

Brazil now has some of the strongest regulation against electoral falsehoods after its election four years ago was marred by disinformation, culminating in an attempted coup carried out by those who argued—without evidence—that the electronic voting system was compromised. Regulation has since been taken up by the courts, with the Supreme Court finding tech companies’ immunity from liability for third-party content unconstitutional and the Superior Electoral Court demanding platform compliance plans.

The Lula administration also empowered public authorities to investigate platforms, enforce compliance, and ensure transparency, including in the form of specific decrees preventing digital violence against women. Today, Brazil is a rare case of a country in a position to act before the election; most others do not have strong enough institutions.

Tech companies are powerful political actors that exert potentially decisive political influence, which can be wielded for ideological or partisan ends, as in Brazil, where engagement-maximizing algorithms reward polarizing, inflammatory, and populist content. The answer must be to challenge that power, which can be done through three proposals for reform.

Brazil’s left-wing president won a historic fourth term in office.

The first step is to reduce society’s dependence on one or a few companies that have the capability to dominate the political or digital landscape. Here, competition law can be enforced, so that a single actor cannot sweep up a whole range of apps and close them off to rivals. Brazil’s public payment system Pix, for example, broke the dominance of private card systems by charging lower merchant fees and zero cost to customers. Because the central bank required all other banks to integrate Pix into their existing mobile apps, it also expanded digital financial inclusion for hundreds of millions.

Second, our societies’ digital architecture needs to be overhauled and constructed anew around open protocols. These are publicly documented technical standards that allow different services, applications, and devices to communicate using shared rules rather than through proprietary systems. Lula’s digital army demonstrated that they can be used or designed at scale for the public interest, in line with democratic principles.

The third is to build public options. Unregulated markets cannot be expected to produce fair outcomes that benefit everyone. Just as governments use their power as investors in the realms of defense, energy, semiconductors, and other infrastructure, they can also procure open-source software and open standards for their own services. Industrial policy and public funding can unlock alternatives to Big Tech companies. They can also help build open-weight and public interest AI models, which will provide access to local developers.

Elections show how high the stakes can be, but they represent only the most visible part of a wider struggle for truth, voice, and the fundamentals of human dignity. At its core, dignity, in the digital age, depends on people’s ability to have access to reliable knowledge, remain free from manipulation, and challenge decisions that shape their families. It demands regulations ensuring that everyone is treated equally; that they are protected from digital violence; and that they are free to live without being watched, profiled, and manipulated. None of these essential elements of human dignity can be left to the goodwill of a single corporation.

Equally worrying is that we are beginning to normalize the intense, aggressive digital messaging that bombards voters as part of electoral races—for better or for worse. Digital campaigning has a place to strengthen democratic participation and promote free and fair elections, but we are also seeing that it can cross the line in distorting, suppressing, and damaging the will of the people.

What Lula honored in his victory speech was what the algorithm could not block—namely, the resolve of millions to have their voices heard and their votes to be counted. Yet by virtue of their concentrated power and platforms through which public discourse occurs, tech companies will continue to be a chokepoint on democratic governance, so countries must learn not simply to regulate but more importantly to build an open, democratic digital architecture that works for all. Otherwise, these decisions will be made by someone else.

AI SRE may help you fight fires, but Foresight AI helps before the spark

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI SRE tools help teams recover once something breaks. Gremlin Foresight AI finds reliability risks, delivers fixes and checks that they work before customers notice.

Artificial intelligence is fundamentally changing how software is built, deployed, and managed. AI coding assistants are helping developers ship software faster than ever. Meanwhile, a new generation of AI-powered Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) tools promises to help organizations respond to incidents and recover from outages in record time.

But there’s a problem with this approach: By the time an AI SRE tool springs into action, something has already gone wrong.

What if AI could help prevent the incident from happening in the first place?

That’s the idea behind Gremlin Foresight AI, a new agentic reliability solution from Gremlin, the Chaos Engineering company. Rather than waiting for systems to fail and using AI to accelerate recovery, Foresight AI proactively identifies reliability risks and recommends and delivers fixes. It then validates that those fixes work, all before customers experience an outage.

The distinction represents an important shift in how organizations think about AI and software reliability. Instead of simply getting better at fighting fires, Gremlin wants to eliminate the conditions that cause them.

The rise of AI-powered software development has created an interesting paradox for engineering organizations. Developers can now generate and deploy code at unprecedented speeds. But that acceleration also creates more opportunities for bugs, configuration errors, and architectural weaknesses to find their way into production.

Meanwhile, the operational complexity of modern applications continues to grow. Cloud-native architectures, microservices, distributed infrastructure, and third-party dependencies have created increasingly interconnected systems where a seemingly minor issue can cascade into a major outage.

For years, organizations have invested heavily in observability, monitoring, and incident management tools to address these challenges. More recently, AI SRE solutions have emerged to automate troubleshooting, identify root causes, and help engineers resolve incidents faster.

These capabilities are valuable. Reducing the time it takes to recover from an outage can significantly limit its financial and operational impact.

But AI SRE largely addresses the consequences of a reliability failure rather than preventing the failure itself.

Gremlin believes that distinction is becoming increasingly important as AI accelerates the pace of software development.

“AI-driven development means shipping code at 10X velocity; it also means 10x the opportunity for bugs, risks, and failures,” said Kolton Andrus, CEO and founder of Gremlin. “While the rise of AI SRE tools are great for helping teams respond to incidents faster, it’s still cleanup after something breaks. Gremlin Foresight AI finds risks proactively, delivers the fix, and verifies the fix worked by rerunning the test. This provides teams with the assurances needed to move confidently.”

The philosophy behind Foresight AI isn’t entirely new. Gremlin has spent years helping organizations proactively discover weaknesses in their systems through Chaos Engineering. It is a discipline that deliberately introduces controlled failures to understand how applications behave under adverse conditions.

The challenge has always been making that process accessible and repeatable.

Traditional Chaos Engineering often requires specialized expertise, considerable engineering effort, and time that development teams don’t always have. Engineers must determine which failure scenarios to test, interpret the results, identify potential fixes, and verify that the changes actually improve resilience.

Gremlin designed Foresight AI to automate much of that work.

The platform uses specialized AI agents to analyze applications, identify potential failure scenarios, execute controlled reliability tests, and recommend targeted remediations. These fixes can take the form of configuration patches or infrastructure-as-code changes that fit into existing engineering workflows.

Importantly, the process doesn’t end when a fix is proposed or implemented.

Foresight AI reruns the original reliability test to verify that the weakness has actually been addressed. It can then continue testing as applications and infrastructure evolve, helping organizations identify regressions before they become production incidents.

This creates a continuous cycle of identifying risks, fixing weaknesses, and validating resilience.

Consider an application that relies on infrastructure deployed in a single cloud availability zone. Everything may appear healthy under normal operating conditions. Monitoring dashboards are green, application performance is acceptable, and customers aren’t reporting problems.

But if that availability zone fails, the application could become unavailable.

An AI SRE tool might help diagnose the resulting outage and accelerate recovery. Gremlin designed Foresight AI to identify the architectural weakness beforehand and recommend changes that improve redundancy. It then tests whether the application can withstand the failure.

The difference is between recovering from a preventable outage and avoiding it altogether.

One of the more interesting aspects of Foresight AI is the technology behind its recommendations.

While many AI-powered operations tools rely heavily on large language models to interpret telemetry, logs, and incident histories. Gremlin has built Foresight AI around its proprietary Failure Atlas.

The Failure Atlas draws on more than a decade of real-world reliability testing, including millions of fault-injection experiments conducted across distributed systems.

That historical knowledge provides Foresight AI with a specialized foundation for understanding how applications fail, which failure conditions are most consequential, and what kinds of remediation are likely to improve resilience.

Foresight AI doesn’t rely solely on an LLM to infer what might go wrong. It combines information about an organization’s actual infrastructure with Gremlin’s accumulated knowledge of system failures and recovery patterns.

The AI agents use that context to recommend relevant tests, interpret outcomes, and develop actionable remediation plans.

This is an important distinction in an increasingly crowded AI infrastructure market. General-purpose language models can offer plausible explanations for technical problems, but reliability engineering requires more than plausible answers. It requires evidence that a system will continue functioning when something goes wrong.

Gremlin’s approach attempts to provide that evidence through controlled experimentation and repeatable validation.

Foresight AI also introduces an agentic approach to reliability engineering, with specialized agents responsible for different aspects of the process.

An Analyst agent examines applications and infrastructure to identify potential weaknesses and recommend appropriate tests. A Tester agent executes controlled experiments to determine how systems behave under failure conditions. An Operator agent interprets the results and provides specific remediation recommendations.

A fourth agent, acting as a Technical Program Manager, helps organizations coordinate reliability initiatives across teams. It also identifies gaps in testing and tracks progress over time.

Together, these agents aim to reduce the manual work traditionally associated with reliability engineering.

Rather than requiring engineers to become experts in every possible failure scenario, Foresight AI helps determine where the risks are, which tests to perform, and what actions are necessary to improve resilience.

Human oversight remains part of the process. Engineers retain approval authority over testing and remediation, allowing organizations to introduce automation without relinquishing control over production infrastructure.

The broader objective is to make proactive reliability engineering a continuous operational practice rather than an occasional exercise conducted by a small group of specialists.

One of the longstanding challenges of reliability engineering is demonstrating the business value of prevention.

When an outage occurs, its impact is often obvious. Organizations can measure downtime, lost revenue, recovery costs, and customer disruption.

But how do you quantify the value of an outage that never happened?

Foresight AI addresses this challenge by incorporating reliability scoring, continuous validation, and reporting that helps organizations measure improvements across applications and teams.

Engineering leaders don’t have to rely exclusively on traditional operational metrics such as mean time to recovery. They can track which risks have been identified, which weaknesses have been remediated, and whether systems continue to pass resilience tests as they change.

This gives organizations a more tangible way to evaluate their reliability investments and demonstrate progress.

It also reflects a broader evolution in the discipline of Site Reliability Engineering. It is moving beyond measuring how effectively teams respond to failure toward measuring how systematically they reduce the likelihood and impact of failure.

Gremlin’s launch of Foresight AI arrives at a time when the relationship between software development velocity and operational reliability is becoming increasingly important.

AI coding tools are accelerating the creation of software. AI SRE tools are accelerating the diagnosis and resolution of incidents. But neither development speed nor recovery speed, on its own, guarantees that applications will become more resilient.

Proactive reliability engineering offers a complementary approach: continuously testing systems, identifying weaknesses, and addressing risks before they affect customers.

The real test for Foresight AI will be how effectively it can translate its knowledge of failure patterns into reliable, actionable fixes across the diverse environments enterprises operate. Identifying a weakness is one challenge; demonstrating that a proposed remediation works safely and consistently is another.

For years, the technology industry has focused on reducing the time it takes to detect and recover from outages. Gremlin is making the case that the next major advance in AI-powered operations will come from reducing the number of outages organizations experience in the first place.

AI SRE may help organizations fight fires faster. With Foresight AI, Gremlin is helping enterprises assess risk and address issues before the spark.
