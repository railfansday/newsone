Batman Just Officially Saved DC Comics From a Terrible Fate

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Reports also indicated DC Comics boss Jim Lee has ‘welcomed’ the news of James Gunn and Peter Safran’s ousting

Business mergers always come with shake-ups, and Skydance's recent acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery was inevitably going to be messy in some way. What was unexpected, however, was this week's announcement that James Gunn and Peter Safran would be stepping down as co-heads of DC Studios. At the same time, Skydance has initiated layoffs in other divisions such as Skydance Games. One area that seems to have escaped the chopping block for now, though, is DC Comics.

Bleeding Cool reports that, according to multiple sources, no one at DC Comics has been offered voluntary buyouts like other divisions were this October. The surprising reason for this comes down to performance. Apparently, DC Comics is outperforming a number of other Warner Bros. departments and is actively turning a profit. Much of that success is directly laid at the feet of Absolute Batman, Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta's tent-pole series for the publisher's phenomenal Absolute line. Absolute Batman has been both a critical and commercial success, helping DC surpass Marvel in sales. As Bleeding Cool puts it, "no one wants to kill that golden batgoose." There is also an animated adaptation of the comic currently in the works.

There may be another wrinkle to the story, however. The report also states that the company's organizational structure makes roles and responsibilities unclear, meaning that Skydance does not want to risk accidently firing someone integral to the success of books like Absolute Batman. Due to this nebulous organization, Batman seems to have saved DC Comics from going through a major shake-up.

A shocking part of Bleeding Cool's report also revealed that DC Comics publisher, president, and CCO Jim Lee has "welcomed the news of Jeremy Latcham's appointment." Latcham is replacing Gunn and Safran. According to Bleeding Cool's sources, Lee has felt like he has been frozen out at DC Studios by the outgoing pair. This has instigated Lee's decision to work more publicly on projects outside of DC, perhaps preparing to exit his role. That may change with Gunn and Safran gone.

At the same time, rumors are swirling that DC scribe Tom King may be ousted as well, or perhaps just be looking to jump ship. The writer has penned a number of well-received runs at DC, such as Supergirl: Woman of Tomorrow, Mister Miracle, Omega Men, and more. He was also tapped by Gunn to be an architect of the new DC Universe, with several projects being based on his comic work. King also helped create HBO's Lanterns. Now, it seems like King could be leaving DC for a possible return to Marvel. With his divisive Wonder Woman run about to end and Gunn and Safran out, it might be the best opportunity for King to go on to other things.

OpenAI lets businesses claim ChatGPT profiles and launch their own agents

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brands get a blue check, mention counts and a chat agent, which OpenAI also sells as an ad.

A mock-up from OpenAI of a verified business profile in ChatGPT, with a button to chat with the shop’s Business Agent.

OpenAI launched Businesses in ChatGPT in beta, letting businesses claim verified profiles, update their details and see aggregated data on how often ChatGPT mentions them. US ecommerce and online-service firms can also launch Business Agents, which OpenAI also sells as ads.

OpenAI is letting businesses claim their profiles in ChatGPT and launch AI agents that talk to customers on their behalf, the company said on Tuesday. The beta, called Businesses in ChatGPT, is aimed at the 1.2 billion people OpenAI says use ChatGPT each week.

Through a tool called Business Hub, verified companies get a blue check and can update their details, prices and stock. They can also see how often ChatGPT mentions them, how many people click through to their website and common topics in those conversations. OpenAI says it does not share users’ chats with businesses.

Eligible US ecommerce and online-service firms can also launch a Business Agent. When a business shows up in a ChatGPT answer, users can open its profile and chat with its agent, which answers using the company’s product details. The chat is labelled and kept apart from ChatGPT’s own answers.

The same agent can be sold as an ad. Businesses can sponsor it as the landing spot for ChatGPT Ads, building on Sponsored Agents, which OpenAI began testing in September. OpenAI says paying does not change how often ChatGPT recommends a business, Digiday reported.

Agents must say they are AI, keep prices accurate and avoid pressure tactics. Purchases and sharing personal data need the user’s explicit approval. For now, buyers are sent to the brand’s own website, though OpenAI is building agent payments with Visa.

The move deepens OpenAI’s push into advertising, which passed a $1B annual run rate in August and now has 100,000 advertisers. It could also squeeze startups such as Peec AI, which sell brands estimates of how visible they are in ChatGPT.

Zum Adds Zoe Conversational AI to School Transportation Operations

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zum introduced Zoe on November 10, 2026, making the conversational AI assistant available inside Zum CMX for school transportation teams, operators and administrators. It analyzes district operating data and proposes next steps, while leaving route changes, driver assignments and family communications under staff control.

Zum reports that more than 7,500 schools across 19 states have relied on CMX. The launch puts conversational AI inside that student mobility platform, with the immediate aim of reducing the reports, spreadsheets and manual cross-checking required to investigate late buses, coverage problems and payroll anomalies.

A user can ask a plain-language question and receive a synthesized answer, a likely cause and a suggested next step, together with underlying data for review. Zoe can be opened through the Zum Assistant button or an Analyze button on a page. It also supplies step-by-step CMX guidance from Zum’s knowledge base.

The documented workflows cover on-time performance, route coverage and payroll. For a late route, Zoe can distinguish an isolated incident from a recurring pattern and examine possible causes such as driver coverage, route design or unscheduled stops. Recommendations can include driver coaching or a route adjustment, with the supporting records available for checking.

That analysis draws on CMX’s integrated records: routes, runs, telematics, ridership, payroll, driver assignments and student information. Connecting those records allows a missed on-time result to be examined alongside extra stops and paid-hour patterns without separate exports. A payroll review can therefore start with unusual paid hours and narrow to specific shifts involving repeated extra stops or unexplained dwell time.

For coverage, Zoe identifies routes that repeatedly need cover drivers or are frequently split, and can compare a current route or run with an earlier version to summarize changes. Other queries include whether a driver stopped at every stop, what changed on a route since the previous week and what is driving a day’s performance.

The official product documentation says Zoe follows Zum’s existing role-based permissions and does not expand the records a user is authorized to see. Student information remains in Zum’s secure cloud, encrypted in transit and at rest, and is never provided to AI model companies for training. Zum does not sell or share student data.

Answers come from the district’s own data rather than the open internet, and recommendations include evidence that users can inspect. At launch, Zoe investigates, analyzes and recommends; it does not change routes, reassign drivers or contact families. Live support remains available alongside the assistant when a question needs a person or a user has a safety concern.

Tomas Beccar Varela, interim executive director of transportation at San Francisco Unified School District, said his district was evaluating Zoe and saw promise in making complex transportation data easier to access, interpret and analyze. He described an evaluation of the capabilities, not a completed districtwide deployment.

Greg Massa, a transportation systems specialist at Virginia Beach City Public Schools, described the effect on route reviews in Zum’s technical announcement: “With Zoe, we can quickly see what’s changed on a route, understand how those changes impact performance and identify adjustments to improve reliability.”

Zum says it is building toward actions such as reassigning a cover driver, adjusting a route or following up with a family, subject to approvals and the same privacy protections. That roadmap would move Zoe from investigation into execution, where a recommendation can become an operational change. The approval process and evidence shown to staff then become the control point for preserving human accountability as the assistant’s role expands.

The good news keeps coming for photographers using Adobe’s software. Alongside big Lightroom Classic updates, Adobe Camera Raw (ACR) has been updated today to version 19.0, ushering in a wide range of tweaks, some of which are major.

Adobe Camera Raw is an extremely important part of Adobe Photoshop for photography workflows, especially for photographers who capture RAW photos. The underlying RAW processing and editing technologies found in ACR overlap heavily with Lightroom, although sometimes a feature arrives first in ACR, like Glow, which debuted in Adobe Camera Raw a few months ago before coming to Lightroom today. Another example is Reflection Removal, which arrived first in Adobe Camera Raw before eventually making it into other Adobe photography apps.

Speaking of Glow, it has been upgraded today. There are new Motion and Streak styles. Users can fine-tune the length, direction, color, and softness of the new Glow variants.

In terms of all-new Adobe Camera Raw features, it’s a bulky list. Virtual Copies, a longtime feature for creating multiple versions of photos inside Lightroom without duplicating original files, are now in Adobe Camera Raw. Each version’s edits can be preserved between sessions as well.

Generative Remove selections can now be applied to all selected photos inside a batch within ACR. So, for example, if a photographer has a group of photos that all have the same distracting element, such as a telephone pole, it’s possible to select the element in one photo, remove it, and then apply the same process to the entire group without needing to manually mask and remove it from each shot.

It’s also now possible to organize photos inside an ACR session. Related or similar photos can be grouped into stacks manually or automatically using capture metadata. It’s worth noting that Auto-Stack by Capture Metadata is currently only supported for recent Canon and Nikon cameras that support the DNG file format. However, all photographers can still group photos into stacks manually.

For those who use Adobe Camera Raw to process many photos at once, navigating through a crowded filmstrip or Grid view can be tedious. ACR 19.0 lets users temporarily hide photos or show only selected shots during a session, which significantly cleans up the workspace.

Finally, Adobe Camera Raw 19.0 introduces new camera and lens support. The list of newly supported cameras is very short, including just the two cameras on the new Apple iPhone Duo. However, new lens support has arrived for Sony’s new FE 8-14mm f/3.5 Fisheye G lens, FE 400mm f/4.5 GM and FE 600mm f/6.3 GM telephoto primes, and new zooms from Sigma and Tamron. There’s also support for Laowa’s 50mm f/2.8 2x Ultra-Macro APO lens across many mounts.

Stephen Miller Into the Breach: How Susie Wiles’s Failures Portend Miller’s Future

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

There’s a time-honored tradition for new White House chiefs of staff: The outgoing chief invites former holders of the office, Republicans and Democrats, to a get-together (usually in the West Wing), where the newcomer is offered advice. But Donald Trump’s new chief, Stephen Miller—appointed in a jaw-dropping Truth Social post on Friday—won’t be getting a party. First, because the tradition applies only to a president’s first chief. Second, because no Democratic chief would likely want to be caught dead with Miller, a longtime basher of the opposition party. Shortly after hearing that Trump had picked the hard-right firebrand as his new chief, James Carville sent me a text that summed up the prevailing attitude: “This is like Caligula’s appointment of his horse as consul.”

It was different when Susie Wiles came to town. On the evening of November 19, 2024, she was the guest of honor at a dinner party thrown by Jeffrey Zients, Joe Biden’s outgoing White House chief of staff. They were joined by seven of Zients’s predecessors. It was an occasion for bipartisan bonhomie, a tradition started in 2008 by George W. Bush’s chief Joshua Bolten. In a spirit of goodwill and generosity, Bolten had thrown a gathering in his West Wing office for Barack Obama’s incoming chief, Rahm Emanuel. The informal meetup was intended as a way to convey the chiefs’ collective wisdom and experience—and to offer, in miniature, a nuts-and-bolts version of the transition of executive power that is a hallmark of American democracy.

Now evicted from her West Wing corner office, with the president’s agenda in tatters, Wiles has learned that fealty to Trump almost always ends badly.

On the night of the dinner for Wiles, however, a cloud hung over the occasion. Virtually no one at the table thought she could succeed as Donald Trump’s chief of staff.

For almost two years, Wiles was the second most powerful person in the West Wing. Had Trump been interested in governing, she might have helped him solidify a GOP majority in Congress for years to come. But she was unwilling, or unable, to save Trump from himself. On Wiles’s watch, he squandered his second term with a reign of revenge, retribution, and recklessness. Now evicted from her West Wing corner office, with the president’s agenda in tatters, Wiles has learned that fealty to Trump almost always ends badly.

And if past experience is any guide, Wiles’s replacement, Stephen Miller, may well crash and burn in even more spectacular fashion.

There was no one the president relied on or trusted more than Wiles. From their first meeting in 2015 at Trump Tower to her impressive stint as his winning 2024 presidential campaign co-manager, the “ice maiden” had Trump’s back. And he had hers—until last Tuesday’s epic Democratic blowout. Wiles would resign two days later. Indeed, the Republicans’ midterm apocalypse was an extinction event that not even Wiles could have survived.

Ironically, Trump’s savvy political strategist was the architect of what turned out to be a big blue bloodbath. “We’re going to win the midterms,” Wiles told me boldly when I interviewed her for Vanity Fair just a year ago. Even then, three months before Trump initiated a war on Iran, that was a ridiculously rosy prediction. The president’s approval rating was in the gutter. He’d failed to address, much less solve, voters’ primary concerns: affordability, inflation, health care, and housing—and had still not released the remaining Epstein files. How could a battle-hardened political pro like Wiles be so starry-eyed? The answer, I believe, is that in the fog of MAGA-land, she’d lost touch with the real world.

In almost every presidency, the West Wing is a bubble where like-minded acolytes engage in groupthink. But in Trump’s West Wing this phenomenon is exponentially magnified. It’s a parallel universe, one in which the 2020 election was stolen, violent insurrectionists are “patriots,” and the prosecution of political enemies—with scant evidence—is a presidential perk. (“Who would blame him?” Wiles said to me of Trump’s campaign to settle scores.) That, to my mind, is why Wiles seemed so stunned by the firestorm that erupted when her words appeared in my 10,000-word Vanity Fair series last December. She had no idea they would land like bombs on planet Earth.

Wiles invested Trump with almost otherworldly powers. He would lead the GOP to victory in the 2026 midterms, she believed, because of his preternatural ability to rally his base. That’s why, on the eve of the midterms, Wiles was all-in on sending Trump almost everywhere all at once, admonishing people to “vote like I’m on the ballot.” The trouble was voters saw all too clearly what she apparently couldn’t see: Trump was deeply unpopular; on his watch, the country was coming apart.

A year ago, when Wiles made her electoral prediction, I challenged her. “I admire your confidence about the midterms,” I said, “but the stakes are huge, right? And history is not encouraging. Incumbents do stuff and there’s a reaction. And usually, as Obama said, you get shellacked.”

Wiles seemed to shrug off that possibility. “Can you ever imagine Donald Trump saying that?” she said. “No, no.”

Wiles was right. Trump could never say such a thing—because he could never accept the fact that his electoral magic had waned. Neither, it seemed, could Wiles. But as his chief of staff and top political strategist, she was paid to know better.

Even before the election debacle, Trump had reportedly started privately inquiring about her on phone calls, although he lashed out at accounts of such purported conversations. Then, once the carnage sunk in, the die was cast. Wiles offered her resignation. And Trump accepted it.

At the start of her West Wing tenure, I believed Wiles stood a chance of succeeding. If Trump would listen to anyone, I thought, it was the loyal, organized, no-nonsense Wiles. Yet during 10 conversations with her over 10 months, I watched as she abdicated the most important duty of the White House chief: telling the president hard truths. Wiles told me she’d read my book, The Gatekeepers: How the White House Chiefs of Staff Define Every Presidency, and she understood the importance of heeding Reagan chief James Baker’s advice. “You do not serve your president well if you are just a yes man,” he said. In a phone call, she told me, Baker “reminded me that my job wasn’t to be a fangirl.” I asked Wiles, “Have you had any gut check moments where you had to tell the president something he didn’t want to hear?” She paused. “I probably have one a day,” she said. “But they’re over little things, not big.”

Wiles should have known that the midterms would spell disaster. She carried around a list of her priorities, on an index card. Number five: “Don’t forget about organized labor. Don’t forget about young Black men and women and Hispanics…because that’s the future of the country and the backbone of his support. And if we don’t convert those people to being enthusiastic Republicans, then if the Democrats were even just a little bit better, they might win an election.”

Miller is a zealot, a hard-boiled ideologue—an advocate of Trump’s notorious first-term Muslim ban, the brutal paramilitary tactics of ICE, the deployment of the National Guard to Democrat-run cities, and the lethal air strikes on boats in the Caribbean.

They might—and they did. And now Wiles is out, taking up shop, so Trump himself has said, within his political operation. Last November she hinted that she knew this day might come: “You go to bed at night, you say your prayers, you take stock of what the day was and what your day is tomorrow, and you get up and do it again…. So as long as that’s the case and the president’s pleased…” Wiles paused. “He could fire you tomorrow.”

She’s sensitive about her legacy as the first female White House chief. “I’m not an enabler,” she insisted. “I’m also, like, not a bitch. I try to be thoughtful about what I engage in, and what I disagree [with the president] about. I guess time will tell whether I’ve been effective.”

What about Wiles’s successor, Stephen Miller?

Miller is the antithesis of Ronald Reagan’s chief James Baker, a cool pragmatist who could push back on the president’s half-baked ideas. Miller, by contrast, is a zealot, a hard-boiled ideologue—an advocate of Trump’s notorious first-term Muslim ban, the brutal paramilitary tactics of ICE, the deployment of the National Guard to Democrat-run cities, and the lethal air strikes on boats in the Caribbean. He believes in a Hobbesian world, he has stated, “that is governed by strength, that is governed by force, that is governed by power.” Now he’ll be the last person in the room when Trump makes life-and-death decisions about law and order and war and peace.

The man who decreed that the president’s powers “are very substantial and will not be questioned” is now the official charged with questioning the president’s decisions. It’s hard to imagine Miller doing that without a personality transplant. He’s already alienated the House minority leader, and soon to be Speaker, Hakeem Jeffries, who has called Miller “a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist who has American blood on his hands,” insisting, “I’ve got zero interest in working with that kind of individual.”

At the White House last November, after a Vanity Fair photo session, I spoke with Miller at length about the midterms, still a year away; about his portfolio in the Trump administration; and about the role of the White House chief of staff. Perched on a chair in the Roosevelt Room, he eyed me warily. He seemed impatient to get back to work. “I understand that your need for me is quite limited, right?” he said.

I asked Miller about his interactions with the president—specifically an Oval Office meeting in which he and Trump reportedly talked about blowing up boats in the Caribbean. “I would never talk about…whether a meeting occurred or didn’t occur,” he said, “or whether it was in a meeting that did occur, or was not in that meeting if it occurred, which I would never say either way.”

Miller was more forthcoming about Wiles’s prediction—that Trump and the GOP would triumph at the polls in 2026. “What about you?” I asked him. “I am confident that we’ll win the midterms,” he replied. History might be against them, he said, “but we have every expectation that we’re going to defy that history.” “Why?” I asked. Miller replied, “Because of the president’s unprecedentedly successful first nine months in office.”

What followed was a black-is-white, up-is-down litany of Trump’s accomplishments, a revisionist history that might have made Karoline Leavitt, another world-class propagandist, blush. “In no particular order,” Miller said, “the taming and defeating of inflation. As you know, we inherited record double-digit inflation and the president made his first priority to bring down the cost of living…cutting the price of energy by high double digits. For many Americans, gas prices have been cut in half since we took over.” None of this, of course, has proved true—but Miller was just warming up.

“And the passage of the largest package of tax cuts and reforms in American history in the form of the One Big Beautiful Bill. Fully securing the Southern border, the most secure border in history, zero illegal crossings, four months in a row. And also the largest number of deportations in American history…. The president has…achieved more in foreign policy than any president in our lifetimes, ending eight wars in eight months—which includes, but is not limited to, securing the historic peace deal between Israel and Gaza, Thailand and Cambodia, Congo and Rwanda, Azerbaijan and Armenia, Israel and Iran, and so on…”

Miller still wasn’t done. The president, he said, “has executed on every promise he’s made to the American people and that includes also taking back our culture and fighting against whether you call it radical leftism or woke or Marxism—a lot of people just call it plain crazy—but taking back our culture and restoring sanity to American life.

“It is my extraordinary privilege and blessing,” Miller added, “to be able to work for the president of the United States, to implement an agenda that I believe in wholly and completely and with every fiber of my being.”

I was there, of course, because I was writing a story on Susie Wiles and her West Wing team. I asked him, point-blank: “Would you agree, though, that it’s also the chief of staff’s job to tell the president what he doesn’t want to hear?”

“No, I wouldn’t say that,” Miller replied, finding fault with my framing of the question and insisting that this president wants to hear everything. “The chief of staff would never have to encounter the scenario you’re describing.” Miller said. “President Trump craves, he loves the every-minute detail of management, having every piece of information, having every side of everything, having inputs into the Oval Office from every square, every quarter, every person.”

As for the president’s staff, Miller said, their job is “implementing the president’s vision, the president’s directions, the president’s agenda, the president’s plans for the future…. We’re just here on his behalf to see that that is done.”

As the great political columnist Molly Ivins once wrote, “It probably sounded better in the original German.”

Experts tell The Verge that China could exploit access to Nvidia to accelerate its own development, or to impede the US development of AI.

Getting its hands on more Nvidia chips is the most obvious win for Beijing, says Emily Kilcrease, a senior fellow and director of the energy, economics, and security program at the Center for a New American Security (CNAS) think tank. China has advantages in other parts of the AI race — including abundant energy and a solid ecosystem of open weight models — but without the latest Nvidia chips, it “cannot close the gap with US frontier capabilities,” Kilcrease tells The Verge. “With those chips, the US lead could vanish in a matter of weeks or months.”

It is not clear how successful the alleged spying operation has been on that front. There is no public evidence indicating that Huang himself has helped direct restricted Nvidia chips toward China or that sensitive information about them was obtained from him. But companies linked to the alleged spy, Alice Huang — no relation to Jensen — have reportedly enabled at least $7 billion of Nvidia chip sales to China.

Brookings fellow Kyle Chan tells The Verge that a concern for US industry could be that companies with alleged ties to China may have had priority access to Nvidia’s latest GPUs. “In a time of chip scarcity,” that would make it much harder for American companies to access them, Chan says.

Huang is also a prominent critic of American restrictions on chip sales, and Nvidia spends a formidable amount lobbying in Washington. Last year, he successfully secured White House approval to start exporting Nvidia’s H200 AI GPU chips to “approved customers” in China and other countries. Crucially, Huang also has the ear of President Trump as well as other world leaders, giving him great influence over policy decisions.

Hardware is not the only thing of value China could get from close access to Huang, Sunny Cheung, a fellow for China studies at the Jamestown Foundation think tank, tells The Verge. “I actually think too many people are focused on whether somebody managed to steal Nvidia’s trade secrets,” Cheung says, calling it a “very narrow way” of looking at espionage. “I would hope Jensen Huang isn’t foolish enough to hand over the blueprints for Nvidia’s next-generation chips to a suspected Chinese intelligence operative.”

The Nvidia CEO’s relationships with other tech executives, senior policymakers, and Trump himself give him an unusually intimate view into how some of the most powerful people in the US are thinking and planning. Beijing could use information on how American companies are planning major infrastructure expansions, their compute requirements, or broad industry trends to inform its own investments and industrial strategy, Cheung says.

Huang’s intelligence value could extend well beyond AI, too. “That information alone could be extremely valuable,” says Cheung, pointing to insights Beijing could glean into White House thinking about export controls or the private views of Silicon Valley executives on China and Taiwan.

“When so much of America’s AI infrastructure depends on one company, any significant security or governance problem at Nvidia can create uncertainty across the industry.”

But helping China advance is only one side of this equation. Disrupting Nvidia — as this scandal clearly has — could destabilize America’s entire AI industry. More than most companies, Nvidia is remarkably closely tied to the identity and reputation of its rockstar CEO. Huang cofounded the company more than three decades ago, and ever since then he has served as its president, chief executive, and a member of the board of directors. Despite holding a relatively small stake in the company, in many ways Nvidia is Jensen Huang. Nvidia shares have slumped since news of the affair broke.

“When so much of America’s AI infrastructure depends on one company, any significant security or governance problem at Nvidia can create uncertainty across the industry,” Cheung says.

Kilcrease echoes those concerns, warning that the scandal could “destabilize” the company. At the very least, she says the various investigations already underway “will consume an immense amount of bandwidth within the company,” creating a very real risk of weighing down Nvidia’s day-to-day operations. If those distractions begin to affect Nvidia’s ability to execute — slowing decisions, product rollouts, or the allocation of already scarce chips — the consequences could quickly reach customers. The AI labs and hyperscalers such as OpenAI and Anthropic “could have a real issue accessing the vast amounts of compute they need,” Kilcrease says, adding that switching to other hardware “takes time.”

In a race to build increasingly powerful AI, time is a precious resource. Experts say China trails the US closely, meaning that even modest delays for American companies can help Chinese rivals catch up. Nvidia is almost certain to face further disruption as investigations continue, though the extent to which the turmoil will roil the rest of the industry may be difficult to measure.

For China, however, the scandal could be about to backfire spectacularly now everything is out in the open. “The odds of AI chip control legislation being passed in Congress just went through the roof,” Kilcrease says.
