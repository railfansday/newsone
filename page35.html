Toto Wolff says he's "proud" of Mercedes' turnaround to win the 2026 Formula 1 Constructors' Championship and feels very few sports teams have bounced back so quickly.

Mercedes won their ninth constructors' title on Sunday at the Sao Paulo Grand Prix, five years after their last championship, which was their eighth in a row at the time.

Regulation changes in 2022 ended Mercedes' dominance as they struggled to get on top of the so-called ground effect cars, but they have won the first season of the new 2026 cars, and also wrapped up the Drivers' Championship earlier this month with Kimi Antonelli.

"The thing is when you are in these difficult moments, like we all are sometimes in our lives, it's not that easy to keep going," said Wolff.

"Looking back in retrospect, you're saying, 'we needed these tough moments in order to grow as people or to grow as an organisation'. But when you're down, that is quite difficult to feel.

"I think those last four years were very difficult between getting it wrong, then kind of understanding why it was wrong and then realising no we didn't understand. That was a bit back and forth.

"But we will be looking back in 10 or 20 years. When you look at the statistics, it wasn't that we were complete losers over those four years, because we finished second, (second), third, fourth [in the championship], we won races, won many races. So it wasn't that bad at all when you look at the sheer numbers, but it felt not good."

Godzilla Minus Zero Director Reveals Why He Scrapped Original Sequel Plans

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Godzilla Minus Zero director doesn't believe that a sequel would necessarily benefit the massive franchise.

Godzilla Minus Zero director Takashi Yamazaki has opened up about the challenges of following his Oscar-winning film. He explained why he ultimately abandoned the idea of making a sequel designed specifically to surpass the original. The director initially wanted to create a movie that could replicate or outdo the success of Godzilla Minus One. However, he eventually decided that approaching the project with those goals could compromise the quality of the content.

In an interview with IGN, Yamazaki discussed the pressure surrounding his return to the franchise following the overwhelmingly positive reception of his Academy Award-winning 2023 film. The director said that he thought about creating a more dramatic and explosive movie that would be bigger than its predecessor. However, he eventually scrapped that approach after realizing how bigger isn't always better, as well as how only focusing on commercial success and more awards could sacrifice the effectiveness of the storyline. He added that if he went with his original plans, he would have been doing it for the wrong reasons.

Instead of focusing on making the sequel outperform the first film, Yamazaki decided to concentrate on creating an entertaining experience with a meaningful story. According to the director, he dropped the idea that he needed this sequel to beat Godzilla Minus One. As a result, he was able to give himself and his team more creative freedom. He described the change in plans as an important moment for Godzilla Minus Zero, helping him move forward without worrying about living in the shadow of Godzilla Minus One.

In the beginning I thought, ‘Well, how do I outdo its predecessor and how do we make a movie that's going to win all these awards and it's going to be bigger in scale?' But I think it's very dangerous to create a movie with that type of mindset because you're going to risk diluting what makes the movie great in the first place. So those are the wrong reasons to make a movie and it ends up becoming an antagonist in your moviemaking process.

Yamazaki's original ideas are understandable considering the massive success of Godzilla Minus One. Released in 2023, the Japanese monster movie explored the dire state of the country after an awful war while introducing a terrifying new version of the iconic monster. The film was praised by both critics and casual audiences alike for its emotional plot, interesting characters, and amazing visual effects.

The movie made history for not just the Godzilla franchise but also its country at the 96th Academy Awards in 2024, becoming the first Japanese production to win Best Visual Effects. It was also the first installment in the property to receive an Oscar ever. Its achievement was incredibly impressive because its visual effects team was working with way less resources than what major Hollywood blockbusters have access to.

Godzilla Minus Zero is currently playing in theaters now.

12 Most Universally Loved Sitcom Characters Of All Time

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Sitcoms are famous for producing a whole host of lovable characters, but there are a few that come out as clear favorites among wider audiences. The sitcom is one of the most endearing formats on TV, providing audiences with comfort watches that they can revisit time and time again, and a cast of characters who largely remain familiar throughout the show.

With that said, every sitcom character typically needs a set of flaws that make them more interesting and serve as a building block to create narratives around these individuals. And while some flaws are innocuous, there are others that can make a sitcom character insufferable.

On the flip side, there are a handful of sitcom legends who have cemented their place in audiences' hearts thanks to pretty flimsy flaws, or their charming and quirky personalities, or simply being a figure who often sees the best in others, and thus ends up having their best shine through.

With that said, the first person on this list is, of course, Ted Lasso. Played by Jason Sudeikis, Ted is a charming American man who moves to the UK to coach a football team, and while he has little to no knowledge about the sport or English customs, his endless positivity, warmth, and ability to draw out people's best serves him well in his new position.

Ted is also incredibly bright, with a high degree of emotional intelligence that allows him to tap into what his peers are feeling and connect on a level that many others struggle to replicate. Ted's charm and optimism earns him a spot as one of the most beloved characters on TV.

Next up, Phil Dunphy is remarkably similar to Ted Lasso in some ways, due to his incredible positive outlook and his warmth, but he does also possess a great deal more naïveté than Ted. Phil is clumsy, corny, and desperate to get approval from others, whether that be his wife, kids, father-in-law, or clients.

However, despite his surprising lack of self-awareness, Phil's depth of love and support for his family is the thing that makes him an undeniably endearing character that people can't help but fall in love with. Ty Burrell plays the part with incredible confidence and energy, and it all comes together in perfect harmony on the show season after season.

Matthew Perry may have been a more colorful character in life whose choices didn't always lead him down the healthiest paths, but his time as Chandler Bing on Friends will forever keep his memory alive in the hearts and minds of audiences everywhere. The interesting thing about Chandler is the fact that he is such a guarded character, always hiding behind humor and sarcasm.

However, despite his tendency to try and keep his emotions at a distance, Chandler becomes one of the most vulnerable and real characters on the show, especially in the moments he shares with Monica, or as his friendship with Joey evolves. Chandler is a beautiful soul in a bristly package, and it makes him all the more lovable.

Another character who likes to keep his emotions buried deep, Ron Swanson from Parks and Recreation, is the Director of his local Parks and Recreation department of government, which makes his unabashed contempt for all forms of politics and red tape all the more intriguing.

Ron Swanson is a simple man who enjoys simple things, and he has no respect for how government bodies are supposed to run, instead holding onto the job because he finds it to be an easy and low-effort role where almost nothing ever happens, that is until Leslie Knope sets her mind to opening a new park in Pawnee.

Nick Offerman is absolutely fantastic as Swanson, and the character remains a wonderful contradiction whose desire to not work is rivaled only by his intense loyalty to his friends and his commitment to supporting their dreams.

The late and great Catherine O'Hara left her mark on the TV landscape with her spectacular turn as Moira Rose, the matriarch of Schitt's Creek. While Moira is initially a hard woman to love or respect, and her self-absorbed and theatrical nature make her a bit of a pain, Moira's growth throughout the series is something to behold.

As the character begins to warm up to small-town living, and become someone that fans could engage with on a deeper level, it's hard to look back at those earlier moments without a tenderness and admiration for this character, who is ultimately on a journey of growth and understanding.

The 1950s sitcom, I Love Lucy, was a foundational entry in the genre that set the tone from which many other TV comedy shows would draw inspiration. Lucille Ball plays the titular lead, and brings a tremendous physical comedy that, when paired with Lucy's wacky schemes and overtly expressive face, makes her a character that is impossible not to fall in love with.

With that, the show earned its title, and the series continued to prove just how popular it was over six seasons. Today, I Love Lucy may not be the most familiar show for younger viewers, but anyone who picks up the series will ultimately find themselves captivated by the charm of Lucy Ricardo.

A few decades later, in 1985, The Golden Girls launched on TV and became an instant classic. And in no small part, that was thanks to the incredible ensemble cast that brought together four strong and vocal personalities who all had their own distinctive voice, and explored their wonderful maturing friendship.

Still, among these four charming women, Betty White's Rose Nylund stands out as one of the most lovable, thanks to her innocence, naïveté, and her ability to balance out the rest of the girls when things got heated. Rose is a literal treasure who remains one of the most beloved characters from TV, even all these decades later.

Rowan Atkinson struck gold in developing this silent character, Mr. Bean, who became a global sensation without ever saying a word. Employing a great deal of slapstick humor, intense facial expressions, and a certain innocence, Mr. Bean is a character that seems to transcend time and other barriers to engage with fanbases the world over.

He is a simple man, who sets out to complete simple tasks. However, due to his lack of awareness, and his clumsy nature, Mr. Bean often gets caught up in trouble that would never impact on typical people. But all of that simply makes him more engaging and adorable.

Andy Samberg's Jake Peralta is the embodiment of golden retriever energy in the form of an immature New York City detective. Jake is an eternally youthful and playful character who wants to get the best out of life, and is always ready to gamify his situation.

Jake also experiences profound personal growth over the course of the series, as he becomes more vulnerable and forms closer relationships with people like Captain Raymon Holt and Amy Santiago.

Community is a show that is brimming with colorful characters, but while the flaws for many of these individuals are initially overwhelming, Abed's (Danny Pudi) quirk is that he views everything through a meta lens and constantly references pop culture.

While everyone else around him embarks on a journey to become the best version of themselves, Abed is often the anchor that helps others to recognize where they are making mistakes, and course correct. He is intensely loyal, curious about human connection and boldly honest. All of which adds up to make him one of the best characters on TV.

Apple TV's "Unadaptable" 3-Season Series Is Mandatory Viewing for Sci-Fi Fans

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Julio is a Senior Author for Collider. He studied History and International Relations at university, but found his calling in cultural journalism. When he isn't writing, Julio also teaches English at a nearby school. He has lived in São Paulo most of his life, where he covers CCXP and other big events. Having loved movies, music, and TV from an early age, he prides himself in knowing every minute detail about the things he loves. When he is older, he dreams of owning a movie theater in a small countryside town.

By now, it's no secret that Apple TV has a knack for releasing great science fiction; you can pretty much choose one at random, and it will still be a great choice. Saying one of them is the best feels even a little unfair to the others, so we have to raise the bar and look beyond the screen and individual taste, focusing instead on what kind of accomplishment it represents. All that considered, it's almost impossible not to choose Foundation, the adaptation of Isaac Asimov's seminal sci-fi saga. Before its release, these books were considered straight-up unadaptable, so, in many ways, the greatest achievement of Apple TV's Foundation isn't just being an incredible work of science fiction, but that it exists at all, really.

Between original series and adaptations, science fiction has had a boom in productions ever since the dawn of the streaming age. The one work every fan of the genre had pretty much accepted would never get such treatment was Foundation. Even after its announcement back in 2018, everyone only believed the series was actually real when it premiered via Apple TV in 2021. That's because no other work in sci-fi has a scope as huge as Foundation — it was just too much to believe it could ever be made into a TV series.

Set in a future where humankind has conquered the cosmos, the original book comprises many smaller tales depicting humanity's efforts to overcome a thousand years of dark ages. The single common thread is Hari Seldon (Jared Harris), founder of psychohistory, a field of study that uses probability and psychology to predict future events on a galactic scale. Seldon devised a plan for future human leaders to follow, thus reducing the dark ages from tens of thousands of years to a single millennium.

It's a fascinating story and an easy read, filled with equally interesting characters. However, most never interact due to their respective stories being set decades and even centuries apart. This is a major roadblock for any kind of visual adaptation, because focusing on just one of them would mean neglecting centuries of human history in that universe. It just couldn't be done, and those centuries of human history are the whole point of Foundation. Something had to drastically change for these novels to fit the TV framework.

When translating a work of literature into visual media, it's always necessary to make some changes, including, sometimes, some severe ones. Modern television usually demands strong protagonists with whom the audience can sympathize, so, when David S. Goyer and Josh Friedman started working on adapting Foundation, this was one of the very first things to tackle — that and a way to unify and streamline the original narrative, so it could fit the episodic and seasonal framework.

Instead of multiple separate storylines spread out across the timeline, the series brings them together with time jumps and some classic sci-fi narrative devices. Thus, Apple's Foundation takes the main characters in the early Asimov books and turns them into actual protagonists, each with their own complexities and development arcs. Instead of two or three small chapters, then, characters like Gaal Dornick (Lou Llobell), Salvor Hardin (Leah Harvey), and even Seldon himself have central and much larger roles.

Despite its scope, though, the books are quite simplistic in worldbuilding early on, as the Foundation itself is still struggling to consolidate. Sci-fi television demands something greater, though, so the Apple series has grander and more cinematic settings than the books. Every planet has a complex culture and background, and even things like Seldon's vault, where his recording reveals the next steps of his plan every few decades, become central plot devices tied to the protagonists, keeping the story straight and fluid.

There was no way to make Foundation work as a series if it was an exact match with its book counterpart, and a significant part of why it's so good is how it allows itself to go beyond Asimov's original writings. The best example is the Genetic Dynasty, an entirely new idea created for the series. In the books, the Galactic Empire's decay is what causes the dark ages, but it's the series that actually goes into why that is, since the original focuses mostly on the other side, the Foundation.

For that, the series introduces one of the most brilliant concepts in recent sci-fi: the Genetic Dynasty. It perfectly sums up the notion that the Empire is so stagnant, to the point where every emperor is a clone of the same man, Cleon I. Brothers Dawn (Cassian Bilton), Day (Lee Pace), and Dusk (Terrence Mann) are essentially different versions of the same person, each struggling to establish their own identity while living under the shadow of their predecessor. Another great example is Demerzel (Laura Birn), whose role is considerably expanded from the books, made in the Apple adaptation into a central figure in the Empire's political machinations, with a twist coming straight from the author's wider Robot universe.

These additions give Apple's Foundation an identity of its own without losing sight of the ideas that made the original novels so influential. Ultimately, that's what makes it such a great adaptation, focusing more on understanding and expanding on the potential of the original story rather than simply adapting it. Bringing Foundation to television was always going to require some creative liberties, but the series shows just how much can be achieved when an adaptation understands what makes its source material special.

Foundation is available to stream on Apple TV.

Survival after sudden cardiac arrest didn’t improve despite training bystanders, emergency responders

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Liz covers cardiovascular and metabolic disease, asking why we’re not doing better or even losing ground against the world’s leading killer. Send tips on Signal at LizC.22.

CHICAGO — Sudden cardiac arrest statistics are chilling. In the United States, 350,000 people’s heartbeats, breathing, pulse, and blood circulation will all halt outside hospitals this year, seemingly out of the blue. These are not heart attacks. Only 1 in 10 people will survive them.

CPR and defibrillation can save their lives (and brains), but that depends on bystanders and emergency response systems being both nearby and prepared to step in — immediately. Survival chances drop by 10% for every minute that goes by without CPR.

Hints from observational studies suggested better, more comprehensive community training can help, which means educating 911 dispatchers, first responders, and potential bystanders. But results from a randomized controlled trial presented Sunday at the American Heart Association’s scientific sessions and published in the New England Journal of Medicine on Sunday show that approach has not worked, or at least not yet. But examples in Denmark may point the way forward.

“We performed an efficient, large, and high-quality trial engaging large segments of the community in emergency response, and in spite of this, survival remained low and unchanged,” Carolina Malta Hansen, the study’s lead author, said at a media briefing. “A multifaceted community and EMS implementation strategy did not improve survival with a favorable neurologic outcome for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. However, in the final year, there appeared to be an improvement in bystanders.”

Starting in 2021, researchers randomly assigned participants in 60 counties in North Carolina to receive intensive education on cardiac arrest intervention or to continue with standard practice. The intervention entailed training 911 dispatchers to improve their rapid recognition of cardiac arrest and better guide bystanders through CPR. First responders were educated on the use of automated external defibrillators before emergency services arrived. Community members in workplaces, religious groups, government offices, and other settings received training in CPR.

About 8.5 million people live in these counties. During the study’s five years, 22,459 adult residents experienced cardiac arrest outside the hospital. Their median age was 64; 37% were women, 33% were Black; and 29% lived in rural areas.

The numbers were split this way: 11,615 people experienced out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in counties that received intensive education, and 10,844 people had out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in communities that did not receive the intervention.

Amrish Deshmukh, a cardiac electrophysiologist who treats patients with heart rhythm disorders at the University of Michigan Health Frankel Cardiovascular Center, called the study results disappointing, but not surprising.

“Many interventions that have been studied in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and in post-arrest care unfortunately have not been shown to improve survival or neurologic outcomes,” he told STAT in an email. He was not involved in the new trial. “Prior studies have shown that mobile phone alerts, volunteer training, and increased automated external defibrillator (AEDs) availability can increase the performance of CPR and use of AEDs, but not all have shown improvements in survival.”

And yet, study author Malta Hansen, a cardiologist and clinical research associate professor at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital and Copenhagen EMS in Denmark, is hopeful. In the trial’s fourth year, an exploratory analysis showed the percentage of patients who underwent CPR performed by a bystander increased, suggesting that this component of the intervention was effective. More time may be needed to see which elements mattered and whether they affect survival.

“In light of the apparent improvement in CPR for the last year, further evaluation in the longer, more intense intervention is important,” she said.

Malta Hansen cites the case of Denmark, where rates of bystander CPR and survival are much higher than in the United States. The North Carolina study trained almost 5% of the population, at which point researchers started seeing a difference in delivery of CPR between the group that received training and the group that did not.

“Obviously, that’s not enough. We have observational evidence from other countries like Denmark, where 80% of the population is trained and 75% of patients receive high-speed care,” she said.

In Denmark, the defibrillation rate is 11%, compared to 2% in the U.S. In Denmark, survival is about 16%, compared to 10% in the U.S.

That CPR-trained rate in Denmark climbed from 20% in the early 2000s to 80% now, Malta Hansen said. “It’s not something that’s done in two or three years.”

Here’s what helps: To get your driver’s license in Denmark, you have to show that you have certification in CPR. “It’s just an incredibly efficient way to reach everybody,” she said.

Brahmajee Nallamothu, an interventional cardiologist and editor in chief of the AHA journal Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes, had another theory on timing. Starting the trial right as Covid-19 was emerging could have affected educational efforts and could explain why effects of the training were delayed. He also asked if the educational intervention was the right approach.

“I don’t think anyone would argue with the importance of early treatment in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. When we think about bystander CPR, early defibrillation with AEDs, the challenge is: How do you translate that?” Nallamathu, who was not involved in the trial, said. “I think we still struggle with what’s the best way to train individuals so that they have sustained ability to understand and then utilize that tool when that situation arises, that’s an area that’s still complicated and still unknown.”

Malta Hansen’s team will follow this population in North Carolina for at least another year.

“We have established a fantastic infrastructure and network and collaboration, and everybody’s very excited to keep working on this,” she said. “What we need are communities who are very engaged and very eager to keep working on this. So we believe that future efforts to guide us will be successful.”

That won’t be easy, Michigan’s Deshmukh said.

“Implementation science is challenging (as evidenced by this and other negative trials), but is what I suspect will move the needle the most,” he wrote.

Qalo SL-1 vs. Garmin Cirqa: How these minimalist wearables compare

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both screenless bands cost about $200 and work without a subscription, but their specs are pretty different.

Garmin and Qalo both launched screenless fitness bands just 10 weeks apart this year — and also priced them four cents apart at about $200 each. Garmin announced the Cirqa on July 21, and Qalo followed on September 29 with the SL-1. Neither of the bands has a display or requires a paid membership to work, separating them from Whoop, whose bands come with a membership starting at $199 per year.

Looking at their respective spec sheets, Qalo claims the longer battery life while Garmin lists the deeper water rating, more activity types and more training metrics. The Garmin wearable also has an optional paid software tier that Qalo doesn't. We've tested both in depth: our Garmin Cirqa review scored it 7.4 out of 10, while our Qalo SL-1 review gave it 6.8.

Qalo sells the SL-1 for $199.95 with a black silicone strap and a charging dock. Extra straps will set you back $34.95 apiece. No subscription is required to use the band, and the companion app is free.

Garmin's press release says the $199.99 Cirqa tracks its features without the need for a subscription. That said, there is an optional tier called Connect+, which costs $6.99 per month or $69.99 per year. Cirqa's manual lists things like AI-generated insights, nutrition logging, Live Activity Stats, training guidance and a performance dashboard among the features that need the subscription. It also says that guided workouts require a subscription, and that some activities may too. Both bands are eligible for HSA and FSA spending.

Qalo lists the SL-1 at 18 grams and 10.5mm thick but doesn't say whether that weight includes the strap. Garmin's Cirqa is 14.8 grams without the strap and 20.7 total grams with the smaller wristband.

The SL-1 is worn on the wrist, about an inch above the wrist bone, on a silicone strap with a woven one sold separately. Meanwhile, the Cirqa sports a fabric hook-and-loop band that comes in two wrist sizes and can also be worn on the upper arm with a long arm band. Garmin offers four colors, and the Cirqa has one side button, a multicolor status LED and vibration alerts for alarms and a Find My Band feature. Qalo's support pages describe only a charging status light.

Garmin has rated the Cirqa at 5 ATM, which its manual describes as being capable of withstanding pressure equivalent to a depth of 50 meters and includes a Swim activity. In contrast, Qalo's product page says the SL-1 is water-resistant to 10 meters and warns against scuba diving and deep-water pressure. Its support site gives it a IP68 rating. In terms of temperature, the Cirqa's stated operating range is -4 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit, while Qalo lists 14 to 113 degrees Fahrenheit.

Qalo advertises 12 or more days of battery life for the SL-1 and Garmin up to 10 for the Cirqa. In our SL-1 review, the battery still had over half its juice left after a whole week. The Cirqa, meanwhile, charges in up to 90 minutes over a proprietary Garmin cable, while the SL-1 uses a magnetic dock with a USB-C cable. Interestingly, Qalo's docs disagree on how long it takes the SL-1 to charge: the product page says 60 to 120 minutes while the support site says two to three hours.

Both bands come with an optical heart rate sensor and can measure blood oxygen, skin temperature, heart rate variability, stress and sleep stages, and both offer menstrual cycle tracking. Neither has built-in GPS, but the Cirqa can use a paired phone's GPS. Qalo rolls sleep, HRV, movement and stress into a daily "Q Score" from 1 to 100 while Garmin spreads its analyses across Body Battery, Training Readiness, training status, VO2 max and HRV status. The Cirqa lists more than 80 activity types against the SL-1's 54, and the Cirqa can broadcast heart rate to other devices. The SL-1 shares one profile with Qalo's smart rings, which the company also sells without a subscription.

Qalo's product page says the SL-1's 54 activity modes include automatic activity detection, but our review found that the band didn't pick up workouts by itself, not even walks. Meanwhile, the Cirqa's automatic detection worked well enough in our testing that manual recording was rarely needed. Qalo also says its movement tracking is up to 12.5 percent more accurate than "popular alternatives." While our SL-1 review didn't test accuracy, our Cirqa review did count Garmin's reliable accuracy among the band's strengths.

Beyond the specs, we found the Cirqa to be perfectly functional in our testing. That said, the single button was the main irritation, since dismissing the alarm with a mistimed press starts a workout instead. We also found the Garmin Connect app to be pretty heavy on data and light on interpretation, and it was hard to see why the Cirqa costs more than Garmin's own cheaper trackers. The SL-1 fared a little worse. The battery was excellent, still showing 59 percent after a full week, and there's no fee to worry about, but the magnetic clasp on the silicone strap can come loose if a sleeve catches it at the wrong angle, and the app does little beyond aggregating the data it collects.

With both bands at the same price, we'd lean towards the Cirqa, which gets the basics right. The SL-1 would make more sense as a first screenless tracker if it were cheaper.

Coping With an Acute Injury or Health Diagnosis

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Many clients who come to see me in therapy are coping with an acute injury or health change. For example, they may have sustained an injury while playing a sport or been given a new medical diagnosis. A sudden injury or health change can feel shocking and is often difficult to cope with. You may be trying to adjust to changes in mobility and functioning and worried about when and if you will return to your “normal” self.

Often, the most pressing concern is: Will I get better? And when? The uncertainty is difficult to tolerate. While some injuries or diagnoses have clear treatment plans and timelines, more often it is not crystal clear what someone’s recovery or medical journey will look like. The anxious brain likes to jump to worst-case scenarios when uncertainty exists. It takes a “better safe than sorry” approach, leading us to think about all the possible horrible outcomes so we can be prepared. However, these worst-case scenarios may never come to pass, and anxiety can start to take up more and more space in your daily life.

An injury or new diagnosis can feel like a never-ending emotional roller coaster ride that you never wanted to take. It can be challenging to acknowledge the reality of the injury or diagnosis, including sudden changes you may be facing, without thinking that your life is doomed. Below are some strategies for coping with an acute injury or health diagnosis.

It is helpful to be informed about your condition, as a general understanding can help you have effective conversations with health care providers. It is useful to use reputable sources for gathering information, and your medical team may be able to offer resources. Keep in mind that not all of your questions may have firm answers, and there may be limits to what you or your medical team may be able to know.

While it is difficult to tolerate uncertainty, be mindful of not going down an information-gathering rabbit hole that consumes too much of your time and life. If you notice that trying to gather information about your condition is taking over your daily life or that your anxiety is increasing, set time limits for gathering information. For example, 30 to 60 minutes a week.

A sudden injury or diagnosis can create a wave of emotions. You may feel scared, apprehensive, sad, angry, frustrated, etc. Trying to suppress your emotions can intensify them. Find an authentic way to express how you are feeling. For example, take five to 10 minutes daily to write out your thoughts or feelings, say them aloud on a voice memo app, or share how you are feeling with a trusted friend or family member. If it feels unfamiliar and anxiety-provoking to talk about your feelings, consider doing so in a less focused or pressured environment. For instance, you may talk about your feelings while traveling in the car with soft music playing.

It can be unfamiliar and challenging for some people to accept help from others. Maybe you are used to doing everything yourself, and accepting help leads you to feel incapable. However, accepting help from others allows people who care about you to be of assistance during a difficult time. This is beneficial for them as well as for you. Also, spend time with people you are comfortable with and enjoy. It is natural to feel down, withdraw from others, and isolate ourselves during stressful periods. Yet, isolation can worsen mood. Even if you don’t initially feel like it, carve out some time with others. Your desire to be with others may increase over time. Also, it may be helpful to seek out connection with others who have the same injury or diagnosis. Talking with others with a shared understanding and hearing about others’ treatments, recovery, and coping strategies can feel relieving and supportive.

It can be difficult to accept limitations or the reality that there are some things you may not be able to do or need to do in a different way. It is important to give yourself space and time to grieve any losses you are experiencing. Expecting too much of yourself or trying to ignore the current circumstances can lead to increased frustration, anger, and sadness. Also, trying to meet unrealistic goals may deplete you and lead to less functioning and ability than you would have if you set more realistic and sustainable goals.

Just because you have limitations or there are certain things you cannot do does not mean you are incapable. Non-judgmentally ask yourself what you can and want to realistically take on, and aim to set small, doable goals.

A new injury or diagnosis can be anxiety-provoking and scary, and can highlight what or who you care most about. Certain activities may be different now, yet there are likely things or people who continue to be meaningful to you. Carve out time for them. Stay connected, or reconnect, to what feels most important and fulfilling to you. You may find that many of these things are simple and joyful, such as spending time with loved ones or time in nature.

The anxious part of our minds equates uncertainty with bad based on self-protection and preservation. While a good outcome is not guaranteed, neither is a bad one. The future could be incredibly difficult, and it also could be better than you are thinking or neutral. When your mind wants to leap ahead to worst-case scenarios, pause, take a slow breath, and remind yourself, “Uncertain does not mean bad; it simply means unknown.” Then refocus on the present moment and a values-based action you can take today.
