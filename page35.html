How to Recover From High School

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

High school may have been a high for many. But if it was a low for you, you ain't alone. A 2025 YouGov survey found that 12 percent of respondents disliked high school while 8 percent hated it. Consider me among the 8 percent.

Having a lot more "grrrrs" than "rah-rahs" in high school could leave you holding onto Rs—namely, resentment and regret—which could keep you trapped in your high school days—and daze—way longer than you'd want. Here's how to instead truly graduate from all that:

A friend once described high school as "The last time that you had no choice of who is going to be around you most of the time," because your high school totally depended on where your family happened to live and what resources and connections they happened to have. Therefore, chances were quite high that you were around high schoolers who didn't fit very well with you.

My high school, for example, had very little diversity and was filled with people not used to dealing with those of different racial, ethnic, cultural, and socioeconomic backgrounds and ways of thinking. For example, my high school soccer team had never seen someone like me play soccer. So, it was pretty common to hear racial stereotypes and epithets hurled around throughout my teenage years, making me feel quite alone then.

This brings up an age-old problem—or perhaps an age-young one. Your high school classmates were in their mid-teens at the time. Their brains and senses of self were still developing like fruitcake batter that had just entered the oven. That, along with their lack of life experience, left them quite limited in their ability to process things around them, know right from wrong, and think for themselves. There are reasons why rental car companies still charge extra until you've gotten past your 25th birthday.

Does that guarantee that all of your high school classmates will eventually grow out of their high school selves? Of course not. Nevertheless, surely you wouldn't base your worth on what any teenager would say about you now? Heck, I wouldn't even listen to my own high school self.

That's because I was still full of "Never Have I Evers" back then—way before I had actually traveled around the world, met a much greater diversity of people, developed real friendships, and engaged in real romantic relationships. For a while, it was hard to look back at my naive high school self without a ton of cringe. But I have since learned to forgive my teenage self—recognizing that I didn't know better at the time.

Part of healing is forgiving your high school classmates as well. For example, the female classmate who once told me, "You are smart and funny, so I like hanging out with you, but you are so incredibly ugly," probably wasn't used to seeing a diverse range of looks and people. Also, her frontal lobe may not yet have been developed enough to tell her, "OK, maybe don't say something like that if you want that person to help you with your homework or teach you how to play sports."

Of course, the fact that to save money my dad—rather than, you know, a real barber—would regularly cut my hair—leaving my head looking like that of a LEGO person—probably didn't help. But eventually you realize that you can recover from not only a bad haircut but also high school. After all, high school is only four years and not forever—lasting about as long as a pair of heavily distressed jeans.

It is important, though, to treat any distressing high school experience like you would a pair of out-of-fashion distressed jeans. Throw them away so that they don't keep taking up space—space that you could otherwise use for new memories, experiences, people, and growth. In fact, get rid of anything that may trigger low high school memories, such as severing ties with anyone who doesn't recognize that you have outgrown your high school years. If you do find it difficult to move on from those years, get help from others, such as a professional therapist.

You'll need to free up that mental and emotional space because you may have a lot of catch-up growing to do. I certainly did, which probably delayed a lot of things and led to a lot of late blooming. But don't despair. Think of going through life as what the character Kandi said on an episode of "Two and a Half Men" about being with Alan: "[It's like being] in the back seat of a car driven by a really smart kangaroo. He may go up on the curb a couple of times, but he'll get you there." The key then is to learn from your faulty high school days—understand what went wrong, what you did wrong, and learn from it. Adapt accordingly.

Now, throwing stuff out doesn't mean simply suppressing your bad high school memories and feelings. That can be like hiding a ferret in your pants. It could end up eating at your rather sensitive spots over time and emerging at the most unexpected, inopportune times. Instead, consider directly confronting your past and processing it.

In the process, you may even find that your memories don't match reality. The supposedly cool things you missed out on may not have been so cool. Those who made you feel bad may have actually felt very bad about themselves.

In lieu of going to my high school reunion, I had dinner with someone I actually liked from high school. We had never really talked about our high school experiences until then. But it soon became apparent that her high school experience was similarly negative. When you are open about such experiences, you may end up realizing that you weren't quite as alone as you thought you were.

Mortgage Purchase and Refi Activity Hits New Low in Q3

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Data for the third quarter for 2026 continues to showcase the effects of high rates and elevated housing-related costs, as the latest data reveals that the quarterly slowdown was driven by declines in both home purchase and refinance lending.

ATTOM—the California-based property data and real estate analytics company—found that all mortgages and lending in housing fell in Q3 2026, in the latest U.S. Residential Property Mortgage Origination Report.

According to the report, a total of 1.29 million purchase and refinance mortgages secured by residential property (one in four units) originated in the third quarter, which is down 12% from Q2 2026 and down 15% compared to the same period last year.

“The increase in refinance activity we saw late last year and into early this year cooled in the third quarter, with refinancing declining more than purchase lending,” ATTOM CEO Rob Barber said. “Purchase loans also slipped to their lowest third-quarter level since 2012, as higher mortgage rates and elevated average purchase loan amounts continued to strain affordability for many buyers.”

Barber—alongside other economists—attribute the third quarter’s cooling housing activity to the sharp jump in mortgage rates. After the Federal Reserve’s rate hike in September, the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage jumped to 7.40% in mid-October, and has slowly decreased but remains over 7%.

Purchase loans fell by 9% quarter-to-quarter, for a total of nearly 730,000. The report notes that this drop is the lowest third quarter since 2012. Annually, purchase loans were down 12%, although the average purchase loan increased by 3% to nearly $412,000.

Purchase lending was also down in Q3 2026, totaling $300.6 billion or down 10% from last quarter, and down 10% on a yearly basis.

While activity declined, purchase loans accounted for 56.5% of all purchase and refinance originations, which is up from the 54.5% in Q2 2026 and Q3 2025.

The share of refinance originations decreased slightly, from 45.5% in Q2 to 43.5% in Q3 2026. Although not a significant drop in shares, refinance lending declined more drastically than purchase activity by city, falling 89.4% on a quarterly basis.

VA-backed loans were also down, representing 6.9% of originations in Q3 after the previous quarter’s 8.2% and 7.2% the same period last year, or the lowest share since Q2 2024.

Man Of Tomorrow’s Superman Improvements Teased By David Corenswet Amid DCU Concerns

Ryan Northrup is a Senior Staff Writer on ScreenRant's Movie & TV News team, where he has authored nearly 5,000 articles, including features and reviews. A graduate of McMaster University, Ryan began his career in public relations before attending Vancouver Film School. There, he built lasting industry connections and optioned a feature film screenplay.

Expect some Superman upgrades in Man of Tomorrow.

Following his introduction as Superman in James Gunn's movie of the same name last year, David Corenswet is set to return as the DC hero next summer for an anticipated sequel. Man of Tomorrow, which arrives following a shakeup at DC in which Gunn and Peter Safran are to step down from their executive leadership roles, will see Superman teaming up with Nicholas Hoult's Lex Luthor to face a new threat.

As the future of DC remains up in the air, Corenswet dropped by The Rich Eisen Show to discuss his time as Superman and what's to come in Man of Tomorrow. Corenswet comments on what it was like filming the original movie and all the firsts that came with that experience, not just in terms of physically transforming himself, but also in terms of becoming comfortable in the suit. He explains:

I mean, looking back, there were a lot of great challenges that came along with it: gaining the weight for the first time in my life, the wire work, the stunt work was a new thing for me. That was wonderful and challenging. I think back to what Christopher Reeve said about his thoughts going into shooting the original Superman: The Movie which was… Ultimately, it’s just you out there in the tights and nobody’s coming to save you.

And so sometimes the biggest challenge was just standing there for a scene in the tights and the trunks and somehow finding a way to not feel silly and not try too hard to make up for it in some way, not act too tough or shy away from what it is. I think it’s one of the great things about the character is that he’s proud to be the big red and blue and he’ll just stand there in his trunks and maybe you’ll laugh at him, but he’ll be happy to have made you laugh.

While addressing how he maintains his confidence while acting in tights next to his costars, Corenswet touches on Lex's advanced armor suit set to feature in the Superman sequel. The actor then jokingly asks why his character doesn't get his own armored suit:

It was really nice anytime I would walk up to somebody and they’d go like, “Oh my god, it’s Superman.” I go, “Okay, I’m going to take that with me for the next few minutes and hopefully that’ll keep my confidence up just long enough to get through the next scene.” Especially when you’re standing across from Rachel Brosnahan, who’s dressed all cool, and she looks so beautiful, and she’s so sharp, and Nicholas Hoult, and his cool tailored suits, and then his war suit in this movie we just shot. He’s got his big green war suit. Like, why can’t I get some armor?

Though Corenswet is careful not to reveal too much about Man of Tomorrow, he does tease that the film features some exciting flying and fight sequences. Speaking seemingly of the stunt and visual effects teams, Corenswet says that "they really up their game" from the first movie. Check out his comment below:

Love the interview scene with Rachel. That was such a fun scene to shoot. It was the first scene that we shot. It was two days and it was like shooting like a 1950s Cary Grant movie. Just the dialogue, quippy, and funny, and just about these characters. There were so many great action pieces. Actually, in this movie, in the next Superman movie that we finished this summer, there are some incredible new flying and fights. They really up their game there. So, in some ways, those are the ones I’m really excited for people to see those, ’cause those were a lot of fun to shoot and I think will end up being a lot of fun to do.

In addition to Corenswet, Brosnahan, and Hoult, Man of Tomorrow is set to feature the return of Skyler Gisondo as Jimmy Olsen, Wendell Pierce as Perry White, Isabela Merced as Hawkgirl, Frank Grillo as Rick Flag Sr., Edi Gathegi as Mr. Terrific, Mikaela Hoover as Cat Grant, and María Gabriela de Faría as the Engineer, among others.

The Superman follow-up will also feature a host of newcomers, including Lars Eidinger as Brainiac, Aaron Pierre as Green Lantern, Milly Alcock as Supergirl, Xolo Maridueña as Blue Beetle, and Matthew Lillard in an undisclosed role. Man of Tomorrow's upgraded flying and fighting scenes, then, will clearly accompany a much more expansive and star-studded cast.

Of course, the recent leadership shakeup at DC Studios has cast a shadow over Man of Tomorrow and has raised questions about the current iteration of DC heroes. After becoming co-CEOs of DC Studios in 2022, Gunn and Safran are confirmed to be stepping down from their roles effective December 1, 2026.

The development follows Paramount Skydance's acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery on October 6, with the resulting company simply called Skydance. Jeremy Latcham is stepping in as president and chief creative officer, replacing Gunn and Safran.

Man of Tomorrow will follow a mixed series of releases for DC. Superman was a modest hit, grossing $618 million and earning strong reviews. Could Earth have been collecting dark matter for billions of years?

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Heat measurements from dead stars helped physicists narrow down how dark matter might interact with ordinary matter.

Physicists have spent decades building dark matter detectors deep underground, and they still haven’t caught a single particle.

A new study suggests that Earth itself, along with the Sun, Jupiter and a handful of dead stars, might trap certain kinds of dark matter better than any lab instrument.

These bodies could have been collecting slow-moving particles for billions of years.

The researchers used neutrino data and heat measurements to rule out some combinations of particle mass and interaction strength that laboratory experiments couldn’t probe.

The work was led by Alejandro Ibarra from the Technical University of Munich, Merlin Reichard from the Korea Institute for Advanced Study and Gaurav Tomar from the Sogang University in Seoul.

About 85% of the matter in the universe is dark matter. We know it’s there because galaxies spin faster than their visible matter can explain and light bends more than it should as it passes them.

Beyond that, very little is known about it. Physicists are stuck in a frustrating loop.

You can’t identify something you can’t see, and you can’t design a way to see it without some idea of what it is. So they make a guess, build an experiment to test it, and so far the answer has always been no.

One guess is that dark matter isn’t completely dark. Its particles might respond to electricity and magnetism very faintly, carrying a tiny fraction of an electron’s charge or acting like microscopic magnets.

The paper describes five such properties. One of them, the “anapole moment,” is a doughnut-shaped kind of magnetism.

Any of the five would let a dark matter particle occasionally bump into an atom.

Underground detectors wait for those bumps. A slow particle taps an atom so gently that the instrument doesn’t notice.

In practice, these detectors are more sensitive to faster dark matter particles.

Planets and stars work the other way around. A fast particle shoots straight through them. A slow one only needs to lose a little speed in a collision before gravity grabs it and won’t let go.

Over billions of years, trapped particles would sink toward the center and crowd together. Once packed tightly enough, they could meet and annihilate, turning into other particles.

Some of those would be neutrinos, which pass through rock almost as if it weren’t there. The rest would deposit their energy as heat.

“In this work, we considered four celestial bodies: Earth, the Sun, Jupiter and white dwarfs,” Tomar said.

“There are dedicated experiments, for example IceCube at the South Pole, looking for neutrinos from the Sun and Earth. For Jupiter and white dwarfs, we considered the heating possibilities.”

For the Sun and Earth, the team used limits from IceCube, a detector built into the Antarctic ice, and from its smaller companion detector, DeepCore.

That included ten years of IceCube data on neutrinos coming from Earth’s core. There was no excess.

Neither Jupiter nor the white dwarfs generate heat through nuclear fusion.

The Cassini spacecraft measured how much heat Jupiter leaks from its interior.

The white dwarfs are burnt-out stellar cores in M4, a cluster of ancient stars. Each one holds about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass in a ball with a radius of roughly 1,250 kilometers.

If a candidate type of dark matter would make these objects give off more heat than they actually do, it can be crossed off.

Earth turned out to be the best test for dark matter with a tiny electric charge. For particles heavier than about ten protons, it set the strongest limits yet.

There are two reasons. First, a charged particle’s pull reaches a long way, so it is easily drawn in. Second, Earth’s core is full of iron, whose atoms each carry 26 protons, which makes collisions more likely.

The white dwarfs set leading limits on two of the more exotic properties, the charge radius and the anapole moment, across a wide range of particle masses.

For very heavy particles, they also beat earlier limits on dark matter’s magnetic dipole moment.

The anapole limits matter for one popular candidate whose only possible electromagnetic property is the anapole moment.

For that candidate, the white dwarfs reach places that particle colliders and underground detectors struggle to get to.

Jupiter was a letdown. In general, it didn’t improve on limits that already existed. None of the four bodies beat earlier limits on the electric dipole moment either.

Underground labs sit beneath a kilometer or more of rock to block cosmic rays. If dark matter interacts strongly with ordinary matter, that same rock could slow it down or stop it before it reaches the detector.

The shield could be hiding the very particles the experiment is looking for.

Planets and stars don’t have this problem. The more strongly dark matter interacts, the better they catch it.

There is one important caveat. The more dark matter surrounds a star, the more of it the star can capture, and nobody has measured how much dark matter M4 contains.

The team had to rely on estimates from models.

“We do not know the dark matter density there. We used estimated values, so if those values change, our limits could become weaker or stronger,” Tomar noted.

Tomar suggests two places to look next. One is even colder white dwarfs in regions with more dark matter, where any extra heat would be easier to detect.

The other is beyond the Solar System, where exoplanets could serve as dark matter traps too.

The study is published in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics.

Dreaming of Buying a Home Abroad? These 10 Countries Make It Shockingly Simple

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you’ve always wanted to live out your expat fantasies, start here.

Over the last few years, many Americans have daydreamed about life abroad. Moving internationally can often feel like a pipe dream, but with the right planning, it’s possible. However, lengthy visa processes, daunting language barriers, and a difficult-to-navigate legal system can make the dream feel more like a nightmare.

Certain countries, like Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, have bans on foreign buyers. Others, like Thailand, Cuba, and Vietnam, don’t allow outsiders to own property at all. On the other end of the spectrum are countries actively trying to attract foreign buyers, offering enviable incentives like affordable home prices, a smoother customs process, and an easier-to-navigate work-visa approval process.

Choosing the right location can make moving abroad much less intimidating. Ahead, we’re breaking down the ten best countries for Americans looking to buy property abroad. Whether you’re dreaming of relocating or searching for your perfect vacation home, these countries make buying as easy as possible and offer gorgeous landscapes too.

If you’ve ever dreamed of living among medieval architecture and rolling mountain ranges, Georgia might be the key to unlocking your fantasy. A bridge between Eastern Europe and Western Asia, the transcontinental country borders Russia, Turkey, Armenia, and the Black Sea, offering both subtropical and high-altitude climates. Thankfully, the real estate landscape is much less complex, with few legal or bureaucratic hurdles for American buyers, no residency or visa required (for up to a year), and remarkably fast processing times. In fact, the only major restriction in the Georgia market is that foreigners cannot purchase or own agricultural land, a nod to the importance of the local, small-scale farming cultures peppered across the country’s regions.

Uruguay’s buying process could not be more straightforward. South America’s second-smallest sovereign country requires no citizenship, visa, or prior approvals. Under Uruguayan law, foreign buyers are given the same rights as citizens and enjoy the benefits of free capital flow. Locals are just as, if not more, tourist-friendly. The country is known for its hospitality, laid-back café culture, and stunning coastal landscapes.

Instead of retiring to Florida, consider this balmy, beautiful Caribbean country—just a four-hour direct flight from the New York area. Like everything else on the island, the real estate laws are relatively relaxed, making buying a house easy-breezy; and you can buy and own property without obtaining residency status or special permissions, as is the case in many other Caribbean islands. “Foreigners can own property outright in the Dominican Republic, with legal protections for foreign investors,” says Mauricio Umansky, CEO and founder of The Agency. One important consideration is financing: You can certainly get a mortgage in the DR, but it may not be quite as cushy as what you can get in the U.S.—so pay cash if possible.

Great news if you’ve been wanting a more provincial life: Americans face zero restrictions when buying homes in France. The open market allows for statesiders to buy houses, apartments, or plots of land in the city, countryside, and coastal towns in person or remotely without needing citizenship, residency, or even a local visa. However, a purchase does not grant residency, so if you’re hoping to make France your permanent home, you’ll want to apply for a separate long-stay visa and note the Schengen 90-out-of-180-day rule. While buying may be simple, the finances are anything but. Closing fees, including notary fees, typically add seven to ten percent to your purchase. Mortgage rates are also incredibly hard to secure for U.S.-based buyers, thanks to strict debt-to-income caps and FATCA compliance. To simplify the process, many buyers opt to put 20 percent to 40 percent down in cash.

Perhaps the most American-friendly market, Panama boasts a large English-speaking market, virtually no restrictions on foreign property ownership, and uses the U.S. dollar as legal tender in a vastly affordable market. The Central American nation also offers residency programs like the Friendly Nations Visa, clearing the way legally for residency by investment. In addition to an efficient legal system and reliable market, Panama boasts breathtaking beaches, lush rainforests, and exceptional wildlife.

If you’re hoping for a home in the coffee valleys of Colombia, you’ll need to hire a real estate lawyer before you start scouting for mountain chalets, coffee triangle treehouses, and rainforest retreats. The Caribbean country opts out of standardized agent licensing systems, making legal aid essential to verify title and freedom certificates. While citizenship isn’t required, you will need to secure a tourist visa and Colombian Tax ID to complete your purchase and register for property taxes.

The buying process in Greece is lengthy, though it is streamlined, with a clear checklist for American buyers to find success in the market. Hiring a local lawyer or notary is a great first step after scouting properties. Not only are they familiar with title checks and contracts, but they can also navigate the intricacies that come with buying in designated border areas or near military zones, which require special clearance for non–EU buyers. While the most popular areas for American buyers, like Athens and Crete, don’t come with heavy legal intricacies, buying any property in Greece will mean securing a Greek Tax Number—a small price to pay for cities with such rich histories and breathtaking seaside views.

The people in this popular Western European coastal country are famously friendly and inviting, and that extends to real estate. According to Samira Easton, a certified international property specialist with Premier Sotheby’s International Realty, Portugal offers a Golden Visa Program, which makes it simple to buy property and gain residency; clear real estate laws; and a well-established network of English-speaking real estate agents, lawyers, and notaries to help you during the process.

Belize is home to one of the most vibrant, tourist-friendly cultures in Central America, mixing Mayan, Hispanic, Asian, and Creole cultures in its dense rainforests and rolling low plains. Buying here is as easy as it can get, as outright ownership applies to foreigners, and a British-inspired legal system abolishes language barriers and makes contracts easier to understand. Even more, there is no capital gains tax in Belize, and property taxes trend low. The country also boasts vibrant marine life and is home to the world’s second-largest barrier reef.

Spain may have ended its investor visa program in 2025, but the real estate market is still remarkably easy for Americans to navigate. The country currently imposes no restrictions on foreign or nonresident property ownership, but there are a few things you’ll need to procure before you buy, like a Foreigner Identification Number, which is mandatory for taxes. Despite the minor hurdles, the market is booming both on the ground and online. “There are also numerous online resources and property portals available, so it’s easy to find listings and information,” Easton says. This is a boon for those who aren’t able to make the trip over to Europe to house shop in person.Note that buying property does not grant you residency or bypass the country’s 90-day Schengen limit. If you’re hoping to turn your Spanish holiday into your everyday, you’ll have to apply for a separate visa.
