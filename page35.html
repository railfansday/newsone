Disney's Legendary 218-Episode Sci-Fi Series Is the Perfect Anti-'Lanterns' Binge

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

All 218 episodes of this classic sci-fi mystery series are available to stream for free

The fate of Lanterns season 2, and the DC Universe in general, has never been murkier. With the sudden ousting of DC Studios' co-CEOs James Gunn and Peter Safran, the status of HBO's hit show series about two mismatched Green Lanterns who get paired together to solve an intergalactic mystery is up in the air like never before. Season 1 was a critical hit that captured the zeitgeist, and Lanterns co-creator Chris Mundy had already assembled a season 2 writers' room. But in a recent interview, Mundy said the odds of the series continuing were likely "50/50.”

That's not great, but thankfully, there's a perfect replacement waiting in the wings that flips the Lanterns formula on its head — and it's got an almost unlimited supply of episodes. That show is The X-Files, and even though it also features aliens and a pair of odd-couple cops, it couldn’t be more different.

Lanterns was a bold new take on DC Comics’ various Green Lantern heroes. While the character has most often been associated with grand, operatic space adventures, Lanterns was a grittier, more grounded affair. Hal Jordan (Kyle Chandler) is the cocky, go-with-your-gut veteran superhero who was selected to be Earth’s first Green Lantern, part of a universal protection force whose members use powerful green rings that can generate pretty much whatever the wearer can imagine out of green light.

Tired of Jordan’s rule-breaking behavior, the Guardians — the alien beings who oversee the Green Lantern Corps — select serious-minded ex-Marine John Stewart (Aaron Pierre) to become Hal’s partner, which is really just a ruse to have Stewart replace Jordan. Together, the two investigate a deadly incident in the small town of Rushville, Nebraska.

The X-Files also starts out with a bit of a ruse. The show is about a pair of FBI agents who investigate cases involving paranormal phenomena. The cases are referred to as “the X-Files” and they’ve become the obsession of Agent Fox Mulder (David Duchovny), who has a strong belief in aliens and other supernatural elements. Prior to the events of the series, Mulder had been a hotshot criminal profiler in the FBI, and the higher–ups at the agency are now looking to bring him back into the fold by giving him a partner who will debunk his work on the X-Files. They select Agent Dana Scully (Gillian Anderson), a trained medical doctor who believes in hard science, and is wildly skeptical about the wilder elements of Mulder's work.

For both Lanterns and The X-Files — and pretty much all media about mismatched cops — the two partners gain a begrudging respect for each other. In Lanterns, Jordan and Stewart come to admire one another's bravery and sense of honor. On The X-Files, Mulder learns to appreciate the tough questions Scully asks, while Scully comes to realize that Mulder’s theories and beliefs aren’t quite as wild as she’d originally thought. Mulder and Scully also develop a romantic relationship over time, but that takes several seasons to come to fruition and it’s done really organically.

While I realize that I’m pointing out a lot of the similarities between the two shows, they have vastly different viewing experiences. Lanterns is just eight episodes and they’re all dedicated to one case. In contrast, The X-Files ran for 11 seasons, totaling 218 episodes. While the show has a central mythology that extends through the series, the vast majority of episodes are procedural, with new standalone cases every week. The series also covers a wide variety of subject matter. Sometimes it’s about aliens, sometimes it’s about ghosts or urban legends or elements of the occult. There’s even an episode where Mulder is seduced by a vampire. (I recommend you skip that one, since it’s pretty terrible.)

The X-Files also has a very different tone. While Lanterns has a moodiness to it inspired by the likes of True Detective, The X-Files had a lot of variety. The show can be deadly serious in one episode, then lean into more of an action/thriller vibe for the next one. Some episodes are even quite funny. The genre-hopping all works really well too, thanks to the chemistry between Duchovny and Anderson. As wild, or as heavy, as the show gets, their partnership is the show’s anchor and that’s why the show lasted as long as it did and why it became a monster success in the 1990s.

In a way, The X-Files is kind of an inverted mirror of Lanterns. With The X-Files, the FBI agents are the most grounded thing about the show, while the cases themselves were more outlandish. In Lanterns, the Green Lanterns and their power set is the most fantastic element, whereas the case is somewhat more grounded. Even with the Manhunter component, Lanterns is interestingly low to the ground.

Plus, there are no power rings in The X-Files, which is a pretty big thing in Lanterns.

The X-Files is available to stream on Pluto TV, Hulu, and Disney Plus.

Samsung Gallery now syncs with Google to back up your photos

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The company ended Gallery's Microsoft OneDrive integration back in September.

If you've ever owned a Samsung phone, you've most likely seen your device prompt you to back up your media to OneDrive at least once in the past. Back in September, however, Samsung removed its Gallery app's ability to sync with Microsoft OneDrive. Well, the phonemaker was likely cooking up another partnership behind the scenes, because it has just announced that its Gallery app can now sync with your Google account.

In the past, if you wanted to back up your photos and videos to your Google storage on a Samsung phone, you've had to use Google Photos instead of (or in addition to) your device's native Gallery app. With this update, you can stick to Samsung's Gallery app if you want to.The feature is currently only available on select Galaxy devices running One UI 9, Samsung's latest custom Android interface, but the company says it will roll out to more devices in phases. To enable the feature, tap the Cloud icon in Gallery or go to Gallery Settings and switch on Sync photos and videos.

Since the feature syncs media files to your Google account, you'll be able to access them in the Gallery app of another Samsung Galaxy device. Simply log into the same account on your other device and enable sync on it, as well. You'll then be able to see previews of your synced photos and videos on that device. If you want to access the original files, just select and download them. In case you want to do the opposite and remove files from your phone or tablet after they've been synced to Google storage, you can use Samsung's other new feature. It offers a Free up space option in the Sync photos and videos settings, which deletes original files from your device while keeping thumbnails visible in the Gallery app.

It's worth noting that Gallery sync will take up storage space on your Google account. Each Google account comes with 5GB of free storage by default, which you can expand to 15GB if you verify a valid phone number. If you want or need more than that, since that storage is shared across Google's services, you'll have to purchase a Google One subscription that will set you back $2 per month for 100GB of storage or $3 for 200GB.

The top three positions on the CCPI remained vacant again this year, as no country has yet done enough to meet global climate goals aimed at preventing worsening weather extremes and climate damage.

"We are seeing a striking contrast," said Burck. "Progress on climate action is often slow and in some countries, such as the United States, things are even moving backwards. But technologically and economically there is enormous momentum."

How AI worsens online violence against Middle East women

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Women in the Middle East already have to deal with an above-average share of online harassment and violence. The proliferation of AI-enabled tools is making it worse.

It happens all the time, says Kholoud al-Ameri, an Iraqi journalist and the editor of a news website.

"Female journalists here are often getting messages, and mostly from men," she explains. "They somehow find you online and then they say they just want to talk or they want to add you on Instagram or Facebook," says al-Ameri, who also heads a network of female journalists working on climate change stories. "Sometimes it's hard because the men say they're our readers and we don't want to discourage them. But I usually tell the women just to block these guys."

Syrian activist Yaman Amer tells of similar experiences as a result of writing about women's rights online — she says she gets hundreds of critical comments. "When I talk about women's rights online in a patriarchal society, that's just how things are," Amer, who is studying to be a lawyer in Damascus, told DW. "Not that I accept it. Out of every thousand offensive comments, there's usually one or two that make me feel like I have to report them because they're particularly vile. As in, they're extremely vulgar or threatening. And then it's more than just harassment. It's on a whole other level, humiliating and degrading."

Online harassment of women isn't unusual anywhere. Studies by a variety of organizations show that somewhere between 40% and 60% of all women worldwide have experienced it, while 65% have witnessed it.

There is no single standard for assessing the level of online harassment because surveys often use different types of questions or research methods. But in general, women around the Middle East appear to be dealing with a higher-than-global-average level of what is known as "technology facilitated gender-based violence," or TFGBV.

The United Nations Population Fund defines TFGBV as a harmful act "that is committed, assisted, aggravated or amplified ... by the use of information communication technologies," or other digital tools, "against a person on the basis of their gender."

According to research by UN Women, the UN body dedicated to gender equality, 68% of women in the Middle East have had to deal with it, while almost all — close to 100% — have seen it online.

In a 2025 study, UN Women found that 49% of women using the internet in the Middle East don't feel safe from online harassment. The number goes up to almost 70% if the women are more publicly visible activists, journalists or politicians.

And it is already clear that the increased availability of artificial intelligence-powered applications is making online harassment worse.

AI has the potential to supercharge this kind of abuse, the UN Women briefing points out, "both by facilitating the spread of targeted disinformation as well as the proliferation of image-based abuse and deepfake pornographic videos."

Of the women who had experienced online violence while working, around a quarter had already identified "abuse that was generated or amplified by AI tools," the briefing said.

The potential for using the latest technology in order to harass people online has always been there, says Siddharth Venkataramakrishnan, an analyst at the London-based Institute for Strategic Dialogue (ISD), which investigates extremism. "But it's just become easier to produce harmful content at scale and in a hyper-targeted way. And the production of non-consensual imagery is a real marker of the democratization of that technology, so it's moving away from primarily targeting celebrities to just regular people."

Venkataramakrishnan has been researching incidents that occurred at the beginning of 2026 when Grok, the AI-powered chatbot belonging to Elon Musk's company SpaceXAI, became able to alter photographs to depict a fully dressed subject as nearly naked. The scandal caused Grok's services to be restricted but, as Venkataramakrishnan pointed out, safeguards across different AI models tend to be inconsistent and users can still find work-arounds to get similar results.

In conservative cultures in the Middle East, the threat of this kind of half-naked picture of a woman being published has serious real-life implications, mostly because of what is and isn't acceptable for women around the region.

What makes the increase in AI-generated fakes even more problematic in the Middle East is that online abuse also has a higher chance of having offline effects.

"Norms related to honor, family reputation and male control can turn digital abuse into real-world consequences," a June briefing by the World Bank notes.

It's also often not easy for people targeted with this kind of abuse to find legal protection or even somewhere to report it. While some Middle Eastern countries have cybercrime laws, most don't specifically address gender-based online violence nor have they been updated to grapple with AI-related abuse.

Last year's UN Women survey found that 36% of women said they were just told to ignore the online violence, while 23% were blamed for it themselves and 12% were subjected to physical punishment by their families because of it.

Other studies indicate that only about 7% of women who experienced technology-facilitated violence later report it to law enforcement. Various concerns stop them from doing so, including having to present sexualized images or answer personal questions in front of strangers — often also anathema to many women in the region.

The women who had experienced online harassment said they would often just block the perpetrators and around a quarter said they then became more careful about what they posted online. Twenty percent deleted their accounts or left social media altogether.

The rapid growth in AI-enabled harassment can also be more problematic in the Middle East because of the situations the women targeted find themselves living in.

Displacement, war, conflict and instability can all make women more vulnerable to online abuse and exploitation. Even the rapid development of AI-related technologies in a country without corresponding legal guardrails or digital literacy can increase the risk.

For example, observers point out that Saudi Arabia, a generally conservative country with strict rules for female behavior, is going through an accelerated program of digital transformation.

And in Syria, after the ousting of the dictatorial Assad regime, many Syrians "rushed into social media to speak freely," journalist Zaina Erhaim wrote for the Arab Reform Initiative in July this year.

Previously. digital polices had been closely policed by Syria's dictator and a political post could see the writer thrown in jail. But now Syrians "are navigating this newfound liberty without guardrails, oversight or protection," Erhaim argues, and female activists are paying a high price for it, especially where "gender intersects with sect."

In a policy paper published in May, the Syrian Women's Political Movement explained just how bad online abuse had become. In focus groups with 120 women from all around Syria, the majority of participants agreed that digital abuse had become a way of shutting them up. Younger activists reported personal pictures were being stolen from social media, then used against them.

"Smear campaigns targeting women's 'honor' and systemic defamation are actively deployed as punitive measures against any women who attempt to express a critical political opinion," the organization wrote, arguing that what was needed was a law that criminalized digital violence.
