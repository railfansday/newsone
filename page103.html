How the New Urbanism took on sprawl and won

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s 1996. You just moved to a suburb of Atlanta, to a roomy house on a quiet street. Every morning at dawn, you kiss your family goodbye, and then hop in your car to drive 35 miles to your office in the city. It takes more than an hour to get there. 

You have plenty of company on the road at that hour, as you’re one of the more than 600,000 people who moved to the northern outskirts of Atlanta over the last six years. Between the traffic and constant construction, the swelling suburbs are starting to show their fault lines.

Its rapid growth during the 1990s makes Atlanta the quintessential story of suburban growth gone awry, though it is far from alone. Between 1950 and 1990, the share of Americans who lived in suburbs jumped from 23% to 46%. Decades of post-World War II expansion had transformed the American landscape from one built around small-scale neighborhood development and center city density to tabula rasa growth on the edge. Families moved to cul-de-sac subdivisions spread across car-dependent exurbs. These places were not so much cities as they were lily pads of domesticity, linked together by steadily growing interstate highways.

For many Americans, a suburban lifestyle was an outward signal of success: big house, good schools, safe streets. But that wasn’t the case for everyone. In the 1990s, at the peak of suburban sprawl, a small but vocal group of detractors began to take shape. Comprising progressive urban planners, designers, architects, and policymakers, this group coalesced around the idea that there should be an alternative way to live—one that embraced the charms of small town centers, the convenience of walkable neighborhoods, and the diversity of mixed-use and mixed-income districts. 

These people would come to be known as the New Urbanists.

In 1996, the New Urbanists wrote a 27-point charter that laid out its core principles for better city planning. At a gathering in Charleston, South Carolina, a line of 262 people ceremoniously walked up onto a stage and signed their names to the charter, legitimizing once-fringe ideas as a new kind of rule book for neighborhood design and city building. Signatories included architects, urban planners, transportation engineers, and government officials, from unknown environmental activists all the way up to the U.S. secretary of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

Over the past 30 years, the charter and its principles of planning and design have gone on to radically change the face of cities and communities across the U.S. and around the world. New Urbanism has grown into a nonprofit organization, the Congress for the New Urbanism, and a movement that’s changed the way cities are planned and built, upending rigid zoning laws, eliminating parking minimums, and bringing walkability back into the hearts of communities. 

The objectives of New Urbanism still resonate today, and have been gathering new currency in the face of worsening housing affordability and growing disappointment with mainstream urban design priorities. The abundance movement, inspired by Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson’s book Abundance, calls for a proactive approach to economic and social development that can affect everything from the supply of housing to renewable energy access. Similarly, a recent push by Stripe’s cofounder, Patrick Collison, and the libertarian economist Tyler Cowen aims to spur the creation of “new aesthetics” that can change the course of cities and the architecture within them. Post-Great Recession, post-COVID-19 pandemic, there is an evident hunger for change.

Anyone now arguing for reimagining cities can take lessons from how New Urbanism emerged and rose to prominence. Three decades after the historic charter signing, Fast Company spoke with the people who were most involved in creating the New Urbanism movement—all still alive, all still working—about how such an unlikely crusade took hold. In a rare retrospective, they look back on how their movement took shape, and the legacies of sprawl that New Urbanism is still fighting today.

Roots of a design movement

The New Urbanism didn’t appear out of nowhere. Its roots grew out of a mix of disillusionment and optimism that started taking shape in the 1970s. At that time, a small group of young planners and architects were starting to feel fed up with the built environment. They believed that many of the social and environmental issues cities faced were the result of development patterns that prioritized economic returns over the experiences of the people who lived there. 

With highways expanding to reach new subdivisions and new subdivisions arising alongside new stretches of highway, the traditional logic of urbanization was being supplanted by a perverse pattern of growth for the sake of growth. How people lived in these places, what they did there, and why they’d even want to be there were seemingly irrelevant. The environmental costs of populating these areas and keeping them going were afterthoughts at best.

A group of young designers believed development needed a more comprehensive, interdisciplinary, and thoughtful approach, and they set out to show how it could work. They included the Miami-based urban planners Andrés Duany and Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, the Los Angeles-based architects Elizabeth Moule and Stefanos Polyzoides, and a regional planner in California named Peter Calthorpe. Now all in their 70s and 80s, their paths began crossing after coming out of various architecture and design programs in the 1970s and working as architects and planners across the country.

“We began practicing when the collapse of suburbia became evident. The promise of suburbia, which was plenty of green, plenty of parking, highways that really work, was betrayed,” says Duany, cofounder of Duany Plater-Zyberk & Co. (now DPZ CoDesign). “You didn’t get nature. You got a whole bunch of traffic congestion in commuting, and all the great parking fields were miserable places. So there was a feeling of betrayal in the air and we were able to perceive it.”

After studying under the influential architectural scholar Vincent Scully, Duany and Plater-Zyberk graduated from Yale University in 1974 and moved to Miami, where they cofounded the architecture firm Arquitectonica, and then, in 1980, Duany Plater-Zyberk & Co., a firm focused on counteracting suburban sprawl. 

Calthorpe had also studied at Yale but left in the mid-’70s before graduating to work under the California architect Sim Van der Ryn, who in 1975 was appointed state architect by Governor Jerry Brown. During Brown’s administration, Calthorpe worked on a new plan for downtown Sacramento built around a burgeoning light rail system, an early example of transit-oriented development, which would become Calthorpe’s specialty.

Meanwhile, Moule and Polyzoides had started an architecture firm in Southern California centered on bringing traditional architectural approaches to housing beyond the typical single family home, designing courtyard homes and clusters of housing that would, decades later, become known as “missing middle” housing.

“All of us were working on parallel paths from the mid-’70s on,” Calthorpe says.

Those paths began to intersect in the 1980s as the designers’ alternative takes on urban and suburban development garnered more attention. An early standout was Seaside, a Gulf Coast resort town in Florida planned by Duany and Plater-Zyberk and founded in 1981. The pastel-toned walkable village-like development, later made famous as the set of the 1998 film The Truman Show, is considered the first New Urbanist community. 

“We were architects who were networking. There was a lot of travel to give lectures in the world of architecture about the latest things people were working on. And we realized there were people around the country that were heading in the same direction,” says Plater-Zyberk.

In 1986, Calthorpe and Van der Ryn published a book called Sustainable Communities, which outlined emerging elements in the planning and urban design fields that were changing the shape of cities. It included case studies of places that were designed to reduce resource consumption and lessen reliance on cars, as well as a focus on utopian-leaning new town development. Later, Calthorpe was teaching a course on transit-oriented development at the University of California, Berkeley, with the architect and urban designer Dan Solomon, who would later become one of the founders of New Urbanism, and they invited Duany to participate in a symposium on new forms of suburban development.

Slowly, the individual and varied work of these practitioners would intermingle. “We were working on different sides of the same coin,” Calthorpe says.

The founders coalesce

By the beginning of the 1990s, the future founders of the New Urbanism movement joined forces. Their first real meeting was around a plate of pasta.

During Calthorpe’s time working under Governor Brown, California had enacted new energy efficiency and building standards, and created a statewide organization called the Local Government Commission to spread best building practices out to all the jurisdictions across the state. Run by Judy Corbett, a pioneer in sustainable development, the commission had a role that was interpreted broadly. Around 1990, Corbett saw an opportunity to tie energy efficiency to larger neighborhood planning, land use, and transportation issues.

“Judy called me up and said, ‘I want to get some design principles that are clear and firm, but something I can pass around to elected officials throughout the state of California,’” Calthorpe recalls. Duany and Plater-Zyberk were in California working alongside Moule and Polyzoides on the Los Angeles redevelopment project Playa Vista, so Corbett and Calthorpe organized a meeting. They saw the chance to use California’s push for energy efficient buildings as an opening to get more people thinking about ways growth and development should change. “We all met in Judy’s kitchen in Davis, California, and had a pasta dinner and talked about shaping some kind of movement,” Calthorpe says.

“I don’t know that we thought so much as being a rebellion as much as feeling like we were profound outsiders and the forces in front of us that were creating the cities that we live in were remarkable,” Moule adds.

This led to Corbett organizing a Local Government Commission-led conference at Yosemite National Park’s historic Ahwahnee hotel in 1991, bringing together architects, planners, transportation engineers, government officials, and policymakers from across California. Calthorpe, Duany, Plater-Zyberk, Moule, and Polyzoides helped drive the discussion.

Moule recalls that during the meeting they created a rudimentary series of tenets called the Ahwahnee Principles that helped define their thinking at different scales, from the building block to the street, neighborhood, district, and region. The principles called for planning “complete and integrated communities,” with a variety of activities, housing types, and jobs, and designs that conserve resources and minimize waste. 

“We knew that if we put a little bit more meat on the bones and fleshed them out a little bit more, and then gathered people together to see what they thought about it, then we might be able to get somewhere,” Moule says.

The group agreed that the Ahwahnee Principles should be considered a starting point for a large-scale rethinking of the developer-led, piecemeal urban development patterns of the previous decades. “We were talking about it and saying, you know, these principles are right but they’re not going to get us there. We need more detail. We need to be more specific,” Plater-Zyberk says. “It was one of the things that pushed us to make our own group that was urban design oriented, with greater detail or depth than planning and much more specific goals.”

Around this time, Duany was having discussions with the architect Léon Krier. A harsh critic of modernism, Krier was the future master planner behind the New Urbanist town of Poundbury, England, initiated in the late 1980s by Prince Charles (now King Charles III). Krier, who died in 2025 at 79, impressed on Duany how modernist architects had had such a deep influence on 20th century architecture and urban planning through a formal organization called Congrès Internationaux d’Architecture Moderne (CIAM), founded in 1928. He suggested the anti-sprawl movement needed its own formal organization.

“I was aware of just a few colleagues that were on the same page. And we basically called them,” Duany says.

Calthorpe says Duany proposed a group that would “repeal and replace” CIAM. “They had congresses and they issued a charter. So we thought, well, we’re going to do exactly the same thing. We can just cut and paste, take out the bad shit and put in the good,” Calthorpe says.

New Urbanism’s first congresses

Around 1992, Duany, Plater-Zyberk, Moule, Polyzoides, and Calthorpe gathered in New York, along with a planner named Peter Katz, who was working on a book about new approaches to urban planning and design (The New Urbanism: Toward an Architecture of Community, published in 1994). The six discussed the overlapping intentions of their design and planning work, and what it would look like to bring a more interdisciplinary approach to creating a more livable urban environment.

“We started with nothing, just fumes,” says Polyzoides, cofounder of Moule & Polyzoides Architects and Urbanists, and now dean of architecture at the University of Notre Dame. “There was so much to learn. There was so much to unveil, really, because the subject is so deep.”

From their work, they knew that reimagining the built environment would be a complicated undertaking. Interrelated forces shape the world as we know it, from land use planning to neighborhood design to traffic engineering to natural resource consumption. Making wholesale change hinged on understanding and reforming how all those interrelated pieces fit together. They walked away from the meeting with a plan to break everything down to three scales: the regional, the neighborhood, and the building. They also came up with a name for themselves: New Urbanists.

Following the model of CIAM, they decided to hold a series of congresses, or concept-focused meetings where the group could collectively debate exactly how each scale of city-making should be redirected. With the Congress for the New Urbanism formally established, Duany and Plater-Zyberk led the organization of the first congress in 1993, focused on the neighborhood level and set inside a columned meeting hall in Alexandria, Virginia, called the Lyceum. Each of the founders invited people to participate. 

“It was really coming out of our Rolodexes. That I even said Rolodex probably gives you an idea of the time frame,” Moule says. “Instead of it being sort of a board meeting, we decided to make it a larger call to arms.”

About 200 people were invited, including planners, landscape architects, traffic engineers, civic activists, and developers. People made presentations about their experience and projects. “It started out as a very diversely disciplined group who were all working on the same kinds of issues,” Plater-Zyberk says.

The next congress, held in 1994, was organized by Moule and Polyzoides at the Biltmore Hotel in downtown Los Angeles, and focused on the building block and the street. “That was quite provocative at the time, largely because Los Angeles was known for being the home of the automobile and the home of suburban sprawl,” Moule says. “It was sort of a stick-it-in-your-eye kind of conference.”

Calthorpe and Solomon organized the third congress in San Francisco in 1995, focused on the regional level, with an emphasis on their work planning transit-oriented development projects. The founders cast an even wider net for attendees, inviting participants from groups like AARP, the Urban Land Institute, the Trust for Public Land, and the Natural Resources Defense Council, as well as heads of departments of transportation, mayors, and government officials. “Unlike CIAM, which was only architects, we really wanted to bring in every last person that we thought had some kind of say in how the urban environment was created and made, so it was pretty diverse group,” Moule says. “It might have had 1,500 people. So there was momentum.”

Organized like conventional conferences, these three congresses had the typical hallmarks of presentations, panels, post-meeting dinners, and post-dinner parties. But unlike most conferences, where the event ends and everyone goes home, attendees were trying to build a movement—and set a new standard for neighborhood design and city building.

“People were doing things and coming back and talking to each other about the way they did it,” Polyzoides says. “We were constructing the theory out of experience, not constructing it out of theory.”

A charter signed, and an early win

New Urbanism’s fourth congress was held on May 4, 1996, in a rented hall in Charleston, South Carolina. The concepts and ideas generated through the previous three annual gatherings had been consolidated down into 27 principles, each roughly 25 to 50 words, and broken into three categories:

  • The metropolis, city, and town
  • The neighborhood, the district, and the corridor
  • The block, the street, and the building. 

The charter, written primarily by the founders, was presented to the invited audience. With a large white paper mounted on an easel in the center of the stage, the attendees were invited to add their signatures to adopt the charter. Though there are no records of how many people were in attendance that day, 262 signed the charter and New Urbanism became an official movement.

“It was signed dramatically. Everybody walked up on stage to sign it,” Plater-Zyberk says.

The charter signing was considered a starting point for New Urbanism, and an opening of the invitation-only organization to a broader membership. And with so much of the American landscape dominated by disconnected concentrations of single family homes or office parks or big box retail clusters, people were ready to get behind an alternative. 

By the end of 1996, CNU would claim more than 500 official members. Attendance numbers would rise with each annual congress, as did interest in the organization’s anti-sprawl sentiment. The organization’s openness and its challenges to the status quo brought attention that grew into a movement.

“We do not protect the ideas or even the use of the term New Urbanism. It’s totally freeware,” Duany says. “You can’t just get a Harvard degree. You can’t just get LEED [Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design] certification. Well, we say these are the principles called New Urbanism, don’t mangle it and get on with it.”

Sounds straightforward, but the principles called for a widespread rethinking of the accepted practices of the day, and systemic changes in the financial infrastructure of real estate development. “Segregated land use was something that was so baked into the system. You had to change how appraisals were done. You had to change how banks lent on buildings,” Moule says. “They wouldn’t lend you something if you wanted to put retail on the ground floor and housing or offices on top. It just didn’t happen.”

Some were skeptical of New Urbanism from the start. Writing in Harvard Design Magazine in 1997, the urban geographer and Marxist scholar David Harvey called out the movement as naive, arguing that it “connects to a facile contemporary attempt to transform large and teeming cities, so seemingly out of control, into an interlinked series of ‘urban villages’ where, it is believed, everyone can relate in a civil and urbane fashion to everyone else.” The head of Harvard’s Department of Urban Planning and Design in the late 1990s and early 2000s, Alex Krieger, was another critic of the movement and its emphasis on designing new communities with traditional styles instead of fixing the communities that already existed.

Not long after its official birth, New Urbanism got the chance to do just that. The HUD secretary Henry Cisneros, the highest profile signatory of the Charter of the New Urbanism, ended up enabling one of the movement’s biggest successes through the HOPE VI program. This was a HUD effort that launched in 1992 to undertake a large-scale redevelopment and revitalization of the country’s public housing projects. Throughout the late 1990s and early 2000s, CNU members and the nonprofit CNU organization itself worked directly with HUD to establish the design guidelines for the program, transforming decaying public housing barracks and towers into mixed-income neighborhoods.

The work spread across more than 260 sites in cities like Atlanta, Boston, Los Angeles, Pittsburgh, San Francisco, Charlotte, North Carolina, and Washington, D.C., and resulted in the redesign of 100,000 units of public housing. Decaying housing towers and bleak low-rise housing clusters were replaced with walkable neighborhoods that blended into their context and erased the line between public housing and the rest of the city. “Most of the members of the early CNU were very involved,” says Calthorpe. “That was really the beauty of the thing. Unlike the architects who are constantly competing with each other, we just wanted to collaborate.”

Research led by Harvard University’s Raj Chetty and released in 2026 found that children growing up in the neighborhoods revitalized through the HOPE VI program earned more money as adults, were more likely to attend college, and were less likely to be incarcerated.

“HOPE VI made policy of the idea that the design of community can have a profound influence on people’s lives,” says Mallory Baches, the current president of CNU. “It takes 30 years to do a longitudinal study on the effects of those projects, but 30 years later we have the evidence that that early influence made a big difference.”

Edging into the mainstream

Over time, many of the main principles of New Urbanism have steadily moved from the fringe of city planning and development to the mainstream. Mixed use and higher density development, and the zoning reform they tended to require, have become commonplace policies in cities across the U.S. Walkable town centers, infill development, alternative transportation, an emphasis on local economies, form-based codes, and transit-oriented development are all normal elements of many cities and their approach to growth and development. (The movement has even spawned a partly serious progressive urbanist online community called New Urbanist Memes for Transit-Oriented Teens.)

“There’s been enormous success,” says Polyzoides. “It is impossible to imagine that in a continental country like ours, you could move within a generation from people doing crap everywhere to doing the right thing on so many fronts when it comes to urbanism.”

New Urbanism and the CNU continue to affect city planning, urban development, policy, and governance, but the movement hasn’t risen to CIAM’s level of modernist influence on the shape and form of the built environment, nor has it thoroughly changed de facto development industry practices.

“New Urbanism has had a big effect, but there’s still the large companies that are buying land to build a single unit-type subdivision in the old-fashioned way,” says Plater-Zyberk. “And there’s too much of that going on. But under the current affordability crisis, in some cases, people are trying to make more of that happen.”

Calthorpe argues New Urbanism is essentially a progressive movement, with roots in the counterculture of the 1960s, and its modest uptake in the planning and development world has a lot to do with the politics of the current era. “In the end, sprawl just kept happening and we were more of a footnote or counterpoint. We never became normative in America,” he says.

One challenge that’s dogged the movement is its aesthetic lean. Style has always been a lightning rod within New Urbanism, with some embracing the movement’s goals but balking at its tendency toward classical or neotraditional architectural approaches. Plater-Zyberk recalls the issue of style being one of the biggest points of contention ahead of the charter signing back in 1996. “That was a very sensitive point,” she says. 

This aesthetic approach has recently taken on political undertones, as President Donald Trump has controversially made traditional architecture the preferred style for federal buildings.

Polyzoides, who is dean of one of the most traditionally-focused American architecture schools, rejects any connection between New Urbanism and Trump’s architectural choices, calling them an aberration. “What ties this particular president to the Mussolinis and the others in the world is they see classical architecture as an opportunity to exert power. But what classical architecture is about at its source is an architecture of community,” he says. “It’s an architecture of common expression, common ideals. So, an architecture of democracy.”

The future of the New Urbanism movement may hinge on larger concerns, and on bringing younger people into the fold. Baches, who now leads the organization, says she’s focused on helping younger generations engage with the principles and ideals set out by the founding generation of New Urbanists, many now at the tail end of their careers.

In 2023, CNU initiated a process of amending the original Charter of the New Urbanism. The amended charter now includes principles around increasing diversity, equity, and inclusion; countering involuntary displacement; ensuring the attainability of housing; and contextualizing the built environment’s role in climate change. These new principles, she argues, will keep the movement relevant into the future, while also making the output of New Urbanism—a better alternative to sprawl—even more attractive.

“Communities across the country have so much more of an appetite for great urbanism, but there’s a limited supply. And until we have created more, or adapted sprawl into better urbanism, the attainability of living the kind of quality life that great urbanism can provide is going to be only available to those who can afford it,” she says. “We need to expand access to that great urbanism.”

CNU’s 35th congress will take place in June.

This new software maps out a better bike network for your city

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In most cities, bike networks are rolled out in disconnected pieces. A bike lane on one street might not reach the next part of a route, so you can’t necessarily ride safely across town. A new set of software tools helps planners, bike advocates, and citizens design more complete networks, automatically filling the gaps in existing infrastructure or creating the whole thing from scratch.

Called BikeNetKit, the free, open-source software is based on eight years of research on bicycle infrastructure at Denmark’s IT University of Copenhagen.

When you type in a city name, the software pulls street data from OpenStreetMap, including a map of existing bike lanes. (Getting started requires a little technical knowledge: You have to know how to use Python, and then have to enter a few lines to install and run the algorithms.)

“Planners mostly think about the small picture—what if I add a lane here? What’s on the right side and left side? But they don’t think about the network and the whole city,” says Michael Szell, an associate professor at the IT University of Copenhagen, who led the project. “Our methods are built on network science methods that look at the city from a big picture.”

One of the tools, called GrowBikeNet, can build a network from scratch—particularly useful in the U.S., where most cities have sparse bike lanes. The algorithm creates a grid of “seed points” over a city. It connects those points to any existing bike infrastructure, drawing a new map of suggested bike routes.

“The first links that are suggested are the links where you expect the most cyclists to ride,” Szell says. Rolling it out this way would create a functional network from the outset, with strong connections already in place.

Users can upload additional data to help plan a network—for example, where traffic crashes have occurred in the past, or where people typically travel each day. The software also gives the option to plan bike lanes around schools. Another tool, called Superblockify, suggests places to add car-free neighborhoods; LinkBikeNet connects gaps in otherwise well-connected networks, while FixBikeNet tells cities which gaps are most important to fix first.

The tools don’t specify what the bike infrastructure should look like. Instead of bike lanes at the side of the road, for example, some streets could be dedicated solely to people on bikes and pedestrians. Of course, that’s the opposite of most transportation planning now, which focuses on the flow of cars. “Planning for cycling is always some kind of afterthought,” says Szell.

The BikeNetKit website includes hundreds of analyses of European cities, from growing a new bike network in Kyiv, Ukraine, to fixing gaps in Copenhagen’s network, to potential car-free neighborhoods in Berlin. To try it out yourself, the open-source code is available on GitHub.

How Zohran Mamdani is convincing New York’s capitalists to give a little socialism a chance

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zohran Mamdani has not, it turns out, attempted to seize the means of production. At least not yet.

During his mayoral campaign, you couldn’t throw a stone without hitting a CEO worried about the effect he’d have on the city’s economy. After his first year in office, business is proceeding as usual, which—New Yorkers being New Yorkers—means that everyone is feeling angsty.

“I was very concerned when he was elected,” says Neil Blumenthal, cofounder and co-CEO of the nearly $3 billion eyewear company Warby Parker, headquartered in New York. Now Blumenthal is waiting to see what the most senior member of the Democratic Socialists of America in the city, if not the country, will do.

“The DSA’s official policies . . . include things like government takeover of private companies, increasing income taxes, and abolishing the police. Publicly, he’s said some things to moderate those positions,” Blumenthal says. “[But] at times he’s advocating for some of those policies, which are deeply unpopular and aren’t going to lead to growth and prosperity.”

Still, 15 chief executives have kept open enough minds to join the mayor’s Business Advisory Council. The group represents more than 100,000 employees in industries spanning real estate, film, sports, and healthcare.

It includes Hamdi Ulukaya, the billionaire CEO of Chobani, who advocates for immigrants and once played street soccer with the mayor. It also includes numerous doubters and former naysayers. There’s the real estate CEO who gave $250,000 to Mamdani’s mayoral opponent, Andrew Cuomo. The investor who wrote an op-ed saying that Mamdani would bankrupt the city. The former bank CEO who talks on Fox News about “prioritizing capitalism.” The chair of the finance committee of a hospital where Mamdani supported striking workers.

Mamdani is bringing startup culture to government

These titans of industry may not support every facet of the mayor’s ambitious plans, but they’ve signed on to his broad vision of building a better functioning, more affordable New York. Their first meeting, in September, kicked off with an icebreaker about their first jobs, and they discussed a key point of alignment with the mayor’s agenda: The high cost of housing and childcare is making it difficult to hire in New York. (Neither Mamdani nor the council members agreed to be interviewed for this story, though top administration figures happily did.)

“I’ve seen this TV series for a lot of episodes,” says Anthony Shorris, head of the New York City Economic Development Corporation (NYCEDC), the city’s primary economic development vehicle. Shorris has worked alongside business leaders, often as a public servant, since the 1980s. “They love mayors, they get mad at mayors, whatever. At the end of the day, I think what they really care about is the city’s economy being strong.”

Mamdani has brought an undeniable sense of energy and optimism to the job, which had “been missing for quite a while,” says Shorris.

He’s also brought some much-needed elements of startup culture to city government. That includes quick response times, vibrant interactive marketing campaigns, improved city websites, and hands-on servant leadership. He tackled certain small—sometimes symbolic—things right away, such as filling potholes quickly (the city has fixed more than 200,000 so far in 2026, more than it did the entire year prior, under Eric Adams), replacing nets in basketball hoops, offering free opera tickets, creating public viewings for World Cup and Knicks playoff games, providing evening childcare for parents who need a night out, and installing modular public bathrooms.

It’s part of a larger plan. When constituents “feel a greater sense of confidence in government, that gives us more room to tackle even bigger things that people in the past have not thought of as the fundamental basic role of government,” says Julie Su, in the newly titled position of deputy mayor for economic justice.

Even skeptical business leaders can see that Mamdani currently enjoys an eye-popping 69% approval rating. All five of the ballot initiatives he championed——like speeding up bike lane projects and construction—passed handily.

In the aftermath of a generational wave of Democratic victories at every level of government across every part of the country during the midterms (including eight DSA members elected to Congress), it’s hard to generalize about what, exactly, individual voters were responding to. But Mamdani’s relentless focus on affordability was echoed by politicians across the Democratic Party.

And fear that Mamdani’s vocal support of democratic socialism would haunt Democrats was certainly misplaced. Earlier in the year, House Speaker Mike Johnson threatened to make him the face of the national Democratic Party in order to drag down Democratic chances in Middle America. Today, aligning with Mamdani is a PR win, though some leaders will keep queuing up “mad at mayor” episodes no matter what.

But as Mamdani moves past these early victories and begins tackling his more ambitious agenda, the détente that’s existed with the business community since his inauguration is starting to be tested.

His to-do list is long and only fractionally complete: city-owned grocery stores, free childcare for 2-year-olds, faster buses (with a plan to make them free, which would require state-government support), expanded access to union organizing, rent freezes for one- and two-year leases (roughly 1 million units total, about 40% of the city’s rental units), a pathway for citizens to sue businesses or landlords they feel are breaking the law, zoning rules that make it easier to build new (and more affordable) apartments, fewer hurdles and fines around small business operation, and the enforcement of city laws, like winning a recent $131.5 million action against DoorDash for underpaying delivery workers.

And then there are his even grander plans, some of which have a more quintessentially “eat the rich” bent: a “pied-à-terre tax” on expensive second homes in the city (that remains tangled in legal challenges), increased minimum wage, and higher taxes on the wealthy and corporations—all of which require support from a state government less aligned with his vision.

What the city’s CEOs really think about their mayor

The business impact of Mamdani’s agenda won’t be measured in immediate increases to market cap or earnings per share. It will be felt obliquely. Corporations benefit when employees can access affordable housing and childcare and customers have disposable income to purchase their products.

America’s captains of industry know this. Despite the rending of garments before the election last year about New York turning into Soviet Russia, many of them took similar positions not long ago.

The Business Roundtable, comprising some of the largest corporations in America, issued passionate statements in 2019 about the value of stakeholder capitalism, the same year that Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan touted large wage increases for workers and Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff proclaimed that “capitalism as we know it is dead.” Recently, CEO Scott Kirby of United Airlines told The New York Times that “wealth inequality has become a danger to our society, and we need to address that. We should have a national wealth tax.”

After the midterms, other leaders began giving voice to such values. Appearing on CNBC the day after the election, Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi noted: “If you look over the past 20 years, return on capital has increased very, very significantly. You can’t say the same thing about return on labor.” (Continues…)

To pursue his daring agenda, Mamdani has put some ballast in place. In addition to deputy mayor Su, who led the U.S. Department of Labor as acting secretary for two years under President Biden, Mamdani brought on Shorris, a former first deputy mayor under Bill de Blasio and Port Authority executive director, to be the president and CEO of the NYCEDC.

He’s paired with new NYCEDC board chair Lina Khan, the former Federal Trade Commission chair known for finding ways businesses were crushing regular people and regulating them out of existence, making her a bugbear of corporate interests. Khan tells me via email that she’s focused on wielding the NYCEDC’s toolkit to grow “the economy in a way that delivers affordability, widespread prosperity, and real opportunity.”

The next three years—or seven, should Mamdani be reelected—could end up being nothing but a tense standoff. Or they could usher in the most progressive economic intervention since the New Deal. I spoke with members of the administration, business leaders, political insiders, and policy experts carrying out parts of his agenda to answer a fundamental question of his first term: whether the democratic socialism that propelled Mamdani to victory can coexist with the capitalist engine powering the financial heart of America.

Democratic socialism and its deep-pocketed foes

What does it mean, in practice, for Mamdani to be a democratic socialist? It’s tricky: Ask two socialists to define socialism and you’ll get five answers.

Mamdani gave his most digestible definition in October to podcaster Theo Von: “Socialism means you will have whatever you need to live a dignified life—and that it is the government’s job to deliver on that.”

For many, the sentiment has become a rallying cry. Since he became mayor, DSA membership has grown to more than 120,000 nationwide—the New York chapter now includes 15,000 members, more than the entire national organization had in 2016. (Full disclosure: I’m a former member of the San Francisco chapter of DSA and a former union organizer. I’ve also been a business editor for more than a decade—a living embodiment of the tension I’m writing about here.)

Mamdani has tapped into a sense that socialism “really cares about excellence,” says Corey Robin, a political science professor at Brooklyn College. His vision “gives people a hint of the kinds of things that the government can provide to make life better, not as a default for what the private sector doesn’t provide, but perhaps as a baseline of what we can all expect for everybody.”

If the opposition to Mamdani’s movement has a spiritual home base, it might be outside Citadel CEO Ken Griffin’s 24,000-square-foot four-floor penthouse on the southern edge of New York’s Central Park.

To announce the pied-à-terre tax—which would tax second homes in New York valued by the city as $5 million or more for houses and $1 million or more for condos and co-ops (at rates between 0.8% and 6.5%)—the mayor released a video of himself standing in front of the hedge fund leader’s second home and crowing about the tax dollars Griffin would soon be paying. The tax, Mamdani said, was “designed for the richest of the rich: those who store their wealth in New York City real estate but don’t actually live here.” The video’s soundtrack sounded remarkably similar to the theme song from HBO’s Succession.

For some in the upper echelons of industry, it was a bridge too far. “New York City is the financial home of the world, and to have a mayor that truly believes that capitalism and jobs are evil . . . publicly humiliating Ken Griffin and actively trying to drive [him] away is a pretty terrible outcome for the day-to-day New Yorkers who need jobs,” says Bradley Tusk, a venture capitalist and political consultant who worked in Mayor Michael Bloomberg’s administration. “More tax revenue means you can help more poor people. . . . Instead, he is actively pushing people away.”

Mamdani “was playing to his base and sending a negative message,” says James Whelan, president of the Real Estate Board of New York (REBNY), a trade group that represents some 14,000 members. “We want people to invest here in the city, create jobs here. There was no upside to that video in terms of what was in the city’s best interest.”

Hedge fund manager Bill Ackman called the video “a huge mistake” at Fast Company’s Innovation Festival in September. He admonished the mayor for having “scared away or encouraged people who are among the most productive New Yorkers to go to other places, like Miami.”

Griffin himself claimed that the video “put me in harm’s way” and threatened to halt plans to build a new Citadel headquarters in Midtown.

Compared to the rhetoric coming out of D.C., Mamdani’s video seems tame—and comes nowhere near the language that, say, FDR used when talking about the “economic royalists” of his era, according to Robin, the Brooklyn College professor. Yet there’s still the question of the mayor’s other tax proposals.

During the campaign, Mamdani teased a slate of increases: raising the income tax by 2% on people earning more than $1 million (projected to raise $4 billion annually), adjusting property taxes so wealthier property owners pay more, and raising the state’s corporate tax rate on some businesses from 7.25% to 11.5% to match neighboring New Jersey’s (projected to raise $5 billion a year).

But only the state legislature and Governor Kathy Hochul can raise these taxes. And while she supported the pied-à-terre tax, Hochul seems currently uninterested in signing any other large tax proposals. (On Election Day, when asked if he had spoken to the governor about new tax proposals during her next term, Mamdani sidestepped the question).

In some ways, it’s all a bit of theater. The mayor can energize supporters by calling for a tax he can’t raise. CEOs can wring hands about the possibility of a tax that will not be enacted. But business leaders hate uncertainty, so “words matter,” says Blumenthal. “If we are trying to make New York City the best place to build a business, and you are telling folks, ‘Hey, we’re going to be raising taxes’ or are generally promoting an anti-business sentiment, those business leaders have lots of options” in terms of moving to other cities, he warns.

“It’s cordial.”

While evidence of people and businesses fleeing the city will take years to quantify, initial numbers don’t scream of any danger. Available office space just fell to its lowest level since 2020, and in the second quarter, contracts on $10 million to $20 million properties were up nearly 40% over the same period last year.

Despite Griffin’s protests, he’s continuing his Midtown office project. Ackman announced plans to open a $660 million brain research institute on the city’s West Side. Higher corporate tax rates could certainly drive businesses away, or suppress entrepreneurship, but Mamdani’s proposal was aimed at the highest-earning companies; a new startup would be exempt.

Is Warby Parker toying with the idea of leaving? “We’re a New York brand, so we’re here, and we’re going to do everything possible to continue to invest in this city,” Blumenthal says, citing the company’s continued charitable commitment to give free glasses to New York schoolchildren.

Some of the tension seems entirely avoidable. Business leaders, used to knowing what levers to pull and what phone call to make, are having trouble getting used to an administration that isn’t as focused on the usual glad-handing. “He’s so charismatic that if he was willing to spend a little bit of time meeting with VCs, with startups, he could make them feel good about being here,” says Tusk.

“Many in the business community, myself included, would love to see a more pro-business approach,” says Julie Samuels, the president and CEO of Tech:NYC, a trade group that represents the city’s tech industry. Even so, she acknowledges, the administration is making an effort: “There is outreach.”

It helps that the city’s vibes have been good. The Knicks’ championship run, which prompted a jaded populace to party in the streets, coupled with the mayor’s constant barrage of smiling, feel-good videos about small wins (another pothole filled!) have created a sense of possibility. “As long as New York is a place where people want to live, tech companies will continue to expand and hire here,” says Samuels. Plus, “this administration is, especially at an interpersonal level, very pleasant. That goes a long way for those of us who work in this space.”

(“It’s good that we communicate,” says Steven Fulop, the president and CEO of the nonprofit Partnership for New York City, which represents more than 300 of the city’s largest corporations, who is often deeply critical of the administration. “It’s cordial.”)

“There’s certainly common ground, and that’s not always reported,” deputy mayor Su says. A big part of her job involves meeting with CEOs—and sometimes cleaning up. She happened to have a meeting scheduled with Whelan the day after the Griffin video dropped. “It went fine,” says Whelan.

Su cites the Business Advisory Council as evidence of the administration’s willingness to engage with some of its biggest critics: “This is not a performative exercise” but “a real desire to learn from one another.” Those don’t sound like the words of an administration that’s trying to end private capital. But that doesn’t mean another video targeting the 1% is off the table.

Mamdani’s grocery plan: “I think it’s incredibly stupid, but it’s at least a real attempt at socialism”

Errol Schweizer remembers being shocked when he saw the video. In December 2024, Mamdani, then a long-shot candidate running for mayor of New York, promised that if he won, he’d open city-owned grocery stores. There were scant details about how this might work, but it sounded like a revolutionary idea.

“He pulled it out of his ass,” jokes Schweizer, a consultant and former food executive who hosts the Grocery Nerds podcast. “There was nothing else to it. He was like, ‘We’re going to open five publicly owned grocery stores.’” For Schweizer, though, it resonated. A former vice president of the grocery division of Whole Foods, he has been writing about the logistics of city-owned groceries for years.

Schweizer eventually got in touch with the campaign and, after Mamdani’s election, helped the NYCEDC design the city’s $70 million pilot program. The five outposts, the first of which is slated to open by late 2027, will be spread across the five boroughs and be run by private operators. The administration says they’ll charge 30% less on average than nearby stores on all fresh produce, meat, seafood, and roughly 20 additional categories of pantry staples, dairy, and refrigerated goods.

Of all the administration’s projects, the grocery stores are the most forward- looking and traditionally “socialist.” They’ve also drawn the most criticism. The grocery business has infamously tight margins, and since the plan is for the city to make payments to the store operator to compensate for its losses, critics say the stores will cost the city too much to subsidize. Even many left-leaning wonks say there are simpler alternatives to lowering the price of food for needy New Yorkers, like increasing payments to existing food-subsidy programs.

Schweizer says the existence of other options misses the point. By controlling the grocery stores, the city gets to play a role in the supply chain. If the cost of food is adding to the city’s affordability crisis, the administration doesn’t want to let private operators or other market forces exclusively determine that cost. “New York does a lot of this stuff already in other sectors,” says Schweizer. Its ferry system, for instance, is publicly owned but outsourced to private operators. In this case, public institutions become “a primary purchaser of food for customers,” he says.

As one of the most radical of the administration’s proposals, the stores have become a lightning rod. “I think it’s incredibly stupid, but it’s at least a real attempt at socialism,” says Tusk. A coalition of small grocery stores and the National Supermarket Association have both sued the city, saying the plan is illegally anti-competition. In response to the proposal, John Catsimatidis, the billionaire owner and CEO of supermarket chain Gristedes, threatened to close his stores if Mamdani won the election. (He is also a former Republican mayoral candidate and the owner of a right-wing radio station, so he may not be motivated strictly by the bottom line.)

Schweizer acknowledges that the stores will have an impact on surrounding businesses, which the city will need to mitigate, perhaps with additional subsidies. But he sees an upside for the city’s economy: “There are 1.5 million New Yorkers who are food insecure.” And they spend money too. “That is an enormous customer base that’s not being serviced by the current market. It’s a market failure.”

In a few years, there will be data—and it won’t require much complex analysis. “The measure of success here is, will people shop?” says Schweizer.

It’s just one example of how the city may intervene in the local economy. One of the next “failed markets” the city is looking at fixing, Shorris says, is building insurance, which drives up the cost of housing development. Additional new initiatives could address “monopolistic practices or limited suppliers,” he says. “We want to look at the range of tools we have—regulatory, policymaking, use of our land and resources, investment, and scale—to see if we can create opportunities that the market might not [be creating] for the people we’re concerned about.”

The symbolism of toilets

“Everyone would tell us, ‘Oh man, New York City, it’ll take, minimum, a year and a half to get something through the procurement process, and that’s going to be a slog,’” says Jessica Heinzelman. As the cofounder and COO of Throne Labs, which has installed modular restrooms in six U.S. markets, she had been eyeing New York for years but thought the red tape would be daunting.

Then free public toilets appeared on the mayor’s agenda for his first 100 days. Five weeks after the inauguration, a request went out for proposals, and Throne—which sells toilet stalls that operate on a 10-minute timer and don’t require water lines or electricity hookups—won. By September 16, Throne was installing the first of what will be 17 units as part of a one-year, $4 million pilot. (Heinzelman says OSHA rules prevented the mayor from helping install a toilet himself at the launch event. When she suggested they stage it, she was told Mamdani won’t do anything fake.)

This is not the speed at which cities usually move—even one that never sleeps. The mayor, says Shorris, “is impatient” and devotes a lot of attention to the “key areas that were commitments of his.” The business world is noticing. “Obviously, what they’re doing and saying is resonating with a lot of New Yorkers,” says Samuels, of Tech:NYC. “And a lot of people in tech, particularly on the investor side, see that and want to know what’s going on. What is it that’s resonating?”

Take, for example, the city’s Parents’ Night Out babysitting project. One Sunday in August, rec centers around the city opened from 4 p.m. to 8 p.m. for children ages 6 to 13, providing free activities and childcare for up to nearly 300 registered children. In a city of 8 million, 300 sets of parents getting four hours off is an almost meaningless statistic. But that a government would even think of it is the point.

That may have been a one-off event, but childcare is a top issue for the administration, and an area where it’s forging some of its most productive relationships with business leaders.

With a year of childcare now costing sometimes even more than a year of college, the mayor worked with the governor to launch a pilot expanding former mayor de Blasio’s universal 3-K program—which in 2017 seemed revolutionary but is now part of the city fabric—to 2-year-olds, up to 10 hours a day, 260 days a year. Initially, the program will serve roughly 2,000 children, with plans to expand to 12,000 by fall 2027. After that, it becomes a question of how to pay for more, with business leaders likely to balk at raising taxes.

What’s interesting is that everyone—even business leaders put off by the mayor’s socialism—seems to agree that childcare should be free, a public good provided by the city.

In September, a group of businesses—including the Partnership for New York City, Tech:NYC, and REBNY—established the NYC Child Care Business Council to advocate for public investment in childcare. “The market is not able to solve this problem,” says Grace Rauh, executive director of Citizens Union, which is home to the 5BORO Institute, a New York policy think tank that convened the group. “Frankly, private businesses aren’t able to provide the kind of fully robust childcare benefits that their employees need.” But they increasingly recognize, she says, that for the city’s economy to grow and for it to attract the best talent, “we need affordable and accessible childcare.”

Mamdani’s real estate turnaround

Not everyone is satisfied with the administration’s overall pace of change. Andrew Rigie, executive director of the New York City Hospitality Alliance, which represents restaurants, bars, and clubs, feels that Mamdani’s OPEN for Small Business initiative—more than 50 proposals for eliminating red tape and fines—doesn’t go far enough. “While none of them are bad,” he says, “they’re certainly not transformative.”

But even some of Mamdani’s fiercest critics are realizing the benefits of a city that makes things happen. Two months before the mayoral election, an email circulated among housing developers, the Times reported: “If we fail to mobilize, the financial capital of the world risks being handed over to a socialist this November. We cannot—and will not—let that happen.”

But Mamdani, who once advocated for “moving toward the full de-commodification of housing,” came around over the next several months to the potential of private real estate development, citing building booms in Austin and Minneapolis that seemed to lower rents. The Economist declared in September, nine months into his administration, that “developers are learning to love Zohran Mamdani.” REBNY’s Whelan confirms it (while also decrying the rent freeze and Mamdani’s “punitive” attitude toward building owners): “When it comes to the issue of housing production, our goals are aligned,” he tells me. “We have a housing-supply issue here in New York City. The mayor has ambitious goals, and he should. Now it’s a matter of whether execution will meet ambition.”

The real estate industry certainly supports Mamdani’s effort to speed up permits and building approvals and his goal to increase supply by 200,000 new affordable apartments over the next decade. That’s a lot of new business for developers. “Real estate is an asset class that can’t move” out of the city, says Whelan. “So it’s in our interest for the city to succeed. If this mayor makes progress in making the city more affordable, creating more housing, we’re going to be a more competitive city, and the real estate industry is going to be stronger.”

Mamdani has even seemingly charmed New York’s most legendary real estate developer, receiving gushing praise from President Trump at joint appearances in recent months despite the president’s once calling him a “100% Communist Lunatic.”

Trump has also given him a template for one of his biggest moves since he took office: having the NYCEDC invest $29 million into the building of a quantum lab at New York University. In return, the city will take 15% of any future revenue from the IP generated on city-funded equipment. A few years ago, this would have seemed like a radical socialist endeavor. But given that the president has spent the past two years negotiating equity stakes for the federal government in major tech and mineral companies (including a nearly 10% stake in Intel), this deal feels positively quaint as an act of government intervention.

The quantum computing arrangement is a prime example of how the NYCEDC will be thinking about its bigger economic development projects. “This mayor’s made pretty clear what his priority is, which is to find opportunities for growth that advance the objective of social justice,” Shorris says. Some projects will be solely about creating jobs, and others—like the grocery stores—will be focused on improving economic opportunity. But the ideal one will sit in the middle. “I don’t think the growth and justice objectives are incompatible,” Shorris says.

Good vibes, it turns out, are good for business

Since Mamdani’s electrifying mayoral run, politicians across the country have tried to emulate his video strategy, with limited success (just ask Mayor Karen Bass in Los Angeles, whose own pothole video failed to land). They’re having better luck on the policy front: delivering key services while gently prying the capitalist system open to greater government control.

In Boston, Mayor Michelle Wu is clashing with business interests over tax increases and development, while testing programs like free buses. She crushed her most recent primary challenger, the son of New England Patriots owner Robert Kraft, by 49 points.

In L.A., city council member and DSA-endorsee Nithya Raman, running for mayor on a strikingly Mamdani-esque platform of improving city services and increasing housing development while focusing on the city’s lower-income residents, defeated Bass, the incumbent—while Washington, D.C. and Providence, Rhode Island, also elected new socialist mayors.

(On the other hand, Brandon Johnson, the Chicago mayor whose own grocery pilot failed, and Katie Wilson, the recently elected socialist mayor of Seattle, are floundering, hinting that ideology must be paired with execution to fully inspire.)

A quarter of the way into his term, Mamdani has yet to be tested by a crisis or severe economic downturn. The AI bubble could pop, the bond market is creaky, and tensions between him and his base about policing—and the continued employment of Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch—could crest. (Retaining Tisch from the previous administration was universally cited by business leaders I spoke with as the best decision he’s made, making her seem like a foundational piece of any bridges being built.)

Meanwhile, in order to enact some of his most progressive proposals, he’ll have to persuade state-level lawmakers to support and pay for them—and face down corporate leaders. “He hasn’t yet had the kinds of confrontations with private capital that are going to have to happen for some of these big changes to happen,” says Robin, the Brooklyn College professor. But political capital is currency: A near-70% approval rating means you have room to try things.

“There’s tremendous power inside government agencies that has not been fully utilized for the good of the many, to build an economy for the many,” says deputy mayor Su. If more politicians figure out how to unleash it, the movement could gain even more traction—in New York and elsewhere.

By doing things like cutting red tape for small business and appointing chief savings officers to eliminate wasteful spending in city departments, while also pushing city-owned grocery stores and universal childcare, perhaps the mayor has replaced the usual binary of “more government” or “less government” with a new paradigm: Can we find the right amount of government to solve each problem?

Industry leaders can help answer that question. Billionaires, industry groups, and corporate lobbying arms spent tens of millions of dollars trying to prevent Mamdani from being elected. Now, they’re trying to figure out their place in a city where his agenda (or, at least, his presentation of his agenda) has become quite popular. Good vibes, it turns out, are good for business. So are functioning cities, and employees who aren’t struggling to afford housing, childcare, and groceries. Mamdani’s success could end up saving capitalism in New York from the die-hard capitalists themselves.

Wary business leaders have a list of indicators they’re watching to determine the administration’s success. Have jobs left the city? Is the number of millionaires increasing? Is new housing being built? “Does New York become a better place to build a business, raise a family, and invest for the future?” offers Blumenthal, citing New York’s shrinking population. “Is homelessness down? Are reading scores significantly better? Are the streets safer and cleaner?”

Shorris says the vision is to hit those metrics. “A New York that spreads its benefits across all of its people has been and can continue to be the most powerful economic engine anywhere,” he says. There’s a place at the table for business leaders to help build that city under a democratic socialist administration. They’re just going to have to get used to the fact that they’re no longer the ones controlling the agenda.

5 social signals brands should act on now

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Social media leaders are expected to know what is happening everywhere, all the time, and to have a point of view on what to do about it. But most social tools were built to monitor and measure. They report what happened. They rarely explain what it means.

That gap is where real opportunity sits. Across the brands we analyze each week, the most useful findings are rarely the biggest numbers. More often they are the shifts underneath them.

Here are five that marketing teams should be paying attention to.

Audiences are tiring of hyper-polished, solo "it-girl" glamour and gravitating toward content that feels like friends in a room. A candid carousel recapping a New York Fashion Week party reached nearly 740,000 accounts and drew more than 21,000 engagements, outperforming standard step-and-repeat coverage.

The action: Retire the solo interview. Try conversations and group chats where co-hosts or friends interview each other. Lightly produced and unscripted. Shift production focus from rigid scripts to creating environments that foster authentic interpersonal dynamics and natural conversational flow.

For one consumer tech brand, the most powerful emotional anchor was not a feature. It was relief from small, familiar anxieties: a dying battery, a package that might arrive while you are offline. Humorous, relatable posts about those moments drove strong reach and shares on Instagram and Facebook.

The action: Build bite-sized, highly visual scenarios that show the product solving an everyday problem. Focusing messaging on specific, common friction points rather than broad feature lists quickly builds emotional resonance and everyday relevance with your audience.

Many brands treat Stories as a place to repost the grid. But more often, short form content, like Instagram Stories, acts as a high-intent conversion powerhouse, generating massive click-through rates that can surpass follower reach.

The action: Rather than treating Stories as a secondary syndication feed, start treating them as optimized, high-velocity checkout funnels. Consider story-only offers and unique promo codes that turn intent into sales you can attribute, and design ephemeral content tailored for rapid action with clear, direct calls-to-action.

Relatable humor and conversational copywriting significantly outperform traditional feature-benefit structures in driving post engagement and click-through actions. We’re seeing meme-style copy ("My package: out for delivery / Me: I'm gonna be offline...") consistently outperforming feature-and-benefit announcements.

The action: Write the first three seconds of a Reel the way people actually post, using script-style overlays, familiar phrasing and emoji cues. Adopt native platform vernacular and visual formats to capture scroll attention and increase viewer retention.

Communities are starting to exhibit strong skepticism around highly marketed aesthetic features. For one technology brand, this was showing up in cases where customers felt basics were unaddressed: camera quality, software availability, stability. Sentiment scores alone would have missed the credibility gap.

The action: Show features in real-world moments where they are useful, not in isolated product shots. Frame product capabilities within everyday consumer workflows or challenges to demonstrate tangible value and maintain brand credibility.

Manscaped, the men's grooming brand, built a following by being very good at one kind of humor. But as the brand looked to expand into face and beard care, it needed to adapt its comedic approach to resonate beyond its below-the-waist roots.

Rather than doubling down, the team used cultural AI to find its voice by watching audience signals and how they responded to new creative. That surfaced new creator partners, including two University of Southern California student filmmakers who helped create one of the brand’s most viral pieces of content to date. It also helped validate the creative concept behind the brand's fall 2025 "Send Face Pics Instead" campaign, which contributed to Manscaped’s broader efforts to expand its reach and become the #3 brand in the U.S. Total Electric Shave Market.

All because cultural AI delivered rapid insights in a form leadership could confidently act on. The brand’s social director described it as "a quick postcard brief to make your point on why we need to act on something and to get the social teams heard."

None of these signals required more data. They required context: what is changing, why people are responding and what to do next. That’s what happens when you stop tracking the signals and start reading the room.

Congress is coming back to Washington. Here's what to expect

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Lawmakers are returning to Washington this week to finish up work before Democrats take control of the House and Senate in January. Zayrha Rodriguez/NPR hide caption

It is the final hoorah for the 119th Congress. Republicans have just weeks left in control of the House of Republicans and the Senate. The main thing on the agenda: keeping the federal government open. Funding is set to run out on December 11, when a stop-gap measure passed about a month ahead of the midterms expires.

Any funding extension is all but certain to be done through another short-term bill, known as a continuing resolution. The typical government funding process — 12 carefully negotiated appropriations bills — would take time and political will that lawmakers currently lack.

Congress will also need to find a path forward on the National Defense Authorization Act, long considered a must-pass annual military funding package. This year, the bill is quagmired in the unforgiving politics of the Iran war. The conflict is opposed by a majority of Americans — something President Trump has acknowledged was a key force in driving his party out of power.

The House is also likely to vote on an impeachment effort targeting Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. Introduced by Republican Thomas Massie of Kentucky, a key figure in forcing the administration's release of the Epstein files, the impeachment measure lists a number of charges, including that the administration failed to properly consult Congress on the military campaign.

Democrats are unlikely to be in a compromising mood on legislation, just weeks away from taking control of both chambers. That means continued work on big-ticket items like the Protect College Sports Act, meant to introduce new regulations around college athlete compensation, and a bipartisan energy permitting overhaul will likely wait until the new term.

With slim majorities in both chambers, they could also have to contend with some attendance problems after a demoralizing performance in last week's midterm elections. That further complicates passing legislation through chambers already governed by the thinnest of majorities.

Mike Ricci, a Republican strategist with the group Seven Letter and aide to former Speakers of the House John Boehner and Paul Ryan, told NPR that a number of forces could help draw reluctant lame duck legislators back to Washington, including winding down their offices, finding new work and attending the White House Christmas party.

Supreme Court weighs effort to shield federal law enforcement as ICE complaints rise

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court is seen on Oct. 1 in Washington, D.C. J. Scott Applewhite/AP hide caption

Five years ago, a gang riot broke out inside a federal prison in Hawaii.

Kekai Watanabe was beaten with a makeshift weapon known as a "lock in a sock."

When he complained about serious back pain, a nurse told him to "stop being a cry baby."

Seven months later, doctors diagnosed Watanabe with a fractured tail bone, and he sued the nurse for violating his Eighth Amendment right against cruel and unusual punishment.

On Monday, the Supreme Court will hear his case, which could determine whether federal law enforcement officers can be held to account when they violate constitutional rights. The claim by Watanabe, now 40, echoes far beyond the walls of prison — and into American streets where immigration officers have been accused of beating and running over migrants.

"What's at stake here is whether, basically, we as a country are going to hold federal law enforcement officers accountable when they violate clearly established constitutional rights," said Georgetown law professor Steve Vladeck, who filed a "friend of the court" brief in the case. "I mean, it really is, I think, that stark."

It's already difficult for these claims to proceed, because the court requires people to show the facts in their cases are identical to precedent. In the federal prisons, the landmark case dates to 1980. The prisoner in that lawsuit died from untreated asthma. The nurse's lawyers say Watanabe, the inmate from Hawaii, survived the gang attack in 2021, so his case is different enough that it should be dismissed.

Mary Hampton Mason worked at the Justice Department for nearly 35 years, in an office that evaluated these kinds of legal claims. Mason said rogue officers are rare, and problems are typically systemic.

"It's a result of agency culture, it's a result of training, it's a result of all sorts of things, but these failures are really institutional," Mason said.

The Supreme Court created a right to sue federal officers for wrongdoing in a 1971 case known as Bivens. But Mason said that precedent has been hollowed out for years, and the court should finally overrule it because it gives plaintiffs false hope.

"In fact in the last 20 years, I'm only aware of one Bivens case that has resulted in a judgment for the plaintiffs that … didn't get reversed on appeal, and that's in thousands and thousands and thousands of cases filed," she said.

But supporters of the prisoner say that number doesn't account for settlements or how these lawsuits can deter other bad behavior. If individual federal officers and agents can be on the hook financially, the argument goes, they could think twice before breaking the law.

"That's why damages are so important," said Gregory Cui, an attorney at the MacArthur Justice Center, a civil rights legal organization. "Because they're a way to try to fix things, to give a person the means to pay for medical care or rebuild their life."

In a "friend of the court" brief, Cui reported that the grievance process for people inside federal prison almost never works for inmates, who succeed just 1% of the time in changing things such as access to medical care or better conditions behind bars.

Cui and other legal experts said the Supreme Court agreed to hear this case at a time when abuses by federal immigration agents and others are in the spotlight. Now, they said, is the wrong time for the court to eliminate one of the few avenues of accountability for those errant officers.

"A world with no Bivens is a world in which federal law enforcement officers would enjoy absolute immunity, where no matter how egregious their conduct is, no one can hold them liable," said Vladeck, of Georgetown Law Center. "As alarming a prospect as that may have been five years ago, it should be an especially terrifying one today, and it ought to be one that even this Supreme Court is worried about endorsing."

Both sides agree Congress has the power to weigh in and give people whose rights have been violated by federal officers a clear way to sue. But so far lawmakers have not advanced legislation to hold those people to account.

Democrats won big. Can they lower your grocery bill?

A supporter holds a sign during a campaign rally for Democratic Senate candidate Dr. Annie Andrews in Myrtle Beach, S.C., on Nov. 1. Andrews defeated her Republican opponent, Sen. Darline Graham. Bill Clark/CQ-Roll Call, Inc via Getty Images hide caption

It's been a feature of campaign ads and stump speeches for months: grocery prices are out of control.

Affordability concerns, including high food costs, were top of mind for voters heading into the midterms, as the Trump administration's tariffs and the war in Iran continued to drive up prices. That economic strain helped Democrats win control of both houses of Congress on Tuesday.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries said the incoming majority would make tackling high prices a priority come January. "It was a tremendous victory for the American people and a strong rebuke of MAGA extremism, which has made life more expensive for everyday Americans despite their promises to lower the high cost of living on day one," he told NPR on Friday.

The question is how Democrats will do that with President Trump still in the Oval Office, willing to wield his veto pen. Senate Democrats won't have a filibuster-proof majority, and Trump may have little appetite to roll back his signature tariffs or reverse course on the Iran war, two of the main causes of high food prices.

Lael Brainard, who leads a public policy group called The Kitchen Table Project, said Americans are nevertheless expecting relief from Washington — and quickly.

"They are feeling like they are getting squeezed by high prices that don't need to be that high," she said.

Despite the political hurdles, Democrats said they intend to move ahead with plans to cut grocery bills, including by rolling back Trump's tariffs. Those tariffs have not only raised the cost of certain foods and drinks imported to the U.S., but also created other pressures on the food industry by making it more expensive to import things like machine parts and packaging.

The Iran war has led to a surge in energy prices, which has made it more expensive to transport just about everything. Fertilizer costs also grew when the closure of the Strait of Hormuz cut off a significant portion of the global fertilizer trade.

But getting Trump to backtrack on tariffs or the Iran war — or his immigration crackdown, which has led to labor shortages on U.S. farms — could prove tricky for Democrats.

"I think the president would certainly want to find ways to reduce the cost of groceries. Democrats [also] see that in their interest," said Lehigh University political science professor Chris Borick. "The mechanisms, the tools, the levers that would be most readily available to do that — there's quite a bit of distance between the president and Democrats that'll be taking over both the House and the Senate."

Several incoming Democratic senators also floated other proposals during their campaigns that aim to make groceries more affordable.

James Talarico of Texas said he would push back against "attacks" on the federal food assistance programs WIC and SNAP, the latter of which is undergoing a major overhaul by the Trump administration and has seen a precipitous drop in participation since last summer. Kansas' Adam Hamilton announced his support for a national ban on "dynamic pricing" in grocery stores. And multiple candidates, including Roy Cooper of North Carolina, vowed to block food industry mergers they say would raise consumer prices.

Beyond the blue wave in Congress, Democrats also made gains in state capitals across the U.S. on Tuesday. At least 28 states will have Democratic governors beginning next year, and ten state legislative chambers flipped to Democrats, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures.

Incoming state governors have floated their own ideas for how to give residents relief in the grocery aisle.

Keisha Lance Bottoms proposed allowing municipalities in Georgia to suspend or fully eliminate their local sales tax on groceries, baby formula, diapers and more. Gina Hinojosa, the governor-elect of Texas, promised to outlaw "predatory surveillance pricing" and price gouging in grocery stores. In New Mexico, Deb Haaland said the state would explore a policy similar to one in Albuquerque that temporarily waives permitting fees for markets that sell healthy and affordable food in so-called "food deserts," the Albuquerque Journal reported.

However state and federal officials decide to go about lowering prices, Americans say they want to see cheaper groceries soon. According to a poll released by the Kitchen Table Project and Global Strategy Group in September, nearly half of respondents said their most important goal was seeing their grocery bills lowered within six months.

Behind-the-meter power: An effective tool for bringing large utility customers online faster

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Data centers and AI infrastructure are intensifying the strain on existing power grids across the U.S.

Large-load facilities are built quickly, only to face years-long waits for grid interconnection and new generation capacity, such as gas turbines. Behind-the-meter (BTM) power is becoming an effective solution to bridge the gap between what the grid can provide and what data centers need to operate smoothly.

Utilities and large-scale customers alike are embracing BTM power: 80 percent of utilities view BTM power generation as permanent infrastructure and expect it to serve a significant share of large-load demand by 2030, according to Bloom Energy’s 2026 Utilities Insights Report. Nearly two-thirds of data center developers cite onsite power as their primary response when grid timelines slip. “BTM addresses a major issue, which is avoiding increases in grid retail prices,” says Nik Nunes, Chief Executive Officer at Industrial Development Funding (IDF), a top investment partner of Bloom Energy. “For that reason, several governors have come out in support of it.”

BTM power isn’t always a simple fix. It involves complications related to regulatory requirements, power storage and permitting. Yet, when used in tandem with local utilities in front of meter power resources, it can provide an effective bridge for bringing large-scale customers online faster, helping them bypass lengthy wait times for a grid connection. BTM can also be affordable over the long term relative to other power sources.

To launch a successful BTM power effort, data center developers must address growing public concerns about noise, water usage and emissions. In many jurisdictions, permitting has become a challenge, and BTM power projects are often delayed. To simplify air permitting, it’s helpful for BTM solutions to produce few or no combustion-related emissions. Bloom Energy fuel cells run on natural gas today and are fuel-flexible—they can also operate on biogas, hydrogen or blends. They generate energy without combustion, cutting criteria pollutants like NOx and SOx to near zero. Other factors that can simplify permitting include minimal water use, low noise and a small footprint. “Prioritizing these needs can ease local zoning and community concerns,” Nunes says.

Utility coordination is another complication for developers. Depending on the data center’s location, developers must align with local interconnection and compliance requirements to ensure safety and regulatory compliance. To streamline permitting, large-scale utility customers should look for a BTM power solution that adheres to local regulations.

BTM power systems emerged to solve a problem: the power gap between the average time to construct a data center (18–24 months) and the average time to achieve a new grid interconnection (4–7 years). Large-load customers could wait years for a grid connection, making BTM power a much-needed bridge.

But BTM power is more than a stopgap solution. Today’s utility leaders see BTM power generation as a permanent fixture in the modern grid. Many developers do not plan to shut down BTM power operations immediately upon achieving grid interconnection. Instead, they intend to continue operating in tandem with the grid. “Utilities are considering BTM as a permanent solution to supplement the grid, given extended equipment lead times, interconnection queues and the expectation of continued energy demand growth,” says Nunes. “BTM is shifting from a backup role to a primary power strategy.”

BTM power deployments, such as microgrids with low-emissions fuel cells, can provide essential power to data centers and manufacturers and support the wider grid through interconnection. Bloom Energy’s modular framework facilitates the redeployment of fuel cells when they are needed elsewhere. According to the 2026 Utilities Insights Report, 36% of utility leaders see distributed power as a vital supplement for peak shaving and grid backup.

As large-scale energy users build out onsite power generation, utilities should consider how BTM power fits into a long-term growth strategy. With rising demand, utilities are taking steps toward flexible energy architectures that integrate onsite power generation into the wider grid.

Both regulated and unregulated utilities are exploring the possibilities of utility-owned BTM assets to create fresh revenue streams. Such BTM power systems can serve as a much-needed capacity reserve to support large customers and the grid itself during peak-load periods.

For example, a large investor-owned utility in the Midwest recently faced mounting demand from two data center operators that needed more capacity than the utility could deliver. To meet this need, the utility presented a plan to install solid oxide fuel cells at both sites, which the state regulator approved. The data center customers covered the full project cost through long-term agreements that protect ratepayers, and the utility now owns and operates the fuel cells. “Ultimately, the project gave the customers speed to power while the utility continued its long-term grid investments,” says Nunes.

Nunes stresses the importance of preserving long-term flexibility by designing a BTM installation that can change roles over its lifetime rather than being removed once it’s no longer the primary power source. Choosing modular, scalable technology helps utilities maintain flexibility. “Capacity should be addable, movable or able to retire in increments consistent with needed power, such as Bloom Energy’s fuel cells,” he explains. “It’s also helpful to bring regulators in early, as an approval framework built for the first project makes the next one quicker.”

Tolling agreements and co-development partnerships also offer utilities a chance to participate in the growth of BTM power. With a tolling agreement, utilities can generate recurring revenue by contracting with customers to provide power from utility-owned BTM power facilities. Co-development partnerships allow utilities to partner directly with developers to design, finance and build power projects. Both types of arrangement allow utilities to invest in BTM power and incorporate it into their ongoing operations. The contract should define who owns the asset. In the case of Bloom Energy, its investment partners, such as IDF, can provide a cost-effective lease solution that allows the utility to acquire the asset after a specified period.

Utilities continue to explore how BTM solutions can bridge grid constraints today while moving toward a more flexible future grid. “Utility leaders should consider planning a hybrid grid,” Nunes says. Large-load customers such as hyperscalers and neoclouds will increasingly rely on both grid power and onsite generation, a shift that should be reflected in integrated resource plans and load forecasts. “Requiring large customers to carry the cost and risk of their incremental capacity will help protect ratepayers. Given the long backlog of large-capacity turbines, utilities should partner with technology providers to secure early access to manufacturing slots.”

Beyond megawatts: Nuclear’s other capacity expansion challenge

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nuclear growth plans are accelerating as data centers, large-load customers, uprates, restarts, extended operations and new generation projects reshape the industry’s outlook. But the challenge of capacity expansion extends far beyond megawatts. Every utility must also expand the capacity of its workforce.

The workforce challenge is not simply about hiring more people. Recruiting is critical, but so are retention strategies, qualification timelines, knowledge transfer processes and the availability of experienced employees and contractors. Utilities must continue operating existing plants safely and reliably while extending asset life, modernizing infrastructure, preparing for growth and developing the next generation of workers. In many cases, the same people are asked to do all of it.

An experienced engineer supporting an uprate may also be needed for aging management reviews. The supervisor helping qualify new employees may also be responsible for outage preparation. The skilled craft resources needed for capital projects are often the same resources supporting ongoing plant reliability and maintenance activities. To execute an ambitious growth plan, your workforce needs support and clear expectations as it navigates the demands of the present and the future.

That reality makes employee engagement and retention critical operational necessities.

Across the industry, organizations are managing a growing list of demands. Employees are operating plants, supporting outages, resolving equipment issues, onboarding new hires, transferring knowledge, implementing modernization efforts and preparing for future growth. Subsequent license renewal and long-term operations programs add further demands through inspections, evaluations, documentation and equipment replacement activities.

At the same time, many organizations continue to face shortages in experienced operators, engineers, technicians and other critical roles. Long qualification timelines and retirements only increase the pressure.

Growth introduces another challenge that is less visible but equally important: cognitive load. Experienced employees must continually sort through competing priorities, interpret changing guidance and determine where to focus limited time and attention. When this becomes a constant state, organizations risk change fatigue, burnout and disengagement.

For nuclear leaders, the consequences extend beyond employee experience. The loss of experienced personnel can slow qualification programs, reduce knowledge transfer, limit an organization’s ability to respond to emergent issues and increase execution risk across both life-extension and capacity-expansion efforts.

As a result, workforce planning must address not only how organizations attract talent but also how they retain, support and fully leverage the people already in place.

Three leadership responsibilities can help organizations strengthen workforce capacity while navigating growth and modernization: simplify priorities, enable leaders and engage employees.

A common challenge in growing organizations is that new priorities are added without older priorities disappearing. Individually, each initiative may be justified, but collectively, they can create confusion about what matters most.

Organizations should begin with an integrated view of operational commitments, major projects and workforce capacity. Leaders must identify where critical resources are supporting multiple high-priority activities and make deliberate decisions about sequencing, staffing, or scope rather than assuming employees can absorb additional work indefinitely.

One nuclear organization recently addressed this issue by consolidating multiple layers of strategic imperatives into a small number of employee-facing priorities. These priorities allowed employees to make a clearer connection between their daily work and fleet strategy, while leaders retained the layers behind the scenes. Simplifying the message at the right level reduced complexity without reducing rigor.

Even clear priorities require interpretation. Supervisors and managers play a critical role in helping employees understand what work matters most, where trade-offs exist and how decisions connect to organizational goals.

To do this effectively, leaders need more than communication materials. They need context, practical guidance, opportunities for discussion and the authority to resolve or escalate conflicts when competing priorities emerge.

Workforce plans should also acknowledge the time experienced employees spend mentoring, coaching and qualifying newer workers. Organizations cannot develop future capability if their most knowledgeable employees are fully consumed by current demands. Creating capacity for knowledge transfer is an investment in future operational performance.

Employees closest to the work usually identify constraints long before they appear on a project dashboard or management report.

Whether discussing outage activities, modernization efforts, operational improvements, or growth initiatives, leaders should regularly seek employee input on assumptions, risks, bottlenecks and execution challenges. Just as importantly, organizations must demonstrate how that feedback influences decisions.

When employees see concerns acknowledged and acted upon, trust grows. When feedback disappears into a process without visible outcomes, engagement often declines.

One utility has invested in dedicated engagement coordinators to help leaders stay more connected to employee feedback, concerns and ideas. By creating additional channels for communication, sense-making and listening, the organization is strengthening its ability to identify issues early and improve execution across the fleet.

Delivering the next generation of nuclear capacity requires leaders to address workforce capacity constraints that go beyond the ability to hire and train new employees. Nuclear organizations must also create space for the existing workforce to sustain today’s operations while helping build tomorrow’s future.

By simplifying competing priorities, equipping leaders to translate strategy into daily work and engaging employees as active contributors to change, nuclear organizations can strengthen retention, protect institutional knowledge and create the workforce capacity needed for growth.

The people being recruited to support tomorrow’s nuclear expansion will depend on the knowledge, experience and commitment of the people doing the work today. The next wave of energy storage capacity is already on the grid

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Next week, much of the industry will gather in Las Vegas for RE+ to talk about what gets built next. It’s an important conversation, but I’d encourage asset owners to bring a second question with them: what more can we get from what’s already been built?

This question matters more than ever as electricity demand climbs and customers feel it on their monthly bill. EIA expects consumption to grow another 3.1% in 2027, driven largely by data centers and other commercial loads, while residential customers will pay nearly 5% more for power this year.

The industry is working hard to add capacity, but getting it built takes time. According to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and GridTracker, the median project built in 2025 spent more than five years moving from interconnection request to commercial operation and only 13% of the capacity that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020 had come online by the end of 2025. New generation is essential, but the fleet already operating is an opportunity we can’t afford to pass up.

With nearly 52 gigawatts (GW) of utility-scale battery storage operating in the U.S. as of mid-2026, the installed base is too large to overlook and performance across it varies widely. When a battery energy storage system (BESS) falls short, the instinct is often to blame the battery and reach for augmentation or replacement, but in many cases the bottleneck is the controls, software and integration around it.

Higher prices should raise the value of delivering power when and where the grid needs it, yet revenues for many operating assets are moving in the other direction. In ERCOT, the average battery earned 84% less in 2025 than at its 2023 peak, as more storage competed for the same services. With incentive economics shifting too, asset managers face real pressure to get more from existing assets.

At the same time, utilities are leaning on storage for grid stability. As more gas units face revenue pressure and retirement, the grid loses the spinning mass that provides inertia in the first moments of a disturbance. Grid-forming inverters can replace that inertia, often by upgrading existing storage rather than building new. Providing that support, alongside peak shaving, load shifting and voltage and frequency regulation, takes controls that respond faster than many legacy SCADA and EMS platforms were built to handle.

Compliance requirements are moving just as quickly, with FERC Order No. 901 directing NERC to develop new reliability standards for inverter-based resources, including batteries, through 2030. Many plants operating today were designed before those requirements existed, increasing the importance of controls that can evolve as grid codes change. The same logic also applies to solar and wind plants. Once control functions are proven in the field they can easily be applied to other assets across the whole market.

A recent Gridmatic study of 30 California batteries shows what’s at stake, with fleetwide availability averaging just under 80% and execution and bidding strategy driving a wide gap between top and bottom performers. Had every asset matched the leader, the group would have earned an estimated $98 million more over two years. The lesson isn't that every performance gap can be solved with software. It's that owners shouldn't assume the battery itself is the limiting factor.

Before committing additional capital, owners should examine the full system: controls, software, market strategy, integration and plant-level coordination alongside battery health and hardware performance.

Think about the smartphone in your pocket. Its hardware may be several years old, but software updates can improve how it performs and add capabilities that weren’t available when you bought it. Storage assets aren't so different: the physical equipment may still have years of useful life, while the controls and software around it can evolve to meet new market and grid requirements.

A modern energy management and plant control platform layered onto existing assets can help owners:

We saw this firsthand in the U.K., where our team, then part of Wärtsilä, worked with Zenobē to retrofit an existing 100 MW/100 MWh battery site with a new GEMS power plant controller. The upgrade took over energy management, secured grid compliance and added stateful grid-code functions such as de-load, along with control for multiple tenants sharing one point of interconnection.

The batteries stayed in place, but the site gained new capabilities and is better prepared for future requirements. That same approach translates to U.S. markets, where interconnection rules and local regulations are evolving quickly.

New deployments will remain critical, but the next wave of value may come from making today’s fleet work harder. As the industry gathers at RE+, owners should ask not just what they need to build next, but what more the assets already on the grid could deliver. Before replacing hardware or adding capacity, diagnose the whole plant. The constraint may not be the battery cells.

Every virtual megawatt needs a meter

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Last month, Google announced a deal with Voltus for a virtual power plant (VPP) of up to 1,070 MW in PJM, roughly the output of a large nuclear reactor. Rather than build a plant, Voltus will offer homes and businesses financial incentives to trim usage when the grid is strained, through smart thermostats, batteries and flexible equipment.

The deal represents an inflection point for the power and utilities sector, where “speed to power” innovations are defining the moment. It also underscores the importance of the equipment that will decide how far such programs go: the electric meter.

NEMA's 2026 Grid Reliability Study projects U.S. economy-wide electricity demand as much as 55% by 2050, with data center consumption alone growing 300% over the next decade. New generation and transmission can't keep pace, as permitting, capital needs and equipment and skilled labor backlogs protracting project delivery timelines.

As a result, operators are looking for capacity in the system they already have. On the transmission side, grid-enhancing technologies show what's possible: PJM modeling finds they could unlock between six and seven GW of usable capacity.

At the grid edge, the opportunity may be larger. Demand response contributed 33.3 GW of enrolled wholesale capacity in 2024, about 6.5% of peak demand in organized markets and could reach 20% of U.S. peak by 2030. Behind-the-meter resources, which include microgrids and aggregations of customer-sited resources (i.e., VPPs), are scaling too.

What these grid-edge resources share is a need for measurement. A demand response event or VPP dispatch only has value if someone can prove the load dropped and pay the customer who dropped it. That job falls to the electric meter.

Despite the momentum, only 6.5% of U.S. electricity customers are enrolled in retail demand response programs. NEMA's research points to several reasons: metering and communication rules written for large power plants that burden small participants, too little public data on how flexible resources perform during grid emergencies and market rules that vary by region.

Underneath them is a question of trust. Some grid operators and utilities still doubt that distributed resources will deliver when called. But confidence at scale requires consistent, verifiable measurement across millions of devices and many vendors.

That is the role of ANSI NEMA C12.1-2026. C12.1 sets performance criteria for new types of watthour and demand meters, defines accuracy classes and test methods and establishes acceptable in-service performance for revenue meters. When a meter reading decides whether a customer is paid for curtailment, or whether an operator can count an aggregation as capacity, those criteria make the number credible.

Timing makes this pressing. Millions of first-generation smart meters, many installed between 2010 and 2015 with American Recovery and Reinvestment Act funding, are reaching end of life. Utilities in every region are planning or executing large-scale replacements through the late 2020s, driven by aging hardware, obsolete communications and the need to support distributed energy resources, EVs and resilience. Indeed, as NEMA member company Honeywell has noted, first-wave meters lack the speed and accuracy of newer models and networks and security practices evolve faster than meters can be replaced. This is a replacement market, not a first deployment and the meters chosen now will likely stay in service for 15 to 20 years or more.

The 2026 edition of C12.1 arrives at the right moment. It folds the former C12.20 accuracy-class standard into a single document covering the entire metering code and adds guidance on non-Blondel metering. Referencing C12.1-2026 in procurement specifications gives utilities, regulators and vendors a common baseline for safety, accuracy and interoperability across vendors and networks and protects ratepayers' two-decade investment.

The same long-term lens applies to where grid equipment comes from. NEMA's Make It American® program is an industry-led, third party-certification program. Accredited auditors evaluate manufacturers against the NEMA 70901-2026 process standard and certified facilities and products are listed in a public Build America, Buy America registry. The Federal Highway Administration and the Texas, Arkansas and Montana transportation departments recognize it and a pending bill in the U.S. Senate features language treating Make It American documentation as sufficient evidence of domestic-content compliance.

NEMA has prioritized product-level specifications for the equipment utilities buy most, including high- and medium-voltage distribution equipment, grid management and automation equipment and wire and cable. That lets procurement teams verify domestic content product-by-product, with certification that reflects actual production practices.

Virtual power plants may be the fastest new capacity on the market. But a virtual megawatt is only as real as the meter that proves it was delivered.
