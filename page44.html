America's hyperscale bit barns draw as much power as entire states

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Harvard study claims emissions attributable to datacenters are two to three times higher than in 2018

Just 403 hyperscale datacenters consumed an estimated 1.8 percent of US electricity in 2024-25, and their carbon dioxide emissions were roughly two to three times an earlier forecast for 2018.

Research led by the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the Harvard Data Science Initiative looked specifically at hyperscale facilities, the largest class of server farms that typically support cloud computing and AI processing.

The published study, "Assessing the carbon emissions and energy consumption of US hyperscale data centres," estimates their combined electricity use approached that of a single state such as Indiana, Michigan, or Tennessee under the highest-load scenario.

The researchers identified 403 hyperscale facilities by using a larger datacenter dataset, satellite imagery, and building footprints on OpenStreetMap.

Rather than reading electricity meters, the researchers worked out consumption from each site's electrical capacity, the hours in a year, and assumptions about how heavily the facility was running.

Across four operating scenarios, ranging from low-intensity use to AI-heavy workloads, electricity draw was believed to total 68-99 terawatt-hours (TWh). Each of those states consumed approximately 100 TWh during the study period.

The researchers also calculated carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions using eGRID2023 plant-level generation and emissions data from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Emissions ranged from 21 million to 31 million metric tons of CO2 across the scenarios, with a central estimate of 25.7 million. That compares with 10.5 million for 2018.

The study covered May 2024 to April 2025, so electricity demand and carbon emissions are likely to be even higher now, the researchers say.

"What we're seeing is already striking, but it's only a snapshot of a rapidly expanding industry," said Gianluca Guidi, lead author on the paper and postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics at Harvard.

"The datacenter boom hasn't slowed down since our study period ended in April 2025. New facilities have been built, existing campuses have expanded, and demand for AI computing continues to grow. That means the electricity use and carbon footprint we're reporting could already be substantially larger now in 2026."

Harvard's report also considers the mix of fuels being used to generate the electricity that powers all those hyperscale data houses. The mix varies considerably between regional grids, so where a hyperscale facility is built makes a substantial difference to its attributed emissions.

Across the sites studied, roughly 54 percent of the electricity attributed to their consumption came from fossil fuels, mainly natural gas and coal. The electricity attributed to these facilities nevertheless had a lower average carbon intensity than the US grid overall.

The researchers acknowledge limitations, including that there wasn't enough available data to study all hyperscale sites in the US, and that the data did not allow them to differentiate between power used for general-purpose compute versus AI-specialized operations at individual campuses. ®

JJ Gabriel says goodbye to Man Utd amid Real Madrid agreement

Real Madrid reached an agreement with Manchester United earlier this month to sign JJ Gabriel; The teenager requested to leave United in September and has not trained with the club since; the 16-year-old and his camp are confident there is a clear pathway to the first team at Real Madrid

JJ Gabriel has announced he is leaving Manchester United as he looks forward to his "next chapter" at Real Madrid.

Sky Sports News revealed earlier this month that Real have reached an agreement to sign the 16-year-old prodigy from United.

Gabriel is registered with Manchester United's academy until the end of the season but Real Madrid are intending to sign him in January.

There is a FIFA loophole that allows European clubs to tie down young players to professional contracts if they have a European passport which Gabriel qualifies for through both parents.

In a farewell post on Instagram he said: "Dear Manchester United, I arrived in 2020. as a 10-year-old boy with big dreams.

"Here I grew as a player and as a person. From scoring at Old Trafford to receiving the Jimmy Murphy Award.

"You gave me moments I'll always treasure. To every coach, every member of staff, to my team-mates and everyone who supported me, thank you for believing in me and helping me grow.

"As I begin my next chapter, Manchester United will always be a part of my story. Thank you for everything. JJ."

Gabriel did not make a competitive first-team appearance for United, but did make a senior appearance in United's pre-season friendly defeat to AC Milan this summer.

The teenager requested to leave United in September and has not trained with the club since. When Gabriel's camp made it clear he wanted to move on, they asked to be de-registered immediately for the player to become a free agent.

Sky Sports News understands a big factor as to why Gabriel is leaning towards a move to Real Madrid is because he and his camp are confident there is a clear pathway to the first team at the Bernebeu.

The annual GM Meetings came and went last week with two significant transactions. The Braves signed reliever Evan Phillips (three years and $35 million) and the Red Sox signed slugger Jake Bauers (four years and $58 million). Bauers will give Boston some desperately needed home run power. Phillips will pair with Robert Suarez in the late innings for Atlanta.

Otherwise, we're still waiting on the winter's first true blockbuster, and the clock is ticking with a lockout expected upon the collective bargaining agreement's expiration at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1. Here are our top 50 free agents and top 25 trade candidates, and the important offseason dates and deadlines you need to know. Here now are Tuesday's hot stove happenings.

Teams are calling the Rangers about left-hander MacKenzie Gore, who is a year away from free agency, according to The Athletic. There is some reluctance to trade for players with only one year of control because games could be lost to a work stoppage next year, but pitching is so in-demand that at least some clubs are exploring a deal for Gore.

Now 27, Gore is one of only six pitchers to make at least 30 starts in each of the last three years. His under-the-hood numbers (3.87 FIP and 4.19 expected ERA) were much better than his 4.58 actual ERA, plus he has long been considered to have untapped upside. Gore will give you innings, at minimum, and there's a chance his best days are ahead of him.

The Red Sox are exploring the trade market for righties Kutter Crawford, Tanner Houck, and Johan Oviedo, according to WEEI. They are also believed to be preparing to trade Jarren Duran, reports MassLive.com. Duran is a potential non-tender candidate if the Red Sox are unable to find a trade partner prior to Friday's deadline. The Mariners and Phillies are among the teams that are "poking around."

Crawford, Houck, and Ovideo all missed significant time with injuries in 2026 (Crawford has not appeared in an MLB game since 2024) and are far down Boston's rotation depth chart. Duran has been pushed out of the everyday lineup by the recent Jake Bauers signing. Exploring trades for these four players in a seller's market is a sensible approach.

The Mets had an in-person meeting with top-ranked free agent Tarik Skubal on Monday, reports the New York Post. Owner Steve Cohen and president of baseball operations David Stearns were among those in attendance, according to SNY. Skubal and agent Scott Boras will meet with the Blue Jays, Dodgers, Padres, and potentially two other teams in the coming days.

Skubal is expected to sign before the lockout and is therefore working on a tight schedule, much tighter than usual for top Boras clients. Nolan McLean and Christian Scott give the Mets two promising young arms to build around, but they need to improve the rotation around them, and Skubal would give the club a no-doubt ace to lead the pitching staff.

The pitching-needy Cubs have shown interest in free agent lefty Trevor Rogers, reports The Athletic. Chicago needs multiple starters this offseason and has cast a wide net in their search. Justin Steele (UCL) and Ben Brown (neck), ostensibly the Cubs' No. 1 and 2 starters, are both recovering from surgeries, and Brown is expected to miss the start of 2027.

We ranked Rogers as the third-best free agent available this offseason because he is very good as is, and because he'll make analytical models hum with the quality of his arsenal and strike-throwing. Nine pitchers made at least nine starts for the Cubs in 2026 and only two -- Javier Assad and Edward Cabrera -- currently remain under contract/team control.

The AL East rival Orioles and Yankees are among the teams with interest in free agent slugger Randy Arozarena, according to the Baltimore Banner and New York Post. The O's also have some interest in re-signing Luis Robert Jr. Other than Dylan Beavers and maybe Tyler O'Neill, Baltimore's outfield is wide open right now. They have DH at-bats available too.

It is likely the Yankees checked in on Arozarena out of due diligence more than serious interest. His righty bat would help their lefty-heavy lineup, though he would be a clunky roster fit as a corner outfielder who really needs to DH on a team that already has plenty of corner outfielders who really need to DH. If Arozarena's market is slow to develop, perhaps New York would pounce.

Teams around the league believe the Rockies are more open to trading All-Star catcher Hunter Goodman now than they were at the trade deadline, reports MLB Network. Colorado is said to have set a very high asking price for Goodman, who in 2026 became just the tenth primary catcher with a 40-homer season. He hit more homers on the road (25) than at Coors Field (16) too.

Ryan Jeffers and Carson Kelly represent viable free agent alternatives to Goodman as right-handed offense-first catchers, though Goodman is several years younger than both and will have a lower salary the next three years through arbitration. Teams will likely feel more confident in Goodman holding his production for another few years than Jeffers and Kelly.

Ryo Asai’s ‘In the Megachurch’ Sweeps 3 Charts on Inaugural Billboard Japan Book Charts Year-End Lists

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Yuu Nagira's Nanji, Hoshi no Gotoku came in at No. 2, followed by Mina Miyajima's Naruse wa Tenka wo Tori ni Iku at No. 3.

Billboard Japan announced the winning titles and authors of the first annual Billboard Japan Book Charts, its comprehensive book charts, at the year-end awards ceremony held Saturday (Nov. 14). The tracking period for the year ran from Oct. 27, 2025, to Oct. 25, 2026, with the exception of the Hot Shot Books chart, which ranks surging titles, tracked from Nov. 24, 2025, to Oct. 25, 2026. The ceremony was one of the programs at BOOK MEETS NEXT 2026’s special event exploring the possibilities of an award showcasing the appeal of Japanese books to a wider global readership. BOOK MEETS NEXT is the autumn reading promotion month organized by the Japan Publishing Industry Foundation for Culture (JPIC).

Ryo Asai’s In The Megachurch was No. 1 for the year on the all-genre Japan Book Hot 100 list. By metric, it placed No. 1 in social media, No. 7 in subscriptions and No. 13 in brick-and-mortar sales. The novel earned its most points in April, the month it won the 2026 Hon’ya Taisho (Japan Booksellers’ Award). Its points also rose during the year-end and New Year holidays, Golden Week and Silver Week, when demand for reading is high.

“I’m very grateful this book chart exists, because it adds one more way for books to reach the world,” Asai said. “From that point of view, it’s probably not a great thing for the world at large that the Japan Booksellers’ Award winner and the No. 1 book on this chart are the same title. But for me personally, it means a lot, and I’m really, really happy.”

Yuu Nagira’s Nanji, Hoshi no Gotoku came in at No. 2, followed by Mina Miyajima’s Naruse wa Tenka wo Tori ni Iku at No. 3, Eiichiro Oda’s ONE PIECE Vol. 113 at No. 4 and Asako Yuzuki’s BUTTER at No. 5. In The Megachurch also topped the Hot Bungei Books literary fiction genre chart, and the Reiwa Books era chart that groups titles by the year their first edition was published, for a total of three No. 1s.

Among the other genre charts, ONE PIECE Vol. 113 led the Hot Manga chart for the year. Ryo@Ribe Daigaku Gakucho’s Honto no Jiyu wo Te ni Ireru Okane no Daigaku topped the Hot Economy Books business chart, and Fuma Kikuchi 1st Photobook “Latido” led the Hot Culture Books chart.

On the era charts, Yukito Ayatsuji’s Jukkakukan no Satsujin took No. 1 on the Showa Books chart, while Asako Yuzuki’s BUTTER topped the Heisei Books chart. Jujutsu Kaisen ≡ (Jujutsu Kaisen Modulo) Vol. 1 by Gege Akutami and Yuji Iwasaki was No. 1 for the year on the Hot Shot Books chart, which picks out books whose total points grew by a set percentage and lists only the titles drawing a sudden surge in attention.

The Billboard Japan Book Charts are Japan’s first comprehensive book charts, aggregating data across print (brick-and-mortar and e-commerce), e-books, libraries and social media. While new manga releases with strong print sales tended to reach the upper tier of the weekly Japan Book Hot 100, literary works stood out near the top of the year-end chart, supported by steady reader activity such as library lending and word of mouth on social media. The full year-end results are available on the Billboard Japan website.

Humanity braces for an alien invasion in 3 Body Problem S2 teaser

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Wake up! They haven’t come 24 trillion miles to negotiate. They are coming to exterminate us.”

You might be excused for forgetting about the existence of 3 Body Problem, Netflix’s heady sci-fi series adapted from the award-winning novel The Three-Body Problem by Liu Cixin, the first book in his Remembrance of Earth’s Past trilogy. After all, it debuted in March 2024. But it was an exceptionally well-done, thematically rich series, and we’re finally getting the second of a planned three seasons in January. Netflix just dropped the first teaser.

Created by David Benioff, D.B. Weiss (Game of Thrones), and Alexander Woo (True Blood), the first season followed two primary timelines. In the earlier one, a young Chinese astrophysicist, Ye Wenjie (Rosalind Chao), witnessed her father being beaten to death by Red Guards during the Cultural Revolution. Ye figured out a means of sending an interstellar message to possible extraterrestrial civilizations, receiving a response from a planet called Trisolaris.

The planet had three suns, which wreaked havoc on Trisolaris via unpredictable “chaotic periods”—hence the novel’s title, which refers to a classic problem in celestial mechanics. Despite being warned that the aliens intended to invade and conquer Earth, Ye responded to the message and invited them to do so, bitter and disillusioned by the state of the world. She eventually became an established professor (Zine Tseng played the older version).

In the present day, the consequences of Ye’s fateful decision began to play out, beginning with a series of mysterious suicides by scientists, many of whom had possessed a highly advanced VR gaming headset. Among the dead: a particle physicist named Vera Ye (daughter of Ye Wenjie). Five of her former students (known as the Oxford Five) joined forces to figure out what was going on, eventually finding themselves caught in a global fight to save Earth from the invading Trisolarians (or San-Ti). Another human cult faction was just as intent on enabling the San-Ti invasion, believing they would be spared as a reward, while Ye Wenjie still believed the entire human race deserved to be annihilated.

The second season will adapt the second novel in the series, The Dark Forest, with the third book, Death’s End, serving as source material for the third season. Per the official S2 synopsis: “As the alien invasion nears, humanity prepares to meet them—on Earth and beyond. While a space fleet embarks on a mission that spans centuries, a secret locked inside of one man’s mind might be humanity’s only hope for survival.”

We lost two members of the Oxford Five in S1: Jack Rooney (John Bradley), killed by cult member Tatiana (Marlo Kelly); and physics teacher Will Downing (Alex Sharp), who was dying of cancer and chose to euthanize himself and donate his cryogenically frozen brain to a bold effort to stave off the invasion. (While the probe carrying Will’s brain veered off-course in the season finale, the original plan was for the San-Ti to use the brain to reconstruct his body, so some version of Will could show up again.)

Kelly’s Tatiana is returning for S2, along with surviving Oxford Five members Jin Cheng (Jess Hong), Saul Durand (Jovan Adepo), and Auggie Salazar (Eiza Gonzalez), whose pioneering nanofibers were memorably used in S1 to slice through a ship in the Panama Canal, killing all the pro-alien cult members on board in gruesome fashion. Also returning are Liam Cunningham as Thomas Wade; Benedict Wong as MI6 agent Clarence “Da” Shi; Sea Shimooka as Sophon, an avatar in the show’s mysterious VR game; and Saamer Usmani as Jin’s Royal Navy officer boyfriend Prithviraj Varma.

New cast members include Claudia Doumit as Captain Van Rijn and Ellie de Lange as Ayla. In addition, Alfie Allen, David Yip, and Jordan Sunshine will appear in as-yet-undisclosed guest roles.

The second season of 3 Body Problem drops on Netflix on January 14, 2027.

‘Both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed’: Fed advisor Marc Andreessen on AI going from boom to bust

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Marc Andreessen got the last word at the Federal Reserve’s big AI conference in San Francisco on Monday, and he used it to tell the central bank that nearly everyone in the room was going to be let down.

“My line has been both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed,” the venture capitalist said at the San Francisco Fed, “specifically disappointed by the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact, and specifically the economic impact.”

Andreessen co-leads the Productivity and Jobs Task Force, which Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh set up to tell the central bank what super intelligence—“SI,” in the White House’s preferred term—will do to growth, prices and jobs. On Monday, Andreessen previewed what he’ll contribute to the group’s report. Without using the name, he described one of the oldest problems in economics: William Baumol’s “cost disease,” the reason flat-screen TVs get cheaper every year while hospital bills, tuition and rent go the other way. His case is that AI won’t cure it, and could even make it worse.

The Fed hasn’t quite laid out that outcome.

The task force is advisory, and Andreessen has no vote on interest rates. He co-leads it with Stanford economist Charles Jones and Microsoft executive Asha Sharma, one of five outside panels the Fed named in June to rethink how the central bank works. Its charge, per the Fed, is to assess the economic impact of “new general-purpose technologies, including super intelligence, to inform the Federal Reserve’s policy judgments.” The panels report to the rate-setting committee with recommendations due by year-end, though Warsh said in October that “some, but not all” would turn their homework in on time. The Fed’s task force page lists no ethics or recusal terms for the panels. Andreessen’s firm, Andreessen Horowitz, raised more than $15 billion in new funds in January, as venture firms raced to keep up with the AI boom.

Andreessen is also Silicon Valley’s best-known accelerationist. His 2023 “Techno-Optimist Manifesto” declared, “We believe in accelerationism,” which he defined as “the conscious and deliberate propulsion of technological development.” It listed @BasedBeffJezos, the pseudonymous account behind the “effective accelerationism,” or e/acc, movement, first among its “patron saints,” and it named the enemy: “Our enemy is stagnation.” When OpenAI’s board fired Sam Altman a month later, Andreessen posted simply “E/acc!” This is the man who told the Fed that stagnation might win out over an AI-powered industrial revolution on Monday.

Warsh opened the day by laying out three “illustrative scenarios” Fed board staff have begun to evaluate. In one, AI lifts productivity, the gains are broadly shared and the economy stays close to full employment. In another, growth still runs above its long-term average, but automation pushes unemployment higher. In the third, AI “could disappoint after significant capital investment and stock market gains,” stocks fall, the economy slows and it eventually recovers “without the productivity gains.”

All three turn on whether AI arrives in force. Andreessen’s version is duller: most of the economy just doesn’t let it in.

He splits the economy in two. The “fast sectors,” like consumer electronics, media and commodity goods, take up new technology quickly, and “what those sectors all have in common is they have rapidly falling prices.” The “slow sectors”—health care, education, housing, and law and government—“are not legally allowed to have rapid change, like literally not.”

The machine “cannot get an MD, cannot prescribe medication,” he said. “The machine cannot get admitted to the bar.” The slow sectors, in his telling, pair restricted supply with subsidized demand: you can’t easily build new housing or open new hospitals, and when voters revolt over the cost, Washington steps in with student loans and housing subsidies. “So, of course, prices can only go up.”

Then he followed it to its end. As prices crash in the fast sectors, they shrink as a share of GDP, and the slow sectors take over. Health care, he said, “is already a fifth of US GDP and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%.” (The government’s count is 18% in 2024, or $5.3 trillion, per CMS actuaries.)

“You might interpret this as good news because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is productivity growth just simply may not grow very much. Economic growth just simply may not grow very much. We may just continue to stagnate.”

Andreessen called himself “extremely optimistic,” saying that AI gives the country “a magic formula” and comparing it to being “handed this elixir to boost productivity growth.” But policy is a problem, he suggested. The decisions about what to do with AI, he said, “are going to matter actually way more than what the technology is capable of,” because without them “the technology will simply not get rolled out.”

That landed in a room where, three hours earlier, Warsh had brought up “secular stagnation,” the 1930s worry that economists returned to after the financial crisis, and asked whether superintelligence “dramatically” changes that picture.

Baumol, the late New York University and Princeton economist, worked this out in the 1960s, starting with the economics of the performing arts. A string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same playing time it did in Mozart’s day, but those musicians have to be paid wages set by an economy where factory workers keep getting more productive. So live music, and later education and medicine, keep getting more expensive relative to everything else, and keep taking a bigger share of spending.

In his 2012 book, The Cost Disease, Baumol projected that if health costs kept growing at their recent pace, they would rise from 15% of the average person’s income in 2005 to 62% by 2105. He was an optimist about it. “No matter how painful rising medical and educational bills may be, society can afford them,” he wrote, because productivity elsewhere makes everyone richer. The real threat, he added, was “foolish public policy.”

For Baumol, the disease was built into the work: you can’t speed up a string quartet. For Andreessen, it’s built into the law. That matters for the Fed, because an agency that sets interest rates can’t fix zoning boards or medical licensing.

The economist who has done the most to bring Baumol into the AI debate was in the audience. Jones, who is on leave at Anthropic, stayed quiet because the company is preparing to go public, as Andreessen remarked to the crowd. In a 2017 paper for the National Bureau of Economic Research, Jones, Philippe Aghion and Benjamin Jones argued that “growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve,” crediting the idea to Baumol.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly asked a panel of executives where their “weak link” was, and Erik Wexler, CEO of the nonprofit hospital system Providence, gave the slow-sector answer. “We’re part of the weak link,” he said. Providence operates in seven states, and as each writes its own AI rules, “it’s almost like sometimes being in seven different countries.”

Earlier on the same panel, though, Wexler complicated Andreessen’s picture. AI tools that listen in on appointments and write up the notes are saving some physicians “two to four hours of time a day,” he said, and doctors have called it “life-changing.” In 35 years in the industry, he said, he’d never heard them say that about anything else. So the hours are being saved inside a slow sector. The open question is whether they ever show up in the price of a hospital visit.

Andreessen’s forecast cuts against the case Warsh made before he got the job. In a Wall Street Journal op-ed late last year, Warsh wrote that AI “will be a significant disinflationary force, increasing productivity and bolstering American competitiveness,” as Axios reported. In speeches and writing, he argued AI-driven productivity could justify lower interest rates, an echo of Alan Greenspan’s Fed holding off on hikes in the late 1990s.

As chair, he has hedged. “My judgment is we have seen an improvement in productivity; we can’t measure it precisely,” he told reporters on Oct. 28, adding that “most of that change in productivity precedes everything we’re talking about—super intelligence.” The Fed held rates at 3.75% to 4% that day, and a reporter told him most of his colleagues “seem to be on board with raising rates in December.” Warsh declined to prejudge the meeting.

On Monday, Warsh didn’t push back on Andreessen. He restated the argument in Fed terms, as product prices versus services prices, and moved on. He closed the day “cautiously optimistic,” calling it “a hinge point for the US economy,” and said, “I’d rather have our problems than anyone else’s in the world.”

Andreessen has been making this case for years. In 2017 he warned that the slow sectors were “basically just going to eat the economy,” and in March 2023 he wrote that “AI is simply already illegal across most of the economy.” Back then the argument was a rebuttal to the job-loss doomers. On Monday, in front of the Fed chair, it came with the other half of the bargain: if the jobs are safe, the productivity boom may be stuck too.

For companies, the slow sectors are a line item. Workers now pay an average of $7,471 a year toward employer family health coverage, up 9% from 2025, according to KFF’s annual survey. That’s the part of the economy that, by Andreessen’s account, AI isn’t legally allowed to touch.

For the Fed, the question is what kind of disinflation AI brings, if any. Earlier in the day, Warsh asked a panel of economists whether AI might do to services what the China shock did to goods—“productizing services”—and push prices down. Stanford’s Nick Bloom said falling token costs made him “kind of bullish.” Stanford President Jonathan Levin gave Baumol’s answer without the name: health care and education are “the parts of the economy where the disinflationary pressures from automation have not hit,” where “the costs have continued to rise with wages.” Whether that changes depends on what people want, he said, “and there’s also regulation.”

If Andreessen is right, AI’s price cuts land mostly where prices were already falling, and services, where inflation has been hardest to bring down, stay out of reach.

Stripe CEO Patrick Collison sketched the range for the room. At one end sits the old complaint that new technology shows up “everywhere but in the productivity statistics,” a line that goes back to economist Robert Solow in 1987. At the other is a bet between two people Collison described as having “considerable influence” over AI, on whether global energy use doubles in a single month sometime in the next decade. Andreessen is betting on a third outcome: a decade where nothing that dramatic happens, and the prices Americans complain about most keep climbing.

Warsh has asked his task forces for recommendations by year-end, and the Fed’s next rate decision comes Dec. 9. Andreessen put the choice to the central bank as “a political economy” question: “what kind of pace of change do we want, how much productivity growth do we actually want?”

Jensen Huang Says He'd Be 'Honored' to Return as Nvidia CEO, Despite Board's Resistance

Jensen Huang said Tuesday he would be willing to return as Nvidia CEO, publicly addressing the campaign to reinstate him less than a week after his abrupt departure. His offer came despite reports that the chipmaker's board has no plans to bring him back.

“If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO,” Huang wrote in a statement on X.

The Nvidia co-founder also apologized for the conduct that triggered his exit. “I did not live up to the standards expected of everyone at NVIDIA, and I have made serious mistakes in my personal life,” he wrote. “I created problems for the Board that I deeply regret.”

“I own my mistakes completely, and I am committed to cooperating with the Board’s independent investigation,” he added.

Over the past week, I’ve been overwhelmed by the love and support of friends, industry colleagues, and people I've never met. Thank you to everyone who has shown kindness to my family and me. It means the world to me.

His X profile underwent a separate change around the same time. By Tuesday, Huang had removed “CEO” from his bio, which now identifies him only as “Founder of NVIDIA”; the company banner and corporate affiliation badge also disappeared.

Huang resigned last Thursday after 33 years leading the chipmaker he founded in 1993. His departure followed disclosures about extramarital relationships involving Nvidia employees and a business executive whom U.S. officials suspect of connections to Chinese intelligence. Those intelligence allegations have not been established as fact.

The board's independent investigation is examining his conduct and whether sensitive company information or national security were put at risk. Nvidia has said it has no reason to believe company secrets were compromised; chief financial officer Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO.

“Since my departure, I’ve seen an outpouring of calls from NVIDIA’s employees, investors, business partners, and customers for me to return as CEO,” Huang wrote. He said many were reaching out directly as Nvidia navigates “complex technology, market, competitive, and geopolitical dynamics.” “NVIDIA is a once-in-a-generation company,” he added.

But the board's position—at least as of Tuesday—is that directors are focused on the investigation, not reinstatement.

The company is simultaneously looking for a successor. Directors reportedly approached Arm CEO Rene Haas, a former Nvidia executive, according to the Financial Times, although Nvidia said it had not formally engaged candidates and called speculation premature.

The movement to restore Huang has nevertheless attracted powerful allies. President Donald Trump said Monday he “would certainly not mind” a comeback, while Elon Musk backed a petition demanding Huang's reinstatement that had drawn nearly 192,000 signatures by Monday.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman offered a more qualified defense. Altman, who was briefly fired and reinstated at OpenAI in 2023, said Huang had “long been a hero of mine” and that “heroes have flaws,” but stopped short of saying Nvidia should rehire him.

Huang also made a patriotic appeal, especially considering his outsting was especially rushed not because of his affairs but because of the possibility of him leaking secrets to China. “I came to the United States 53 years ago, and becoming an American citizen is one of the greatest gifts and proudest moments of my life,” he wrote.

“NVIDIA has been my life’s work,” he continued. “Nowhere but in America could a company like NVIDIA be possible. I love our country, and I love NVIDIA.”

He closed by thanking supporters and telling his family, “I love you, and I will spend the rest of my life being worthy of yours.” Nvidia, meanwhile, is continuing its permanent CEO search, with Kress scheduled to lead the company's quarterly earnings call later today.
