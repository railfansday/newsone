Maven Clinic, a virtual provider for women’s and family health, is making its new DTC vertical in-network with over 475 commercial health plans.

The first program to be in-network, starting now, is hormone care. Starting on Jan. 1, 2027, its GLP-1 program will also be covered. It is able to tap into the vast network through direct contracts and a third-party partner that it can’t name on the record. Coverage and applicable copays will vary by plan.

“We’re really doing this because of access [gaps] … the in-network coverage removes that barrier,” Alex Kubal, general manager of Maven's Consumer Division, told Fierce Healthcare.

Also in 2027, Maven plans to roll out in its DTC vertical, several additional specialties, including OBGYN care, maternal-fetal medicine, nurse practitioners for general women’s health and nutrition. Winter weather can be devastating to outdoor furniture, appliances, and structures, which is why many people store as much as possible in a covered shed or garage throughout the cold season. Even though not everything can be neatly tucked away, such as your deck, walkways, and gates, you can take steps to prepare them for harsh winter conditions before snow starts to fall.

Outdoor winterization projects that involve painting, staining, or sealing help surfaces create a durable barrier against freeze-thaw cycles, moisture intrusion, and corrosive deicing salts. It’s important to complete these protective jobs before the first hard freeze so you can prevent deep structural damage during the winter months.

Leaving wood decks, fences, or patio furniture bare during winter allows moisture from snow and ice to seep into the wood pores. When trapped water freezes, it expands, causing the wood to split, warp, and rot. A fresh coat of waterproof sealant or oil-based stain acts as a hydrophobic barrier. Water pools on top of the sealed surface rather than penetrating the wood grain.

Before applying a compatible exterior stain or water-repellent finish, clean the surface, remove failing finish, and allow the wood to dry sufficiently. For decking and steps, choose a winterization product specifically approved for horizontal surfaces and foot traffic.

Marc Sharkey, owner of Dynamic Painting Inc, says that “the finish reduces water absorption from rain and melting snow. Repeated wetting and drying can contribute to checking, splitting, and warping; prolonged dampness also creates conditions for decay.

“Pay particular attention to exposed board ends and areas where the old finish has worn away.” He also warns: “A new coating cannot repair rotten wood.” Given that Sharkey’s business is based in Alberta, he has experience dealing with some of the harshest winters in North America.

Winter preparation is important to safeguard your home. Chipped paint on steel or iron railings, furniture, and gates exposes the raw metal to oxygen and winter humidity, rapidly accelerating the oxidation process. Road salt or salt-based sidewalk ice melts further expedite this corrosion, eating through the metal structures. Applying a coat of exterior, rust-resistant enamel or oil-based paint seals out moisture and oxygen, halting the chemical reaction that creates rust.

It’s a budget-friendly winterization project that also forms a slick, chemical-resistant barrier against salt splash-back. Before applying a coating, remove loose rust and flaking paint and clean away dirt and salt. Then apply an appropriate rust-inhibiting primer and exterior metal finish. Check compatibility carefully: steel, aluminum, and galvanized metal can require different preparation and primers.

“An intact coating limits exposure to moisture and oxygen, helping slow corrosion. Left exposed, rust can spread beneath surrounding paint, causing more peeling and pitting. Over time, severe corrosion can weaken components. Painting over loose rust does not solve the underlying problem,” explains Sharkey.

Moisture from ice and snow can cause significant damage to concrete because it is a highly porous material. When snow melts on walkways, driveways, or patios, the concrete absorbs the water. Subsequent freezing temperatures cause that internal water to expand, which leads to surface flaking, scaling, and deep cracking.

Unlike surface coatings, penetrating sealers chemically bond inside concrete pores. They create an invisible, breathable hydrophobic barrier that prevents liquid water from sinking into the stone, while still allowing internal vapor to escape.

Sharkey notes that “for compatible, absorbent concrete steps, walkways, or patios, a penetrating sealer rated for exterior concrete and freeze-thaw exposure can reduce water absorption. Confirm compatibility with any existing treatment and follow the preparation instructions.”

Reducing water and deicing-salt penetration helps protect against winter deterioration. However, just keep in mind that a sealer is preventive maintenance; it does not repair cracks, failing concrete, or drainage problems.

Databricks on November 11, 2026, introduced a new Genie that it describes as an autonomous AI coworker for continuous, long-running enterprise work. The assistant can run scheduled or event-triggered jobs, follow up on missing information and manage several assignments at once, while users decide which actions require approval.

Databricks says thousands of customers already use Genie in production. The new version is intended to take responsibility for the work around an analysis: monitoring changes, requesting input and acting as new information arrives. Its examples include routing insurance claims that move outside underwriting guidelines and proposing marketing budget changes for approval.

At the center is Genie Ontology, now generally available. It automatically builds reusable knowledge snippets from dashboards, SQL queries and notebooks, while Unity Catalog Pages and Metric Views preserve explicitly governed definitions. Databricks’ OntoRank system weighs source authority, certification, user relevance, popularity and recency to select context and resolve conflicting definitions.

Genie Search is also generally available for Google Drive, Jira, Confluence and Workday, with SharePoint in beta. It indexes content from those systems, enforces their permissions before returning results and can combine the index with SQL and MCP connectors, which retrieve live information and take actions in external tools. Organizational directories, also generally available and beginning with Workday, add reporting relationships and collaboration signals to help rank relevant knowledge.

Not every component is generally available. External BI knowledge sources are in beta, initially supporting Power BI, and Ontology Manager for reviewing and curating the ontology is coming soon in beta.

Alongside the core assistant, Genie Agents can now analyze PDFs, slide decks and other documents alongside governed tables in Public Preview, returning page-level citations. Native MCP connectors are generally available, allowing agents to retrieve context and take actions such as creating tickets, updating records and posting messages. OpenSharing and Genie Clean Rooms are in beta for use with partners and customers.

OpenSharing provides a governed agent preloaded with data, instructions and knowledge across Databricks accounts. Clean Rooms instead lets recipients ask questions without seeing the underlying data or proprietary logic, with conversation and output-size controls. Databricks reports that General Motors has deployed more than 1,000 Genie agents across sales, engineering, supply chain and HR operations, illustrating the multi-department model the company is pursuing.

The autonomy is paired with Unity Gateway controls. Traces capture the question, answer, cited objects, response time and actions, giving administrators a queryable record of Genie’s work. Administrators can restrict MCP tools, require approvals, set budgets and spending caps, and review usage. Genie acts under each user’s identity and can prepare an access request, but administrators and data owners retain approval authority.

Genie One, the interface for this work, also creates slide decks, dashboards and interactive pages, with scheduled refreshes and shared skills for repeatable workflows. Databricks says Genie One is free for users through January 31, 2027, and that more detailed cost controls will be available before then.

To support its emphasis on context, Databricks released a benchmark developed with Turing. In the company’s evaluation, Genie passed 91% of task runs, compared with 58% for the stronger of two anonymized frontier agents, while averaging 431,829 tokens and 100.3 seconds per run. Each agent ran every task five times in the same workspace for a fictional UK energy supplier containing tables, notebooks, dashboards, saved queries and documents, including outdated and duplicated assets.

Databricks cautions that the test reflects an established deployment with accumulated business knowledge; differences may be smaller in new deployments or when table schemas alone are sufficient. That limitation defines the central enterprise requirement in this launch: broader autonomy depends on governed, accessible institutional knowledge, with administrators deciding which jobs and actions Genie can take on.

Google DeepMind’s AlphaGenome 2 Predicts Cell-State Variant Effects

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google DeepMind announced AlphaGenome 2 on November 11, 2026, a deep-learning model that predicts how DNA variants affect gene regulation in individual cell types and states. Trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles, it is available for non-commercial research and in private preview for commercial research.

The release addresses a blind spot in tissue-wide genomic models: a variant can matter only in a specialized cell or in a cell activated by disease, leaving its signal diluted in a tissue average. AlphaGenome 2 instead conditions each prediction on a selected tissue, cell type or cell state, including conditions generated from a researcher’s own single-cell experiment.

The earlier AlphaGenome 1 predicted molecular activity across broad tissues, while AlphaGenome Atlas offered predictions for all 9 billion possible single-letter changes in the human genome. AlphaGenome 2 is a separate model release, not an extension of that precomputed atlas.

AlphaGenome 1 generated fixed outputs for bulk tissues and cell lines simultaneously from a one-million-letter DNA sequence. AlphaGenome 2 evaluates one condition at a time and supplies that condition throughout the network, allowing it to represent how the same DNA segment is read differently across cell types. Its readouts include gene expression, RNA splicing and chromatin accessibility, with CpG DNA methylation added to the original model’s capabilities.

The technical paper describes training on human and mouse bulk and single-cell data. Researchers can supply single-cell RNA-seq data, a snapshot of gene activity, from a new experiment, and a cell encoder turns it into a condition without further fine-tuning. That allows predictions for datasets and cell states the model has not seen before.

The same design can fill in missing experimental readouts. Given gene-expression data alone, AlphaGenome 2 can predict unmeasured properties such as chromatin accessibility—whether stretches of DNA are open to regulation—helping researchers prioritize subsequent experiments. The trade-off is computational: every condition requires a separate prediction, making the model more intensive to run than fixed-track multitask models.

DeepMind’s paper reports that on TenK10K, a single-cell immune dataset spanning 28 cell types, AlphaGenome 2 recovered 30% of putatively causal expression-linked variants at 90% precision, compared with 16% for AlphaGenome 1 fine-tuned on single-cell data. It recovered 43% of variant effect directions at 90% accuracy, versus 23%. On genome regions excluded from training, the model also explained a median 34% of within-tissue, cell-type-specific gene-expression variance, against 23% for the same fine-tuned predecessor. These evaluations measure regulatory differences between related cells, the problem the conditional architecture is designed to address.

DeepMind also reports two research examples of cell-state-specific analysis. In an inflammatory bowel disease analysis, AlphaGenome 2 flagged rs2836882 from a seven-variant credible set, linked it to ETS2 approximately 290 kilobases away and localized the predicted effect to gut monocytes and macrophages, reproducing a mechanism previously established experimentally. Using single-cell data from a COVID-19 mRNA vaccine study that was new to the model, it distinguished vaccine-activated T cells from resting T cells and predicted a stronger effect for a variant associated with severe COVID-19 in the activated state. Together, the examples illustrate how a variant’s predicted effect can depend on both cell type and current state.

DeepMind precomputed two resources for researchers who do not want to run large-scale comparisons themselves. One maps enhancer–gene links across 640 primary single-cell reference conditions and 39,323 protein-coding and long non-coding RNA genes; enhancers are regulatory DNA segments that can act far from their target genes. Another scores more than 6.8 million unique common and trait-associated single-letter variants across 510 human conditions to nominate possible causal variants, target genes, effect directions and relevant cell states.

At launch, AlphaGenome 2 and expression-conditioning Colab tutorials were available through the AlphaGenome API for non-commercial research, with commercial research in private preview. The official documentation says non-commercial API use is free and query rates vary with demand. DeepMind also plans AlphaGenome 2 Skills for Google Antigravity, its agentic development platform. Genomics PLC CEO Peter Donnelly said he looked forward to incorporating AlphaGenome 2 into the company’s Mystra platform and described sequence-prediction models as tools for moving from association signals to information drug-development programs need.

The paper cautions that even at high precision, AlphaGenome 2 recovers only a minority of the expression-linked and disease-associated variants that statistical analyses nominate as likely causal. It can underestimate effects outside accessible chromatin, at very distant regulatory elements or through mechanisms acting after transcription. Transient, stimulated and disease states remain underrepresented in training and the precomputed variant panel, and fine-grained neuronal subpopulations remain difficult to distinguish.

Those condition representations come from observational, steady-state profiles and therefore capture correlations rather than causal relationships. It remains uncertain whether the model can predict responses to gene knockouts, overexpression or dynamic cell-state transitions. DeepMind restricts both AlphaGenome versions to research use; neither has been validated or approved for clinical or diagnostic purposes.

The central consequence is a more targeted route from a disease association to a candidate variant, target gene and cell state. AlphaGenome 2 can narrow that search across hundreds of conditions, but its incomplete variant recovery and correlational view of cell states mean the resulting hypotheses still depend on experimental follow-up.

Fractile Secures Over £500M in Series C With UK State Backing

The UK government announced on November 11, 2026 that its Sovereign AI Venture Fund and the British Business Bank are backing Fractile in a Series C round worth more than £500 million, valuing the London- and Bristol-based AI chip startup at more than £5 billion.

CapitalG, Lightspeed and Redpoint are also investing. The British Business Bank said its backing is intended to help Fractile access long-term capital to scale its AI hardware technology in the UK, while Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds said the company plans to expand its presence in Bristol.

The release does not break out the public investors’ individual contributions, describing them as commercially sensitive. Sovereign AI’s typical equity investments are around £1 million to £10 million, although each company’s circumstances differ.

The Series C follows Fractile’s May 2026 announcement that it had raised $220 million to build next-generation inference hardware.

Fractile was founded in summer 2022 around the premise that the time AI systems take to produce useful outputs would limit their impact. The company says it is building chips and systems to generate thousands of tokens—the units of output produced by a model—per second while supporting large models at very long contexts.

Its full-stack work spans chips, software and machine-learning research. The objective is faster, more energy-efficient and less costly hardware for inference, the stage at which a model generates output.

The earlier company essay illustrated the scale Fractile is targeting. It said a model generating 100 million tokens at roughly 40 tokens per second would take about a month, while approximately 1,200 tokens per second would be needed to reduce that to a day. The essay identified limited memory-bandwidth scaling on current architectures as a key constraint.

Fractile argues that faster, cheaper inference could support longer sequences of work and make more demanding applications economically viable, pointing to potential uses in drug discovery, software engineering and materials discovery. That links the company’s speed target to the commercial rationale for its hardware program.

For the UK government, the investment also serves an industrial-policy purpose. It says backing British chip designers and hardware companies is intended to strengthen domestic capability in critical technologies, reduce dependence on fragile overseas supply chains and retain more AI jobs, expertise and economic value in Britain.

The British Business Bank describes its broader role as helping innovative companies access finance to start, scale and stay in the UK. Alongside the Fractile announcement, the government highlighted a further £140 million for Sovereign AI confirmed in the Budget: £60 million to support fund investments and £80 million for its research-and-development procurement scheme.

Sovereign AI is also expanding into physical and industrial AI, including robotics, autonomous systems and advanced manufacturing. Fractile’s round therefore sits within a wider effort to support both AI companies and the computing capabilities behind them, with Bristol’s planned expansion providing a local dimension to that strategy.

For Fractile, the next test is turning that private and public capital into hardware that meets its speed, efficiency and cost goals. That outcome will determine whether the round advances both the company’s inference roadmap and the government’s effort to retain more of the AI hardware industry’s value in Britain.

No Tongues! AI Researchers Address Missing Anatomy in Facial Animation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Missed in 2026: It’s that time of year when the research scene dips and spikes in quality and volume in lockstep with the last few conferences of November and December. To traverse these sporadic famines, we’re taking a look at some of the more interesting papers that nearly made the cut in 2026, but were ousted by other works, in more competitive submission periods.

Until recently, deepfake practitioners, amateur and professional alike, had bigger concerns to address than whether or not the reconstructed human appeared to have a tongue. After all, the tongue is the most mercurial of facial features – not evident at all in ‘mumblers’, and only intermittently observed in most normal contexts.

Consequently, when you do need it, as far as AI reconstructions are concerned, it’s not there. In a climate where amateur diffusion-based reconstruction has reached a stunning level of realism, and our critical faculties in regard to AI humans is becoming ever more discerning, it is perhaps time to address the issue.

In July an academic collaboration between Australia and China offered a pipeline to address the lack of tongue simulation in AI workflows. Titled TongueReenact, the proposed framework throws practically every method in the deepfake toolkit at the problem, including Gaussian Splatting, parametric humans, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), and diffusion-based video generation (sorry GANs, you didn’t make this cut).

Above, the driver (real) capture whose characteristics are transferred onto the TongueReenact render underneath. Source

Though the project’s promised code does not appear to have been delivered since the paper was published, and though no supplementary video is yet released, this is nonetheless a landmark offering, since it is the first to address the tongue in AI-based reconstruction, outside of the medical sphere – and to address the critical lack of annotated data, which is essential for any proposed solution.

The researchers made innovative use of diverse existing frameworks and data, in order to create a tongue-segmentation framework based on SegmentAnything, and to generate pseudo-labels for the newly-segmented data.

‘[We] present the first framework for cross-identity tongue dynamics transfer in face reenactment. Given a source frame and a driving video, our method transfers both facial motion and tongue articulation onto the source identity.

‘The central insight of our work is that tongue synthesis cannot be treated as a generic inpainting problem. The tongue must be spatially grounded to the source face geometry, which itself changes with every driving frame.’

The system begins by transferring the driving person’s facial movements onto the source face, using 3D Gaussian Splatting and the FLAME facial model. This produces an animated face with the correct pose and expression, but still lacking a tongue.

The TongueReenact workflow. Top: Tongue images are identified, segmented and manually refined to train a detection model. Bottom: Facial movements are transferred from a driving video to a source face, before a diffusion model generates the missing tongue in the appropriate position.

A Stable Diffusion model is then used to generate the missing tongue, guided by its appearance in the driving video and the geometry of the reconstructed face.

Regarding the need to address this shortfall in facial reconstruction, the authors observe*:

‘During expressive actions such as laughter, disgust, or surprise, [the tongue] can dominate the visible oral region entirely. Yet nearly all existing reenactment and avatar systems either model the oral interior as a textureless void or leave it entirely unconstrained.

‘This neglect is not coincidental, as the tongue is a uniquely transient structure, fully concealed at rest, intermittently exposed during speech, and suddenly dominant in extreme expressions, making it fundamentally resistant to the static parametric modeling applied to the rest of the face.

‘Addressing this overlooked challenge is the central motivation of our work.’

In tests, the new method substantially outperformed five competing facial animation systems, achieving a more than 100% accuracy advantage over its closest rival on key measures of tongue positioning and size – though it has to be conceded that tests of this kind are hard to contrive when one is breaking new ground, and when prior frameworks address the new challenge minimally, or not at all.

Let’s take a closer look at the new approach.

To develop a system capable of identifying tongues in ordinary facial footage, the NERSemble dataset of multi-view recordings of human heads was used alongside synthetically generated frames from the VFHQ dataset, which features over 16,000 high-quality facial videos:

Press to play An interestingly ‘lingual’ example of Radiance Field reconstruction from the Nersemble project site. However, the tongue seen here is treated as generic geometry, and has no distinct domain. Source

The MaskGAN face-parsing model was used to locate the mouth in each image, after which the TongueSAM medical detector was used to identify the likely tongue region, before passing it to SegmentAnything, from which an initial outline, (i.e., pseudo-label) of the visible tongue was generated.

Samples from the TongueSAM dataset, intended for analytical medical usage rather than evaluation of facial expressions. Source

The resulting masks were divided into three groups, according to how much of the tongue was visible. Incorrect examples were discarded, and promising masks manually corrected to distinguish the tongue from surrounding lips, teeth, and other mouth tissue. These refined annotations were used to train the BiSeNet image segmentation network.

The model was then applied to additional NERSemble subjects and synthetic VFHQ frames, with its predictions checked and corrected by humans before further training – a process that was repeated until performance on previously unseen subjects stabilized:

Pipeline for parsing existing data into ‘tongue-domain’ data.

For tongue synthesis, the oral region identified by BiSeNet was slightly enlarged before being passed to the diffusion model, giving the generated tongue room to extend beyond the initial segmentation, as the mouth changed shape.

Generation was performed in the latent space of Stable Diffusion Image Variations, with separate guidance derived from the reconstructed facial geometry and the tongue visible in the driving frame, and only the identified mouth/tongue region regenerated.

The generative pipeline. The DINO V2 reference in upper left quadrant encapsulates the use of GAGAvatar (see below), which use DINO V2 appearance features in concert with FLAME.

BiSeNet was trained on two output classes derived from the aforementioned data processing stages: background and tongue. Training was carried out over 50,000 iterations at a learning rate of 10-3, using 512x512px images on batch size 4, on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU with 48GB of VRAM. The training data consisted of the aforementioned NERSemble and VFHQ frames.

For the reenactment portion, the pretrained GAGAvatar Gaussian Splatting backbone was used. The Stable Diffusion Image Variations checkpoint was also used, with the VAE and CLIP encoder frozen. The Guidance encoders and denoising UNET were then trained on the NERSemble data for 85,000 iterations at batch size 8, with 512×512 inputs. For inference, 20 DDIM steps were used (for many current diffusion models, this is considered a high amount, designed to favor detail, with certain inevitable tradeoffs).

Rival avatar frameworks tested were GPAvatar; the feted LivePortrait; X-NeMo; and X-Portrait. Each of these was evaluated against 9,000 cross-identity VFHQ source/driving pairs.

Metrics used were Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS); Tongue Presence (the proportion of frames where a tongue is detected); Tongue IoU (the overlap between the generated and driving tongue regions); and Tongue Area Similarity (how closely the generated tongue’s size matched the driving tongue).

Test results comparing TongueReenact with five rival face-reenactment frameworks. TongueReenact achieves the best result across all four tongue-specific metrics; arrows indicate whether lower or higher scores are preferred.

Of the initial comparison, the authors state:

‘Our method achieves the best performance across all four tongue transfer metrics, with a Tongue IoU of 0.3582 and Tongue Area Similarity of 0.4338, more than double the nearest competitor, confirming substantially more accurate tongue dynamics transfer.

‘Tongue Presence of 0.7891 further demonstrates that our method reliably synthesizes visible tongue articulation where existing methods frequently produce none.’

Some highlights from some of the extensive qualitative examples shown in the source paper. Here we see comparisons between the new approach and the five prior contenders, none of whom are equipped to generate tongues. Please refer to the original paper for more examples and superior resolution.

Though it’s not something that we can judge for ourselves, due to the current lack of supplementary video material, the authors also tested the system for temporal consistency using T-LPIPS:

Results of tests for temporal consistency.

‘Our full model achieves substantially lower flow jitter (0.218543 vs. 0.622901) and flow magnitude (0.513165 vs. 1.254073), confirming that geometry-anchored denoising produces smooth and stable tongue motion.

‘Lower T-LPIPS further confirms that tongue synthesis does not compromise temporal coherence.’

The uncanny valley effect is being gradually overcome by some of the latest diffusion-based video models. However, there is a certain novel psychological barrier beginning to manifest, whereby people have a negative take on AI-generated material for reasons other than the quality of what they are watching.

This is set to complicate the business of qualitative evaluation, though I suppose that metrics based on ‘blind’ tests (where the user wants to downgrade AI output, but fears to downgrade real output) could help to develop a kind of ‘shadow’ scale for AI realism.

Therefore, though there are strong research strands for the reproduction via AI of secondary identity traits such as hair, emotion simulation, gaze, and now tongue movement, they may, for the moment, be moot points.

Serve reports hundreds of thousands of completed deliveries across more than 150 neighborhoods in 20 U.S. cities. Outside applications can now initiate deliveries directly instead of requiring an integration with a large delivery platform.

The announcement follows Serve’s November 5 launch of Physical AI Services, which opened the technology behind its robots to companies developing new types of robots. This release addresses a different layer: access to deliveries on Serve’s existing fleet.

The official Delivery API documentation describes three principal REST calls: request a quote, create a job from an accepted quote, and poll the job until its status becomes dropoff_complete. A quote response supplies an identifier, fee and estimated drop-off time. Each quote expires after five minutes and can be used only once.

An instant-jobs endpoint combines quoting and job creation for applications that always accept the price. Developers can also retrieve a job’s planned route and display it on their own map.

The application or AI assistant supplies pickup and drop-off details, confirms the delivery and tracks the robot. Serve retains responsibility for dispatch, routing, navigation and supervision, allowing developers to integrate physical delivery without operating robots themselves.

The sandbox runs the complete delivery lifecycle against simulated robots, so testing does not dispatch a physical vehicle. Developers can force eight rejection conditions covering pickup or drop-off locations a robot cannot traverse, excessive delivery distance, requests outside operating hours, unreachable pickup or drop-off deadlines, busy couriers and internal errors. Sandbox access does not automatically authorize live deliveries. Real-robot dispatch requires Serve’s approval, with early-access invitations opening one neighborhood at a time. Serve said robots dispatched through the API remain subject to its standard remote monitoring by trained supervisors and the safety systems built into each vehicle.

API deliveries must stay within active service areas and operating hours, and items must fit inside the robot. The documentation describes an insulated compartment that can hold up to four large pizza boxes plus drinks and sides.

Serve presents inter-office transport, merchant deliveries to customers, expanded local-business delivery and delivery features inside existing apps as potential uses. We Decoyed Big Bull Moose in the Alaska High Country

As we loaded our gear into the bush planes, Bruce Werba offered one last piece of advice. “Be sure to use all 44 stakes. It’ll get windy up there—I guarantee it.”

I’d spent enough time in the Alaskan backcountry over the years that he wasn’t going to get an argument from me.

Our squadron consisted of three Super Cubs with tundra tires. I folded myself into the cramped space behind the pilot where the rest of my gear was stored, my shoulders touching both sides of the fuselage. We lifted off the gravel runway that’s the primary connection between the 160 souls who call Holy Cross, Alaska, home and the rest of the world, and banked over the wide and muddy Yukon River before my pilot, Matt, put us on a westerly bearing.

We were in the air for nearly an hour, heading into the Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge, a massive region that borders the Bering Sea and is bigger than the state of Maine. I took mental note of the terrain we flew over, a mix of pine-­covered mountains, tundra, vast patches of willows, and countless bogs, ponds, and rivers. It occurred to me that if, for whatever reason, we had to trek out on foot, we’d probably never make it back.

Over the roar of the engine, Matt shouted that we were approaching our landing zone. He made a large circle over an exposed flat that was surrounded by mountains on three sides to make sure he could put us down safely.

He pointed the nose of the plane into the stiff easterly wind for the final approach, and we bounced to a standstill in a matter of feet. He jumped out of the cockpit and signaled by hand to the other pilots to help them land.

As soon as the three planes were down, the pilots quickly pitched our gear onto the tundra and just as quickly took off. “Good luck,” Matt said before firing up his engine. Time is money for these guys, and they weren’t wasting any of it.

The three of us looked around at the place that would be home for the next 12 days. I was with my longtime friend Jason Nash, who works at Federal Ammunition, and photographer Tom Fowlks. This was Jason’s first moose hunt, and we had decided to do it on our own terms: getting dropped off in the wilderness during the rut and calling bulls.

We had to manhandle our tent, which was bucking in the strong wind, to get it set up. The forecast said the prevailing winds for the next 10 days would be out of the south, so we oriented the entrance toward the north and, with Werba’s admonition in mind, pounded all 44 stakes deep into the rocky soil with large stones.

Werba, who runs Alaska Pike Safaris with his wife, Connie, is a bit of a legend in Alaskan outfitting circles. He has the friendly demeanor of everyone’s favorite grandpa (he’s in his 70s), but is almost inhumanly tough. That spring, a few months before our trip, he’d had a heart attack while guiding pike fishermen and wasn’t able to get to a doctor in Anchorage until four days later—yet he was back on the job in a matter of days, doing work that would grind down a man half his age. His reputation for putting both his guided and self-guided hunters on big bulls is why I sought him out. I’ve been on half a dozen moose hunts over the years and have killed three, but I’d never had a chance on a true monster—one of those gaggers in the mid-60s that just takes your breath away—until now.

While Jason and Tom set up the cots in the tent, I dug a pit and lined it with a tarp that I weighted down with rocks to collect drinking water from the rains that were sure to come. Once the camp was set, we fired up the stove, heated water, and ate freeze-dried spaghetti and beef stroganoff straight from the bag. We had some bourbon to celebrate the start of the adventure, half a bottle disappearing with alarming speed. We’d only purchased two bottles back in Anchorage, a nod to modesty that the three of us instantly regretted. For the rest of the trip, we invoked emergency rationing.

It rained heavily that first night, and the wind beat on our tent, making the rainfly snap and pop like a flag in a gale. The next morning, I fiddled with the guide lines until I got the rigging right, and the tent quieted down.

After downing mugs of instant coffee, we explored our outpost. The broad flat was covered in tundra with patches of gravel and a few small stands of pine and willow. Everywhere I looked I could see bushes laden with ripe blueberries, and dark crowberries carpeted the ground.

Both ends of the flat, which was about three-quarters of a mile long, gave commanding vistas of the country. The north end overlooked a long, broad valley rimmed by mountains that descended to a river about a mile away. To the south, we had views of more mountains and the upper reaches of a pine-forested draw that dropped steeply down to god knows where. I hoped that if a good bull were down there, I could coax it out of the timber—because there was no way on Earth I’d chase one into that hellhole.

A large grizzly came through our camp at lunch. We were back at the tent, comparing notes and eating sandwiches, when he appeared. He angled slowly toward our tent, head down, eating berries. He was a big brute: small ears, broad across his shoulders and rump, sagging belly. I couldn’t tell what his hide would have squared—I’m not particularly good at that—but he was a toad. If I were chasing grizzlies, he’s exactly the kind of bear I’d be looking for.

As it was, the three of us just watched him strut our way. The wind was at his back, and he hadn’t noticed the interlopers on his turf. He dropped down and rolled on his back like an enormous dog, trying to deal with the swarm of bugs around him. We stood there, Jason and I, with rounds chambered in our .30/06s, as he cruised past us at 75 yards. Later that afternoon, while glassing for bulls, I saw him through the spotter about 2 miles away on a mountain, still going.

Our hunting technique wasn’t complicated. We had two cow moose decoys and a makeshift moose paddle that consisted of a white mesh game bag I had stretched over a wire hanger. Because the wind rarely let up, we put one decoy in a small willow-choked gap on the edge of the northern lookout, where any bull to the north or west of us would see it.

From a spot above and behind the decoy, we’d glass while calling every 10 minutes or so. Cupping my hands over my mouth, I’d wail like a lonely estrous moose, emptying my lungs for all they were worth. Sometimes I’d mix in bull calls, a deep, guttural mwah that to me always sounds like an old man grieving some terrible loss.

The wind picked up, and fog and rain rolled in, which cut our visibility to a couple hundred yards but provided relief from the swarms of tiny black flies that pounced whenever the breeze died. We had Thermacells to help with the mosquitos, but they didn’t dissuade those psychotic little bloodsuckers. Only our full-face bug nets and a liberal dousing with pure DEET kept them at bay.

On the fourth day, I spotted my first moose. I was trudging across the tundra, working toward the south end of the flat to glass, when I saw the cow in a small patch of willows. She’d spotted me too, and even though 700 yards separated us, that was too close for her, so she turned around and loped over the hill and out of view. Watching moose cover ground is an impressive sight. Standing still, they are awkward-looking creatures, but nothing moves through rough terrain the way they can, like skaters on ice. They can run through timber blowdowns that would stop any two-legged hunter in their tracks.

I posted up on an exposed knob that was flanked by several large willows, giving me some cover from which to glass the area. At one point, I watched a nervous-­looking black bear work back and forth across the hillside that dropped into the hellhole. He was eating berries in the same patch I’d walked through a couple of days before. Between mouthfuls, he’d pop up on his hind legs and look around before grabbing some more. After 20 minutes, he saw or smelled something he didn’t like and beelined into the depths of the dark timber.

By the end of the day, I was tired and scanning on autopilot. So when I swung my binocular to the right and got a full-frame view of a grizzly walking toward me, the adrenaline surge felt like a physical blow.

Once my lungs and heart started to work in concert again, I took another look. Like the grizzly that had strolled through our camp, he was a big bear, walking with the assured saunter of an apex predator.

He was 300 yards away, and while he wasn’t walking fast, his every step was bringing him right to me. It was getting late, and the light was failing. Even though I was well-armed with both my .30/06 and a .44 on my hip, I wanted to give the bear his space. So in the spirit of General O.P. Smith, commander of the 1st Marine Division at the Chosin Reservoir, who said, “Retreat, hell! We’re not retreating, we’re just advancing in another direction,” I packed my gear and headed back toward the tent, glancing over my shoulder every few seconds to make sure the griz wasn’t closing the gap.

Before I could make it to camp, I spotted Jason and Tom walking toward me. They were both grinning.

Jason told me that earlier that day, he had called in a bull and gotten a good look at him. He judged it at slightly larger than 50 inches. Many places in Alaska impose a 50-inch minimum on nonresidents, but we happened to be in an any-bull area, so his main concern was just making sure it was a mature bull. The most common way to size up a moose’s rack is to wait for it to face you, then compare the width of the antlers to the distance between its eyes.

Measured from the outside corners, a bull moose’s eyes are about 10 inches apart. So, if the antlers extend at least two eye-widths off the side of its head, you’re at 50 inches.

During that first encounter, Jason let the bull melt back into the willows. We still had many days to hunt, the rut hadn’t hit its peak, and the promise of a mythical 65-incher kept his finger off the trigger.

But later, when the bull reappeared, again drawn by Jason’s calls, that logic flew out the window. Jason couldn’t resist, and his 160-yard shot took the bull through the shoulder, and it piled up in a shallow ravine in the willows. His smile told me he had no regrets.

The grizzly that had been trailing behind me vanished, but we still had bears on our mind as we put on our headlamps to recover the bull. The terrain down in the willow thickets is featureless in daylight; at night, it’s a maze.

Eventually, we found the moose and got to work. It was a long, wet night. A pea-soup fog rolled in and gave the kill site an otherworldly feel. We managed to get the hide off, the moose quartered, and the first loads—the rear hinds—strapped to our backs. The fog rendered our headlamps nearly useless, blinding us more than lighting our way.

The hike to camp was less than half a mile, but it was all uphill and through the boggy tundra with more than 130 pounds of meat and bone on our backs. By the time we crested the final hill, my legs were shaking, and it was 5 a.m.

Jason groaned. “That’s some Navy SEAL shit.”

We each took a shot of bourbon and tried to dry off as best as possible before crawling into our sleeping bags, but by this point in the hunt, everything was wet, and our tent smelled like a sweatshop. The next day, we hauled the remaining meat, cape, and antlers back to camp. By then it was raining hard, and our first priority was to protect the venison. Tom took the camp ax and cut down a few small pine trees so we could build a lean-to against the side of the tent to keep the meat dry. We chopped off the boughs to make a bed, and tied sections of the trunks together with paracord to form a frame over which we draped tarps.

After removing the meat from the bags, we dumped a Costco-size container of pepper on it to keep the flies away. We spread the game bags on the branches and put the meat on top of the bags. As the smell of the spiced fresh meat hit my nostrils, I salivated like one of Pavlov’s dogs.

I called Bruce Werba on the sat phone to let him know we had a batch of meat to fly out. But the clouds and fog were keeping all the planes grounded.

When I asked when the conditions might shift, he said, “Be patient.” When I told him about our scant moose sightings, he repeated that mantra. “Be patient. It can change at any moment.”

Of course, we didn’t have a choice in the matter. The weather was going to do what it would do, and the same with the moose. So we went back to glassing as the rain and the temperature fell.

The very next day, I spotted a good bull high on the mountains behind us to the east. He was feeding up a draw to a saddle. I cow-called and he paused but didn’t turn around or look my way. Eventually, he crested the ridge and vanished.

It rained nonstop for the next three days, a cold downpour that finally managed to defeat my Gore-Tex rainwear, making for some truly miserable glassing. Every few hours I’d return to the tent to heat water and make coffee or hot cider to stop the shivering.

At night, our tent offered a fragile haven from the elements, rocking back and forth like a ship in the violent winds that Werba had predicted. The lantern above our heads created a small pocket of warm air at the top of the tent, and we hung our wet gloves and socks around it each night in the hopes they would dry. Much of our conversation turned to how best to stay warm. We stuffed the edges of our sleeping bags with extra clothes, and I started wearing my down jacket as I slept, even though it, too, was wet.

On day 10, I saw two more bulls. The first looked to be in the mid-50s and had three brow tines on each side. He was angling toward the river, and I last saw him at 500 yards as he went into the timber. I figured he’d show again later so I could get a better look at his antlers, but I was wrong—he was gone for good.

At the end of the day, I called in a bull in the low 50s. It had gotten foggy again, so we didn’t have much visibility, but I watched him come my way through the willows as he tipped his rack from one side to the other. He walked right below my perch, almost within bow shot.

That night, another bear came into camp and dragged Jason’s rack away. We found it 50 yards downhill the next morning. A couple of the points on one paddle had been chewed, and we could see where the bear had scraped bits of meat off the skull. Fortunately, the bear hadn’t worked up the courage to raid our stash of meat under the tarp’s lean-to. He had, however, left an impressive pile of brilliant purple scat next to the tent that, in my addled state, looked like the filling for a delicious blueberry cobbler.

The fog that morning was the thickest it had been. We couldn’t see more than a hundred yards in any direction. But this was the last full day of the hunt, so I went to the northern lookout and stared into the mist. Eventually, the clouds lifted, and I glassed a pair of antlers that appeared like the sails on a man-of-war emerging from a fog bank about 800 yards away.

He was with three other moose: a cow, her calf, and a spindly-horned young bull. After a closer look, I realized he was the smaller bull that we’d already called in several times. There was no calling him today, however: His intentions regarding the cow were unmistakable. He separated her from the calf like a cutting horse. She tried to get back to her calf a dozen times, but he was faster and stronger and herded her off into the willows as the younger bull and the calf watched.

That night we polished off the bourbon and ate the final bags of Mountain House lasagna, which we had saved for the occasion. I got Werba on the sat phone the next morning. The good news was the weather had cleared and the Super Cubs could take off from Holy Cross to collect us. More good news came in that his self-guided hunters who had been on the river had done well. One shot a 68-inch bull, and another had dropped a 74-incher.

Werba said he was sorry the big boys hadn’t worked their way up higher, and that none of the mountain hunters had shot toads. The weather had been too warm to get the huge bulls moving, and that was that. Whenever your heart is set on a trophy animal, leaving with an unpunched tag is always a possibility. Truth is, I didn’t mind. Jason got a terrific bull, we’d had the great adventure we came for, and I knew at some point I’d return to the Lower Yukon to try again.

After I hung up with Werba, the three of us pulled the 44 stakes from the ground, packed up, and looked to the sky, waiting to hear the drone of motors of the airplanes that would take us home.

Moose are big animals, but they aren’t especially difficult to bring down. Even moderate cartridges work well on them. In Sweden and Canada, more moose have been killed with the 6.5×55 Swede and the .303 British than anything else, and neither are punishing to shoot. As always, shot placement and bullet construction are the key factors.

On this hunt, Jason and I were both carrying .30/06s. My rifle (above) was built for the elements. I used a stainless Shilen DGR action and Shilen barrel with a Manner’s EH-1A stock and topped it with a 2.5–15×44 Maven RS.1. The rig handled the cold and wet without any problems.

One reason I went with the .30/06 was to try out some of Federal’s new loads. After several years’ hiatus, Federal is working with bullet-makers beyond the Vista Outdoor family of brands. (Vista is Federal’s parent company.) So, once again, hunters can get Federal Premium cartridges topped with bullets from Barnes, Nosler, Hornady, Berger, and others. I’m glad these companies have decided to kiss and make up.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Peanut allergies have been on the rise in recent decades.

According to statistics provided by Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), an estimated 6.2 million children and adults in the United States are allergic to peanuts.

However, new research published in the November 2026 issue of The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology suggests that over a third of infants thought to have this diagnosis might not actually have a peanut allergy.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

Lindsey Forstie, FNP, a family nurse practitioner with clinical training and experience in allergy and asthma care at Carefree Allergy in Phoenix, AZ, added that while further evaluation may be needed to confirm a peanut allergy diagnosis, it’s important for parents not to try introducing peanuts at home.

“Instead, they should speak with their pediatrician or allergist about appropriate testing and whether a supervised food challenge is safe and necessary,” she said.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

Voyager 1 went silent in deep space right before reaching a historic milestone of one ‘light day’ away from Earth

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA briefly lost contact with Voyager 1 in October 2026. The signal returned just weeks before the spacecraft reaches a historic milestone.

On Oct. 18, 2026, the antennas of NASA’s Deep Space Network tried to pick up Voyager 1 during a scheduled contact and found nothing. A second attempt 24 hours later also failed.

The signal came back on Oct. 20, and the team at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California has kept in touch with the probe since then.

NASA schedules Nov. 18 as the day Voyager 1, already the farthest human-made object, reaches one light-day from Earth, according to JPL. They had not previously said what caused the October problems.

The Deep Space Network has large antennas at Goldstone in California, near Madrid in Spain and near Canberra in Australia. The largest dish at each site measures about 230 feet (70 meters) across.

Those antennas handled the two contact attempts in October. Mission controllers logged no detectable signal on Oct. 18 or Oct. 19.

Kareem Badaruddin, the Voyager mission manager at JPL, said the episode was new to his team. “This is the first time we’ve seen the spacecraft do something like this,” he said in the JPL statement.

He also said the mission has reached a stage where the team has “to expect the unexpected.” The probe has surprised its operators before, including the time it began talking through a radio system not used since 1981.

After contact returned, the team worked out that one group of thrusters on the spacecraft had gone offline and that a backup group had turned on.

The onboard computers carry instructions to make that swap by themselves when they detect a problem with the active thrusters.

JPL calls this kind of automatic reaction fault protection. It puts the probe in a safe, stable state and pauses normal operations so that engineers on the ground can study the problem.

In March 2025, engineers revived a set of roll thrusters that had stopped working in 2004.

Early in September, about six weeks before the probe dropped out of contact, JPL engineers sent the commands that turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and turned on three lower-power substitutes.

Inside the team, the job goes by the name Big Bang. The devices double as a source of warmth for the spacecraft, so JPL could not do them one at a time and all three changed at once.

Voyager 2 got the treatment first, and NASA reported on Aug. 4, 2026 that the swap there should keep three instruments powered for at least one more year. An instrument on Voyager 2 would otherwise have gone dark before the end of 2026.

Nothing in the Oct. 29 note ties the September commands to the thrusters.

Both probes run on radioisotope generators, which turn the heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. The output on each probe falls by about 4 watts a year and, with margins now thin, JPL has been shutting off whatever is not essential.

Ten science instruments went up on Voyager 1 in 1977. A NASA status page updated in April lists only two still running on it, the magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem.

Controllers switched off the cosmic ray subsystem on Feb. 25, 2025, in one of a series of shutdowns meant to stretch the remaining power. The low-energy charged particle instrument was next, on April 17, 2026.

Without the Big Bang swap, another Voyager 1 instrument would likely have been turned off as early as summer 2027, JPL said.

Voyager 1 launched on Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after its twin, passed Jupiter and Saturn, and entered interstellar space in August 2012. It is moving at about 38,000 miles per hour (61,155 kilometers per hour).

NASA’s light-day page sets the crossing on Nov. 18, when the probe will sit about 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) from Earth.

Light covers the distance to the Moon in 1.3 seconds. Artemis II astronauts needed about three days for the same trip.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory explains how far away Voyager 1 is when it reaches one light-day from Earth. Credit: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Voyager 2 took a different route so it could visit Uranus and Neptune, and NASA expects it to reach the same distance in November 2035. The nearest star system, Proxima Centauri, lies about 4.24 light-years away.

Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd described what the distance does to a conversation. “If you want to say ‘hello’ to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response,” she said in the JPL statement.

JPL’s Oct. 29 note does not say what knocked the thrusters offline, and engineers are still investigating. It also does not confirm that the thruster switch was what interrupted the signal.

The note does not name the three devices replaced in September or give the amount of power saved. It also says nothing about when the mission might end, although the team expects more surprises before summer 2027.

The light-day crossing itself will change nothing on board or around the spacecraft, JPL said. Its value lies in showing the length of the mission and the size of the distances involved.

At one light-day, a command sent from Goldstone, Madrid or Canberra needs a full day to reach Voyager 1. The answer needs another.

Klarer has often received plaudits beyond his ability to perform the defensive basics at such a high level due to his passing range and composure on the ball, as well as having his own threats in opposition boxes.
