Fingers Point to RFK Jr. as U.S. Loses Measles Elimination Status

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On Tuesday, the Pan American Health Organization announced that the United States, as well as Bolivia and Mexico, had lost their measles elimination status, a situation that requires “urgent attention,” said Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, director of PAHO, at a press conference. “Elimination must be continuously protected.”

Calling measles “probably the most contagious virus,” he said that vaccination is “the main measure to protect” against it, and that vaccination coverage must remain “very high, above 95 percent” in every community.

Amid sinking vaccination rates, the same strain of the virus has now circulated unabated in the U.S. for a year, meaning that the virus is now endemic. This determination ends America’s hard-earned measles elimination status, achieved in 2000, which had been unequivocal proof that the country was doing something right in its effort to thwart spread of the disease.

The statistics are stunning. As of November 12, according to records maintained by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, there were 5,412 cases of measles reported in 2026, spread across 46 states and the District of Columbia, and 10 deaths.

Contrast that with 2024, the final year of the Biden administration, which saw a total of 285 cases.

The U.S. is not alone in seeing a rise in cases.  As The Atlantic pointed out, “In January, six other countries — including Austria, Spain, and the United Kingdom — lost their elimination status. Measles was declared endemic in Canada again last year.”

But, despite a worldwide decline in vaccinations attributed to the lost momentum of the Covid-19 pandemic and rising public-health mistrust, it’s not impossible to earn or maintain the measles elimination status conferred by regional commissions of the World Health Organization.

Cabo Verde, Mauritius, and the Seychelles did it last year, eliminating measles and rubella and becoming the “first sub-Saharan countries to attain this milestone,” according to a November 2025 WHO press release. The method was not rocket science, but increased vaccination and improved disease surveillance. Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

Why PicS Stock Cratered Today

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Investors are fleeing following the company's financial report.

Shares of PicS (PICS -26.02%) plunged out of the gate Tuesday morning, falling as much as 31.6%. As of 1:35 p.m. ET, the stock was still down 26%.

The catalyst that sent the Brazilian fintech specialist lower was the company's financial report, which investors found wanting.

PicS, the parent company of PicPay, reported its third-quarter results, which contained both good news and bad news. Revenue of 4.7 billion Brazilian reais (roughly $812 million) grew 73% year over year, fueling adjusted net income of 271 million reais ($46.9 million), which jumped 157%.

Other metrics seemed equally bullish, as PicPay's credit portfolio grew 88% year over year to 35 billion reais ($6 billion). Management also noted that revenue per client grew by 58%, and lower fixed expenses improved its adjusted efficiency ratio by 11 percentage points.

This all sounds like good news, so why is the stock plunging today? It all comes down to changes in the company's write-off policy. PicPay is reducing the period for delinquent accounts from 360 days past due to 120 days past due, citing its "more mature portfolio and robust historical data." That will result in a one-time charge of 325 million reais ($56.2 million) in the fourth quarter. As a result of the new policy, PicPay adjusted its full-year net income guidance to 733 million reais ($126.7 million), down from 1.06 billion reais ($182.8 million).

The growing credit portfolios among fintech companies in Latin America have caused some investor concern. Since many of these accounts have limited histories, it's difficult to assess the delinquency or default rate in the event of a regional or global recession. Moreover, given the history of inflation in that geographical area, those concerns carry greater weight.

PicS also has a limited history, having only gone public in early 2026. Since its debut, PicS stock has shed 73% of its value. On the other hand, at just 3.5 times forward earnings, there isn't much growth baked into its valuation. For investors who have the stomach for a little risk and the potential for significant volatility, PicS might be worth a look.

The Sotheby’s statement records that the auction house is “pleased to confirm that an agreement has been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer regarding the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs”. The sale will now proceed as planned.

The work is estimated at around $180m ($207m with fees) and has been guaranteed by Sotheby's to sell. This is far in excess of the previous record of $117m, paid at Christie’s in 2022 for Van Gogh’s Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888).

The Blaquier-Arrieta family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees in 1975. As the Sotheby’s statement explains: “In recognition of the painting’s complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement.”

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. The Blaquier-Arrieta family commented: “We are pleased to have reached an amicable resolution with the Fischer heirs. We appreciate the constructive dialogue that made this resolution possible and look forward to an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a new generation of collectors.”

No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. I was afraid of not having enough or of being unable to find another job, and I just had to face that fear.

The AT forced me to confront my own limiting beliefs around money. In the years since that first thru-hike, I’ve gotten a little more relaxed about spending money on things I know I can afford without feeling guilty about it. I haven’t abandoned my frugal tendencies, but they don’t need to dictate every decision I make. I credit the trail for that growth.

“I wonder if I can get another 200 miles out of these shoes before I replace them. I wonder if I should get on a cheaper insurance plan next year. Do I have enough money to buy name-brand peanut butter in the next town? Should I get a different credit card? Should I go stoveless to save on fuel? “

I’ve written a few articles over the years about my peculiar hiking-related neuroses. As always, I want to clarify in the midst of all this doom and gloom that thru-hiking was and is one of the great joys of my life, despite my assorted pathologies and the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

Every thru-hiker will face struggles both external and internal; learning to deal with and overcome them is part of the experience, and this is just one more example. As always, do everything you can to prepare for your thru-hike, accept that you can’t plan for everything, and then enjoy the journey, hardships and all.

The new film about the founder of the Hershey Company begins with a loving, beautifully lit close-up of one of the company’s signature products, a Hershey’s Kiss. The film, which an opening title card informs us is “based on the unlikely true story,” begins in 1870 with a young Milton (James Kelly) growing up in a Mennonite community in rural Pennsylvania. The episodic screenplay by Sharon Paul and Timothy Michael Hayes chronicles Hershey’s rise to prominence in dutiful fashion, including his eventual transition to producing chocolates. This comes about after a visit from a salesman representing a German chocolate company, who, after being rejected by Hershey because the product tastes bitter, warns him, “I think you’re missing out on what’s next.” Sure enough, Hershey changes his mind, realizing that all the chocolate needs is some milk to make it taste better. OMCO Solar now offers a version of its OMCO Star Tracker Control System powered entirely by solar energy, allowing tracker controls to operate without AC power at the site.

The system, first launched last year, is designed for OMCO Origin Factory-Direct Trackers. Its tracker controllers, array controllers and sensor bridges use all-weather battery packs charged by solar modules.

According to OMCO, the technology eliminates the need for AC power to tracker control components and an uninterruptible power supply. It also allows tracker controls to operate and protects modules against wind, snow and hail before AC power is available.

“Our industry has solved so many problems as we have grown – we’re now the leading source for new electric energy capacity in the US. This addition to the OMCO Star product line solves one more – how to protect modules if there’s no AC power to the site even for a short time. We’re excited to offer this solution to our existing customers and to new partners,” said Matt Kesler, vice president of solar technology at OMCO Solar, in a statement.

The OMCO Star system uses LoRa, or long-range, wireless technology with a communication range greater than 1 mile. According to OMCO, this reduces the number of network controllers required and allows direct communication with every tracker without relying on a mesh network.

The company says LoRa is 100 times less susceptible to electromagnetic noise than Zigbee and better able to connect to distant trackers despite obstacles.

OMCO says its all-weather battery packs allow the solar-powered controls to operate reliably in cold climates. The system includes field-replaceable batteries, a phone app that eliminates the need for buttons on controllers and a remote monitoring and control portal.

The portal allows operators to move trackers for activities such as mowing, snow plowing and module cleaning. The system is developed, manufactured and supported by OMCO Solar in the United States.

Defendants argue that the order isn't authorized by the Mississippi's Protection from Domestic Abuse Law or by the Mississippi rules governing ordinary temporary restraining orders. But it also argues (I think correctly) that is an overbroad prior restraint that violates the First Amendment:

The order does not merely prohibit threats or harassment. It prohibits speech about the Plaintiffs, their families, and their lawyers—whether truthful or false, threatening or harmless, harassing or not. And it does so before that speech occurs. The Court need not decide whether Mr. Hughes's commentary was fair, tasteful, or justified. The question is whether the law permits this order. It does not. The order should be dissolved, and the motion for a preliminary injunction denied….

A judicial order "forbidding certain communications when issued in advance of the time that such communications are to occur" is a prior restraint…. "Any system of prior restraints on communication bears a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity." Such restraints are unconstitutional "except in exceptional circumstances." .As the Supreme Court held in Organization for a Better Austin v. Keefe (1971), {the party seeking to suppress speech} "carries a heavy burden of showing justification for the imposition of such a restraint." …

The remedy for false or injurious statements lies in "actions under libel laws providing for redress and punishment, and not in proceedings to restrain the publication." [But t]he October 12 Order … does not impose consequences for particular statements adjudicated unlawful. It prohibits Mr. Hughes from speaking about entire categories of people in the future. That is a prior restraint in its most direct form which is presumed unconstitutional….

Test Masters Educ. Servs., Inc. v. Singh (5th Cir. 2005) … recognized that a court may enjoin "harassing communication" and "repeated invasions of privacy." But an injunction prohibiting all communication, rather than limiting itself to "threatening or harassing conduct," "swept too broadly" and constituted an unconstitutional restraint. Id.…

Plaintiffs cannot justify the restraint merely by characterizing Mr. Hughes's commentary as malicious, coercive, or injurious. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Keefe. There, the Court held that an individual's "interest in being free from public criticism of his business practices" did not justify an injunction against speech…. Plaintiffs may have remedies for unlawful conduct. But those remedies do not include an order forbidding Mr. Hughes from speaking about them at all….

Defendant also argues (again correctly, I think) that the order was unconstitutional because defendant wasn't given an opportunity to be heard before the order was entered:

The absence of notice presents a separate constitutional defect. In Carroll v. President & Commissioners of Princess Anne (1968), the Supreme Court set aside a short ex parte order restraining expression because of "a basic infirmity in the procedure by which it was obtained." The order "was issued ex parte, without notice to petitioners and without any effort, however informal, to invite or permit their participation in the proceedings."

There is a place in our jurisprudence for ex parte issuance, without notice, of temporary restraining orders of short duration; There is no place within the area of basic freedoms guaranteed by the First Amendment for such orders where no showing is made that it is impossible to serve or to notify the opposing parties and to give them an opportunity to participate.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that a later hearing could cure the problem. The availability of a subsequent opportunity to be heard "does not overcome the infirmity in the absence of a showing of justification for the ex parte nature of the proceedings."

The reason is straightforward. "The value of judicial decision-making … is substantially diluted where the process is ex parte, because the Court does not have available the fundamental instrument for judicial judgment: "an adversary proceeding in which both parties may participate." And where First Amendment freedoms are at stake, any order must be "couched in the narrowest terms that will accomplish the pin-pointed objective."

The October 12 Order fails both principles. It was entered without notice or an adversary hearing. It contains no finding that notifying Mr. Hughes was impossible. And far from identifying a narrow category of unlawful speech, it prohibits virtually any online commentary concerning the Plaintiffs, their families, or their counsel.

Carroll is directly controlling on the constitutional defect. An ex parte order restraining expression cannot stand without a showing that notice and participation were impossible. No such showing appears here….

Bigger armies will not stop climate change, and may make the world less safe

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Military emissions stay out of national climate tallies, and two BMJ authors say bigger defense budgets may leave people less safe, not more.

Many governments are raising their military budgets, and climate change is now one of the reasons they give for doing so. The thinking is that regions turning dangerously hot, wet, or dry will grow unstable and may go to war, so a bigger military is a precaution.

Two articles in a medical journal’s climate issue say that logic has it backwards.

War and the daily running of armies are themselves a large and mostly uncounted source of the gases heating the planet, their authors wrote. Spending more on them may leave people less safe, not more.

The articles are opinion pieces, not data studies, and they appeared in The BMJ on October 21. Neta Crawford, a professor of international relations at the University of St Andrews, is the author of the first. The journal commissioned it and didn’t send it for outside peer review.

Mohammed Abba-Aji of Washington University in St. Louis and Nason Maani of the University of Edinburgh are the authors of the second article.

In an interview with Earth.com, Abba-Aji described the comparison behind that article. “What struck me most was the contrast,” he said. The contrast is between what the world spends on weapons and what it gives away as aid.

Security officials describe climate change as a “threat multiplier,” Crawford wrote. The phrase means a warming climate could stress societies until they fight. The U.S. military, the U.K. Ministry of Defence, NATO, and the U.N. Security Council have all used it.

Crawford reversed the claim: war, she wrote, is a climate change multiplier.

The steadiest source of military emissions is the ordinary running of a military, from powering bases to the fuel burned in training and in moving troops. U.S. and NATO military doctrine stresses long-range air strikes, which produce more emissions than defensive doctrines do.

Everyday military emissions don’t appear in national greenhouse gas inventories. Crawford traced the gap to the Kyoto Protocol talks of the 1990s, when the U.S. Department of Defense pushed for a military exemption.

The department was the country’s largest petroleum user and feared being required to cut its emissions. The Pentagon is still the largest user, she wrote, and its annual emissions are larger than those of most countries.

Arms makers add to the total. BAE Systems, the biggest defense company in the U.K., reported about 377,000 tons (341,686 metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent for 2025 from its own operations and purchased energy.

Fighting raises the numbers fast. Crawford cited an accounting group’s estimate for the first four years of the war in Ukraine. Military operations alone produced about 126 million tons (114 million metric tons) of emissions in the form of carbon dioxide equivalent.

Destroyed forests and airliners rerouted around the war zone added more, and rebuilding will add more again. Those three sources come to roughly 190 million tons (170 million metric tons).

Strikes on oil and gas sites are worse still. The Nord Stream pipelines under the Baltic Sea were sabotaged in September 2022, and an estimated 513,000 tons (465,000 metric tons) of methane escaped, a 2025 study published in Nature found.

Crawford put that at about 15 million tons (13.85 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent, three times what Albania emitted during the whole of 2024.

Ukrainian drones hit Russian oil facilities this September, Crawford wrote, and strikes hit oil and gas infrastructure in the Middle East during the war with Iran this year.

The fuel shortages and price jumps were tracked. In contrast, the greenhouse gases released have gone essentially uncounted.

The second article is about the money. World military spending reached $2,887 billion in 2025, Abba-Aji told Earth.com. This is close to 2.5 percent of everything the world’s economies produced.

Official development assistance, the aid that richer governments send to poorer ones, fell to $174 billion the same year, the largest one-year drop on record.

Abba-Aji and Maani describe a cycle. Climate stress can make conflict more likely, through competition over resources or by making it easier for armed groups to recruit.

“Conflict in turn justifies more military spending, and that spending draws money away from the measures that could break the cycle,” Abba-Aji said.

Wealthier governments may stay out of the fighting, the two wrote, and still help create the conditions for it. They supply the weapons that keep a conflict going while cutting aid to the countries affected.

Crawford also objects to the idea that a hotter world leads to war on its own. The link between climate change and armed conflict is neither strong nor necessary, she wrote, citing a 2017 study that re-examined the Syrian civil war.

Governments can support their populations instead, she wrote, and neighboring governments can negotiate over shared resources. Those are political choices, not unavoidable consequences of climate change.

Climate change is already killing people without any war at all. In some regions, heatwaves, wildfires, storms, and floods made worse by warming now kill more people a year than armed conflict does, Crawford wrote.

By the Met Office‘s estimate, more than 1,100 deaths in England and Wales during the May and June heatwaves of 2026 were due to climate change.

The threat-multiplier frame has also changed where militaries operate. For years, Crawford wrote, armed forces have worried that a melting Arctic Ocean will open shipping lanes and a contest for minerals.

The U.S., NATO, Russia, and China have all increased their Arctic operations, at the same time as the region’s climate has become less stable and those operations harder to run.

Some armed forces present themselves as green, Crawford wrote. The U.S. Navy publicized a “great green fleet” in 2016, and the U.S. Army promoted its investments in green technology until mention of climate change was all but banned under the Trump administration.

Moving bases off coal does cut emissions, she wrote. But even forces that use fuel-efficient vehicles, batteries, solar power, and microgrids remain major emitters.

When spending grows, so do training, operations, and purchasing, and the emissions grow with them.

“Substantial emission reductions will occur only when armed forces reduce their overall size and footprint and transition to lower emitting doctrines, including those that do not require long range aircraft missions,” Crawford wrote.

Asked by Earth.com what governments should do first, Abba-Aji said they should “weigh the trade-offs between military and social spending for their health and climate consequences and set aside a share of security funding for climate adaptation, especially in the most vulnerable regions.”

Crawford’s own answer is to spend more on health, the environment, and the energy transition rather than take money from them.

“While some form of defense is required, the focus should shift to how to ensure effective defense at lower emission levels and reduced costs,” she wrote.

The size of the source that both articles describe is still unknown. Day-to-day military emissions are left out of national greenhouse gas inventories, and the gases released by this year’s strikes on oil and gas facilities haven’t been added up either.

Counting them would be the first step toward knowing its magnitude.

The full study was published in the journal The BMJ.

"The Toxic Avenger" has a demented sense of humor, and it doesn't give us a typical hero either. Toxie isn't a square-jawed, muscle-bound vigilante. Snoopy's interior life became more fleshed out than many of Peanuts' more ancillary characters.

To get to that point, there had to be a first time Snoopy had a thought. Or otherwise, the first time the audience was privy to what Snoopy was thinking. 8 War Movie Masterpieces Praised By Real-Life Veterans

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

War movies movies have been a consistent genre throughout the history of cinema. Hollywood has repeatedly tapped into the stories of real-life conflicts, showing dramatized versions of what really happened. These efforts came with great rewards, too, as countless war movies have been hugely successful at the worldwide box office.

Audiences typically look for a lot of things when choosing their next war movie to watch — action, drama between characters, and potentially world-defining stakes. Yet, there’s another element that typically comes into play, too, and that is what real-life veterans think of a given film. After all, getting their stamp of approval is mightily important to any war movie, as it shows that the story they told is (for the most part) accurate to what really happened.

Some war movies have been praised for their portrayal of the conflict at hand. Andrew Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge, which tells the story of a soldier who, due to his strong religious beliefs, opts not to carry a weapon of any kind during battle, is one war film that has been praised repeatedly. These accolades have come not only from historians who have spent their entire careers studying the conflict, but veterans who were actually there, too.

War films praised by veterans aren’t just limited to World War II, either. Movies that have centered on more recent conflicts, such as the wars in the Middle East, have also been lauded by those who served for their accuracy of their portrayal of history. Regardless, war movies remain an incredibly popular subgenre in Hollywood today, and if they’re praised by real-life veterans, then chances go up that it’s an excellent film as well.

Andrew Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge is one war movie that has consistently been praised by real-life veterans. As stated, the film tells the story of Desmond Doss, a Seventh-Day Adventist who objected to conflict of any kind. However, when drafted into the United States military, he was forced to either forgo his religion or risk criminal punishment for not fighting.

Miraculously, Doss found a solution to this problem — not carrying a weapon at all. During combat, such as at the Battle of Okinawa (he later received a Medal of Honor for his actions during the battle), Doss had no weapons on him. He had no rifle, no pistol, and nothing that could cause harm to another human being, as Doss was adamant that he wouldn’t hurt anyone. Instead, he served as a medic, thus saving the lives of those on the battlefield, and earning a reputation as one of the bravest soldiers in history in the process.

When Hacksaw Ridge released in theaters in 2016, the question naturally arose about how much of the film’s story was accurate to real events. After all, while some war movies are based in realism, others also take plenty of story liberties. Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge wasn’t one of these films, however, and has instead been praised by several veterans.

For example, the film’s director, Mel Gibson, showed the finished product at the Disabled American Veterans national convention in Atlanta, Georgia. The film received great applause upon its conclusion, with many veterans praising its accuracy. Dan Clare, an Iraqi war veteran, noted that Hacksaw Ridge was a surprise hit with the crowd.

“These were people who avoid war movies because they fear they will either be unrealistic or that they will trigger post-traumatic stress. To their surprise, most people felt that it was a realistic depiction of combat — one of the most accurate ever — and, by and large, they appreciated the way it portrayed PTSD. Bottom line? Unlike most Hollywood movies, this one felt like it represented us.”

Ridley Scott’s 2001 film Black Hawk Down tells the story of two Black Hawk helicopters that were shot down during the Battle of Mogadishu. Once again, this film was praised for its depiction of the battle that its based on, with many veterans who fought during this conflict noting the film’s accuracy. Not only that, but one veteran stated that “90–95% of the film” is accurate, a high number that shows just how much attention to detail went into this movie.

Another more recent title, The Outpost is based on a non-fiction book by newscaster Jake Tapper. The film tells the story of the Battle of Kamdesh and the United States soldiers stationed at the Combat Outpost (COP) Keating in Afghanistan. A fatal design flaw of the base meant that the soldiers were vulnerable to ambush from the surrounding mountains, and the film then shows their desperate fight to survive.

One of the things that makes The Outpost such a unique film is that it not only received praise from veterans, but was actually worked on by veterans, too. Director Rod Lurie is a graduate of West Point and served in the U.S. Army, and not only that, but the film also recruited several survivors from the actual events to serve as advisers on the story. This helps to make The Outpost feel raw, lived in, and true to real-life events.

Another recent film with plenty of veteran involvement is Warfare, which tells the story of an encounter that a group of Marines had shortly after the Battle of Ramadi in 2006. Interestingly, the film is told in real time, with each second passing at the same rate for both the audience and the characters. Not only that, but the script was also based solely on personal accounts that survivors gave after the attack occurred.

These methods were praised by veterans, including survivors, for adding to the film’s overall authenticity. In particular, veterans praised the “smaller moments” for depicting the quiet moments of battle, which many war films skip over. The performances of the cast were also praised, as actors like Joseph Quinn and Will Poulter did extensive research to make their portrayals as accurate as possible.

One of the earliest films on this list is The Best Years of Our Lives, which released just one year after the conclusion of World War II, in 1946. The film tells the story of three United States soldiers who return to their small towns after serving, but find the transition back to civilian life especially difficult. For example, Al Stephenson (played by Frederic March) struggles with alcoholism upon his return and, at the same time, also has a very difficult job at a bank, in which he is given the task of approving or denying loans for fellow veterans.

The Best Years of Our Lives is timeless in this regard. The film may focus on World War II veterans, but it can just as easily translate to virtually every conflict in modern human history, with soldiers struggling to find their footing after returning home. The realistic-ness of this portrayal was praised by veterans upon its release, many of whom felt seen by the film. Amazingly, many of the film’s actors were veterans themselves, including Harold Russell, who, just like his character, lost both his hands during the war.

The head of Veterans Affairs at the time, General Omar Bradley, praised this film upon its release. In fact, he was such a fan of the movie that he organized a showing for VA headquarters staff, and later went on to write to the film’s producer to express his appreciation.

Steven Spielberg has directed so many iconic films in his career, and Saving Private Ryan is right up there among the very best. The film, which released in 1998, tells the story of a battalion of U.S. soldiers who set out on a mission to rescue Private James Francis Ryan (after his brothers had already tragically been lost in battle). Tom Hanks plays Captain John Miller, the leader of the rescue mission, and Matt Damon takes on the role of Private Ryan.

Many World War II veterans, some of whom actually fought in the specific battles that Saving Private Ryan depicts, praised Spielberg’s film. D-Day veterans noted that the Omaha Beach sequence captured the true violence of the war, with paratrooper Jack Evans saying: “A lot of people will see this and think it's gruesome, but that's the way it was. One of the most recent films on this list, Atonement tells the true story of a deadly battle during the Iraq War and its longstanding aftermath. Starring Kenneth Branagh and Boyd Holbrook, Atonement begins with an extended sequence showing American soldiers patrolling the streets of a populated Iraqi village. Atonement is based directly not just on real events, but on real people, too. The Khachaturian family were the ones impacted by this tragedy, and Boyd Holbrook’s Lou D'Alessandro was (one of) the real soldiers in question. Meanwhile, Kenneth Branagh’s Michael Reid was the journalist who covered the events (and who facilitated a meeting between both parties years later).

During the Q&A after the film’s premiere, Van Dyk noted that he and his team had received the stamp of approval from veterans who served during the War in Iraq, with particular praise going to the intensity of the combat sequence that then sets up the rest of the movie.

Maxwell, was originally filmed as a miniseries but was released for a limited run in theaters after positive reception. Because of this, it remains the longest film released in theaters in U.S. history.

Because of how long ago this war was, no direct veterans were around to see this movie. However, veterans of future conflicts noted that Gettysburg has a surprising commitment to depicting all aspects of war. Not only that, but historians praised Maxwell for including real battlefield formations, re-enactors, and for filming near the real locations where key battles took place.

Why scammers try to keep you on the phone (and how to spot their tactics)

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If something feels off, hang up and call the company back on their official number.

That unexpected call from someone claiming to be from your bank can be worrisome, especially if they're calling to alert you about a suspicious money transfer. Your savings are at risk, but fret not, the caller who somehow got hold of your number says they can stop it — as long as you stay on the line.

But more than the emergency itself, their instruction to stay on call is the real catch. Scammers are counting on you to not hang up, as that valuable time helps them earn your trust. It then leads to turning up the pressure, which eventually leads you toward a decision you otherwise wouldn't take. That's when they might ask for a verification code, or straight up ask you to make a transfer. Keeping you occupied just long enough is the plan, so you don't end up calling the bank for a second opinion.

Dedicated scammers are known to stay on the line as the victims login to the accounts to transfer funds, buy gift cards or make a visit to the nearby ATM to withdraw cash. Some even go as far as to school you on what to tell bank employees when questioned about that odd transaction. When the person "helping" you protect your savings is deterring you from speaking to anyone else, that's a pretty obvious sign that something is off.

One possible way in could be a compromised bank account. A creative scammer could inform you about open accounts under your name or even scare you with an arrest warrant. The plan is to make you feel that following their instructions is the only way out, so hanging up doesn't feel like a plausible option.

Sometimes, the whole script includes more than just one scam artist. Back in August 2026, the FBI reported that scammers posing as bank employees told their victims that their information had been used to open an account to purchase illegal firearms. The scammers then transferred the victims to someone posing as an FBI agent, but disconnected after the victims were encouraged to verify the number.

Shortly after, they received a call displaying the FBI's Boston office number, which had been spoofed. These "FBI agents" could then ask the victims to continue the conversations using an encrypted messaging app. The FBI didn't specify how many people were targeted by this scheme.

But the threatening villain persona is not the only way these scammers are out to get you. Some of them act professional, stay patient and even go a step further to sound sympathetic. Now, when these friendly voices start to discourage you from reaching out to the organization directly or getting a second opinion, that should get your alarm bells ringing. After all, con artists don't want all of their efforts to result in failure.

Once the small talk is done, call handlers may switch to a dictating tone in order to get the outcome they want. This is a clear warning sign. When the caller demands you stay connected or instructs you to keep the conversation private, be sure to trust your instincts and end the call.

When they ask you to move funds to a "safer" account, it's time to hang up. Any request for passwords or even one-time verification codes should also be met with a call termination. Since scammers are often savvy enough to spoof authentic phone numbers, caller ID isn't all that reliable either.

Once you hang up or end that text conversation, reach out to the organization, but only using its official app or the number on your bank card or statement. Steer clear of contact numbers that the caller gives you, and trusting the customer-service numbers from sponsored search results isn't safe either. If it's a bit late to heed this advice and you've already shared sensitive information or worse transferred money to the scammer, contact your bank or payment provider right away.

Did you know your phone can filter out some of these unwanted calls? On iPhones running iOS 26 or newer, head to Settings > Apps > Phone > Screen Unknown Callers to enable extra incoming call checks, which include silencing unsaved numbers or asking callers to state who they are before they're connected.

If you own a supported Samsung Galaxy phone, try out spam filtering under Phone > Settings > Caller ID and spam protection, and Google Pixel owners can set up Call Screen using the Phone app's settings on supported devices in eligible regions. The downside is that legitimate callers can sometimes get caught by these filters.

Companies like Meta, Microsoft and Starlink have already worked to help take down over a million scam accounts. If you want to help save other unsuspecting consumers, it's best to report suspected scams to the FTC.

The Stock Market’s Biggest Risk Isn’t a Crash. It’s What Investors Might Do When One Arrives.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A market decline hurts. An improvised response can make it worse.

Imagine seeing your nest egg lose 30% of its value. In this scenario, the market has already delivered a painful setback. Selling may seem like the obvious way to regain control, but selling also creates a new problem: When will you get back into the market?

In reality, panic selling -- and delaying reentering the market -- can lead to enormous opportunity costs for investors. Let's explore why.

Seeing your portfolio lose value is never fun. Indeed, psychological studies have shown that investment losses have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains, a phenomenon known as loss aversion. Therefore, it's completely understandable that many people seek immediate relief when the market enters a sell-off, correction, or crash.

However, the psychological relief of liquidating investments creates a new problem: determining when "it's safe."

In practice, most investors simply wait too long. Many wait for recessions to end or for bullish headlines to appear. But bullish headlines often lag positive price action. As a result, investors on the sidelines often miss out on massive recovery rallies. As we'll see next, that can have a significant impact on long-term growth.

Consider a $10,000 portfolio. If it drops 30%, its value will fall to $7,000. To get back to $10,000, the portfolio will need to gain $3,000. Since the overall value has fallen, it will need to generate approximately a 43% return on $7,000 to reach $10,000. So to wipe out a 30% drop, it needs to gain 43%. These amounts differ because the starting amounts differ.

Beyond the arithmetic, there are other costs. Transaction costs, lost dividend payments, and taxes have to be considered. In short, selling might ease one type of headache but create several others.

Despite understanding the math and the other implications of selling, many investors can't resist the urge to sell when the stock market is falling. One way to train yourself not to sell is to imagine a similar situation in a different market.

For example, let's say you own a house. Now imagine that another house in your neighborhood sells for half of what might normally be expected. That sale will decrease the value of all homes in the neighborhood, perhaps significantly. But even if the price of your own home falls 10%, 15%, or even 25%, that doesn't mean one of the bedrooms in your home disappears.

Stock portfolios work the same way. Prices can fall, but that doesn't necessarily mean that the businesses behind the stocks have deteriorated to the same degree.

For a buy-and-hold strategy to work, investors must build a portfolio that can withstand periods of volatility. Leveraged positions and meme stocks can wreck a portfolio during a stock market correction or crash. Therefore, savvy investors know to limit their exposure, or, better yet, avoid such investments altogether. By building a diversified portfolio based around well-run businesses, an investor can avoid much of the worst effects of a market downturn.

Market crashes are terrible, but thankfully, they're also rare. However, to prepare for them, it's best to have a plan. Writing it down can help you manage your emotions, think carefully, and avoid panic. At the end of the day, the math and the studies are clear: It's best to ride out market downturns rather than sell and try to time reentry perfectly.

If you can keep a cool head and remember this in the moment, you'll likely come through the next financial downturn in better shape than many other investors.

But a couple hours later, he writes me again: “People don’t want me to die. I guess I should be flattered.”

Sheep grazing in solar fields offers farmers economic, ecological gains

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two new research papers shed light on the growing practice of grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays. One demonstrates that, in the right numbers, sheep grazing increases populations of beneficial insects like bees and lady beetles compared to mowing around solar panels. The other research describes a decision-making support tool to help farmers profitably grow their solar grazing herds.

A team from the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (CALS) found that when two sheep per hectare graze around solar arrays, the abundance of wild bees, honey bees and lady beetles jumped by 38%, compared to a control plot that was mowed. The increase was driven primarily by an 83% increase in honey bees alone, according to the team’s findings in “Low solar-grazing densities support beneficial insects in agrivoltaic systems,” published Oct. 7 in Environmental Research Letters.

“Sheep grazing and beneficial insect habitat are two prominent conversation topics around solar development right now,” said Leana Zoller, the paper’s first author and a postdoctoral associate in natural resources in the Cornell CALS Ashley School of Global Development and the Environment. “But what’s been missing is research that treats them as parts of the same system. When we talk about sustainable solar development, integrating these discussions into a single framework is really helpful to farmers and community decision makers.”

For three years, Zoller and a team of entomologists, ecologists and animal and plant scientists tested various stocking rates – how many sheep per hectare are allowed to graze a certain plot of land – using Cornell’s teaching sheep grazing at the university-owned Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, New York. At four sheep per hectare, they found beneficial insect abundance was similar to the mowed control site, while vegetation management effectiveness was greater than at two sheep per hectare. At eight sheep per hectare, vegetation management was maximized and sheep flock health remained high, but honey bee abundance dropped by 45%.

“We zeroed in on stocking rates because that’s a concrete number that sheep farmers understand and work with every day, and we want this research to be useful,” said Steve Grodsky, paper senior author and assistant professor of natural resources in the Ashley School. “With solar grazing, people, energy, agricultural animals, plants and insects are all part of this ecosystem. We’re trying to understand the connections between those pieces so that when solar developers, municipalities looking at solar development, or farmers are faced with a decision, they can have scientific information about potential trade-offs and synergies.”

Grodsky is also assistant unit leader of the U.S. Geological Survey New York Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. Additional co-authors are sheep farmer and solar grazing consultant Nikola Kochendoerfer, Ph.D. ’20; Antonio DiTommaso, professor in the School of Integrative Plant Science; John Losey, professor of entomology; Scott McArt, Ph.D. ’12, associate professor of entomology; Paige Muñiz, formerly a technician in McArt’s lab; and Todd Ugine, Ph.D. ’04, research associate in Losey’s lab, all in CALS. Sarah Carson, director of Cornell’s Campus Sustainability Office, was key in coordinating site access between researchers and the solar company that manages the Cascadilla solar farm.

Another Cornell research team, from the Cornell SC Johnson College of Business, has developed a tool that helps farmers whose sheep graze in solar arrays to make better decisions about their operation. The team described the tool in “Flock Management for Solar Grazing,” which published Sept. 28 in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

After a chance encounter with Caleb Scott, Ithaca-based sheep farmer and founder of United Agrivoltaics, Vincent Slaugh, assistant professor at the Cornell Peter and Stephanie Nolan School of Hotel Administration and first author of the paper, began thinking through the economics and logistics of solar grazing. He recruited Linwei Xin, associate professor of operations research and information engineering, and Shane Henderson, Charles W. Lake, Jr. Professor in Productivity, both in the Cornell Duffield College of Engineering, to work on the topic.

Together, they developed a spreadsheet-based model that helps sheep farmers to optimize procurement and sales decisions, based on factors like target flock size, costs, anticipated revenues, and expected vegetation in particular solar sites; if a farmer doesn’t have enough sheep to keep the grass down, they’ll have to cut it some other way. It also helps solar developers understand growers’ needs and develop contracts that are more profitable and sustainable for all parties long-term. United Agrivoltaics has already incorporated the tool into its onboarding process.

Slaugh sees opportunity for U.S. shepherds to grow their operations – especially with solar contracts offering a steady source of income and animal feed. Current domestic sheep production for meat and wool doesn’t come close to meeting domestic demand; about half of all lamb and mutton consumed in the U.S. is imported, primarily from New Zealand and Australia.

“If we grow the sheep industry, will the American diet eventually include more lamb? Could that be a side effect of the solar energy revolution? I think the answer is very likely yes,” Slaugh said. “And then when that happens, we’d expect processors and supporting industries to come back, for banks to be willing to give more loans, and for supply chains to follow. All of that grows the economy, and rural economies in particular.”

Kochendoerfer, who grazes 700 ewes on solar installations in New York and Vermont, said that economy is already growing.

“I work with a lot of farms that are now scaling up because of solar,” Kochendoerfer said. “There is a new wave of sheep farmers who are capitalizing on this access to good land. With solar grazing, you get paid to graze as opposed to having to pay to graze. That leads to sustainable, meaningful economic income that these farmers can use to expand, build a barn, buy equipment or hire someone. It’s enabling a lot of folks to build sound, viable agricultural businesses.”

Funding for these research projects came from the Cornell Atkinson Center for Sustainability, the National Institute for Food and Agriculture and the National Science Foundation.

The lost years of Lamar Jackson -- and the greatness still within reach

Some years, Lamar Jackson's thumb hits another player and is fine. Some years, his ankle just gets hit rather than rolled up on. And those years, he has been -- and will be -- a player who can win an MVP and perhaps a Super Bowl.

Jackson's latest injury was almost too cruel to believe. After missing the previous three and a half games with a high ankle sprain, the two-time MVP was finally back. So, too, was the Ravens' hope. Jackson had looked phenomenal -- MVP-level phenomenal -- in new offensive coordinator Declan Doyle's system. Not only was he back, but he was back in a big spot: against fellow MVP Josh Allen in a crucial matchup vs. Buffalo on the first day of November, with Baltimore trying to end a two-game losing streak.

And then, on the Ravens' first play, Jackson hit his thumb on Greg Rousseau's arm. Jackson looked down at his injured right thumb, trotted toward the sideline and then took a knee.

"Oh boy," NFL on CBS analyst Ross Tucker said.

Medical personnel surrounded Jackson, draped a towel over his hand and brought him down the tunnel, off the field.

What more was there to say? Lamar Jackson, one of the defining players not just of the era but of NFL history, was hurt. Again. And it was serious. Again.

Jackson officially landed on IR with a thumb injury that will require surgery. It is "potentially" season-ending, and with the Ravens 4-5 and losers of four straight, it's looking like a lost season in Baltimore. This is the fourth time in the last six years the 29-year-old has missed at least four games. It has robbed the Ravens of their Super Bowl aspirations. It has robbed the NFL of a superstar. And it has robbed Jackson, a Heisman Trophy winner and twice an NFL MVP, of even higher levels of all-time greatness.

On the positive side, the thumb injury is the first ailment to land Jackson on IR. He has never had an ACL tear, an Achilles tear, etc. On the negative side, Jackson has racked up so many injuries of so many different kinds that it seems like something is constantly getting in the way, whether it's nagging at him or keeping him out of games. He even said the ankle pain entering the Bills game last week was merely "bearable," not gone.

Since the start of last season alone, Jackson has had hamstring, ankle, toe, knee, back, and thumb ailments. Not all of those kept him out, but they add up. He admitted he came back from last year's hamstring injury too early, impacting his play all season.

And yes, for what it's worth, it's bad luck. If Jackson's hand hits Rousseau at any other angle, he might not hurt his thumb enough to need surgery. If a diving Jermaine Johnson II doesn't awkwardly trap Jackson's ankle to the turf in Week 3, Jackson might not miss the next three games. Josh Allen and Patrick Mahomes have both had hundreds of similar plays and, with the exception of Mahomes' torn ACL last year, avoided injury for the most part. Sometimes, it's simply human anatomy, something Jackson cannot change.

For as prolific a runner as Jackson is, he does an excellent job avoiding hits on those plays. None of his injuries have been on runs. It has just been a constant parade of misfortune. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, the NFL rolls on, whether you're lucky or not.

Perhaps time and injuries have taken their toll and blocked our collective memory from how incredible Jackson has been. That would be truly unfortunate.

In 2019, Jackson's second year of the league, he threw 36 touchdown passes and ran for 1,206 yards. In NFL history, there are ...

In fact, only one other player ever has posted 1,200 rushing yards and merely double-digit passing touchdowns in a season: Spec Sanders of the New York Yankees (football version) in the All-America Football Conference in 1947. But he only threw 14 touchdowns. Jackson did things modern football has never known.

Jackson became the youngest quarterback to win MVP and joined Tom Brady as the only unanimous MVPs in NFL history. There are many ways to measure individual season greatness, but Pro Football Reference's "Approximate Value" is as good as any. Jackson had an approximate value of 25 in 2019, tied for the second-best season on record; LaDanian Tomlinson's 2006 season, in which he scored an NFL-record 31 touchdowns, is the only one better.

Jackson was healthy in 2020 and had another strong season, including a second straight 1,000-yard rushing effort. He has as many 1,000-yard rushing seasons as all other quarterbacks in NFL history combined (Michael Vick and Justin Fields both have one).

Then the injuries started. In 2021, Jackson was on pace for another 1,000-yard rushing season, and the Ravens led the AFC North when Browns linebacker Jeremiah Owusu-Koramoah dove at Jackson's ankles after Jackson released a pass. Jackson got carted off and didn't play in any of the final four games. The Ravens lost all four and missed the playoffs.

As you’d expect, those points are redeemed centrally through the program, and then the loyalty program has to “buy” that room from the hotel. Note that the number of points you redeem for a stay doesn’t directly determine reimbursement for a hotel. In other words, a hotel doesn’t get paid X amount per redeemed point. Similarly, if a program offers a fifth night free on award redemptions, the hotel is getting reimbursed for all five nights, as it’s the loyalty program incurring the cost there.

Instead, points redemption rates are set by the loyalty program, and are intended to reflect the cost incurred by the program. That’s why in many cases you’ll find that redemption rates don’t correlate directly to how much a property would cost in cash, but rather also factor in average occupancy at a property.

A hotel loyalty program would probably rather you redeem your points at a $1,000 per night hotel that’s 50% full, rather than a $500 per night hotel that’s 95% full, because the latter redemption will cost the program more. Hotel loyalty programs use historical data to determine points pricing, and the costs they incur.

Regardless of adherence, the figures were 38.4% and 2.1%. Both groups were put on an eating plan with fewer calories and encouraged to move more for the length of the study.

The trial enrolled 167 children, and more than 85% had class II or III severe obesity at baseline, Novo said. Doses reached a maximum of 1.7 milligrams or 2.4 milligrams, depending on baseline weight. At week 104, BMI had fallen an estimated 9.4% with semaglutide and risen 8.4% with placebo when all children were counted. Novo said the week 104 results were not adjusted for multiplicity and cannot be used for inferences.

Safety and tolerability matched earlier Novo pediatric and adult semaglutide trials, and no unexpected safety concerns or concerns about growth or pubertal development emerged, the company said. The 2.4-milligram injection carries a boxed warning for possible thyroid tumors, including cancer.

"Effective treatments for severe childhood obesity may shift a child's trajectory back towards one of improving weight related health," Ania M. Jastreboff, a professor at Yale School of Medicine and director of the Yale Obesity Research Center, said in a statement.

Semaglutide injection is not approved in the U.S. for children under 12, and Novo said there is no guarantee it will become commercially available for that use. Wegovy is approved for obesity in children 12 and older, and no medications are approved for younger children, according to CNBC.

Close to half of the children in the trial had at least one obesity-related complication, among them hypertension, elevated cholesterol and sleep apnea, according to CNBC.

Dr. Andrea Traina, Novo's medical director lead of obesity, said the two-year study cannot show if starting therapy sooner alters how the disease unfolds over a lifetime, or what ongoing care would require once these children become adults, calling it "an evidence gap," according to CNBC.

Childhood obesity touched about 177 million young people between the ages of 5 and 19 around the globe in 2025, according to the World Obesity Federation, as cited in Novo's release.

Months after American Airlines rejected his pitch for a merger, United CEO Scott Kirby remains convinced that U.S. carriers will have to consolidate to thrive.

Kirby’s original pitch to American shocked the aviation industry, as it would have brought together two of the country’s largest commercial airlines and given the combined company a market share of just over 34%, a level of dominance not seen by any U.S. carrier in years. My murderous wine merchant whines for a chance at republican rule in Crusader Kings 3’s Silk and Silver expansion, which isn't only about the money

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Longing for the Venetian Dogehouse, stuck in the Venetian doghouse

The tentacles of my trade empire snake up towards France in the west, down towards Africa in the south, and off into Hungary in the east. Yet, I still can’t reach out and grasp the throne that sits right in front of me. I’m Marco Sanudo, and the throne of the serene republic of Venice eludes me.

I’ve shed blood in its name, the blood of someone else named Marco no less. I’ve managed to avoid punishment for that crime, despite the fact it earned me a fiefdom to rule while I’ve tried to up my patrician score enough to be the lucky party the next time a ruling Doge dies in office (or runs out their 10 year term). Sadly, Doge Leonardo, the mysterious masked lord who’s had an iron grip on power since the first couple of old blokes to hold the position in this playthrough popped their clogs, is proving incredibly tough to oust.

It should, in theory, help that he’s my best mate, thanks to a well-timed feast. It certainly did in being granted a pardon for offing that other Marco, but not even that and a plague which infected nearly everyone in the realm aside from me have paved the way to Dogedom. In the meantime, I’ve striven to gain wealth and influence. I’ve worked up to having four trade routes running at once, with goods like wine, iron, and textiles flowing from market to market across southern Europe and bringing a steady supply of coin back to my Venetian estate. I’ve built trade posts at the extremities of my routes, giving me the option of starting from further afield and reaching new lands if the cash flow dries up closer to home.

This is where Crusader Kings 3’s Silk and Silver expansion has blended a bit with the game’s traditional feudal moneymaking to supercharge my coffers. The land holding I killed a dude for, the Proveditorate of Zara, means I’ve also had tax money from modern day Croatia to add to both my trade profits and the income my domicile generates on its own. I am, as the medieval kids say, minted.

Getting to the point of serious pocket clinkage is, as you might imagine given Silk and Silver’s focus on filling the game’s world with travelling merchant companies and wheeler-dealing republican patrician families, the beating heart of the expansion. A near-constant stream of notifications about prices of different goods I’m trying to hawk in different locations encourage me to regularly prune and re-work my routes so that max profits are being earned and no carrying capacity’s being wasted. The bubbling up of wars and plagues impacts prices in affected cities, making it a never-ending battle if you’re desperate to optimise everything within an inch of its life. I’ve found that degree of micromanagement unnecessary if the goal is just to keep a decent profit margin ticking over. Just remember to replace the trader assigned to a route if they die, recall them every few years so they don’t stress themselves to death, and check in every so often to re-evaluate whether you’re running goods that are suitably in demand.

If you stick at it for a decent amount of time, with a certain type of good - alcohol or jewellery, for example - your expertise in dealing in them increases, making it easier to sell for better prices. Trade routes left running between the same markets also gain boons from being in place for a long time, offering a counterpoint to rejigging them every five seconds to wring out a few more coins. Most of my routes have run along a path that includes stops at four markets, with the occasional location boasting a monopoly I’ve bargained for with the local ruler, so I don’t have to worry about competition at every stop.

When you’re playing as a merchant who isn’t locked into the ruling inner circle of a republic, making your routes as lengthy as possible is one of the three moneymaking areas your company can specialise in. That first one’s caravaneering, and is the one I leant into in a non-Marco Sanudo playthrough. Beyond that, you can become an expert loan shark, lending cash to rulers or paying their ransoms in order to recoup interest and collect hooks you can use for favours. Manufacturing expensive artifacts that can be sold individually to collectors for a profit is the third option. The latter two I’ve only dabbled in a little bit as of writing, having earned enough gold mostly from caravanneering to buy the land containing the city with my home market and be anointed a Sheikh. So, beginning as a landless merchant is a nice alternative to Crusader Kings 3’s long-established wandering adventurer starting point, with both allowing you to work your way up from lowly serf to landed lordly dynasty in your location of choice.

If you’re after a more specific historical scenario, Silk and Silver has Guelphs and Ghibellines. This is where we find Marco Sanudo, as well as the other patrician family head I’ve played as thus far, Speronella Dalesmanni. The setup is that the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor have had a bit of a tiff, essentially rooted in who should have certain powers, with different cities and powerful families in northern Italy taking either the Guelph (Pope-likers) or Ghibelline (Holy Roman Empire-enjoyers) side depending on what suits their goals or principles. I’ll be honest, aside from being another way to easily earn friends or enemies in the ruthless crucible of merchant republican politics, where votes and reputation-bolstering aid from other nobles are key, the situation’s not had a hugely obvious bearing on my playthroughs thus far.

I think the closest it’s come is during my Dalesmanni run, if largely due to the existence of the By God Alone expansion allowing a new branch of Catholicism, dubbed Italian Christianity, to be created. The initial conversion push from the bishop responsible led a number of my fellow northern Italian nobles away from the Roman Rite headed by his Popeliness, making the Guelph/Ghibelline split more clearly defined.

In the early hours of both my Sanudo and Dalesmanni playthroughs, I’ve been far more focused on simply trying to push the respective patrician families up the hill from posh toffs with naught but a vote and a manor to elected rulers of their great cities, with land holdings to match. Speronella’s situation in Padua is made incredibly tough by the fact she’s a woman in a world where every succession rule is designed to put blokes first, so I’ve had more success with Marco and his quest to become the Doge of Venice.

As the head of a patrician family, one of select few elite clans who steer the oligarchical republic, I was faced with earning Marco enough respect among his fellow family heads that they’d consider voting him to the top of the succession list, thus securing his power whenever the current Doge got the boot. Pulling that off, while also not kowtowing to the requests and schemes of those same peers, isn’t easy. A large cocktail of factors go into determining whether another noble likes you enough to vote in support of your succession. There’s your patrician rating, which is upped by doing things which specifically show you to be good at this Italian noble house lark, such as establishing trade monopolies for the republic, gaining the Pope’s favour, or having close relatives who also hold key positions in the republic. However, how close a friendship you have with the target and how their character traits align with you also play a huge rule.

Thus it was that, at one point, Marco was best mates with the current Doge and had a decent patrician rating, but had only amassed 68 of the 72 points needed for said Doge to vote for him because the Doge was generous, and therefore didn’t care for Marco’s ambitiousness. That same Doge either liked or respected Marco enough to grant him a council position and the status of regent, but he would not back Marco to replace him permanently for love nor money. This is the same man who liked Marco so much that when I pulled off that not-so-sneaky murder on another noble house head in order to inherit the keys to the Proveditorate of Zara, the Doge was instantly willing to pardon me and prevent Marco suffering any punishment for the crime.

Not even a couple of serious plagues hitting Venice, which at one point had all three people ahead of Marco in the queue suffering from smallpox, could get him on that throne. Eventually, it took a bit of lucky timing when his Doge mate died of old age - a few years after beating smallpox - to finally install Marco as Doge. Given that delayed promotion, as of writing I’ve not yet had enough time in the big chair to give a proper verdict on how it differs from regular feudal rulerdom in the long run.

What I can do instead is tell you a bit about the fun of council votes. The same pool of voters who in a republic like Venice, get a say in who succeeds the ruler, also vote on the passage of laws and decrees. One proposal you can put forward is a vote to change who exactly is allowed a seat in this voting pool. It defaults to just being heads of patrician families, but can be extended to include all vassals, or tightened up so only the Doge’s councillors or the ruler themselves have a say, that being the way despotism lies. As you can imagine, passing a change to who can vote can drastically change the state of play, either tightening up the number of folks who might oppose or support you, or bringing new folks into play in a fashion that dilutes individual power, but gives you more pieces on the board to mind or manipulate to your advantage.

I’ve found it suitably tough to orchestrate successful votes to change laws and customs which medieval Italian noble families, expectedly, have firmly entrenched stances on. Getting rid of checks and balances, such as the requirement to vote on banishments or wars - so the Doge can rule as a despot - has proven one such challenge, as has changing laws governing men being prioritised over women. I tried to pass equal succession as a substitute for men being put first while playing as Speronella, but met an insurmountable wall of resistance due to the region’s culture and faith. So, all I could do was try to put my young male heir in the best position possible to make the sorts of gains I couldn’t.

Much easier to weasel into place, either through some canny swaying or the offering of hooks, are laws giving state backing or other priority to trading in certain types of goods. Alcohol, for instance, which certainly hasn’t hurt the profits of Marco’s two wine trade routes.

Thus, cash continues to flow through the tentacles of my trade empire in sizable globs. As he’s now Doge, I’ve got Marco using the ample funds of Venice’s treasury to build a new piazza and have him focused on making the city’s already-strong trade league even more formidable, by attracting new members from the few other northern Italian blocs.

While I’m fairly sure there are folks who’ll find Silk and Silver's constant barrage of trade route notifications annoying, or that their power-grabbing skills and sheer multitude of money-making means combine to make playthroughs less challenging than they could be, so far I've found it another enjoyably sonorous string to Crusader Kings 3’s bow. Above all else, playing as a merchant gives more reason to invest in stewardship skills beyond just maximising your tax yields, while the republican politics of the DLC's main region fleshes out rewarding intrigue-focused builds. Diplomacy and martial skills are less key, but still have their place at times.

If you’re an absolute stickler for historical accuracy, you may well find other things to gripe about, but aside from the Guelph and Ghibelline conflict fading into the background, I’ve found plenty to like in my quest to become top Doge.
