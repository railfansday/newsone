Boy Meets World Cast Reveals Major Salary Differences in Final Season

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Boy Meets World alum Rider Strong revealed how a misunderstanding of his contract led to him receiving a much lower salary than his costar Ben Savage.

Rider Strong was meeting a world of salary differences.

The Boy Meets World alum got candid about the vast pay disparity between himself and costar Ben Savage on the seventh—and final—season of the series in Book Meets World: The Definitive Inside Story of Hit Sitcom Boy Meets World, a book he co-wrote with costars Danielle Fishel and Will Friedle.

As Strong explained, he was uninterested in starring on season seven of the show—which ran from 1993 to 2000—but had “misread” his contract and believed that he was already “locked in” for the season, according to an excerpt from the book shared by Entertainment Weekly. The misunderstanding led to a call from creator Michael Jacobs, who assured him there would be no season eight, only the final, seventh season.

“I was already in college,” Strong wrote, per the outlet, “and I didn’t want to do the show anymore.”

However, 46-year-old recalled that Jacobs pitched him “hard,” telling Strong he could head to college the following year.

“I stopped him, and I said, ‘You realize, they’re talking about adding an eighth season.’ He was like, ‘No, we’re not. We’re talking about adding a seventh,’” Strong wrote, adding that Jacobs allegedly told him, “‘Okay, so we’re just thinking about adding a year that you already thought was there, so we’re good.’”

The meeting left the former child star feeling “confused,” although he knew his manager would be happy that he signed on for another season. Still, he felt alone throughout the negotiation process.

“I thought, why is nobody looking out for me and just saying, ‘Rider, if you don’t want to do this, you don’t have to?’” he said. “There was no sense of that.”

“I had my own relationship with my manager, my own relationship with my agent, which is why I walked into a meeting with Michael by myself and got completely screwed,” he continued, noting that “if he could go back, it would have ended differently.”

And while the Pod Meets World host wishes he hadn’t stayed on for a seventh season, he did notice that costar Savage “held out” on committing to the final season after getting accepted to Stanford University, which Strong said led to Savage receiving a “huge pay increase.”

“There was a lot of miscommunication. If we had actually talked to each other, we probably should have just said, ‘We’re all equal here, let’s go [negotiate together].’ But there was no sense of that,” Strong claimed in the book. “It seemed that Ben getting into Stanford was his biggest negotiating tactic, because the contract ended. They wanted the seventh season, but he was like, ‘Are you going to not allow me to go to college?’ He stuck to his guns, and it worked out.”

As for how much Savage’s pay was increased? According to Strong, he was able to negotiate a salary close to that of costar Bill Daniels—an established actor who was the highest paid actor on the show—earning “close to six figures per episode.”

And as Strong’s costar Friedle noted in the book, he and the rest of the cast “were making nowhere near that,” adding that he regrets not entering negotiations with Strong “so that the two of them were making the same salary.”

E! News has reached out to Savage and Jacobs for comment, but has not heard back.

For a look at what more stars have shared about their own salaries through the years, keep reading.

The Jackass star reflected on the "comical" amount of money he was paid for doing death-defying stunts on the MTV series' first season in 2000.

"When it was all said and done, after taxes, I was paid less than $1,500 for the entire first season of Jackass," the daredevil told Playboy in June 2026. "Before the show came out, my sister kicked me out of the house. I was broke, unemployed, and homeless, and a star on this big MTV show."

Oscar winner Jennifer Lawrence was paid $25 million to star in Adam McKay’s ensemble disaster comedy Don’t Look Up. But her costar and fellow Oscar winner Leonardo DiCaprio got $30 million.

“Look, Leo brings in more box office than I do,” Lawrence told Vanity Fair for its’ December 2020/January 2021 issue. “I’m extremely fortunate and happy with my deal. But in other situations, what I have seen—and I’m sure other women in the workforce have seen as well—is that it’s extremely uncomfortable to inquire about equal pay. And if you do question something that appears unequal, you’re told it’s not gender disparity but they can’t tell you what exactly it is.”

Her sanguine attitude was hard-fought: When the disparity between Lawrence’s compensation and that of her male costars for 2013’s American Hustle was revealed in the 2014 Sony email hack, it prompted an industry-wide conversation about the gender pay gap in Hollywood.

"I'm so fortunate to have my job. My problem is not money," Lawrence told the U.K.’s Channel 4 News in 2017. "I wasn't upset that I only got this many millions for a movie. That's ridiculous. I was angry about the unfairness and inequality."

The *NSYNC alum revealed that he and his fellow bandmates JC Chasez, Joey Fatone, Chris Kirkpatrick and Justin Timberlake didn't make much money during their years with the group.

“We had a per diem: $35 a day,” he revealed on The Zach Sang Show in 2026. “Our first check was a $10,000 check after years of No. 1 albums, sellout tours. I don’t know how many millions they made off of merchandise because merchandise was insane.”

“And then they finally gave us a check for $10,000,” he recalled. “You’re like, ‘Oh, thanks.’ We should be grateful for that, been working for free for two, three years now.”

Less comparable to DiCaprio’s salary: The $60,000 “before commissions and taxes” that Jonah Hill was paid for 2013’s The Wolf of Wall Street, while the titular wolf made $10 million.

“They gave me the lowest amount of money possible,” Hill recalled on The Howard Stern Show in 2014. But it was the chance to appear in a Martin Scorsese movie, so the Moneyball alum wanted to get the deal done before anyone could change their mind.

“I would sell my house and give him all my money to work for [Scorsese],” explained Hill, who earned his second Best Supporting Actor nomination for his turn as a squirrelly substance-abusing stockbroker. “This isn't what you make money for. You do 22 Jump Street or you do other things, and you can pay your rent. I would have done anything in the world. I would do it again in a second. This isn't about money. You should do things that you care about."

According to Dylan Sprouse, his residuals from filming The Suite Life of Zack & Cody with twin brother Cole Sprouse is anything but suite.

"The only residual check I've ever received for The Suite Life at all was a crossover episode with Hannah Montana for five cents," the Disney Channel alum shared during a 2026 episode of his Wildmen podcast. "In a time where residuals would have been great, not a single paycheck for being arguably one of the most popular shows on television."

While there's no cash prize for nabbing Dancing With the Stars' mirrorball trophy, “whoever makes it to the finale, you all get paid up until then," Jenna Johnson, who won season 33 with partner Joey Graziadei, shared. "And you do get a nice bonus.”

"If you win," Johnson, who noted pro contracts are dependent on seniority, continued. "it’s not like you get a million dollars. You’re just getting a cute trophy together.” As for those who say farewell to the ballroom early, well, as she explained, "You’re guaranteed until a certain amount of weeks.” According to multiple reports, dancer salaries start at around $1,200 to $1,600 an episode, which can increase to up to of $100,000 per season. As they cha-cha further into the competition, stars  receive additional bonuses with the semifinalists and finalists getting paid up to $50,000 extra.

But in actuality, she praised the talents of her costars for their performances.

“I felt I did OK, but Jennifer and Courteney? Amazing,” she raved. “David and Matt? They had me laughing so hard. And then Matthew [Perry]—he was just beyond us all.”

To hear Joe Giudice tell it, his and ex-wife Teresa Giudice's salary for season one of The Real Housewives of New Jersey was nothing to flip over. "We got paid nothing the first season," he detailed on daughter Gia Giudice's Casual Chaos podcast in August 2025. "I'll even say, it was like $25,000. It was ridiculous. But, whatever."

And the ingredientses they put into that initial fame-making season weren't cheap. "I think the first year we had that big party in my great room," the father of four reflected. "I think that show cost me $150,000 the first year."

Oprah Winfrey happily accepted only a little bit of green to star in 1985’s The Color Purple.

“They were only offering $35,000 to be in this film, and it is the best $35,000 I ever earned,” the billionaire media mogul told Essence in 2023. “It changed everything and taught me so much.”

The actress revealed that she made about $20,000 per episode of Two and a Half Men with a 5 percent raise each season, making about $43,000 for one episode in the final season.

When she scored the role of late Tejana singer Selena Quintanilla in the 1997 biopic Selena, Jennifer Lopez became the first Latina actress to earn $1 million for a film.

“I was too young and didn’t know what the hell was going on,” Lopez told Variety in 2019, reflecting on her breakout movie. “It was great they offered me a million dollars. I feel like everyone was making a statement.”

And it paved the way for her to make hundreds of millions more, though sometimes her participation doesn’t cost a thing.

“I do things because I love them,” Lopez explained to GQ in 2019. “I didn't get paid a whole bunch of money for Hustlers. I did it for free and produced it. I bank on myself, you know? Like Jenny From the Block—I do what I love."

Adrien Brody didn’t do The Brutalist for the money. Rather, he took on the role of a Hungarian architect suffering for his art in post-WWII America for $250,000 and won his second Oscar for Best Actor.

“I need a studio movie now, because I’ve poured it all into this,” Brody quipped to Variety, referring to the “barn that looks like a castle” he’s renovating in upstate New York.

“Do you guys know what they pay us?” Pete Davidson cracked to New York magazine when asked ahead of Saturday Night Live's 50th anniversary special how he spent his money when he was first hired at the age of 20 in 2014. “It’s like three grand an episode, so I think I got dinner.”

Interestingly, the salaries have remained proportional for SNL newcomers, with season one stars John Belushi, Jane Curtin, Gilda Radner, Dan Aykroyd and Chevy Chase reportedly earning $750 an episode back in 1975. Which was roughly equivalent to $3,300 in 2014, according to the Consumer Price Index Inflation calculator. (Though the $750 of 1975 is $4,428 in 2025.)

While Kenan Thompson makes a lot more than $3,000 per episode of SNL now that he’s in his record 22nd season, the former Nickelodeon star had to start somewhere.

“That first commercial when they paid me, it was $800,” the All That alum revealed in Demi Lovato‘s 2024 documentary Child Star. “I was 12 so that may as well have been a million dollars."

It turns out Gilmore Girls’ status as a beloved rewatchable classic is literally priceless.

“There really are no residuals on Netflix,” Lauren Graham, who played Lorelai Gilmore for seven seasons and then reprised the role for 2016’s Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life, said on Jimmy Kimmel Live in March 2025. “But I’ve been paid in love and appreciation.”

Feeling not so appreciated, Tommy Dorfman got specific about her 13 Reasons Why paycheck during the 2023 SAG-AFTRA strike—which was prompted in part (as was the WGA strike) by streamers like Netflix not making it a practice to pay additional compensation to cast and crew once a show had become a big hit.

"my earnings for the entire first season of 13 reasons why were $29,953.24 prior to agency and manager fees (20%) and taxes," Dorfman wrote on Threads July 24. "8 episodes over six months." She continued, “i did all of the promo and had KEY ART for this show, flew round trip from NYC to SF to shoot for every episode, was kept for days without pay/working. i barely qualified for insurance."

And, Dorfman added, “within the first 28 days of release, the show's season 1 garnered a total of 476 million view hours. this is why we strike."

Without naming names or numbers, Dax Shepard told fellow Parenthood alum Lauren Graham when she appeared on his podcast that he knew he made “among the lowest of all the actors” on their NBC drama, which ran for six seasons.

But, the Armchair Expert host stressed that he did not mind.

So he made a point to purposely not seek out such info about his Parenthood family, which helped with enjoying the experience even when he did get an inkling of where he was on the pay pyramid.

[Actor, cowriter and coproducer Grant Heslov] and I each made a buck for doing it.”

His labor of love about CBS News journalist Edward R. Murrow speaking truth to power in the 1950s had legs, though: Making his Broadway debut, Clooney wrapped his months-long stint playing Murrow in a stage adaptation of the film  in June 2025. And with the play having grossed a Broadway record (for a nonmusical) $3.3 million during a preview week before it officially opened April 3, he made a lot more than $1.

He made so little, Bale shared, that he remembered “sitting in the makeup trailer and the makeup artists were laughing at me because I was getting paid less than any of them.”

However, the actress—who played Daenerys Targaryen for eight seasons between 2011 and 2019—noted that the money she made throughout the HBO show did provide her with enough financial security to pay off her parents’ mortgage.

His uncle may have been Rico, but Jon Heder confirmed in 2010 that he was “initially” only paid $1,000 for Napoleon Dynamite.

After the irreverent comedy became a sleeper hit, he was able to renegotiate for a piece of the profits, telling the New Zealand Herald, “They went a little bit higher.”

Djimon Hounsou said he’s “definitely underpaid” in Hollywood despite his prestigious list of credits, including Amistad and Blood Diamond.

"I've been in this business and making films now over two decades," the Beninese actor told CNN’s Larry Madowo in January 2025. "And still—with two Oscar nominations and been in many big blockbuster films—and yet I’m still struggling financially to make a living."

Rebel Wilson detailed in her 2024 memoir Rebel Rising that she was paid $3,500 to do Bridesmaids, “a fee that I then had to pay directly to the Screen Actors Guild to join the union. So really, I got paid nothing.”

Still, she wrote, the experience was “everything!”

And the Australian actress’ star rose ever a-ca higher, with her fee going from the SAG minimum scale of $65,000 for 2012’s Pitch Perfect to $10 million for 2017’s Pitch Perfect 3. Wilson wrote that she raked in $20 million total for the threequel, Isn’t It Romantic and The Hustle, though—reality alert—she “lost almost 50 percent to taxes, 10 percent to agents and 5 percent” to her lawyer. But even after those obligations and paying her publicist, business manager and assistant, Wilson “netted what to me was an absolute fortune.”

In late 2017, Ellen Pompeo negotiated a new Grey’s Anatomy contract for $575,000 per episode, a seven-figure signing bonus and two full backend equity points worth an estimated $6 million to $7 million.

“I’m 48 now, so I’ve finally gotten to the place where I’m OK asking for what I deserve, which is something that comes only with age,” Pompeo told The Hollywood Reporter in 2018. “Because I’m not the most ‘relevant’ actress out there. I know that’s the industry perception because I’ve been this character for 14 years. But the truth is, anybody can be good on a show season one and two. Can you be good 14 years later? Now, that’s a f--kin’ skill.”

Reflecting more recently on how she always made less than costar Patrick Dempsey, even though she was the Grey in question, Pompeo said she didn’t begrudge him his money.

But, she said on a March 2025 episode of Call Her Daddy, “Just being that I was the namesake of the show, I deserved the same and that was harder to get. I wasn't salty about him getting what he got. I was salty that they didn't value me as much as they valued him and they never will.”

Jamie Lee Curtis recalled getting paid $2,000 a week—so $8,000 in total—for 1978’s Halloween, her movie debut. “I mean,” she told Rotten Tomatoes in 2018, “nobody got paid, anything.”

Yet while the scream queen earned four figures, director John Carpenter’s friend Nick Castle felt like a king making $25 per day to share the role of Michael Myers. (It’s Anthony Moran whose face you see when the killer’s mask comes off for a freaky second, and editor Tommy Lee Wallace also logged screentime as Michael.)

“That was a lot at the time!” Castle, who attended film school at USC with Carpenter, told Vanity Fair in 2018. “You have to remember: My interest in doing the film was being on set, so I could demystify the experience of filmmaking and directing. I expected to hang around the set for no money. But hey, $25 per day, and all I had to do was wear a rubber mask.”

Taraji P. Henson earned a Best Supporting Actress Oscar nomination for 2008’s The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, but years later she was still fired up by the $150,000 she accepted to be in the movie—after producers rejected the $500,000 quote she brought to the table.

As for the film’s leads Brad Pitt and Cate Blanchett, “You don’t hear a lot of $20 million quotes anymore, but at this time that was happening,” Henson said on the Ladies First With Laura Brown podcast in 2021. “And rightfully so—I’m not saying they shouldn’t have paid Cate and Brad what they deserved.”

But, she continued, “I’m bringing a certain amount to the seat too and I felt like what I was asking at that time in my career was fair, was fair to the ticket sales that I would contribute to this big film. Wouldn’t do it.” Henson was “gutted” when they offered $100,000, she recalled. “When it was all said and done I got $150,000, but I had to swallow my pride, baby.”

Breaking it down, “I know people go, ‘$150,000, that’s a lot of money!’” Henson acknowledged. “I don’t ever want people to think that I’m ungrateful because that is not me.” But, she calculated, “Uncle Sam is going to take 50 percent of that, so now you’re left with, what? $75,000. Now before Uncle Sam takes the money, I have to pay my team before taxes, 30 percent. So once Uncle Sam takes his 75, then I got another 30 that’s coming off of that 75, so I may have made $40,000?”

While Blanchett has made plenty of money over the years, it didn’t all come from playing royal elf Galadriel in The Lord of the Rings franchise, though it grossed $2.9 billion at the worldwide box office.

“Are you kidding me? No,” she retorted on Watch What Happens Live in August 2024 when Andy Cohen guessed LOTR was her biggest paycheck. “No one got paid anything to do that movie…I mean, I basically got free sandwiches, and I got to keep my ears.”

They weren’t paid a precious amount, that is. Orlando Bloom, who played elven warrior Legolas, shared on The Howard Stern Show in 2023 that he made $175,000 for the whole trilogy.

Damian Lillard is back and Ja Morant is here. How exactly is this going to work? | Bill Oram

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the history of basketball, we have certainly seen some why-would-they-do-that? pairings.

Jerseys and sleeves. Kawhi Leonard and a tree sponsorship. Utah and "the Jazz."

Once, there was even Detlef Schrempf and a headband, if you can believe that.

We're about to find out if they belong on such a list.

Because of all the things you might have said the Trail Blazers needed as they prepared to welcome Lillard back after making a playoff run without him, a slight-of-stature ball-dominant guard with a questionable jump shot would not have topped anyone's list.

Depth on the wing? Yes. Shooting? Definitely. Defense? Always.

Morant doesn't particularly address any of those, and yet, he's here, a dynamic two-time All-Star in need of a second act, being paid a team-high $42.1 million to play the same position as not just Lillard, but Jrue Holiday and Scoot Henderson.

Heck, the Blazers' most valuable point guard last season was forward Deni Avdija.

"I'm sure there will be questions about fit," General Manager Joe Cronin said Monday.

The Blazers have little more than three weeks before the regular season begins in which to figure that out. At the team's annual media day on Monday, Morant appeared in a fresh white No. 1 Trail Blazers jersey. It's happening.

"It's easy for people to be like, 'Man how is this going to work,'" Lillard offered. "'They can't do this, they can't do that.' But being on the floor with them … we can be a dangerous team and I really believe that."

The onus is on the Trail Blazers starting this month to prove that they bet right in acquiring Morant. And that pairing him with Lillard isn't the boondoggle conventional wisdom suggests it will be.

Lillard noted how many teams that succeeded in the playoffs last year, think San Antonio with its four point guards, relied on numerous playmakers. To his credit, Morant said he would be comfortable coming off the bench, if necessary.

But they would not be the first NBA team to fall into the trap of believing they are capable of adjusting or that they will be comfortable sacrificing. It isn't uncommon to see players in those situations default to familiar habits and sensibilities once the season is underway or struggles come.

That's what made a lot of the positive energy around what sure seems like an awkward roster ring so hollow. We're going to need to see it to believe it.

Morant tried to lay out a vision for how it could look. If he drives to the paint and is double-teamed, someone else is inevitably open. That's how basketball works.

"That allows me to have more space to be the best Ja I can be," Morant said.

The 36-year-old Holiday, who would be a more natural complement next to Lillard or Morant than they are to each other, made his own case for why the point guard rotation makes sense.

"It's about being a complete basketball player for myself," he said, "and I think we have a lot of guys like that."

There is a long history of teams talking themselves into superstar additions that make little sense. The Los Angeles Lakers once made a conscious decision to acquire Russell Westbrook and pair him with LeBron James. Nobody made them do that!

The talent and star power of a Lillard-Morant backcourt is undeniable. Still, I remain unconvinced that Cronin, with a very hands-on new owner in his ear, made the deal with winning basketball games at the front of mind.

Although, admittedly, Cronin's biggest wins as a GM have been in the trade market.

The haul of assets he collected for Lillard, only to get him back two years later, will go down as an all-time great swindle. So, too, will the oft-questioned Avdija trade a year later.

Maybe he sees something in a Lillard-Morant pairing that many of us can't.

Morant could return to the All-Star form he had achieved before injuries and off-court issues derailed his ascent. And if he does, the Blazers can either trade him for pieces that fit better or clear out the logjam and build around him.

But to sell the current roster as a basketball strategy doesn't quite pass the smell test.

Neither Lillard nor Morant has achieved stardom due to his defensive acumen. Each player needs the ball in his hands to thrive. If the duo is on the floor together, Avdija is no longer the secondary playmaker, he becomes tertiary.

So, you are left to wonder whether the Blazers sold themselves on Morant because of how he can help the current collection of talent or if they simply saw the opportunity to land a 27-year-old All-NBA talent for cheap, regardless of what it meant for the existing roster construction.

And if you're being honest, you probably don't need to wonder which of the two it is.

"We were very focused and we'll continue to be on … adding to our talent base as much as we can," Cronin said.

That thinking right there may tell you just how far ahead of everyone else the San Antonio Spurs and Oklahoma City Thunder are in the Western Conference. They are positioned to dominate for the next half-decade, if not longer. The only chance any other team has of disrupting that is taking bold risks.

But I thought the goal was to go all in on the twilight years of Lillard's career?

"Dame literally just wants to win," said first-year head coach Micah Nori, whose charge is to sort out arguably the most talented glut of point guard talent in NBA history. "I think that's the only thing left he really has to (do). He's already proven he's a winner, but at the highest level."

Does adding Morant move Lillard closer to a title? Or simply give Portland a potential off-ramp if Lillard is no longer the same player after tearing his Achilles tendon?

Morant was without question a low-risk, high-reward addition. He came at the reasonable price of Jerami Grant and Kris Murray. If it becomes clear that Morant is one point guard too many, or that he's just not the player he once was, some will argue that it didn't cost the Blazers much to try.

However, there is an opportunity cost to messing with Lillard's swan song. If you don't get it right, you risk wasting the gift of his voluntary return last summer. And, not for nothing, Lillard has the option to become a free agent again after this season.

The franchise icon certainly hasn't said anything that would suggest he is unhappy about Morant's presence, although he stopped short on Monday of a full-throated endorsement. He said he knew about the trade before it went through.

When I asked if he thought it was a good idea right away or if he needed time to get on board with the plan, Lillard said, "My position on it was, I'm prepared for whatever."

It's good that he's already thinking about other positions. Because his old one is getting awfully crowded.

The distinction between net flows and assets under management matters as well. A fund’s reported assets can fall when Bitcoin’s price drops even without corresponding redemptions. These numbers are current through the November 9 US trading session, not an estimate of November 10 while trading is still under way. A better measure of sentiment combines a completed ETF flow series with spot volume, derivatives positioning and the changing rates outlook. Global carbon emissions from fossil fuels are projected to decrease by 0.4% in 2026, according to a report presented at the COP31 climate talks hosted in Turkey and published in the journal Nature.

That change was driven by a drop in oil emissions, which will be about 1.9% less than the year prior. 

Oil emissions declined as gas and diesel pump prices rose 10% to 50% across the United States, China, and the European Union, compared to 2025, the researchers note. 

But this change in global emissions is “not a sign that emissions have peaked from the transition to cleaner energy,” they write.

In other words, there hasn’t been a global turn away from fossil fuels and toward renewables. 

Instead, this dip was a short-term response to the high oil prices and supply chain snags caused by the Iran war. And that means we have more to do to expand clean energy and stop the surge in plant-warming greenhouse gas emissions.

“A decline in fossil CO2 emissions is encouraging, but a reduction driven partly by geopolitical shocks is not a sustainable climate solution,” Pierre Friedlingstein, director of the Global Carbon Budget Office at the University of Exeter and leader of the study, said in a statement.

The situation mirrors what happened after the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, when lockdowns that halted commutes and shuttered businesses led to a record drop in carbon emissions.

That drop came about not because of a push toward smart climate policies, but because of one destabilizing event. There was no indication then that this drop in emissions would continue year over year, which is what’s required to decrease emissions enough to meet climate goals.

The impact of the war in Iran brings the same threat: that the world will return to its fossil fuel business-as-usual behavior once oil prices drop and supply chain issues are solved.

“The clean energy transition is delivering real gains: solar, wind and electric vehicles are now offsetting roughly two-thirds of the emissions growth from rising energy demand,” he added. “But those gains are being swallowed by continued and growing fossil fuel use elsewhere: in industry, in emerging economies, and across sectors where the energy transition has barely begun.”

“Changing suppliers will not eliminate this exposure,” he continued. “The only answer is to reduce dependence itself—through clean energy, grids, storage, efficiency and electrification.”

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

More than one in five U.S. home listings received a price cut in August, but a lower asking price does not necessarily mean the property has become a good deal. Price reductions are becoming harder for homebuyers to ignore. In August, 20.4% of U.S. listings had a price cut, matching the rate recorded a year earlier, according to the latest Realtor.com housing report.

The median asking price fell 1.3% year over year to $424,500, marking the tenth consecutive month of annual declines. Meanwhile, pending sales slipped 0.2%, ending an eight-month run of year-over-year growth.

For buyers, the growing number of reductions may look like an invitation to start bargain hunting. However, a crossed-out asking price provides little evidence of the property’s actual value.

“A price cut tells you the seller’s first number did not work. It does not tell you what the home is worth,” says Jeremy Olsher, a principal at Mizner Residential Group. “Some reductions create a legitimate opportunity. Others simply bring an unrealistic price closer to where it should have started.”

Olsher recommends three checks before treating a reduced listing as a deal.

Compare the new price with completed sales. Buyers should begin with recently sold properties that are genuinely comparable in location, size, condition and features. Active listings reveal what other sellers hope to receive, while completed sales show what buyers have accepted.

A $40,000 reduction may look substantial, but it has limited meaning if the home remains $50,000 above comparable sales.

“The percentage reduction is the part buyers notice first because it is easy to understand,” Olsher says. “The more useful question is whether the new price makes sense beside the homes that have actually sold.”

Read the entire listing history. The number and timing of reductions can reveal how the seller is responding to the market. A single adjustment after several quiet weeks may indicate growing flexibility. Several small reductions can suggest the seller is chasing the market downward without fully accepting current buyer demand.

Buyers should also check whether the home was previously listed, withdrawn and returned with a new price or description. A fresh listing date does not always mean the property is new to the market.

“Days on the market need context,” Olsher says. “I want to know whether the seller made one meaningful correction or has spent months making small changes that never addressed the real pricing problem.”

Account for condition and ownership costs. A reduced property can remain expensive once repairs, insurance, taxes, association fees and immediate maintenance are considered. Buyers should compare the home’s total financial exposure with competing properties, rather than evaluating the asking price in isolation.

A lower price may represent genuine value when the cost of resolving known issues has already been reflected. It becomes less attractive when the discount is smaller than the work or ongoing expenses the buyer will inherit.

There may also be room to offer below the revised price, particularly when comparable sales support a lower figure or the property has remained available after multiple reductions. Olsher advises buyers to base that offer on evidence rather than choosing an arbitrary percentage below asking.

“The strongest offer is not necessarily the closest to the seller’s latest number,” he says. “It is the one a buyer can explain with recent sales, property condition and realistic costs. A reduction opens the conversation. The numbers still have to hold up.”

Colorado nonprofit launches plug-in solar program ahead of new state law

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Marie DeFreitas, Contributor | November 10, 2026

A new Colorado nonprofit is looking to bring distributed solar to households that have traditionally been left out of the residential solar market.

Solar Access Colorado, which bills itself as the state’s first organization dedicated to plug-in and balcony solar systems, has launched with pre-orders for small-scale solar kits ahead of the implementation of Colorado House Bill 26-1007, also known as the Improve Customer Use of Distributed Resources bill. The legislation establishes consumer protections for qualifying portable-scale solar devices, including provisions intended to make adoption easier for renters and homeowners living in communities governed by homeowners associations.

The Denver-based organization is positioning plug-in solar as an alternative for residents who cannot install traditional rooftop systems because they rent, live in multifamily buildings or lack suitable roof space.

“Colorado has taken an important step toward expanding access to distributed solar energy,” said Adam Harris, founder of Solar Access Colorado. “We’re launching Solar Access Colorado to help renters, apartment residents and homeowners participate in the clean energy transition with practical, accessible solar solutions.”

HB26-1007 creates a framework for personal, portable-scale solar generation devices. Beginning Jan. 1, 2027, the law will protect qualifying systems from unreasonable restrictions on their installation, use and operation.

The legislation also addresses HOA restrictions by treating qualifying portable-scale solar devices as energy efficiency measures under Colorado’s common-interest-community laws. This could help expand access to solar for residents who previously faced restrictions on making property modifications.

However, the law does not eliminate all installation requirements. Reasonable safety-related restrictions remain permissible, including provisions addressing secure mounting, fire prevention, electrical overload and potential property damage.

Solar Access Colorado plans to help consumers understand the new regulatory landscape and navigate product requirements and installation considerations as the law takes effect. Its initial product offering is designed for balconies, patios, fences and other outdoor spaces, giving residents additional options for generating electricity without committing to a conventional rooftop installation.

Beyond product sales, Solar Access Colorado plans to work with municipalities, housing organizations, property managers and community groups to broaden access to plug-in solar. The nonprofit also intends to prioritize education and outreach to low-income communities and multifamily households.

As states explore policies supporting smaller, customer-sited generation systems, plug-in solar could become another entry point for residential clean energy adoption. Colorado’s upcoming legislation is providing a framework for that market to develop.

D.C. Defends Ticket Resale Cap Amid Lawsuit from SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, TickPick & Gametime

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Secondary ticketing marketplaces and their trade group, Ticket Policy Forum, are seeking to block the district's newly enacted RESALE Act.

The District of Columbia says it should be allowed to cap concert ticket resale prices in the face of a legal challenge brought by a coalition of secondary marketplace operators.

Resale trade group Ticket Policy Forum, along with its members SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, TickPick and Gametime, sued last month to block D.C.’s newly enacted Restricting Egregious Scalping Against Live Entertainment (RESALE) Act as unconstitutional. Scheduled to become enforceable in 2027, the RESALE Act limits secondary ticket price markups at 10% above face value.

The resellers argue that a 10% cap would force them to “operate at a loss” and actually raise consumer prices by driving ticket sales to the black market. They want a judge to issue an immediate injunction barring enforcement of the law. But in a response brief on Monday (Nov. 9), the D.C. Attorney General’s Office says that is both unnecessary and unfair.

“The will of the people plus their interests in protecting consumers and a vital, cultural industry far outweigh the pecuniary interests of a handful of corporations,” write the government lawyers. “Plaintiffs include multi-billion-dollar companies that can easily absorb any hypothesized losses from complying with one city’s law while litigation proceeds. Plaintiffs may later be able to recover those losses, but the harms to the district and its people from a preliminary injunction are truly irreparable.”

D.C.’s attorneys say their resale cap was properly enacted after residents “became fed up with soaring ticket prices,” and that the bill “sailed through” both local government and congressional approval even as ticket resellers lobbied hard to kill it. “Striking out with the democratic process, Plaintiffs dressed their policy objections up in constitutional garb and turned to this court for a judicial veto,” reads Monday’s response brief.

According to D.C., there’s simply no merit to the ticketing companies’ claims that the RESALE Act is unconstitutional. Among other arguments, the resellers have said there’s a First Amendment issue with a portion of the law that requires them to list a ticket’s original, face value cost next to the resale rate. The companies claim this is unlawful “compelled speech” — but D.C. says that argument “strains credulity.”

“The original ticket price is hardly irrelevant to a consumer — without it, the consumer cannot fully understand the value of the resold ticket and make an informed decision about whether to buy it,” write the district’s attorneys.

A hearing on the injunction motion is scheduled for Nov. 19 in D.C. federal court. In a statement to Billboard on Tuesday (Nov. 10), the Ticket Policy Forum said, “The court should bar enforcement of the RESALE Act to prevent the significant and lasting damage it would cause.”

“The law violates the dormant Commerce Clause and D.C. Home Rule Act by regulating sales outside of D.C.; the price and fee caps in the law and arbitrary, confiscatory and violate the Due Process Clause of the U.S. Constitution; and the law compels speech that marketplaces cannot verify in violation of the First Amendment,” added the trade group. “Enforcement of the act also will drive ticket sales into the shadows, subjecting fans to greater risks from street-side scams and increased fraud.”

D.C. is among more than a dozen local governments that have made moves in recent years to cap ticket resale prices. Such laws have passed in Maine and Vermont, and similar bills are under consideration by statehouses in Massachusetts, New York and Tennessee. A proposed resale cap failed in the California legislature this summer after resellers spent millions of dollars lobbying against it.

James Gunn’s DC Universe Was Weird, Ambitious, and Ultimately Unfinished

The writer-director is a casualty of Skydance’s acquisition of Warner Bros. He was building to a future that fans will now likely never see, for better or worse.

The hierarchy of power at DC Studios is changing again. On November 9, news broke that writer-director James Gunn and his producing partner Peter Safran left—later reported as ousted from—their jobs as co-CEOs and co-chairmen of DC Studios. Jeremy Latcham, a former Marvel Studios producer whose past credits include The Avengers and Gunn’s own Guardians of the Galaxy, will replace them on December 1.

Suddenly, DC fans woke up on a Monday to a new regime. But how will we look back on the four years that a prolific genre writer and director, who came up under Lloyd Kaufman’s B-movie studio Troma, oversaw an entire superhero universe?

It was a time of a lot of promise, not all of it fulfilled.

Before we start the eulogies for the DC Universe, it’s important to note that things aren’t over. Yet. Latcham will take over Gunn and Safran’s posts, so DC Studios is still up and running with more movies in the pipeline, many of which bear Gunn’s fingerprints. Man of Tomorrow, for example. The 2027 movie is a direct sequel to Superman, also written and directed by Gunn. Among its biggest moves is introducing a new Wonder Woman (rumored, but not confirmed, to be Adria Arjona). The Batman: Part II, the long-awaited sequel to Matt Reeves’s The Batman, will also open in 2028 with Gunn and Safran involved as producers. Gunn also said on Instagram they have been “engaged on another DC project which I’m writing.”

Both Gunn and Safran are expected to stay at DC, with Deadline confirming they will “continue to produce on various DC projects.” That said, Matthew Belloni at Puck published a story citing sources who say the DCU will reboot, again, after Man of Tomorrow. And at The Hollywood Reporter, Borys Kit and Aaron Crouch detailed the friction Gunn faced while at the studio, and how Gunn and Safran’s exits were forced by new Skydance leaders David Ellison and Ynon Kreiz. THR reports the DC heads felt secure after the big Warner-Paramount merger; Clayface had grossed $100 million, and Gunn was preparing a draft for the third Superman movie when they were told they were out. You can say the DCU is in a crisis right now.

For now, we can say that a critical chapter of DC is over. Gunn will no longer apply his vision and filter across all DC projects, which he must feel a little relieved about. By his own admission, which he wrote on Instagram on November 9, Gunn expressed feeling stretched thin, having to exert energy on movies and shows he wasn’t emotionally invested in to begin with. Said Gunn: “[T]he thing my ADHD hyperfocus has had the most difficulty with has been dividing my time between projects I’m incredibly passionate about and the ones I’m not so much and all the things I have to do as a studio head.”

Gunn and Safran got their jobs in late 2022, but it wasn’t until 2024 that people saw the first glimpse of their reign. Though Gunn had already worked for DC on 2021’s The Suicide Squad and created the spin-off Peacemaker, those projects were separate from other things at DC, then run by Walter Hamada. At the time, its biggest bet was Black Adam, Dwayne Johnson’s long-in-development antihero project that fell flat and led to nowhere. When Gunn took the reins of DC soon after, the former Marvel director had carte blanche to tailor the sandbox to his vision.

In January 2023, Gunn revealed his plans for DC Studios in something he called “Chapter 1: Gods and Monsters.” In a six-minute YouTube video, Gunn ran down his plans for the movies and shows that made up his master plan for the next decade of DC Studios. But for every mention of this movie or that HBO Max show, the first project carried significant weight. It was the first step, the real first impression, as a showcase of what Gunn would do with a DCU bent to his will. What icons would make it to the screen? What legend would get their spotlight? What iconic storylines might be retold on the big screen? What, really, will James Gunn’s DC become?

His first order of business was... Creature Commandos.

In fairness, Creature Commandos was a great show (if you saw it). It was an adult animated series where horror and pulp-inspired superheroes teamed up as a black ops strike force. While a show like that wouldn’t be anyone’s idea of a great introduction, Gunn’s background as the writer of movies like Tromeo & Juliet and his success with Guardians of the Galaxy for Marvel at least made sense. It basically said without ambiguity that this new DCU is being molded by no one else but Gunn.

Then, Superman premiered in summer 2025. Initially written by Gunn, the DC Studios head eventually named himself the director of DC’s first theatrical release under his stewardship. Despite my own misgivings about the movie, it was the reorientation a refreshed superhero universe needed. You simply can’t have a DC Universe without the Man of Steel, whose first appearance in Action Comics #1 in 1938 kicked off the modern superhero as we know it.

Superman made a solid first impression for DC at the cineplex. So there was plenty of optimism for the 2026 spin-off Supergirl starring Milly Alcock. Largely based on the acclaimed Tom King and Bilquis Evely graphic novel Supergirl: Woman of Tomorrow, the pseudo-sequel was poised to keep up DC’s newfound momentum. It didn’t. Supergirl hit a wall, much like Black Adam. No superhero universe is immune to duds, but given it was just DC’s second time at bat, a blemish on what still felt like a new, fresh-out-of-the-box DCU felt grave.

But one dud wasn’t enough to kneecap the DCU. The same summer saw the premiere of Lanterns, which fostered a faithful audience on HBO Sunday nights during its buzzy eight-episode first season. Despite mixed reviews, Clayface has grossed a profitable $100 million against a slim budget. Between the next Superman and Batman sequels, there was still plenty to look forward to. Until now.

James Gunn’s reign wasn’t impervious to bad news either. Since his January 2023 reveal, many of the projects have wound up on the back burner: The Authority, which had been a “passion project” for Gunn (with plenty of setup for it in Superman); Waller, a TV show with Viola Davis reprising her role from the Suicide Squad films; Paradise Lost, a political epic set in Wonder Woman’s home; and Booster Gold, a superhero comedy about a goofball from the future. And throughout his reign, Gunn put up with questions about his Batman (not Matt Reeves’s), which he would have introduced in The Brave and the Bold. Gunn sweated over the character so much that a cameo of Batman in Creature Commandos was shrouded in shadow, because he said he was still deciding on what the costume was going to look like.

Since the start of Gunn’s DC, it was clear his architecture came from a place of affection for the brand, and for very specific corners of it. His body of work reflected his scraggly upbringing as a punk creative, with empathy for characters who were too weird for their own Saturday morning cartoon. The Suicide Squad elevated such figures, like Weasel and Polka-Dot Man, whose climax involved a fight with Starro, a colossal evil starfish. Though his Superman was a sunny throwback to the Christopher Reeve classics, it was also jam-packed with the likes of Green Lantern, Mister Terrific, Metamorpho, and a villain Gunn was clearly setting up to be Bizarro.

Multiple times on social media, Gunn shared artwork from one DC book in particular: Kingdom Come, written by Mark Waid and illustrated by Alex Ross. Published in 1996, the story was a treatise of sorts by Waid, who lamented the state of superhero comics at the time. The story sees an older Superman who comes out of retirement to reform the Justice League, only to meet physical and philosophical resistance—predominantly from Batman.

Gunn’s allusions to Kingdom Come, including the exact shape of Superman’s “S” in his film, suggested he was laying the groundwork for a Kingdom Come-style event movie. It would have been an ambitious endeavor, oddly fitting to Gunn’s sensibilities. In the few years we saw Gunn at work for DC, he was building a universe where tried-and-true icons of superhero comics brushed up against the prickly and the bizarre. Where weirdness leaked from the orifices of a summer tentpole with an action figure set.

It often feels like Gunn was just getting started, but four years with two (soon three) movies written and directed by him, several TV shows—and Supergirl and Clayface, whatever you think of them—leave a big enough impression to suggest that Gunn’s DC had no shortage of imagination, even if his signature flourishes had grown familiar. Not every superhero film needs to have shock F-bombs and action scenes set to ironic licensed music, but Gunn had a lane. He excelled at staying in it.

It’s doubtful Gunn and Safran (who took on the business end of things) will stay on at DC under Skydance leadership for long. They will probably see Man of Tomorrow and The Batman: Part II through. If those movies do well, they might stick around to keep making movies, because for the most part, they have a magic that works. If the new movies fail to take off, then Gunn might finally find new stories to tell. It’s hard to say which path is the better one.

Gunn’s Kingdom Come, whatever it might have been, pointed to a future that never became a promise. In his farewell message, Gunn wrote that “the characters we set up will live on.” Whether the next era will let the DCU stay weird is another matter.

NYC Property Transfers Show Growing Warning Signs of Deed Theft

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

More than one-third of New York City transfers of one- to three-family homes from individual owners to companies last year had gaps in their ownership records that researchers say could signal deed theft or other predatory schemes.

The findings come just days after Victor Quimis, 40, became the first person convicted under New York's criminal deed theft law, after admitting to stealing the home of an elderly Queens widow who was receiving hospice care.

Quimis and an accomplice forged Renuka Bherwani's signature to transfer ownership of the Kew Gardens Hills home she had owned for decades, according to prosecutors—first to themselves and then to a corporation controlled by Quimis.

He subsequently obtained a $552,500 mortgage against the property, pocketing more than $300,000 after paying off existing debts. Bherwani's family discovered the theft only after receiving a city notice congratulating Quimis on becoming the home's new owner.

“My mother lived in that house for nearly 40 years, and she was in hospice care in it when her home was stolen out from under her," said Kamal Bherwani, Renuka’s son and the executor of her estate. “Deed theft targets the elderly and the vulnerable, and I urge any family that sees something wrong with a deed to call the Attorney General's Office immediately."

Researchers at New York University's Furman Center are now seeking to better detect potential fraud through patterns in public ownership records, too.

Their latest analysis found that 34.5% of transfers from individual homeowners to companies in 2025 involved a mismatch between the seller on the new deed and the buyer on the previous deed—the highest share in at least a decade.

The share of transactions with mismatched ownership records has climbed from 26.8% in 2021, while an additional 1.4% of transfers in 2025 involved properties with no previous deed on record.

While these discrepancies don’t necessarily indicate fraud—they can also arise through legitimate transactions like foreclosures—researchers say they can help identify properties warranting closer scrutiny.

In New York City, that scrutiny is particularly important. Decades of rising property values have left longtime homeowners and their heirs with a huge amount of wealth that could be vulnerable to exploitation.

Earlier research from the center identified approximately 45,000 homes worth a combined $34.7 billion with at least one indicator of vulnerability to title-related problems or predatory tactics.

The latest report narrows its focus to 2,300 properties at particularly high risk because at least one listed owner is deceased and the property has no recorded deed or one dating back more than 30 years.

That can create difficulties for heirs from establishing their legal rights to the house—an issue that can compound by cutting off access to home equity or financial assistance, or even create difficulties paying necessary bills.

Indeed, nearly 13% of these high-risk properties owed more than $500 in property taxes, compared with fewer than 2% of all individually owned one- to three-family homes. And they were nearly seven times as likely to have appeared on the city's 2025 tax lien sale list.

The findings come as New York City expands efforts to combat deed theft. In April, Mayor Zohran Mamdani established the Mayor's Office of Deed Theft Prevention to coordinate investigations and protections for vulnerable homeowners.

“This is exactly the kind of crime the state's new anti-deed theft law is intended to deter," Queens Borough President Donovan Richards Jr. said in a statement following Quimis' plea. "Queens homeowners can sleep better knowing our Attorney General will be aggressive in using this anti-deed theft law to protect the homes they have worked so hard for.”

The borough accounted for more than half of the city's high-risk properties, according to NYU's latest research. Meanwhile, the Bronx had the highest prevalence relative to its housing stock.

Jamaica and Hollis in Queens also recorded the greatest number of transfers with mismatched ownership records between 2015 and 2025, followed by Queens Village.

Neighborhoods with the greatest numbers of high-risk properties and flagged transfers tended to have larger shares of Black, Asian, and foreign-born residents than the city overall.

The Furman Center has also developed a restricted mapping tool to help government agencies, law enforcement, and legal services providers identify properties that may need intervention.

Researchers say more detailed property records, targeted legal assistance, and estate planning could help protect homeowners before ownership disputes or fraudulent transactions put their homes at risk.

Poland’s Parliament Votes 240-202 to Let Officials Freeze Crypto Before Court Review

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Poland's Sejm passed a sanctions bill Friday that lets the interior minister freeze bitcoin and bank funds of people on a national list, and a court appeal would not pause the freeze.

Poland’s lower house of parliament voted 240-202 on Friday to let the interior minister freeze crypto assets, bank accounts and other property of people placed on a national sanctions list, before any court reviews the order.

The Sejm, Poland’s lower house, passed the Act on Restrictive Measures at its 68th sitting, with one member abstaining and 17 not voting. The Senate received the bill Monday and has 30 days under Article 121 of the Constitution to adopt, amend or reject it, after which it would go to President Karol Nawrocki.

The law, a government bill that also implements European Union sanctions rules, treats crypto assets as financial means that can be frozen, using the definition in the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation. A freeze, as the text defines it, is meant to stop any movement, transfer, use or other change that would let the holder benefit from the assets.

The freeze applies only to people and entities the interior minister adds to a public national list, either on the minister’s own initiative or at the request of agencies that include the financial watchdog, the central bank, the financial intelligence unit and the internal security service. Grounds include supporting a country already under EU sanctions, serious human rights abuses, or initiating or financing information manipulation that poses a serious threat to Poland’s national security.

A listing on that last ground requires a showing of a real and serious threat to Poland’s interests, borders, security or elections. The bill carves out ordinary journalism, political speech and publishing, unless that activity is a knowing campaign of the kind the law describes.

The interior minister’s decision takes effect the day after it is posted in the ministry’s Public Information Bulletin and is immediately enforceable. There is no request to the minister to reconsider. A listed person may file a complaint with an administrative court, but the law says the decision remains in force until a judgment becomes final, and it sets aside the usual rule that would let a court pause a challenged order.

The measures also bar making funds or property available to listed persons and can exclude them from public contracts. In some cases, the economy minister may appoint a temporary administrator to sell frozen assets or, under a separate order, take them over for the State Treasury.

The Poland bill does not target ordinary bitcoin holders in Poland. It reaches crypto assets owned, held or under the control of people and firms on the sanctions list. Banks, exchanges and other parties face administrative fines if they fail to freeze those assets or make them available, and compliance is checked by the financial intelligence unit and the Polish Financial Supervision Authority for the firms they already oversee.

Clouds Were Once Photography’s Greatest Challenge

The Met Museum’s “Chasing Clouds” is a pleasing romp through the technical problem of capturing clouds at photography’s beginning.

The original 1996 Windows XP default background is arguably the most viewed photo of all time; its cumulus puffs drifting across a way-too-blue sky are seared into my and countless others’ Y2K childhoods. Such images of fluffy clouds are so ubiquitous that it’s hard to believe they once presented one of photography’s greatest technological challenges. The Met exhibition Chasing Clouds is a pleasing romp through the history of this problem through a union of art and science, spanning the early 1800s to 1929 — roughly photography’s first century. While the medium showed promise as “the pencil of nature,” its first processes were overly sensitive to blue light, registering the sky as a blank white space, impossibly overexposed.

While this lent some images a minimalist mystique, like John Beasley Greene’s “The Nile in Front of the Theban Hills” (1853–54), other photographers resorted to painting clouds into their landscapes by hand. An initial breakthrough came when French photographer Gustave Le Gray figured out how to stitch together two pictures — one calibrated to sky, and the other to land and sea. (Photographic fakery is as old as the medium itself.)

Though the exhibition begins with painted cloud studies and ends with photographic modernism, visual discoveries unfold in a dialogue between painting and technology rather than as a scientific march from one to the other. This makes for a much more interesting story; obsolescence is often a capitalist public relations trick.

It was a Victor Wembanyama-to-Stephon Castle connection, not the other way around.

Inside Designer Carla Fernández’s Vibrant Mexico City Home, Full of Indigenous Textiles and Art

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When the Mexico City-based writer and curator Su Wu started working on her new design book, a theme began to emerge: She kept noticing the presence of pre-Hispanic architectural and design traditions in modern Mexican interiors.

It was from this realization that Mesoamerican Modern: Ancient Ideas in Contemporary Mexican Homes was born. The book features 16 homes across Mexico—those of artists, architects, designers, and more—all shot by photographer Luis Garvan.

Wrought iron doors designed by Carla Fernández.

“When I began this project, I thought that we would be documenting a history, like the aura surrounding the artifacts in a museum, the apparitions of the past,” Wu writes in the book’s introduction. “Instead, what I’ve found is the vibrant, living presence of Indigenous ideas in Mexico.”

“In the guest bedroom, pink silk curtains dyed by Fernández cast a glow in the room. The bedspread is from her personal collection of Mesoamerican textiles, made of undyed wool in Teotitlán del Valle, Oaxaca.”—Su Wu

One space that exemplifies those ideas is Casa Flora, the colorful brutalist Mérida home and studio of the Mexican fashion designer Carla Fernández. As a designer, Fernández seeks to preserve the textile traditions of Indigenous and Mestizo communities through collaborations with artisans, bringing ancient practices to a contemporary audience. Fittingly, the home is full of Mesoamerican art and textiles.

Fernández led the renovation of the house, which dates back more than a century and now hosts workshops for Taller Flora, her design lab, which focuses on traditional Indigenous clothing techniques. (You can also stay in the home; the designer lists it for stays on Airbnb.)

Fernández’s husband, the artist Pedro Reyes, created several elements of the space, including a concrete outdoor shower, as well as some of the tiles throughout the space. His sculptures—including a prototype of Mitla Sillas, chairs inspired by the pyramids of the Mitla archaeological site in Oaxaca—are found throughout the home.

The outdoor shower designed by Pedro Reyes.

Reflecting on Fernández’s home, Wu tells Vogue: “I was very moved when writing the book with the idea of the ‘void’ in Mesoamerican architecture, and how so many architects, going back to the writings of Teodoro González de León, described the patio as a central, defining feature of the Mesoamerican home, a space that is at once enclosed and open to the elements. And in Carla’s home, I think there are many moments that exemplify this idea of the boundary between interior and exterior being not a physical one, but rather a marker of movement across psychological states.”

“A latticed window, known in Mexico as a celosia, is a central part of Mesoamerican architecture, used for cross-ventilation and to create a permeable sense of separation and shadow.”—Su Wu

Prototypes of Reyes’s Mitla Sillas chairs.

For Wu, Casa Flora is also a prime example of an idea that chef Elena Reygadas wrote about in her foreword to the book, “which is that handmade things are meant to be lived with no matter how precious, or especially if precious.”

It ended with someone telling us all silly, made-up stories about a warlock named Daniel.

Somehow, this time doesn’t feel like such a waste anymore. It’s…soothing, I think. The slowness, the friction. A lot of this was always present in WoW Classic, sure. But I think the little added touches in Foreverâthe extra quests for things like torches and sleeping bags, the campfires, the layering ensuring that my world is always packed with other peopleâare lending the experience some new magic that wasn’t there before.
