U.S. jury convicts Huawei in a criminal trial over fraud and trade secrets

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Chinese technology giant Huawei was convicted Tuesday in New York on multiple federal criminal charges, including fraud, U.S. sanctions violations and conspiracy to steal trade secrets, following a three-month trial, according to the U.S. Department of Justice.

The verdict marks a major development in a long-running criminal case against Huawei, with charges first publicly disclosed in 2019.

A federal jury found Huawei and three of its subsidiaries guilty of multiple offenses, including bank and wire fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering, the Justice Department said in a statement.

"No person or company is above the law," U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche said, accusing Huawei and its subsidiaries of conducting business in the United States through deception and theft.

The Justice Department said Huawei misrepresented its activities in Iran to banks to secure billions of dollars and retain access to U.S. dollar-clearing services. The company also supplied surveillance cameras that enabled Iranian authorities to monitor protests, the department said.

Among the examples of intellectual property theft detailed by prosecutors were cases involving Cisco and T-Mobile.

Huawei copied source code used in Cisco routers, with its engineers referring to Cisco by spelling its name backward in an attempt to conceal the theft.

Huawei employees also sent photographs and technical details of T-Mobile's touchscreen-testing robot to China, with one employee later removing a component from T-Mobile's laboratory in a backpack, according to the Justice Department.

"For over two decades, Huawei built its global empire on a foundation of fraud, theft, and deception," said Assistant Attorney General Tysen A. Duva of the Justice Department's Criminal Division.

Huawei pushed back against the verdict in a statement issued Wednesday, while welcoming its acquittal on the racketeering conspiracy charge.

"We believe the government's remaining charges are equally without legal or factual merit," Huawei said, adding that the five trade secret-related disputes cited by prosecutors had previously been resolved as civil matters.

The company said it would review the verdicts and take necessary legal action to defend its interests, while remaining focused on maintaining uninterrupted business operations and investing in research and development.

Huawei and its subsidiaries face potential financial penalties, including fines, forfeiture and restitution, with sentencing scheduled for Sept. 14, 2027, according to the Justice Department.

U.S. prosecutors first publicly disclosed criminal charges against Huawei in January 2019, a month after the company's chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou, was arrested in Canada.

Meng returned to China in September 2021 after entering into a deferred prosecution agreement with U.S. prosecutors.

The kingdom's civil defense directorate did not name the tower, but posted an image showing damage to the PIF tower. Eyewitness reports by Al Jazeera also indicated that the building that was hit was the PIF Tower in Riyadh.

The injured included two Saudi citizens, two Indians, three Nepalis, and four Egyptians.

The attack comes as tensions have flared up again between Saudi Arabia, which is leading an international coalition to restore the internationally recognized government of Yemen after it was forced to flee in 2015, and the Houthis.

Turki al-Maliki, a spokesperson for the coalition, said one of the towers of the financial center was targeted in a "cowardly terrorist attack," referring to the financial center in the Saudi capital of Riyadh.

He added that authorities were conducting an on-site investigation at the scene of the attack.

On Wednesday, Houthi brigadier general Yahya Saree warned on X that the port of Jeddah and all Saudi ports along the Red Sea coast will become "targets for our forces," if Riyadh attacks the Yemeni Red Sea port of Hodeidah.

The coalition had asked civilians to evacuate the port "for their safety," on Nov. 20.

Attacks by the rebel Houthis have been escalating on Saudi Arabia, with the group targeting one of Saudi Arabia's oil refineries on Sunday, according to Reuters. The Houthis also launched drone and ballistic missiles against the country's airports, most recently targeting the King Khalid International Airport on Oct. 25, according to reports.

The new attacks come amid wider strikes by Tehran and Iran-linked forces in the Gulf, with Bahrain also reporting that it was attacked by Iran over the weekend. Bahrain is home to the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet.

"As a result of the heinous Iranian aggression, a civilian industrial facility was targeted, causing a fire at the site," Bahrain's Ministry of Interior said on X.

The ministry later said two people, a Bahraini citizen and a Pakistani resident, were injured in the attack.

Tesla (TSLA -0.20%) and Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -5.65%), Elon Musk's two trillion-dollar companies, took a step closer to merging on Tuesday.

Both companies have appointed a total of five directors, three from Tesla and two from SpaceX, to guide two special committees to handle merger negotiations.

The committees have also hired investment banks to develop financial models for the all-stock merger, and a deal could be reached before year's end. If the merger were timely enough, it would also allow SpaceX to be included in the S&P 500 earlier than it would as a stand-alone company.

Naturally, merging two companies that have the same CEO is easier than merging two unrelated companies. Musk has a majority of SpaceX's voting power and 20% of Tesla's voting power, and could own a majority of the combined company.

The dynamics of getting the merger approved versus reinstating Huang are much different, however. Trump just needed to get the board's approval to get Huang back as Nvidia CEO. The Tesla/SpaceX merger, on the other hand, will face a battery of regulatory hurdles to get cleared.

Your First Look at 7th Avenue's First NYC Showroom

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

You don't need much of an imagination to picture yourself curled up on one of 7th Avenue's sectionals. One look at the extra deep seats and plush cushions is enough to have you planning your movie marathon—pass the popcorn, please. But even so, a couch is the sort of thing you want to try before you buy. With the opening of a new flagship showroom in New York City's SoHo neighborhood, the brand is hoping you'll make a day of it; get comfortable, stay a while.

This month, 7th Avenue cut the ribbon on a 3,240-square-foot storefront in a historic cast-iron building at 476 Broome Street. It's the 30th brick-and-mortar space in the US and first permanent location in NYC to open since the brand's founding as a direct-to-consumer business in 2022. Co-founder and CEO Billy Shaw says that he and co-founder Josh Stinson decided to put down roots in the Big Apple after seeing the way New Yorkers took to their hero product—the "World's Greatest Modular Sofa," they call it—during the years they operated a pop-up shop in the city.

“The main showroom celebrates volume and openness, with soaring ceilings, dramatic columns, and refined architectural detailing,” says Stinson.

Co-founders Stinson and Shaw sourced design details for the showroom from around the world. “[These items] have an Old-World patina that balances the modern, contemporary aesthetics of our sofas,” says Shaw.

"We were inspired to create 7th Avenue to solve for a frustration we kept hearing from customers: Finding a sofa that was both beautifully designed and genuinely practical for everyday living was surprisingly difficult," Shaw says. (The brand famously uses water-repellent and stain-resistant performance fabrics, and all sofa covers can be removed and thrown in the washing machine for cleaning.) New Yorkers appreciated the modular designs, which can fit into petite elevators and through the narrow doors and hallways ubiquitous in city homes (gotta love a pre-war building!). “Our sofas arrive in individual boxes, making those tight spaces easier to navigate,” he adds.

A store in New York was a no-brainer, but that doesn't mean finding a location was easy. Shaw and Stinson, who is COO, considered addresses in the Flatiron District, where shoppers can bop in and out of home retailers—like Herman Miller, Design Within Reach, ABC Carpet & Home, Crate & Barrel, the list goes on—without leaving a three-block radius. But ultimately they couldn't let go of SoHo's "unmatched" energy. "We wanted to find a place that is grand and historic, paying homage to NYC’s architectural heritage," says Stinson. With 14-foot ceilings and a skylight, "the space on Broome Street creates a sense of openness and grandeur from the moment customers walk through the door."

Just how durable are 7th Avenue's furniture pieces? During the SoHo store's opening party, guests were encouraged to drop their drinks on the sofas in a “spill lab.”

Shaw and Stinson, who designed the space themselves, say they used the building's architecture to create different vignettes and experiences for guests. The main showroom, for instance, with its dramatic columns and open space, is perfect for showing off 7th Avenue's larger sectionals. Elsewhere, a daybed is nestled into an alcove for a more intimate scene. "We wanted to demonstrate that great furniture design isn't simply about size," says Stinson. "It's about proportion and the relationship between a piece and the space surrounding it."

The two also wanted to show that "beautiful design should work for real life." "That meant balancing the stateliness of the cast-iron architecture and high ceilings with warm materials, organic textures, and comfortable places to sit," says Shaw.

A light and bright backdrop of European oak and Turkish travertine is dotted with treasures gathered from around the world: coral stone coffee tables quarried from volcanic islands in Indonesia, century-old olive oil jars from Turkey and Morocco, sculptural plants like a Hawaiian dragon tree. "A Queensland Bottle Tree Bonsai, native to Australia and sourced from Los Angeles plant specialists The Plant Daddies, was shaped over two years to achieve its distinctive curved form," says Stinson. These details, Shaw adds, have "an Old-World patina that balances the modern, contemporary aesthetics of our sofas."

Guests readily settled into 7th Avenue's plush sofas at the flagship's opening party.

"We want customers to feel comfortable settling into this beautiful sofa, yet imagining it in their own home and being used in their day-to-day lives," says Stinson. At the opening party, props scattered about the furniture brought this idea to life.

For all the finery, 476 Broome Street feels more like someone's home than an art gallery—and that's very on purpose. "We want customers to feel comfortable settling into this beautiful sofa, yet imagining it in their own home and being used in their day-to-day lives," says Stinson. The co-founders want visitors to interact with the furniture. Indeed, a cheeky neon sign that reads "Come Spill on Me" hangs over an emerald velvet couch; at the store's opening party, red wine was passed with nonchalance and guests were invited to plop onto a double-wide sofa and pose for photos with an artful disarray of newspapers, a pizza box, and an overturned gag martini glass—no need to take their shoes off.

According to Shaw and Stinson, great design should accommodate the realities of modern life, messy things like kids, pets, and parties. "Our clients appreciate beautiful, thoughtfully designed spaces, but they also want to live in them without hesitation," says Shaw. "Ultimately, we feel that luxury should be lived in, not simply admired."

Why asking Trae Young to score less is a band-aid, not a fix for the Wizards' self-inflicted problem

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

The Washington Wizards entered Tuesday with a 7-11 record. In their seven wins, Trae Young is averaging just under 11.3 shot attempts per game. In those 11 losses, he's averaging 13.3. It's a small difference, but a notable one. The Wizards came into play Tuesday on a three-game winning streak, and a meaningful part of that streak has been Young spending less time on the ball.

In Washington's first 15 games, he averaged around 6.6 minutes of possession per game, tied for the third-most in the league. He's down to 4.5 in his last three games. He's dribbling around one fewer time per touch, and his average possession per touch is down around a second. These aren't glaring changes, but they've been helpful ones. It's not a coincidence, for example, that No. 1 pick AJ Dybantsa's two best offensive games have come in this stretch.

Young, 28, has been a mess as a scorer this season. He's shooting just 36.4% from the floor and 32.5% on 3s. He's barely getting to the basket and struggling mightily when he's in the paint. He can't beat defenders nearly as easily off the dribble as he once could. Getting the ball out of his hands a bit more has been essential.

It's been an intentional shift on Washington's part. Wizards officials have asked Young to focus less on his own scoring and instead emphasize his playmaking, according to The Athletic. Washington certainly won't complain about a 24-to-7 assist-to-turnover ratio in that stretch. Young, despite his noticeable decline as a scorer, remains a stellar passer. That's typically how star decline works. It comes in bits and pieces. Even as essential components of a player's game degrade, years of accumulated basketball IQ allow him to continue finding ways to contribute. After years of abject tanking, the Wizards seemingly wanted an offensive organizer around to help give their younger players some structure. Young can still do that.

But we're in an increasingly financially unforgiving NBA, and the Wizards aren't paying Young a contributor's salary. They're paying him star money, and they chose to do so after just five relatively quiet games with him a season ago. His four-year, $212 million max contract will pay him at the top of the market through the end of Dybantsa's rookie deal. With Anthony Davis on the books through the end of Kyshawn George and Alex Sarr's rookie deals, the Wizards effectively traded the roster-building flexibility those cheap rookie deals afforded them for two declining stars.

Davis has justified the investment, at least as an asset. They haven't extended him. They could potentially flip him for more than they paid him. Young has not.

And that's the dilemma the Wizards are now in.

Clearly, less Young leads to more winning for the Wizards. But getting enough out of him to justify that enormous contract almost certainly requires more than they're currently getting. The entire value proposition of employing Young rests on him being a high-usage offensive engine. He just doesn't provide enough off-ball offensive value to justify what he gives up defensively at a max price point.

Take his shooting, for example. "Trae's got so much gravity," Wizards coach Brian Keefe told The Athletic. "When he plays off the ball, he's going to draw people, and then we're getting into actions with him on the second side that have been really good." There's something to this in theory, especially since gravity tends to rest more on reputation than reality. But Young has never been a consistently reliable catch-and-shoot player. He's averaging less than two catch-and-shoot 3-point attempts per game this season and has made around 28% of them. He's never averaged more than two catch-and-shoot 3-point attempts per game in a season.

Maybe he improves if his role demands more of these shots, but this is his ninth NBA season. That sort of shift this late in a career is rare.

It's a daunting change. Young spent most of his seven-and-a-half-year Atlanta Hawks run as almost entirely an on-ball player. He's never scored more than 39 points off of cuts in a single season. He has none this season. He's never been an especially active mover or screener, and even if he's grown on that front over the last few years, that's from a starting point of essentially zero. Asking Young to be less of a heliocentric shot creator is essentially asking Young to be a different player entirely.

That was the danger in paying Young sight unseen. They had no idea how he'd fit on this roster when they ponied up a max deal. If he'd picked up his player option and made this a prove-it season, they could have worked out the kinks and tried to sign him to a more appropriate deal next summer. The obvious argument here is that Young might have left had the Wizards refused to give him a long-term extension, but the counter here is that the Wizards would almost certainly have been better off if that had happened.

Stephen Noh's salary model estimates that Young is worth $26.3 million so far this season. That's a bit less than half of what he's getting. If that persists over the life of the deal, it's among the NBA's worst contracts. If his scoring continues to decline as he loses burst with age, it's a borderline crisis. Young may remain a passing genius, but passing skill is not a one-to-one match with passing value. It's easier to set teammates up when you're a dangerous enough scorer to consistently draw help. If Young isn't generating the same sort of advantages he once did, the passes that were once available to him suddenly become harder.

All of this puts the Wizards in an extremely precarious long-term position. They had to get the ball out of Young's hands more in the present. Too much of their offense involved players more important to the organization's long-term plans -- Dybantsa chief among them -- watching him dribble. A lower-usage Young probably makes the Wizards a better team simply by spreading usage to players who need more of it.

But that doesn't mean that a lower-usage Young is especially valuable. On-ball creation is where much of Young's value is generated. His scoring has declined so much that he's not generating the same value he used to, so all of this has stuck the Wizards between a rock and a hard place. They can try to maximize Young's skill set at the expense of everyone else, or they can try to maximize the team as a whole. But in doing so, they're not getting nearly enough out of a player they're paying to be a centerpiece. The latter is probably the right decision, but it's still a choice between two bad options. It's a band-aid on a self-inflicted wound.

My first Thanksgiving was a lavish 12-person dinner in Connecticut in 2014. I had moved to the US in March of that year and had planned on working from home until a friend generously invited me to go spend the day with her family; an act of unguarded openness that I’ve realized is one of the many things that differentiates Americans from Brits. As soon as we got off the train in Cambridge, Massachusetts, I realized that even though my friend lived in a nasty, overcrowded apartment in Brooklyn, she came from a family that had immense wealth. After dinner, as I helped wash plates in the kitchen, my friend’s mum asked her why she wouldn’t just move into the lovely building they had bought for her. She turned beet red and handed me another dirty plate without looking at me.

Take it from an immigrant: this holiday is full of weird contradictions. Of course, there’s the annual reminder of colonization which requires a careful forgetting of the people who have been colonized. And then there’s the financial aspect of it. At first blush, it seems like the least capitalist holiday of the year—there isn’t the expense of Christmas or Hanukkah gift-giving or the extravagance of Halloween candy. Yet the holiday also marks the start of one of the year's busiest travel seasons when families are taking great expense to reunite, vacation, and explore outside of their every day lives.

As we approach the last Thursday of November, with money very much on our minds, the below well-traveled parents share their hard-won wisdom on pulling off affordable family travel—including how to find treasure in New York City’s Chinatown without paying an arm and a leg, seek out free entertainment in the streets of Oaxaca, and embrace the joys of accessible public transportation in Europe. All of these stories say something about the wealth of the person writing it, whether or not they wanted to reveal that information. I’m obsessed with getting to the truth about someone’s class (it’s why I wrote my book on the subject), and so these family dispatches, which serve as equal parts advice and confession, are a treat I’ll come back to when I’m not busy washing a rich family’s plates. —Mona Chalabi, Pulitzer Prize-winning writer and illustrator

If your hotel room has a refrigerator you can store any pre-cut fruit that you buy, or opt for items that don’t require refrigeration like apples, bananas, and clementines. Bring a lightweight backpack, refill each morning, and watch the hangry meltdowns (mostly) vanish. —Dana Kravis, global executive director, CNT video

Last December in London, we stayed in a quaint Airbnb in Primrose Hill and explored different neighborhoods on foot. When our kids, then eight and three, got tired, we hopped on a double-decker bus and headed upstairs, hoping for front seats. Suddenly, getting home became an amusement park ride: Christmas lights, bustling streets, panoramic views. The kids loved it; as a photographer, I loved the vantage point. London caps daily bus fares when you use the same card or phone, and children under 11 ride free. We’ve done this in Singapore, too. Our rule: skip the sightseeing tour (or Uber) and make public transportation part of the family adventure. —Tanveer Badal, photographer

Avoiding the time and expense of a sit-down breakfast, and indulging in a bit of morning self-care, means we can focus on the adventures and meals we are actually going to remember. —Chris Morocco, food director of Bon Appétit and Epicurious

Here's How Much Of A Flight Airline Pilots Actually Hand-Fly

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

guys_who_shoot | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

On a typical commercial flight, airline pilots only spend a small proportion of their time physically controlling the aircraft. Pilots normally hand-fly takeoff and part of the initial climb, while the autopilot handles most of the flight until approach. According to a 2026 report by the International Air Transport Association (IATA), commercial aircraft operate with automation engaged for approximately 90% of the time.

On a long-haul flight, a pilot may spend just a few minutes controlling a Boeing 787 or Airbus A350, although operating procedures and weather conditions can influence this substantially. Moreover, a pilot not physically moving the controls does not mean the aircraft is operating without human supervision. The crew remains responsible for managing the flight path, monitoring the automated systems, and taking over whenever necessary.

Airborne manual flying normally starts with takeoff, which remains a pilot-controlled maneuver on conventional commercial airplanes. Taxiing is generally manual as well, but is not included in the airborne flight time. The pilot maintains directional control, rotates, and establishes the initial climb. Although various systems can manage engine power, this should not be confused with the aircraft performing an automatic takeoff. The autopilot can only be engaged once the aircraft has reached its approved minimum engagement height.

After departure, the crew can generally engage the autopilot relatively early, sometimes only a few hundred feet above the ground. According to IATA's 2020 Aircraft Handling and Manual Flying Skills Report, many surveyed pilots reported engaging it around 400 or 500 feet above ground level, while others preferred to continue flying manually through 5,000 or even 10,000 feet. The exact altitude depends on the aircraft and airline.

Once engaged, the autopilot can handle most of the climb, cruise, and descent, following the route and altitude instructions managed by the crew. On arrival, the pilot may disconnect it early during a visual approach or keep it engaged until shortly before landing. The final flare and touchdown are normally performed manually, although suitably equipped aircraft can also carry out automatic landings under the appropriate conditions.

Estimates of how much time airline pilots spend hand-flying differ substantially, partly because there is no universal operating procedure. Back in 2011, airline captain Rory Kay, co-chair of a Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) advisory committee on pilot training, said that pilots were using automation "for all but approximately three minutes of a flight".

Another committee member, captain Bob Coffman, provided an even lower estimate for some regional airline operations. According to Coffman, pilots at commuter airlines might fly with the autopilot disengaged for approximately 80 seconds during a typical two-hour flight. Both estimates are historical rather than current industry averages.

Other pilots take a somewhat different approach. In an interview published by Johnny Jet, airline pilot Spencer Marker explained that most airline pilots spend ten minutes or less manually controlling their aircraft. However, he personally preferred to hand-fly departures until reaching 10,000 feet and disconnect the autopilot approximately five miles from the runway when weather and operating conditions allowed.

The length of the flight also makes a significant difference. Ten minutes represents almost 17% of a one-hour flight, but only 1.7% of a ten-hour service. On an ultra-long-haul operation, such as Qantas' nonstop Perth (PER) - London (LHR) route, the proportion can become even smaller.

From filling out paperwork to maintaining communication and constantly checking the instruments, the pilots are kept busy on the flight deck.

Although the autopilot physically controls the aircraft for much of a flight, it is not an independent system that simply receives a destination and makes every decision. Modern airliners use several connected systems, including the flight management system, flight director, autopilot, and automatic thrust controls. These each perform different functions, and the crew must ensure that the appropriate modes are selected and that the aircraft is following its intended flight path.

There is also an important difference between flying manually and flying without any assistance. A pilot can disconnect the autopilot while keeping the flight director engaged, which provides guidance on the primary flight display. Automatic thrust can also remain active while the pilot controls the aircraft's pitch and bank. Flying using basic instrument indications without flight director guidance, known as raw-data flying, demands additional proficiency.

During cruise, the pilots continue monitoring navigation, weather, fuel consumption, aircraft systems, and instructions from air traffic control. Changes to the assigned route or altitude must be correctly entered and verified. Automation also helps distribute workload between the pilot flying and pilot monitoring, especially during departures and approaches where both pilots may be managing several tasks simultaneously.

The time spent holding the controls therefore provides little indication of the crew's overall workload. Even with autopilot engaged, the pilots must decide whether the selected automation remains appropriate.

The pilot landing an ultra-long-haul A350 needs two consecutive hours of rest in the second half of the flight.

Airlines can take remarkably different approaches to manual flying. Some encourage pilots to use the highest available level of automation throughout most of the flight, while others explicitly allow manual flying whenever weather, air traffic, and cockpit workload permit. Manual flying opportunities therefore depend heavily on each airline's standard operating procedures (SOP).

IATA's 2020 survey, which received 5,650 responses overall, found that only 36% of the 5,241 pilots answering the relevant question said their airline allowed and supported manual flying without limitations. A separate question attracted 3,452 responses, of which approximately 37% admitted hesitating to disengage the autopilot and take manual control. Some respondents also feared criticism for hand-flying even where procedures permitted it.

Complex departures, congested airspace, and reduced visibility can increase workload, making automation the safer option. Some approaches and airspace procedures also require automation for precise navigation. An airline therefore needs to balance manual flying opportunities against the operational conditions of each individual flight.

IATA's survey also found differences during landing, with many pilots disconnecting the autopilot around 1,000 feet above the runway when conditions permitted. Others did so earlier or at the minimum altitude allowed by the relevant procedure. An approach flown with the autopilot engaged is not automatically an autoland; in most cases, pilots still take control before touchdown.

Modern airliners are incredibly automated, but pilots remain fully responsible for every decision during a flight. What do they actually do?

One particularly interesting difference concerns the type of flying performed by the crew. Short-haul pilots might operate several sectors on the same day, giving them multiple opportunities to practice takeoffs and landings. Long-haul pilots, on the other hand, can spend most of a working day on one flight, and augmented crews may share the workload during particularly long services. Consequently, each pilot may perform far fewer takeoffs and landings.

A 2017 study surveyed more than 1,500 commercial pilots about manual flying and airline procedures. Only 35.5% of long-haul pilots agreed that they had sufficient opportunities to hand-fly, compared with 79.9% of pilots operating short- and medium-haul flights. Pilots flying aircraft such as the Boeing 777, Airbus A380, and Airbus A330/A340 were among those reporting the fewest opportunities.

The same study also identified a clear difference between airline cultures. Among pilots working in supportive procedural environments, 68% felt they had sufficient opportunities to maintain their manual flying skills during normal operations. That figure fell to just 26% among pilots whose airlines imposed more restrictive procedures.

The difference is also not simply explainable by aircraft age or technology. United Airlines continues operating its Boeing 767 fleet even as newer widebodies enter service, and both generations rely extensively on automation. Accumulating flying hours on long sectors does not necessarily mean practicing basic aircraft handling.

While most landings follow a stable approach, almost all unstable approaches are followed by landing.

Concerns about reduced manual flying opportunities have been raised by aviation regulators for well over a decade. The Associated Press reported in 2011 that a draft FAA study had examined 46 accidents and major incidents, alongside hundreds of voluntary reports and observations from more than 9,000 flights. It found problems involving manual aircraft handling or automated flight controls in more than 60% of the accidents and 30% of the major incidents reviewed. These were findings from a selected sample, rather than percentages applying to all airline accidents.

The FAA subsequently issued Safety Alert for Operators 13002 in 2013, encouraging airlines to provide suitable opportunities for manual flying. A further safety alert in 2017 called for manual flying proficiency to be reinforced through both training and normal line operations. It recommended manual instrument departures and arrivals, upset recovery, and training across different automation modes.

The issue has not disappeared. In its 2026 flight path management report, IATA identified manual handling as an important safety concern and called for training and airline policies that develop both manual and automated flight path management skills. Full-flight simulators can reproduce unreliable airspeed, unusual attitudes, and other situations unsuitable for practice on commercial flights.

BYD in talks to buy Ineos’ French factory as Grenadier moves to US

The French factory Mercedes built for the tiny Smart city car, and that Jim Ratcliffe turned into the home of his Land Rover Defender tribute, could soon be building BYDs.

BYD confirmed today that it has entered exclusive negotiations to acquire Ineos Automotive’s plant in Hambach, France. If it closes, it would be the Chinese giant’s second passenger car factory in the EU after Hungary, and the first time a Chinese automaker buys an existing car plant in Europe.

The other half of the deal is Ineos packing up. Alongside the announcement, Ineos Automotive said it plans to move Grenadier and Quartermaster production to the US, which it says accounts for about 70% of its sales. It has shortlisted US sites, hasn’t named any, and is aiming to start production there in 2028.

The math is simple. Every Grenadier shipped from France to the US gets hit with a 15% tariff, and the Quartermaster pickup falls under the 25% tariff on imported pickups. When 7 out of 10 of your customers are American, building your trucks in Lorraine stops making sense.

Ineos says it will keep building both models at Hambach “for the medium term” if the sale goes through, so the two companies would share the site for a while.

Neither side disclosed a price or a closing date, and the sale still has to go through consultation with employee representatives. BYD will soon find out that France is nothing like China when it comes to employee representation. There’s also no word yet on which BYD models would come out of Hambach or when. Same for the plant’s roughly 1,000 employees: BYD made no commitment on how many it would keep.

BYD executive vice president Stella Li said the company wants to give European consumers “vehicles built in France” that use BYD’s technology.

Hambach opened in 1997 as “Smartville,” Mercedes’ factory for the Smart Fortwo, and it later built the all-electric Smart EQ Fortwo. Ineos bought it from Mercedes at the end of 2020 after dropping its plan to build a new factory in Wales, and Grenadier production started in 2022. Ineos says it has put nearly €540 million into the site since 2019.

Capacity is about 30,000 Grenadiers a year. That’s not much by BYD’s standards. BYD sold 3,561,615 passenger vehicles globally in the first 10 months of the year, which means 30,000 units is less than three days of its output.

There’s also a bit of irony in who’s buying. Ineos unveiled its first EV, the Fusilier, in early 2024 while romanticizing gas cars at the same event. A few months later, it shelved the electric model indefinitely, blaming “reluctant consumer uptake” of EVs. Now it’s selling its only factory to the world’s largest EV maker.

This isn’t a surprise. Back in May, Stella Li said BYD was in talks with Stellantis and “other companies too” to take over underused plants in Europe, and that BYD would rather own and run a plant than form a joint venture. Hambach fits that description exactly.

BYD’s first European car plant, in Szeged, Hungary, started trial production earlier this year and is expected to reach mass production in early 2027, with the Dolphin Surf and Atto 2 first in line. BYD eventually wants more than 150,000 vehicles a year out of it. Its separate factory project in Turkey has been put on pause.

The motivation is regulatory. China-built BYD EVs face a 17% countervailing duty in the EU on top of the standard 10% car tariff, imported Chinese hybrids are now subject to quotas, and the EU’s proposed “Made in Europe” rules would tie EV subsidies and public contracts to how much of a vehicle is built locally. A French plant solves a lot of those problems at once.

And BYD isn’t alone. Leapmotor started building the B10 at Stellantis’ Zaragoza plant in Spain in October, Dongfeng plans to build cars at a Stellantis factory in France, Geely is reportedly lining up a Ford plant in Spain, and Chery already assembles vehicles in Barcelona.

Chery is the one BYD should watch. Through October, BYD registered 320,445 vehicles in Europe, up 133%, according to Automotive News. Chery Group was just ahead with 320,631, up 235%. A gap of 186 cars.

Europe matters more than ever for BYD because home isn’t going well. BYD brand sales in China fell 40% to 1,544,699 vehicles over the first 10 months, and its global passenger vehicle sales are down 2.7%.

This is a smart, cheap move for BYD. Building Szeged took years, including land disputes with private owners. Hambach comes with a trained workforce, a paint shop, a supplier base on the German border, and a seller that’s motivated to leave. You don’t get a much easier entry into France, but the easiest in France is still probably more difficult than anything BYD faced in China when it comes to deploying new production capacity.

The 30,000-unit capacity is small, but BYD isn’t buying it for what it builds today. It’s buying a “Made in France” label ahead of local content rules.

We also can’t ignore the symmetry here. Ineos is leaving Europe to build in the US because of tariffs. BYD is buying into Europe because of tariffs. That’s protectionism moving factories. Europe keeps the jobs, and the workers who used to build Smart EVs might get to build EVs again.

And yes, it’s funny that BYD is moving into the home of the Grenadier, a vehicle that exists because Ratcliffe loved the old Defender, days after a court in The Hague barred BYD’s Ti7 from the EU for infringing Land Rover’s Defender design.

The real question is what BYD puts on the line in Hambach. If it’s battery-electric cars, great. If it’s plug-in hybrids built to get around quotas, Europe is trading one gas-burning 4×4 factory for another.

Condo vs. Townhouse: Which Investment Will Turn Around More Equity After a Decade?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Between 2014 and 2024, the difference in price appreciation between condos and single-family homes was less than 5 percentage points nationally, according to an analysis from Realtor.com®. Townhouses came even closer to matching the performance of detached homes—trailing by just 0.8 percentage points.

It's a remarkable outcome for the buyers opted out of the which picket fence dream, and instead invested in properties that often offer a more affordable entry point into homeownership. But that outcome may be harder to replicate in today's rapidly changing housing market.

Condo values fell 0.8% year over year in August 2026, even as single-family home values rose 1.8%, according to data from Intercontinental Exchange. And as home price growth cools, expenses that were easier to stomach during years of rapid appreciation stand to take a bigger bite out of homeowners' returns.

Even so, those national numbers tell only part of the story. In some regions, condos substantially outperformed townhouses over the decade examined by Realtor.com, challenging the notion that owning a piece of land necessarily leads to stronger appreciation.

Understanding what drove those differences—and how the economics of owning each property type are changing—offers some clues as to where buyers might find the greatest opportunity to build equity over the next decade.

Single-family homes appreciated 87.3% from 2014 to 2024, according to Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones. Townhomes nearly matched that pace at 86.5%, while condos trailed only slightly at 82.7%.

That modest gap are striking given the disparity in purchase prices. In October 2026, the median existing condo or co-op sold for $365,100—nearly $57,000 less than the median single-family home, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR).

“Single-family homes are still the most popular type of homes purchased in the U.S. However, condos do appeal to buyers who want a smaller square footage and less upkeep,” explains Jessica Lautz, NAR deputy chief economist and vice president of research. “Condos are also significantly more affordable than single-family homes, which allows buyers who are downsizing or those just starting into homeownership to embrace condos.”

Still, the numbers show townhomes have been edging closer to single-family performance.

“Over the last decade, condos and townhomes appreciated almost in lockstep, but townhomes have pulled slightly ahead recently as buyers chase ‘house-like’ living without the single-family price tag,” says Jones.

While both property types have delivered strikingly similar returns from a national lens, as in all things real estate, the real gains come down to location, location, location.

In the Midwest, condo prices rose 78.3% over the last decade, outpacing the 70.7% growth of townhomes. The South followed a similar pattern, with condos climbing 66.7% compared with just 53.1% for townhomes.

The dynamic flips in higher-cost regions. In the Northeast, townhomes surged 80.5%, far ahead of the 58.6% gain for condos. And in the West, townhomes appreciated 83.8%, edging past condos at 78.9%.

Affordability explains much of the split, says Jones.

“In the more affordable Midwest and South, condos have gained ground faster than townhomes. In higher-cost regions like the Northeast and West, townhomes have appreciated more quickly,” she explains.

In lower-cost markets, condos benefit from their clustering in downtown areas and their appeal to buyers chasing city living. But in expensive, supply-constrained metros, the premium shifts toward townhomes.

“Townhomes are commanding a growing premium as buyers seek a house-like property when single-family homes are out of reach. In more affordable markets, condos—often concentrated in dense city centers—have captured stronger growth as buyers compete for limited housing options,” she adds.

“Townhomes look set for steadier growth,” says Jones. Part of that is because of what you get when you buy one.

When you purchase a townhouse, you own both the structure and the parcel of land it sits on. That land ownership gives you more control, typically fewer restrictions, and it can be a stronger driver of long-term appreciation.

“Generally townhouses have strong appreciation potential because the land itself appreciates in a more reliable way,” explains Aman Sharma, a real estate agent at SERHANT.

Eric Hughes, a real estate investor and coach with Rental Income Advisors, agrees. “The price growth of townhomes will track extremely closely with the price growth of single-family homes in general,” he says.

That connection to single-family homes is key. The “single-family feel” broadens their appeal to a wider range of buyers—families, downsizers, and even first-timers looking to get their foot in the door.

They also tend to come with lower HOA fees than condos. That not only frees up more buying power for the mortgage itself, but it also makes resale easier since future buyers are less likely to be put off by steep monthly dues or looming assessments.

Builders have taken notice, too. Townhouses accounted for a record 18.7% of single-family housing starts on a one-year moving average basis in the third quarter of 2025, according to the National Association of Home Builders.

But Jones warns that this investment isn’t free of potential headwinds. In markets where single-family inventory is expanding and prices are softening, townhome demand may take a hit as more buyers opt for detached homes instead.

“In tight urban markets, condos can still shine as buyers get creative,” says Jones.

Even though the appreciation rate of condos has lagged behind townhomes and single-family homes nationally over the last decade, they can still deliver strong returns in the right conditions.

“In big urban markets, condos are often the most desirable type of urban residence, and can appreciate strongly,” says Hughes. That means that in places like Miami Beach, New York, and San Francisco—where land is scarce and demand for city living is strong—condos dominate.

“There are certain parts of our city where highly amenitized condo high-rises perform better than townhouses from an investment standpoint,” says Miltiadis Kastanis, executive director of sales at Compass, based in Miami.

Lifestyle amenities are another draw. Concierge service, pools, gyms, and other perks make condos especially attractive to luxury buyers or second-home seekers who prioritize convenience. For full-time residents, those amenities can also translate into higher perceived value when it’s time to sell.

But again, Jones stresses that there is greater potential for some bumps in the road for condos, ranging from rising ownership costs to competition from single-family homes and townhouses.

The experts we spoke to stress that your return on investment isn’t just about appreciation and loan paydown. The hidden costs of homeownership can chip away at returns. However, it’s worth noting that even single-family homes can fall prey to these same traps.

“While single-family homes may not have an HOA fee, some communities do even for single-family homes,” says Lautz. “Maintenance can be more expensive when a homebuyer must account for costly items such as roofs, which a condo owner is unlikely to be responsible for.”

While it’s true that owners of single-family homes must shoulder all their own maintenance costs, condo owners risk getting hit with special assessments that may cover repairs or upgrades to facilities they don’t use, or work that should have been done properly the first time around.

Rick Bannon, global real estate adviser at One Sotheby’s International Realty, points to a condo in Miami where residents were hit with a $1 million special assessment just to bring the building up to safety standards.

That’s why Joe Luciano, broker at Re/Max Bentley’s, takes special care when helping his clients through a condo purchase.

“When advising clients on a condo purchase, we always want to get the meeting minutes to see if there’s any chatter around potential projects coming up and then also the financials to see if they have the appropriate amount in reserve funds to cover any surprises,” he says.

Those reserves are set to become an even more important consideration for buyers. Beginning Jan. 4, 2027, Fannie Mae will raise its standard minimum replacement reserve requirement from 10% to 15% of annual budgeted assessment income for condo projects subject to its Full Review process.

While the change could help protect condo owners against the financial fallout of deferred maintenance, it could also put upward pressure on monthly dues in underfunded communities. And buildings that fail to meet mortgage eligibility requirements could face a smaller pool of buyers.

Outside of special assessments, Luciano says monthly HOA fees can take a big bite out of a homeowner's return on investment.

Nearly 3.4 million American households paid at least $500 a month in condo or HOA fees in 2025, according to data from the U.S. Census Bureau. Roughly 1 million paid at least $1,000 a month.

“When looking at the two as investments side by side over 10 years, the appreciation could be similar, but paying an extra $300 to $500 a month is a sunk cost of $36,000 to $60,000 that didn’t go toward your mortgage principal,” explains Luciano.

At the extreme end, he points to luxury Boston high-rises with $2,000-plus monthly HOA fees that have seen stagnant appreciation in recent years.

If the last 10 years prove anything, it’s that condos and townhouses will both continue to deliver strong returns—just not always for the same reasons, or in the same places.

Townhomes have shown they can reliably shadow the trajectory of single-family homes, powered by land value and a “house-like” appeal that buyers continue to prize. Condos, by contrast, thrive where affordability and density drive demand, making them especially potent in urban cores and lower-cost regions where buyers are squeezed out of detached homes.

The next decade may sharpen these divides. As affordability pressures deepen, first-time buyers could lean more heavily into condos, reviving demand in downtowns that have seen uneven recoveries since the pandemic. At the same time, zoning reforms and the ongoing shortage of single-family homes could keep pushing buyers toward townhouses as the closest substitute for a detached home. Policy choices—from property tax incentives to how cities handle condo reserve requirements after recent safety crises—will also influence which type of property captures the bigger share of appreciation.

For investors, the lesson isn’t to pick one winner and walk away. It’s to understand how local affordability, supply constraints, and regulation interact to tilt the playing field.

Young Adults Living With Parents Surge Fastest in America’s Once-Affordable Regions

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Young adults living with their parents have become a familiar symbol of America's housing affordability crisis. But the geography of that living arrangement is changing, according to new research.

A record 25.2 million Americans aged 18 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, or roughly 1 in 3 young adults. Nationally, that share barely budged between the pooled 2020–22 and 2023–25 periods, according to The Geography of the Crowded Nest, a new report from Realtor.com®.

Beneath that apparent stability, however, the South and Midwest—long considered the nation's affordability havens—recorded the largest year-over-year increases in 2025, with the share living at home rising 1.4 percentage points to 32.3% and 31.7%, respectively.

Meanwhile, in the notoriously expensive Northeast—where living with parents has historically been most common—the rate fell 2.3 percentage points to 36.9%, its lowest level since 2010.

It pushes back against conventional logic that cheaper housing markets offer young adults an easier path to independence.

"This is not a coastal problem that has stayed coastal; it has spread to even the more-accessible parts of the country," writes Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com and author of the report.

Perhaps the clearest illustration comes from two metros that, at first glance, seem worlds apart: San Antonio, TX, and Boston, MA.

One is steeped in cowboy culture; the other, Ivy League academia. In October, the median asking rent in Boston was $2,954, more than double the $1,117 in San Antonio. Yet the share of young adults living with their parents was nearly identical: 33.6% in the Alamo City and 34.3% in Beantown.

“The short answer is that different affordability constraints can produce the same outcome,” says Jones.

Take the Midwest, where the median home listing price is nearly $183,000 below the Northeast's. It's a sizable gap that obscures the fact that prices in both regions have climbed at nearly the same pace since before the pandemic: 48.4% and 50.8%, respectively, according to data from Realtor.com.

Even excluding 18- to 24-year-olds, who may still be establishing their careers and are therefore more vulnerable to economic pressures, the Midwest reached a record: 14.8% of those ages 25 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, the highest rate for that age group in 25 years.

At the same time, many affordable markets didn’t see the same run-up in local wages as they did in home prices, which only deepened the divide.

"In San Antonio, the problem is the wage-price gap," says Jones.

Geoffrey Sanzenbacher, an economist at Boston College who has studied the rise in young adults living with parents, points to another side of that relationship.

"The same things that might make an area expensive—lots of good jobs with good pay—also serve to keep people out of their parents' home," he tells Realtor.com.

"Areas with hot labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't afford an apartment. Areas with poor labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't find a job," he adds.

His September research found that higher rents were associated with greater co-residence among adults aged 25 to 34. Stronger employment and wages were associated with lower rates among men, although the relationship was not statistically significant among women.

Of the 20 major metros examined, 12 recorded increases between the pooled periods, while eight saw declines.

Las Vegas experienced the largest increase, followed closely by San Francisco and San Jose, CA—markets with vastly different housing costs. Even in Texas, San Antonio's rate climbed while Houston's fell.

One possible explanation lies in who benefits when housing becomes more available.

In San Antonio, active home listings are now more than 40% above pre-pandemic levels, according to Jones. Yet the metro recorded one of the largest increases in young adults living with their parents.

"By and large, the market has homes available, but local young adults can't qualify for them at current prices and rates," Jones says, noting that higher-earning newcomers and existing homeowners looking to trade up are better positioned to purchase those properties.

But San Antonio's rental market has also improved. Approximately 6,900 apartments were completed in 2025, according to Northmarq. By year's end, vacancies had reached 11.8% and asking rents had fallen 4.5%.

Boston offers a counterpoint. Jones suggests that new rental construction, combined with the metro's higher-paying industries, may have helped some young adults establish independent households despite a much tighter housing market.

"Markets where supply growth is concentrated in the mid-to-upper tiers still see entry-level households struggle to keep up," Jones says. "The lesson for other markets is about the type of supply that moves the co-residence needle."

There's one last side to the story of young adults moving back home: A growing number are opening their own homes to their parents.

In his research, Sanzenbacher found that the share of adults aged 25 to 34 hosting their parents increased from 1.6% in 1996 to 4% in 2025—a living arrangement outside the scope of the new co-residence figures from Realtor.com.

In Boston, George Sarkis, co-founder and CEO of The Sarkis Team at Douglas Elliman, says some parents are helping adult children with down payments, while other families are considering shared living arrangements.

"It can also work in the other direction, with adult children helping aging parents while allowing everyone to remain close," he says.

Sarkis adds he's seeing buyers considering how a property can serve different generations over time. That can mean finding homes with private suites or separate living quarters that allow family members to maintain their independence.

"Living under the same roof doesn't necessarily mean sacrificing independence. With the right property and layout, it can be a strategic financial decision that benefits multiple generations," he adds.

But those arrangements can introduce another financial hurdle.

A separate analysis from Realtor.com found that homes marketed for multigenerational living in San Antonio received 65.2% more page views than standard listings in 2025. Yet their median asking price was $573,105—nearly 69% higher than standard homes.

Lucy Sante Talks About What Collage Enables That Writing Can’t

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Executive Editor, ARTnews & Art in America

Lucy Sante started making collages long before she established herself as a writer of incisive theories of photography (see Walker Evans, from 1999, Evidence, from 1992, and 2009’s Folk Photography: The American Real-Photo Postcard 1905–1930); of urban studies (see Low Life: Lures and Snares of Old New York, her 1991 chronicle of the city’s underworlds); and of personal history (2024’s I Heard Her Call My Name: A Memoir of Transition). As a teenage émigré in the 1960s, she would clip and assemble images from magazines, a practice she has continued on and off ever since—drawing lasting inspiration from the metaphysical juxtapositions of René Magritte, a fellow Belgian. As a clerk at the Strand, a storied used bookshop in New York, she began working with a wider range of materials, from cast-off paperbacks and technical manuals to medical textbooks and other arcane publications. All the while, she developed a mysterious and poetic style of collage that mixed imagery from different eras and suggested the outlines of stories, however elusive those might be. Over the decades, Sante devoted herself most fervently to writing, with a focus on photography: In 2023, she retired as a professor of writing and the history of photography at Bard College. Her newest book, My Heart & I Agree (2026), is an essay collection that covers subjects including Surrealist photography, cowboy iconography, and Soul Train. There, her keen eye toward camera culture remains evident. She refocused some of her energies on collage in recent years, and her visual work featured this past spring in solo exhibitions at Picture Theory in Chelsea and at the American Academy of Arts and Letters in upper Manhattan, where an early collage from the ’70s joined works made since 2020. From her home in upstate New York, Sante spoke with A.i.A. about learning to look through magazines, moving her way into fine-art collage, and finding poetry in images she gives new life.

What do you remember about your early collages that made you want to keep making them?

I had two sources of materials for my earliest collages: a stack of Arizona Highways magazines from the 1950s and early ’60s, and my parents’ subscription to Life. I remember a collage that I made featuring Jimi Hendrix and a mushroom cloud: That was hip. I was imitating collages I saw—not artistic collages, but collages in popular media. In the early Rolling Stone, on the letters page, almost every week they’d have a collage by this graphic designer named Wilfried Sätty, whose house I stayed in years later, after his death in 1982. He had a particular style: outer space plus old engravings.

I’m colorblind, and that was a big problem for me. I drew, but had to work in black and white. I wasn’t that bad—just not as good as I wanted to be. In high school, I started making band flyers, which I continued when I was living on the Lower East Side in the 1970s and ’80s. I made several for the Del-Byzanteens, a band I also wrote lyrics for. Many people in the band became celebrated in one way or another: Jim Jarmusch as a filmmaker, Jamie Nares as a painter, and Phil Kline as a composer.

How did your work at the Strand Bookstore find its way into your collages?

We started a collective publication called Stranded that was originally going to be like a workers’ gripe sheet. But instead, we published art and poetry and stuff like that. I had collage-like things in all four issues. Within a week of my starting, Fred Bass [fabled owner of the Strand] named me the paperback department—I was the entire department all by myself. Bookmen in that period did not take paperbacks seriously and just put them out in the 25-cent carts. That meant I had to deal with romance books, and when I put those out on Thursdays, they were mobbed by old ladies, and it was a nightmare. But it also meant that whenever they bought a library, they would throw me everything that wasn’t a hardcover book, including magazines, maps, postcards, and all kinds of stuff that I took home and still have. I made collages recently with material I acquired back then at the Strand.

What was the Strand like as a downtown institution then compared to now?

Nobody was very literary on the Lower East Side in the ’70s. People were starting bands and making movies. There wasn’t even painting at the time. We had to wait for Basquiat. In the ’70s, we had some bad poets, a few bad essayists. Nobody [in the neighborhood] wrote fiction. But at that point, the sociology was nothing to compare to now, because you could waltz into New York City hitchhiking from Denver or somewhere and find yourself a cheap apartment and get a job at the Strand. Your salary would pay your rent, and you had money left over to go clubbing. That’s long gone now.

What were you looking for? What made something conducive to collage?

I liked texture and was really interested in contrasting eras. I liked faded color. I was taking inspiration from a lot of different sources. Of the flyers I made for the Del-Byzanteens, one of them [drew from] big posters that a syndicate put out to try to promote a science-fiction comic strip. I X-Acto–knifed out the thought balloons and filled them in with other material. That was because I had a friend who’d just come over from Paris with a Situationist International book, and I got inspired by that.

You mentioned beginning with an interest in popular-media collage. What art world collagists interested you later on?

I was inspired by Max Ernst and Robert Rauschenberg. I found a book by Jess, Robert Duncan’s partner and a great collage artist. I discovered Bruce Conner and, of course, the Surrealists and Dadaists—Berlin Dada, especially, with John Heartfield and Hannah Höch. And the Russians, like El Lissitzky and Alexander Rodchenko. There’s a whole world of collage out there.

How would you describe your decision-making process?

There are collages that I don’t quite know how to resolve. They might need something else, but I don’t know what. Often they’re just made on the spot.

One work that especially caught my eye in your Arts and Letters show was De La (2023). How did you arrive upon the composition for that?

That’s my favorite work of mine. That’s one I’m keeping. It just came together in a particular way, and it zinged. And I can take you through exactly how I made it, because I do remember. The materials go back to 1981, which was the tail end of people selling shit on the street on the Lower East Side. There had been this kind of ad hoc 24/7 flea market around Astor Place. One night, I was walking down St. Marks Place, and there was a guy selling damaged posters. I bought a shopping bag’s worth for 10 bucks; they were all falling to pieces. The yellow and white in De La is from a poster for an early movie by the Italian director Elio Petri, and the lettering comes from miscellaneous papers from a local auction up here [in upstate New York]. It had all kinds of crap, including a poster for a touring Laurel and Hardy anthology film in Spanish. The tire at the top comes from a French car magazine from the ’30s, and the chrysanthemum comes from a seed-company ad in Life. The glove is from a 1950s Punch, a humor magazine that had wonderful advertising. And then the star of the show is the woman with the fingerprints on her back, which I’d been saving up for the longest time. It comes from a French soft-porn magazine from the ’30s. It was magic how they all came together.

What do letters and different bits of text do for you in collage work?

There’s lettering in, I’d say, 95 percent of my collages, and that’s partly because I kind of think of them as movie posters. One of my original inspirations was the posters I saw when I first started going to the movies as a child, for films like Ben Hur (1959) and Hawaii (1966). They had enormous, sculpted letters for the title, and then there would be little figures swirling around them illustrating the scope of the story. In a way, everything I do refers back to those.

Are there writerly aspects to the collage-making process for you? They can’t help but suggest some sort of narrative for the viewer, but I don’t know about the maker, necessarily…

It’s ambiguity central. I started life, so to speak, as a poet. I later realized I was a prose writer, but poetry is at the heart of everything for me. Among other things, that means making a structure from a sort of lean-to of ambiguities—one ambiguity resting on another, all suggesting a story and all suggesting a meaning but never actually giving it to you. If I make a collage and it seems to have a legible message, I have failed.

What makes you turn to collage instead of writing when a creative impulse strikes?

It’s something I feel in my hands—like, “By the pricking of my thumbs, something wicked this way comes.” It’s the feeling that I need to make something. Being a writer does not involve the body the way that making visual art does, and I have that craving. I wanted to be an artist before I ever thought of being a writer.

Because I have so much to do right now, I’m not running down to the basement to make more collages. But in the meantime, I’ve bought things on eBay. I bought a bunch of 19th-century chromolithographs I’m eager to use, and somebody sold me these incredibly dirty comics—I don’t mean dirty like sexy, but dirty like they were lying in a crawl space for 75 years. I like dirt. I like fading. I like things that are crinkled, things that are ripped. I prefer things that are damaged, because they carry their histories. They establish a custodial chain and have a lot of dead people’s fingerprints on them. If something looks like it comes out of the box, even if it was published in 1922, I’m far less interested than if it’s fucked up.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Filardo said that schools’ HVAC systems are complicated. They’re not just for temperature control, but also air filtration and dehumidification. Retrofitting older schools also presents unique challenges.

“In the 1950s, there would be one outlet in a classroom, maybe,” she said. “If you were lucky, one on each wall.”

From the states’ perspectives, Filardo sees a wide range of approaches to start addressing needs. Wyoming, for example, has used coal lease bonuses — mining company payments for extraction permits — and Massachusetts has implemented a statewide sales tax.

Kristen Nye, executive director of Virginians for Modern Schools, an organization supporting the measure, said voter education proved to be very important in Richmond’s case. She added that the measure opened up opportunities that may have previously seemed out of reach.

“Let's say those plans haven't been touched in well over a decade,” Nye said. “They're sort of blowing the dust off of them and taking them back out in the community again.”

“Very often education is a popular talking point on the campaign trail, but when it comes to budget time, somehow it just doesn't make it through,” she said.

Although the measure passed, the school district will need additional funding, said Ricard, noting that she expects the district to continue applying for grants.

“We have 42 school buildings across the district,” Ricard said. “It just gets really costly to maintain all of them. They are old, and their systems are pretty much hanging on by a paperclip, sometimes until they fail.”

Something changed in how people buy airline seats after the pandemic, and Korean Air can see it in its own booking data. Kenneth Chang, Executive Vice President and Chief Marketing Officer at the carrier, told Simple Flying's Sumit Singh that the order in which its cabins fill has flipped. The premium cabins now sell out before economy does, reversing a pattern that held for years.

That shift is the starting point for everything else Korean Air has done to its premium product. Chang described an airline that has raised its investment across meals and service, rebuilt its cabin crew training program from scratch rather than adapting the old one, and put its wine list and menus on a quarterly rotation so repeat customers do not meet the same product twice. What follows is the conversation, and what it says about who is sitting at the front of the aircraft now.

The pattern Chang describes is straightforward and the reversal is complete. "After COVID, a lot of people are traveling more on prestige than economy," he said. "Previously, economy seats sell out first, then we would have some prestige leftovers. Now it's vice versa. Our upper classes are being sold out first before economy, so their expectation is different when they come into the plane."

The first half of that is a demand observation that most carriers have reported in some form since 2022. The second half is the part that matters operationally. An airline whose premium cabins fill last is selling those seats to whoever is left, often at discounted fares or through upgrades. An airline whose premium cabins fill first is selling them to people who sought them out and paid for them deliberately.

That changes who boards the aircraft. Chang's phrasing is precise on this point, noting that expectations differ when passengers come onto the plane. A cabin full of people who chose and paid for the product arrives with a standard in mind, and that standard is set by what else they could have spent the money on rather than by what the airline used to offer. Korean Air's response has been to raise the product to meet it, which is where the rest of the conversation went.

Chang's framing of the premium customer is not about the seat. "Like I said, they're buying a space not just to relax, but they want to buy an experience," he said.

The distinction sounds like marketing language until you consider what it implies about product design. An airline selling a seat competes on measurable specifications, including pitch, width, bed length, and screen size. Those are the numbers that appear in every comparison, and they are the terms on which business class has been sold for two decades. An airline selling an experience is competing on things that do not appear on a seat map at all.

Korean Air has the hardware to make the claim credible. Prestige Suites 2.0, the carrier's current business class, debuted on the Boeing 787-10 in 2024 and reached refitted Boeing 777-300ERs in September 2025. The suites are a customized version of the Collins Aerospace Horizon platform designed by PriestmanGoode, arranged 1-2-1 with direct aisle access for every passenger, offering 46 inches of pitch, a 78-inch fully flat bed and a 24-inch 4K screen. But the hardware is where Chang's argument starts rather than where it ends. Everything else he discussed concerns what happens around the seat rather than the seat itself.

Simple Flying had the chance to try the airline's business class on a flight to London following a trip to Seoul last month. The route is currently served by the 777-300ER, which has the previous generation's cabin offering. However, this will be switched to the 787-10 following the merger with Korean Air in December.

Nevertheless, the premium appeal is there to be seen already. With plenty of space, attentive service, and prime cuts of steak served, there is plenty of value to be had.

Chang was direct about the scale of the commitment. "We've actually ramped up our, I would say, level of investment in the premium classes," he said, pointing to the result: "So far with the new premium classes, with the changes that we did in not only the meals, but the way we serve."

Meals and the way we serve is a telling thing. The first is a product decision and the second is an operational one, and they are being treated as a single investment rather than as separate budgets. The reaction Chang reports suggests the comparison customers reach for is not another airline. "So when customers see this experience, they're like, 'Wow, this feels like a first or five-star hotel, or, you know, a Michelin two-star hotel,' and culinary supports that," he said.

The external validation points the same way. Korean Air took the Best Cabin Service honor at the 2026 APEX Best Awards and holds a Skytrax Five-Star Global Airline rating for a sixth consecutive year. Its wine program has been recognized with gold medals by Business Traveller's Cellars in the Sky awards, and the cabin serves on Armani Casa tableware with Frette bedding. Those are the components that make a hotel comparison plausible rather than aspirational, and they are the areas where the additional investment has gone.

The most substantive thing Chang said concerned people rather than product. New seats meant new training, and Korean Air chose not to adapt what it already had. "With that, with the new seats, we wanted to make sure that our cabin crew, who's servicing them, is retrained," he said. "We don't want to take the old training process and do the same thing because fleets are different, seat configuration is different, customer experience is going to be different."

That is a harder decision than it reads. Rebuilding a cabin crew training program is expensive, slow, and invisible to the customer in a way that a new seat is not. An airline introducing a new business class can install the hardware, update the menu, and leave crew procedures broadly intact, and most passengers would not identify what was missing. Chang's point is that the service pattern a suite with a closing door requires differs from the service pattern a 2-2-2 cabin required, and running the old one in the new cabin produces a mismatch.

One change gives a concrete shape to what tailor-made means in practice. On long-haul Prestige Suites 2.0 services, Korean Air has moved to a dine-on-demand structure. Cabin crew take each passenger's order during boarding rather than working a fixed trolley sequence after departure, and the first service is anchored at roughly 90 minutes after takeoff but can be brought forward or pushed back at the passenger's request.

That is a meaningful operational change rather than a menu one. A crew running a fixed service pattern executes the same sequence on every flight. A crew running dine on demand is managing a different timeline for every suite in the cabin, which is precisely the kind of work the old training program was not built for.

The revamp ran wider than the training itself. "We actually revamped not only the catering or the surroundings around it, like chinaware, we've also revamped our cabin crew training to make sure that they keep up to par in terms of what the customers want, and we try to provide the tailor-made services as much as we can," Chang said. Tailor-made is the operative phrase, and it is only deliverable by crew rather than by hardware. A seat is identical for every passenger who sits in it. The service around it is the only variable an airline can adjust in flight.

Delta Air Lines and United Airlines are producing record results on the back of a surge in premium travel.

The final point Chang raised addresses a problem specific to the passengers an airline most wants to keep. Someone flying the same route in the same cabin several times a year will encounter the same product repeatedly, and familiarity works against an experience sold on being special.

Korean Air's answer is a rotation. "We decided to change our wines every three months," Chang said. "We go through different cycles, so we have three new meals going through every three months, and we change wines as well too, so that frequent flyers do not get stuck with the same crew, same wine, the same experience."

The investment is not finished either. Korean Air has confirmed an entirely new first class suite due to debut in 2028, designed by PriestmanGoode, the same firm behind Prestige Suites 2.0. David Pacey, the carrier's Executive Vice President and Chief of In-flight Service and Lounges, told Executive Traveller that the cabin will appear on incoming Airbus A350s, Boeing 777-9s and Boeing 787s.

The existing Boeing 777-300ERs are moving the other way, losing first class entirely as they receive Prestige Suites 2.0 in refit. That tells you where Korean Air thinks the demand is. The airline is building a new first class for its newest aircraft while converting its existing first class cabins into more business class seats, which is the same bet Chang's booking data describes, placed on the next decade of fleet deliveries.

Team science — or collaborative science across disciplines and institutions — is essential for scientific progress as modern problems are too complex for a single person, laboratory, or even field to solve alone. Some of the best work happens when scientists from different countries collaborate.

However, U.S. policies in place and in development increasingly curtail this vital foreign collaboration. There are foreign funding restrictions, visa and entry restrictions for researchers, mandatory institutional compliance and audits with respect to foreign collaborations, and tight export controls. These policies were implemented as biosecurity measures to protect from “countries of concern,” including China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

Meanwhile, proprietary scientific research is easily accessible to anyone, including foreign actors, through a policy meant to protect Americans’ right to government information. In other words, we are locking the front door while the back window is still wide open.

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1966 in response to concerns that the government was too broad and unaccountable. FOIA gives anyone the right to request access to records held by federal government agencies.

This means that through FOIA, anyone — U.S. citizens, noncitizens, and foreign nationals — can access U.S. government-funded science by completing a simple online form and paying a minimal fee. While FOIA is an incredibly important tool for citizens in a free society, it was never intended to allow anyone around the globe to access proprietary U.S. science.

People are readily climbing in through the open back window. As a scientist in the U.S., I personally experienced the impact of such a FOIA request. I was notified by a FOIA coordinator at an NIH institute that my scientific ideas, supported by a funded grant from the U.S. government, were being requested by a Chinese entity through a simple online request form. They only had to pay a $25 fee.

By law, I had to comply and release the grant to them. The requester was listed as a “Consultant” and identified by a generic email address and physical address in China that could not be verified.

I am not the only one to have this experience. The FOIA coordinator told me offhand that this specific individual had a track record of requesting numerous grants from different NIH institutes every year. More broadly, over a decade, the NIH received more than 13,300 requests for grant proposals through FOIA. (Meanwhile, journalists’ FOIA requests often face major delays and other hurdles.)

This loophole has significant negative ramifications on our society. Scientific grants contain unpublished scientific data and sensitive intellectual property. The release of this material could:

1) harm personal and institutional financial interests by divulging patentable ideas, product concepts, and precompetitive information;

2) mislead the public through exposure to unvetted and untested hypotheses and preliminary data;

3) ruin careers and livelihoods of U.S. scientists; and perhaps most importantly,

4) damage U.S. interests by allowing foreign nationals or foreign corporations to be the primary beneficiaries of work funded by the U.S. taxpayer.

Our policies are fundamentally not aligned to protect science. We say we are blocking competing foreign interests, yet through FOIA, anyone can access this science.

If the U.S. truly wants to protect science, Congress needs to close the open window that FOIA affords. Proprietary U.S. science should be exempt or excluded from FOIA. Failing that, those who can access our material must be scrutinized.

Almost exactly four years after receiving the FOIA request, a research team from China published findings that closely matched my team’s original hypothesis — one our own later experiments did not support. While I cannot say whether the FOIA request, my grant, and this recent publication are related, the timing has made me think more about how research, including my own, is ultimately used in these situations.

Germany's Early Start Pension: Can it fix retirement woes?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Germany plans to pay €10 a month into investment accounts for children to encourage long-term retirement saving. Economists say the measure may help build wealth but won't solve the country's pension funding challenge.

Germany's pension system is under growing strain as demographic change leaves a shrinking workforce funding the pensions of an increasing number of retirees.

In the 1960s, for instance, six workers contributed to the pay-as-you-go public retirement system for each pensioner. Today, that figure has fallen to just two contributors per retiree.

According to the Federal Statistical Office, Germany's fertility rate is currently 1.32 children per woman,and has remained below the 2.1 needed to maintain a stable population since the 1970s.

As a large cohort of baby boomers reaches retirement age, fewer young people are entering the workforce. Germany's working-age population is shrinking by around 1% each year due to demographic change alone, according to the German bank LBBW.

To keep up, nearly a quarter of the federal budget, over €123 billion ($135 billion), is transferred to the statutory pension system each year to help finance pension payments.

Now to help ease the pressure on the pension system, Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government plans to implement a raft of reforms, including a supplementary, capital-funded savings scheme known as the Frühstartrente, or Early Start Pension.

Under the plan, the state would pay €10 per month into an investment account for every child and teenager between the ages of six and 18.

Beneficiaries would not be able to access the money until they reach retirement age, though current plans would allow the funds to be transferred into private retirement accounts. Investment returns would be tax-free during the accumulation phase and taxed only when funds are withdrawn.

Assuming an average annual return of 7%, the €1,440 contributed by the government over 12 years would grow to around €53,000 by retirement, according to government projections.

Parents and relatives would be able to make additional contributions. If they invest an extra €50 per month, the account could grow to as much as €300,000 by retirement.

Starting to save early makes sense because of the power of compound interest, Axel Börsch-Supan, a professor of economics at the Technical University of Munich, told DW.

"But it will not solve Germany's pension problems, which stem from the pay-as-you-go system and costly policies such as the pension level guarantee and retirement at 63," he added, referring to a provision that allows workers with at least 45 years of pension contributions to retire at age 63 with a full pension.

Börsch-Supan argues that government subsidies under the program are likely to benefit higher-income families more than lower-income households.

"That's a waste of taxpayer money, because higher-income individuals are already well-positioned to provide for their own retirement and that of their children," he said.

The Ifo Institute, one of Germany's leading economic think tanks, shares a similar assessment.

It argues that the scheme effectively means "the government hands out money indiscriminately, only to reclaim it through taxes from the same citizens."

The government, however, argues that the plan gives every child a head start in the capital markets regardless of their family's financial circumstances and helps them build retirement savings and long-term wealth.

Similar capital market-based savings and investment accounts for children exist in several countries, including the United Kingdom, Israel, Canada and Singapore.

Many of these programs include government contributions, matching payments or tax incentives designed to encourage long-term saving and asset accumulation from an early age.

But Germany's plan differs slightly as it links state-funded childhood investment accounts directly to retirement provision.

The government also hopes the program will boost financial literacy among children and young adults by familiarizing them with investing, saving and capital markets.

Johannes Geyer, deputy head of the department of public economics at the German Institute for Economic Research (DIW Berlin), says there is little evidence the Early Start Pension will fundamentally change how people engage with financial markets.

"The idea is that people will develop a connection to the capital markets through their experiences with their investment portfolios," he told DW. "But this is not very convincing. We neither know what experiences the future will bring nor what conclusions people will draw from them."

Geyer stresses that the program's impact will not be visible for decades because participants cannot access the funds until retirement.

"If we start now with the 2020 and 2021 cohorts, we're talking about the 2080s. That's a bit too far in the future to be able to draw meaningful conclusions."

In the meantime, he says, the scheme's most immediate effect may be to strengthen ties between families and financial institutions, particularly if account holders make additional contributions.

Opposition parties, including the Greens and the Left Party, have criticized the plan, arguing it would reinforce social disparities and boost inequality since better-off parents could pay more into accounts.

Yet Geyer says the measure won't increase inequality per se. "Sure, wealthy families are more likely to top up their contributions more substantially. But they probably would have done that anyway," he underscored.

"A more interesting question is whether the measure can also encourage people with limited financial means to invest in stocks," he said.

Geyer points out that Germany cannot entirely escape the costs of demographic decline and an aging society.

To address the challenge, he calls for a moderate increase in pension contributions, a broader contributor base that includes groups such as the self-employed, and policies aimed at attracting more qualified workers to the labor market.

Börsch-Supan, who specializes in the economics of aging, says the government's primary goal should be to stabilize the pay-as-you-go statutory pension system.

"A transition to a fully funded pension system would actually be desirable, but it would initially place a heavy double burden on the younger generation, because they would have to save for themselves while also paying contributions for their parents," he explained.

The expert also suggests other measures like restoring the sustainability factor, which is designed to link annual pension hikes to demographic shifts, and linking the retirement age to life expectancy.
