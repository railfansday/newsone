New Homes Offer More Affordability for Buyers: Report

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With affordability remaining an issue at large in housing, new homes continue to represent a tempting alternative in a challenging market.

The latest data from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) found that affordability for new homes actually improved more than that of existing homes in Q3, focused on both home prices and rates.

The Q3 NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index (CHI) found that families earning the national median income of $106,800 needed 33% of their income to buy a median-priced new home, compared to 36% of their income for a median-priced existing home.

Low-income families (those who earn only 50% of the national median income) need 66% of their income to buy a new home, compared to 73% for an existing home.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens noted that this was the “widest affordability advantage (for new homes) since the CHI began,” as builders “are working hard to offset rising costs.”

The report also noted that this is due in part to the difference in price between new homes ($400,400 median price) and existing homes ($416,700 median price), called out as the widest national median price gap since the CHI began.

The other attribution for the new home “affordability advantage” is the incentives and deals offered by builders.

Owens called out NAHB’s recent data on builder confidence, which saw 43% of builders offer price cuts in October—the highest share since 2020. Additionally, 65% of builders offered sales incentives.

A recent Realtor.com® report also found that many buyers purchasing new homes are seeing lower mortgage rates due to builders offering buy downs or other deals with mortgage lenders. The average starting mortgage rate of a new-home buyer was 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers.

While new homes are offering better affordability than existing homes, overall housing affordability is still very much challenged.

For both new and existing homes, families earning the national median income remain fairly cost-burdened (paying more than 30% of their income for housing) or severely cost-burdened (paying more than 50% of their income for housing).

Of the 175 metro areas tracked in the report, 87 are cost-burdened by housing, and eight are severely cost-burdened. Leading the way among the eight severely cost-burdened markets is San Jose, California, where families use 79% of their income on housing.

Owens outlined that in order to move housing affordability in the right direction, “policymakers must do more to reduce red tape, address labor shortages and fix supply chains so builders can expand the nation’s housing supply.”

“As builders contend with rising construction costs, tighter lending standards and persistent labor shortages, policymakers should remove regulatory obstacles, enact permitting reform and create a stronger business environment that enables builders to produce more attainable, affordable homes,” added NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

Europe Spent €16.5 Million Proving Again That Hydrogen Burns in a Turbine

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hydrogen burns. Turbines spin. Their exhaust can make steam. Kawasaki demonstrated hydrogen-fired electricity and heat production at Kobe Port Island in 2018, before Europe funded the first HYFLEXPOWER programme. Six years later, Siemens Energy is still issuing announcements about hydrogen combustion at a single turbine in a French paper mill. European and British taxpayers have supplied roughly €16.5 million for the two research programmes, and the same installation has generated at least 78 identified press appearances. Neither the grants nor the publicity have produced a commercial reason to make hydrogen from electricity and burn it for industrial electricity and steam.

The latest Siemens Energy announcement, dated November 16, 2026, describes an SGT-400 turbine at Smurfit Westrock’s Saillat-sur-Vienne paper mill operating on pure hydrogen through startup, load changes and full-load cogeneration. The equipment can operate under those conditions. Hydrogen combustion and heat recovery were already established. Extending the operating range of one manufacturer’s turbine has not changed hydrogen’s production cost, the losses involved in making it or the available alternatives. No second commercial installation has emerged from the programme.

Siemens’ own March 2024 technical article supplied the most important numbers. The electrolyzer installed at the paper mill produces 16.4 kg of hydrogen per hour. The turbine consumes about 810 kg per hour when producing 10 MW of electricity. Onsite production covers 2.02% of hourly fuel demand. Nearly 50 times that electrolyzer capacity would be needed to supply the turbine continuously at the stated operating point. Later testing used hydrogen brought to the site.

The 2023 campaign accumulated about 90 hours of hydrogen and natural-gas blending tests, including just over four hours on pure hydrogen. Siemens identified limited hydrogen availability as the constraint. The 2026 announcement supplies no cumulative full-load hydrogen operating hours, delivered fuel price or routine cost of electricity and steam. These are the figures an industrial customer needs before buying equipment. Announcing another combustion test avoids the question of who will supply hundreds of kilograms of affordable hydrogen every hour for years.

My complete TFIE Strategy Briefing investigation examines the funding records, Siemens’ published operating figures, the energy balance and an audited chronology of 78 press appearances surrounding the same turbine.

At an illustrative 50 kWh of electricity per kilogram of hydrogen, manufacturing the turbine’s 810 kg of hourly fuel requires approximately 40.5 MW of electricity. The turbine returns about 10 MW of electricity plus usable exhaust heat. Compression, storage and delivery add equipment and expense. The mill needs electricity and process steam; converting electricity into gas and burning it to recover those services introduces losses and costs that direct electrification avoids.

France is an especially poor location for this detour. RTE’s 2025 electricity review reports that 95.2% of French generation was low-carbon, with average generation emissions of 19.6 gCO₂e/kWh. Direct grid power can supply the electrical load. Industrial heat pumps, mechanical vapour recompression, electric boilers and thermal storage can supply process heat and steam in combinations designed around the mill’s operating requirements. The programme should have compared these complete systems before committing public money to more hydrogen turbine retrofits.

A paper mill in Finland is already doing the industrial work. Delfort’s Tervakoski plant has operated a Turboden heat pump and mechanical vapour-recompression installation since January 2026. It supplies about 12 MW of process steam at temperatures up to 170°C, with a reported coefficient of performance around two. It delivers useful steam without a hydrogen-production plant, hydrogen deliveries or a hydrogen-fired turbine. The exact plant integration will vary, but the alternative is operating commercially while HyCoFlex continues issuing test announcements.

The funding was directed at the wrong problem. HYFLEXPOWER received €10.48 million in EU support. HyCoFlex received another €4.44 million. A Siemens presentation identifies approximately €1.56 million more in UK support, bringing the public contribution across the programmes to about €16.48 million. Both programmes were designed around making existing gas-turbine cogeneration equipment compatible with hydrogen. The grants paid for additional turbine operating conditions instead of establishing that hydrogen was a sensible way to provide the plant’s energy.

The programme’s economics were weak before the second award. In October 2023, Siemens presented a scenario requiring electricity below €35/MWh to bring hydrogen fuel costs into parity with natural gas under its 2022 cost assumptions. The HYFLEXPOWER final workshop later examined favourable operating cases with hydrogen substitution and electrolyzer utilization both above 90%. European reporting also identified renewable electricity below €40/MWh as a condition for favourable deployment economics. France’s 2025 wholesale electricity averaged €61.1/MWh. Its negative-price hours were occasional. They could not supply a plant operating more than 90% of the time. These conditions were set out before the research programme ended, yet funding continued for more combustion trials.

Siemens also has a clear commercial interest in preserving turbine markets. Its 2025 presentation explicitly described protecting existing assets and future gas-turbine investments in a decarbonizing world as a benefit of hydrogen compatibility. A turbine supplier is entitled to pursue its products with its own money. The purpose of public industrial-decarbonization funding is to reduce emissions and costs, not to protect combustion-equipment sales from direct-electric competition.

The TFIE media audit counted 19 press appearances in 2020, 13 in the 2022 installation and blending phase, 35 around the 2023 tests and award, four in 2024 and seven through November 16, 2026. Those 78 appearances trace back to a handful of announcements about one installation. They are not 78 independent engineering advances or 78 deployments. The headline count swelled while the operating fleet did not.

Public funders should require equipment-neutral comparisons of delivered industrial energy services before underwriting another hydrogen retrofit. The evidence needed is familiar: a year’s reliable electricity and steam, actual operating cost, lifecycle emissions, fuel contracts, and performance against direct-electric alternatives. HyCoFlex has supplied more hydrogen-combustion publicity without establishing those outcomes. The problem was not that Europe failed to make a turbine burn hydrogen. It was that public money kept flowing after the relevant energy and economic questions already had answers.

What Are Perpetual Futures? How Crypto's Most Popular Derivative Works

Perpetual futures are the engine room of crypto trading. Centralized exchanges processed $85.3 trillion in perps trading volume in 2025 alone, according to CoinGecko's State of Crypto Perpetuals Report 2026, and the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) describes perps as the dominant form of crypto derivatives trading.

They aren't only for crypto, either. Exchanges now offer perps on stocks, commodities and currencies, and Decrypt has become the first news publisher to offer perps trading, powered by Solana exchange Phoenix.

This guide explains what futures are, what makes perpetual futures different, how funding rates and liquidations work, and what to know before trading them.

To understand perpetual futures, it helps to start with ordinary futures.

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set date in the future. Futures have been used for centuries: farmers used them to lock in prices for crops before harvest, and today they are traded on everything from oil and wheat to stock indexes and Bitcoin, on regulated exchanges such as CME.

Traders use futures for two main reasons. Hedgers use them to protect against price swings; an airline might buy oil futures to lock in fuel costs, for example. Speculators use them to bet on price movements without owning the underlying asset.

Traditional futures have an expiry date. When a contract expires, it settles, either by delivering the asset or, more often, by paying out the difference in cash. Traders who want to keep their position open must "roll" it into a new contract, which can add costs and complexity.

Perpetual futures, often shortened to "perps" or "perpetual swaps," work like futures, but with one key difference: they never expire. Traders can hold a position for as long as they like, provided they have enough collateral to keep it open.

The idea was proposed by Yale economist Robert Shiller in a 1993 paper as a way to create markets for assets that are hard to trade directly. Perps took off in crypto. Crypto exchange BitMEX, widely credited with bringing perps to crypto, launched a perpetual contract on Bitcoin in 2016. BitMEX itself closed in September 2026, but the product it pioneered has become the most traded instrument in crypto.

A perpetual contract never actually involves owning the asset. Traders deposit collateral, or "margin," and their profit or loss depends entirely on how the price moves.

When opening a perp position, a trader chooses a direction. Going long means betting the price will rise; going short means betting it will fall. Being able to short as easily as going long is one reason perps are popular: it lets traders profit from falling prices, or hedge assets they already own.

Perps are usually traded with leverage, which lets traders control a larger position than their collateral alone would allow. With 10x leverage, for example, $100 of margin controls a $1,000 position. If the asset's price rises 5%, the trader makes $50, a 50% return on their margin. But if it falls 5%, they lose $50, half of their margin.

Exchanges track two prices to manage this. The index price is the asset's price on spot markets, usually averaged across several exchanges. The mark price, based on the index and the perp market itself, is used to calculate profits and losses and to decide when positions must be closed. Using an averaged price makes it harder for a sudden spike on one exchange to trigger liquidations.

Because perps never expire, nothing forces their price to converge with the underlying asset, as traditional futures do at expiry. Instead, perps use a funding rate.

At regular intervals—most commonly every eight hours, though some platforms use shorter or longer schedules—traders on one side of the market pay traders on the other:

Decentralized exchange Hyperliquid, for example, pays funding every hour, at one-eighth of the rate calculated for an eight-hour period, to stay consistent with centralized exchanges.

Funding payments go directly between traders, not to the exchange. For traders holding positions over long periods, these payments can add up, and they can either boost or erode returns.

Funding rates also offer a window into market sentiment. Strongly positive funding suggests traders are crowded into bullish bets, while negative funding shows that bears are paying to stay short.

Rates for the same asset can also differ between exchanges. On November 19, 2026, Bitcoin's funding rate was 0.01% per eight hours on Hyperliquid, compared with 0.0037% on Binance, according to Hyperliquid's funding comparison tool.

Some traders aim to earn funding payments rather than bet on prices. In a "cash and carry" or basis trade, they buy an asset on the spot market and short the same amount through perps, collecting funding while their positions offset each other's price moves.

Leverage is a double-edged sword. Every perp position has a maintenance margin, the minimum collateral needed to keep it open. If losses push a trader's margin below that level, the exchange automatically closes the position, a process called liquidation. The trader typically loses most or all of the margin they put up.

The higher the leverage, the smaller the price move needed to trigger a liquidation. At 50x leverage, a 2% move against a position can wipe it out.

Liquidations can also feed on each other. When prices fall sharply, liquidated longs are forced to sell, pushing prices lower and triggering further liquidations, a so-called liquidation cascade. On October 10, 2025, the crypto market saw its largest liquidation event on record, with more than $19 billion in leveraged positions wiped out in a single day.

To cover losses when positions can't be closed in time, exchanges typically keep an insurance fund. In extreme cases, some use auto-deleveraging, closing profitable positions on the other side of the market to keep the system solvent.

Crypto perps trade on two main types of venue.

Centralized exchanges, such as Binance, Bybit and OKX, run their own order books and hold users' funds. They account for the vast majority of perps trading: Binance alone held about a third of centralized perps volume in early 2026, according to CoinGecko. Many are unavailable to U.S. customers.

Decentralized exchanges let traders use perps through smart contracts, while keeping control of their funds. Some, such as Hyperliquid and Solana-based Phoenix, run order books onchain, matching buyers and sellers much like a traditional exchange. Others, such as GMX and Jupiter, let traders trade against a shared pool of liquidity provided by other users.

Decentralized perps are growing fast. Their trading volume jumped from $1.5 trillion in 2024 to $6.38 trillion in 2025, led by Hyperliquid, CoinGecko found. Decentralized exchanges' share of perps trading volume peaked at 13% in late 2025 before slipping to 10% in April 2026, while their share of open interest, the total value of open positions, reached 13.5%.

In the U.S., perps developed largely on offshore exchanges, outside the reach of the CFTC, but that has started to change. In July 2025, Coinbase introduced CFTC-regulated "perpetual-style" futures for U.S. customers, with five-year expiries and up to 10x leverage. Two months later, CFTC-regulated exchange Bitnomial launched what it called the first U.S. perpetual futures, with 25-year terms and eight-hour funding. In May 2026, the CFTC approved a Bitcoin perpetual futures contract from prediction market Kalshi and set out a framework for listing more, and in June, Kraken rolled out perps to eligible U.S. customers through Bitnomial, which its parent company had acquired.

Some of these contracts, including Kalshi's and Bitnomial's original design, have long but fixed terms of 25 years, so are not strictly perpetual. The regulator says perps can be offered on CFTC-registered exchanges under federal oversight, and has advised U.S. traders to avoid offshore venues.

The shift has been contested. CME Group, the largest U.S. futures exchange, has sued the CFTC, arguing that perps are swaps rather than futures under U.S. law. The CFTC has nonetheless continued to approve more perps platforms, starting with Backpack.

Perps are now spreading beyond crypto. Exchanges have begun listing perps tracking stocks, stock indexes, commodities such as gold and oil, and currencies, giving traders around-the-clock exposure to markets that normally close in the evening and on weekends.

Those products carry the same mechanics, and the same risks, as crypto perps. They track an asset's price without giving traders ownership of the asset itself, so a perp on a company's stock doesn't come with voting rights or dividends.

What Every Home Needs for an Extended Power Outage

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Americans are “pantry-loading” following a cascade of technology outages that have disrupted email, grounded flights, and interrupted banking services—while feuling fears that the failures could be signs of something bigger.

Costco and Walmart have reported increased purchases of emergency supplies, from food and water to batteries and generators. As of Nov. 15, 21 of the 26 high-priced generators offered on Costco's website were sold out, according to Bloomberg.

"Consumer anxiety following the Microsoft Outlook outage, the ground stoppage for all U.S. airline flights and apocalyptic concerns flaring up across social media have all contributed to increased emergency prep sales," David Bellinger, an analyst for Mizuho, a Japanese investment bank, wrote in a recent research note.

Federal officials say they've found no evidence that the outages are connected or resulted from coordinated cyberattacks. But while the fears driving the rush to stockpile may be unfounded, the threat of prolonged disruptions is all-too real.

In August, severe storms knocked out power to nearly 375,000 customers across Northwest Indiana, leaving some without electricity for nearly two weeks.

A historic ice storm in January caused more than 330,000 outages across Mississippi and Louisiana, where some residents faced similarly long waits.

Both emergencies stretched far beyond the familiar three-day supply of food and water—and exposed how quickly households can lose access to basic necessities.

“A three-day emergency kit is still a good starting point, but I’d consider it the minimum rather than the actual goal,” says Brian Armentrout of Chariot Energy. “In Texas, we’ve seen severe weather events where power outages and other disruptions can last well beyond 72 hours.”

But preparing for the next emergency means more than filling a pantry. It means knowing how long your home can function without the systems that keep it safe—and having a plan for when it can't.

The familiar three-day kit is still a vital part of emergency preparedness, but the American Red Cross recommends that amount for an evacuation kit.

For households sheltering at home, the recommendation is much longer: two weeks of food and water.

Daniel Kilburn, an emergency-planning consultant and retired U.S. Army senior infantry drill sergeant, makes a similar distinction.

“The three-day emergency kit is still good if you're actually evacuating. It should not take more than three days to get to where you need to go,” he says. “However, based on current power outages happening around the country, eight to 10 days is not unreasonable."

But most households aren't prepared for that: 69% of respondents said they had assembled emergency supplies, according to the Federal Emergency Management Agency's latest National Household Survey. Among that group, just 34% said their supplies could last more than two weeks.

Kilburn recommends having enough food and water for every member of the household to last a full two weeks. But that doesn't mean buying two weeks of emergency supplies all at once.

Instead, he recommends gradually adding extra shelf-stable foods and water during normal shopping trips, then rotating those foods through the kitchen so the emergency supply functions more like a well-stocked pantry.

“Water is one of the biggest things people tend to overlook,” Armentrout says. “During an extended outage, water service can also be disrupted, so it’s important to have enough bottled water on hand for drinking and basic needs.”

Winter Storm Uri showed how those failures can overlap. The 2021 storm caused power losses for 69% of Texans and water service disruptions for 49%, according to the Texas State Auditor's Office.

The Red Cross recommends one gallon of water per person per day. For a family of four, that works out to 12 gallons for three days—or 56 gallons for two weeks.

And while food may be more intuitive to store than water, it presents a different problem: how to cook it without power.

A closed refrigerator generally keeps food cold for only about four hours without power, according to FoodSafety.gov. A full freezer can maintain its temperature for about 48 hours; a half-full freezer, about 24 hours.

That makes shelf-stable foods a good choice. But Kilburn warns that households should also have a safe way to boil water or prepare food if the power stays out.

A cellphone may become one of the most useful tools in a blackout, and useless within hours without a way to recharge it.

“Backup power is another area people often underestimate,” Armentrout says. “A portable battery bank, generator, or small solar charger can help keep phones and other essential devices operating when the power is out for several days.”

The Red Cross also recommends a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, backup batteries and chargers, and a paper copy of important phone numbers.

But phones are only one electrical dependency inside a modern home. Kilburn recommends mentally walking through an ordinary day and asking what stops working without electricity.

“Let's say you decide it's time to evacuate and your car is in the garage, how are you going to get the garage door open?” he says.

Medical equipment is also a vital consideration. More than 3 million Medicare beneficiaries rely on electricity-dependent durable medical and assistive equipment, according to the Department of Health and Human Services.

For these households, the Red Cross recommends establishing a backup plan in advance.

Generators can provide backup power, but they carry their own risks: carbon-monoxide poisoning from misuse. Nearly 100 Americans die each year in poisonings caused by portable generators, according to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission.

To stay safe, never operate a generator in an enclosed space like your home or garage—even with doors or windows open. Keep them outdoors and more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents, and make sure you also have a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon-monoxide detector.

Eventually, the biggest question during a prolonged outage may not be how many supplies remain, but whether the home itself is still safe.

“Before an outage, close blinds or blackout curtains to reduce the amount of heat coming into the house,” Armentrout says. “If the power goes out, stay in the coolest part of the home, which is typically a room on the lower floor away from direct sunlight.”

Battery-powered fans can help, but only to a point. The Centers for Disease Control says fans should be used indoors only when temperatures are below 90 degrees Fahrenheit. Above that, using a fan can actually increase body temperature.

That means one of the most important parts of a blackout plan can't be stored in a closet: somewhere else to go.

Kilburn recommends finding out in advance where cooling centers would operate during a major outage. And Armentrout says households should establish their threshold for leaving before conditions deteriorate.

“It becomes unsafe when your home can no longer meet your basic needs,” he says. “That could be caused by a variety of things, from temperatures becoming too hot or too cold, to running low on water, necessary medications, or having the ability to run critical medical equipment.”

So preparing for two weeks without electricity doesn't mean planning to stay inside a powerless home for two weeks no matter what.

It means having enough food, water, and backup power to preserve your options—and a plan for where to go when staying home is no longer one of them.

Peacock's New Workplace Sitcom 'Dig' Unearths Amy Poehler's Best TV Role in Years | Review

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amy Poehler hasn't led a TV comedy in over a decade, and the return vehicle she's chosen for herself is, of all things, an archaeological workplace comedy set in Greece. Dig, Peacock's new 10-episode half-hour series from Poehler, Mike Schur, and J.J. Philbin, based on Kate Myers' bestselling novel Excavations, reunites the Parks and Recreation brain trust, adding in a dash of Hugh Laurie for good measure.

It’s a strong starting lineup, and yet, Dig spends its early episodes trying to be a workplace comedy, leaning on dig-site hierarchies and academic turf wars, when what it actually turns out to be good at is something else entirely. Stick with it. The show finds itself once everyone stops bickering over chain-of-command nonsense and starts working together to solve a mystery (and maybe excavate their own long-buried issues).

Poehler plays Elise DeBellnick, a career archaeologist who has spent what feels like her entire adult life serving as the right hand to Dr. Neville Wycombe (Laurie), a legendary, pompous, impossibly charming British professor who keeps almost retiring every summer. She dreams of leading her own dig in Scotland, if the gods of academic funding allow it, but every season Wycombe announces his grand departure and then just . . . stays. The students clearly love him. What they don’t love is Elise's management style, which is blunt and humorless and entirely too by-the-book for a group of twenty-somethings spending their sun-drenched holiday on the Aegean coast.

The women surrounding Elise are where the show loads most of its more interesting character work, though it takes a while for any of them to land. Geraldine Viswanathan plays Dylan, a former dig favorite who ghosted Elise and the site years ago under circumstances the show treats as a big reveal but lands with a thud. Antonia Thomas is Clare, the hyper-competent lab manager who is roughly a decade younger than Elise and stuck in a Cold War with her that the show gestures at without ever making you feel the weight of. Dylan and Clare have history too, through Clare's fiancé Gary (Jon Pointing), who used to date Dylan. The love triangle, or whatever geometric shape this is, doesn't generate nearly as much tension as the show seems to think it should, mostly because we're never given enough about these people to understand why they'd care about each other this deeply, or at all. Their backstories are a bit too fill-in-the-blank, even for this modern age of television.

Then there's Patty Borkman. Fina Strazza, in a performance that should have casting directors rearranging their call sheets, plays Wycombe's twitchy, overeager assistant like a cartoon mouse who somehow earned a research fellowship. She's the character the show doesn't seem to realize it needs the most. Where the other women arrive preloaded with backstory the writers expect you to care about without earning it, Patty is a blank page, and Strazza fills her in with a goofy, wide-eyed sincerity that feels completely unmanufactured. She endears without trying. She's odd in a way the show badly needs more of, a little left of center in every scene she's in, and her chemistry with Poehler sneaks up on them both.

Kayvan Novak is a highlight as Stavros, the local Greek mayor who is ridiculous and openly besotted with Elise. The show also sprinkles in cameos from familiar Parks and Rec faces like Mo Collins, which land as fun surprises without being asked to carry too much weight. Instead, the focus is on the setting and job it houses.

The central mystery involves the discovery of a rare artifact the team dubs "Pretty Woman," and this is where Dig starts losing me. The discovery itself feels contrived, too perfectly timed to kick the plot into gear right when it needs kicking. It doesn't help that the artifact looks like it was unwrapped from museum gift shop packaging an hour before they shot the scene. For a show about people who spend their lives sifting through centuries of grime and sediment, nothing about this find has any texture. It's too clean, too white, too pristine. (Someone in the props department apparently decided that ancient Greek statues should look like they were 3D-printed yesterday.) It robs the discovery of the weight it needs to carry the back half of the season, and the resulting whodunit, while competently plotted, never feels like much is actually at stake.

But you push past it because what Poehler is trying to do with her characters is genuinely worthwhile. Schur, Philbin, and the team clearly want Dig to be an archaeology Hacks, but the early episodes lean almost too hard into the workplace comedy template with departmental rivalries played for laughs and the whole "dysfunctional team at a quirky job" structure that Schur has built a career on. It's fine. Some of it is sharp. But it's also where the show is weakest, because the dig site never quite feels like a real place with real stakes, and the workplace dynamics can't generate friction when we don't understand why half these people showed up in the first place. Poehler is doing something interesting with Elise even in these early stretches, though, playing a woman whose competence has fossilized into rigidity, whose commitment to one man's project has started to look less like loyalty and more like a failure of nerve. The show just isn't giving her enough to push against yet.

Tina and Amy are the peanut butter and jelly of Hollywood.

Where Dig transforms is around the midpoint, once the mystery forces the four women into actual collaboration. The workplace comedy scaffolding falls away, and what replaces it is a caper driven by friendship. The shift isn't subtle, and it's not entirely smooth, but it works because the actors have been building chemistry in the margins all along. Part of what helps it along is the generational math. Poehler has talked about how Elise sees herself at 19, 29, and 39 in the women around her, and when the show leans into that, it shines. Patty's wide-eyed intensity reminds Elise of who she was before she got cautious. Dylan's restlessness mirrors the version of herself that almost left. Clare's desire to be appreciated for her talents is what Elise feels now. They shouldn't get along, and for most of the early episodes they don't, but the mystery strips away the professional pecking order and forces them to actually see each other.

What saves Dig from collapsing under its own structural wobbliness is the thing you'd expect: the people in it are really, really funny. Laurie, who studied archaeology at Cambridge in real life, starts the season doing what amounts to a prestige comedy cameo, with his kerchiefs and copious Eton references. But somewhere around the midpoint, he unlocks something warmer and more vulnerable, and his scenes with Poehler in the back half of the season become some of the show's strongest. Strazza is the other revelation. She's a dark horse in a cast of heavy hitters, and she commits to Patty's weirdness with a fearlessness that most young actors would sand down into something safer. Instead, she lets Patty be strange and awkward and a little too intense, and it works because Strazza never signals to the audience that she's in on the joke.

Dig has the bones (sorry) of something great, and by its final stretch, it's closer to great than the first few episodes would have you guess. The setting is gorgeous when the show ventures off the lot and actually shoots in Greece and southern Italy. Poehler and Laurie are as good a comedy pairing as you'll find on television right now. The worldbuilding never fully recovers from the early stumbles, and Dylan's backstory stays paper-thin no matter how many scenes Viswanathan throws at it. Once the four women lock in and the show stops trying to be an office comedy with shovels, the gap between what Dig is and what it could be narrows considerably. But Amy Poehler on a television set, doing what she does best with people who know how to write for her, is something we’ve been missing for too long. We’re glad Peacock unearthed her talents again for this one.

Interestingly, at one point Leonardo DiCaprio was circling the part; he wanted to work with Tarantino, and he spoke some German he'd learned from his German-born mother, but he'd have to wait for Django Unchained. Unable to find a suitable Landa, Tarantino was on the verge of cancelling the entire film when German casting director Simone Bär recommended obscure Austrian character actor Christoph Waltz. Waltz had his doubts about the project, but after nailing his audition, he and Tarantino went on to make movie history.

Four criminal masterminds. One of them was always going to be yours. Here is who comes for you, why they picked you out of everyone available, and what they want from the encounter.

Your nemesis does not want to beat you. He wants to know you completely, and he is patient enough to wait for it. The frightening part is how good the conversation is.

Inglourious Basterds centers around a squad of rough-and-tumble Nazi hunters, led by Aldo Raine (Pitt), who are sent behind enemy lines for a campaign of shock and terror. Meanwhile, Nazi-hunting polyglot Hans Landa (Waltz) has been sent to secure a theater in Paris for the premiere of the Nazi propaganda movie Nation's Pride, unaware that the theater is owned by Shosanna Dreyfus (Mélanie Laurent), a Jewish woman whose family was massacred by Landa's men. Quentin was having a full-on panic attack and in danger of falling into a black hole of depression; whichever way around you wished to sell this fucker, it was some dark place. He was in his Berlin hotel room. After a full day of casting and acting alongside every actor Bär had brought into the room, sleep was not forthcoming. Let the rest of Berlin sleep; Quentin was being painfully attacked by night demons.

“I’m the one reading opposite the other actors, so I’m playing all the other characters. It’s when my casting of actors is the best, because I know who these characters are. I know where the jokes are, where the laugh lines are. I know the rhythms. I’m trying to help the actors get the right rhythm. I want them to succeed. I want them to be good. I know how it is supposed to sound, so if they are intelligent, they jump aboard the train because I’m telling them how it is supposed to go. Now, I can get excited if they’re going to throw the train in reverse and try something different that I had never thought of, but there is a rhythm to my stuff for the most part. My mind can be changed. If an actor has a reason and is not doing something different just to do something different for the sake of it, then I can change. But at the end of each audition, I’m absolutely exhausted. I’m wrung out at the end of every day because my scenes aren’t little, they’re big.”

He had cast two main German roles, which should have been a relief, but in his hotel suite that night, sleep wouldn’t come. None of the actors German Casting Director Simone Bär had brought into the room were right for Hans Landa. Quentin was in panic mode. Were his initial fears going to be played out as he prophesied? Was Landa the impossible role?

“The word was out, all right. Hans Landa was one in a million. I was aware enough to know that Landa was the greatest character I had ever written. Everybody knew that after they read the screenplay, and now everybody wanted to play the detective. I heard that a couple of the German actors had an issue doing an American movie and just didn’t come in. They thought what was gonna happen is what often happens, and that is, we were gonna read every German actor, make them jump through hoops and do a poodle show, and then we’re just gonna cast Anthony Hopkins. It happens every single time, an English actor playing a German, and the German actors auditioning have just had to prostitute themselves. But if I say I’m not gonna do that, I’m not gonna do that. I’m not going to cast a Dutch actor, a Swedish actor, an English actor. No. French-, German-, and English-speaking actors are going to play the French-, German-, and English-speaking roles. I wanted the actors to represent the sides that they were on.

“So, I thought that maybe I might have to convince someone that that wasn’t gonna happen, all right? But here was the thing: I was more than a little worried that I had written a character that was unplayable because he has to be both charming and cunning, and then he has to have a facility for languages. He is a linguistic genius. It was not going to work if somebody was going to learn the languages the way an actor was going to do that—it was just not gonna work. Now, the only German actors I’m going to know are if they have been in a big German feature at the time or if they have been in an American movie.

“It was around the time of The Lives of Others, and everybody kept talking about the bald guy, Ulrich Mühe, would be fantastic, but he’s dead. No, that’s the perfect guy, that’s the perfect guy. Yeah, but he’s dead! Let’s fucking move on, all right! If I didn’t cast an actor who was himself a charming linguistic genius, Landa would never be on the screen what he was on the page. I realized that I was going to need a linguistic genius in order to pull this off. There could be no faking it.”

Early on, Leonardo DiCaprio had circled. His mother was German. He could polish his German, for sure he would have to learn French and Italian, but Hans Landa was just the kind of role he wanted to tackle. And it was Quentin Tarantino. DiCaprio had his list of directors he wanted to work with, and you’d best believe that Quentin Tarantino was on that list. Fuck it; he was the top of that list.

A Pitt and DiCaprio combo sure would have been something. The executives at the Weinstein Company and Universal would have been rubbing their hands at that box office. But here was the thing: Quentin never cast just any actor. Now, Leonardo DiCaprio was not just any actor, and no doubt Quentin would have loved to work with him, but Landa had to walk that linguistic tightrope. No, a DiCaprio, Pitt, and Tarantino team-up would have to wait.

In a quiet Berlin hotel suite, he was about to pull the plug because he could not find that high-trapeze artist. As a committee of one, he had come up with the correct decision. If he could not find the most perfect actor to play Landa, the Jew Hunter, the master of detection, the don of language, then he would cancel the movie. Did that hurt? You bet it did. A movie he had worked on for ten years not seeing the light of day, it crushed him.

It was going to be a long dark night. He was seriously despondent, but ultimately it was one of the easiest decisions he had ever made. Inglourious Basterds would not be made without the perfect Landa.

“If I did not find my Hans Landa, then fuck it, I was going to pull the plug on the whole God-darn movie.”

He knew he was going to have to call his producer.

“He called early the following morning. I went along to his room, and he looked awful—he looked like he hadn’t slept,” recalled producer Lawrence Bender.

“He told me that he wanted to pack up and go home. Simone had brought in the best, and among them wasn’t Hans Landa. It was no good. It was a lost cause,” Bender said.

“Okay, hear this,” explained Quentin. “When I tell Lawrence this, I’m not cavalier about it. I’m determined not to make the movie if I do not have the right Landa. So, I’m not cavalier. But from this day forward, I knew I needed to start to emotionally divorce myself from the material. So, I can wield the ax if need be. You know, it is not like I’m burning the script. World War II is not going anywhere. In, say, five years, there will be a new crop of actors, and maybe Landa will be hiding in plain sight amongst them. Maybe one of the guys we’ve seen during this period matures to a more capable Landa five years from now. We’ll see. But as sure as shit World War II ain’t going anywhere.”

“Now, I’m explaining to Lawrence how I cannot go ahead without the Landa, and to give him credit, he didn’t freak out. He didn’t try and talk me out of it. He had been in the room, and there was also kinda the impression that we had seen the best, so, dare I say it, he even agreed with me. This was the best version of Lawrence, the very, very best side of him.”

Like any great producer, Bender listened. Then he gave himself a moment to allow the horrific feeling in the pit of his stomach to momentarily rise. After giving himself that moment, he pushed aside any angst and started to look at the situation from a logical standpoint.

“Here’s the thing: Part of your job as the producer is to fix things, solve problems. A lot of it is creative, but a lot of it is nuts and bolts and making things better,” explained Bender. “After you have dealt with your own disappointment, you go to instinct mode, and my instinct said, ‘Let’s stay.’ So, I said to Quentin, ‘Let’s give ourselves a few days. Here’s what our burn rate is: We’re burning through $100,000 a week, so it is worth you spending another $100,000. Let’s just spend the week looking for Landa. It is worth the risk for you.’”

“Lawrence was 100 percent right. He said, ‘We have got to call the Weinsteins, Universal, Mike, and Carlos, and tell them the situation—and, most importantly, Simone. We have to tell her that for the rest of the week, it is Landa, Landa, Landa, Landa. Nobody else. We ain’t got time to fuck around. We’ve got to find Landa by end of business Thursday night, or we’ve got to shut the film down. It is just that fucking simple. We’ve gotta tell Simone what time it is. It is just that fucking simple. It’s just what we are doing.’ I needed the best of Lawrence, and that was him there and then,” said Quentin.

Casting director Alexandra Montag offered her recollections of the “Finding Hans Landa” casting meeting.

“Simone explained that we had worked to the criteria that Quentin had expressed, and that was that every German-speaking actor speak not only German but also English, French, and Italian authentically. There were very few actors in Germany who had this range, and we agreed that although the actors he had seen were very successful actors, there was something missing. We had seen the tapes.

“But it is also true that not every actor was available on that first day, and he had yet to see an actor Simone believed just right for the role. His name was Christoph Waltz. ‘Please wait for Christoph—Christoph will come,’ she told Quentin. It was her belief that he had all the charm, charisma, and cynicism that fit with Hans Landa.

“I, however, picked up Quentin’s fear as well, and when they had left, I said, ‘Oh shit, shit, shit, what are we going to do?’ But Simone was completely calm and always said, ‘Just wait for Christoph Waltz.’ She was completely confident.”

Making his way to meet with Quentin Tarantino, Christoph Waltz had very little reason to feel optimistic. He had been in his chosen profession since the ’70s, and since leaving the theater world to concentrate on television and film he had become, to a degree, a respected working actor. “Everybody knew who I was; I had played some good parts, of course, I had to do some crap also. But in all those years, Simone Bär had never once cast me in anything. It was odd, but there it was.”

So, not convinced that Bär believed in his talent, Waltz pushed open the door and entered her casting office. He was confident in his own abilities and ready to play the game, like any other actor.

Montag recalled Bär never doubting Waltz’s talent. It was more “because she never found the role for him in the way that this one fit him, the charm, the cynicism, which he could play so well. She knew how good he was. She knew in Landa she had the perfect role for him.”

Waltz, however, was unaware of any such confidence. It was, he felt, another audition he did not need to get excited about.

“We had had it happen many times before: an international filmmaker coming in to cast their film. When Spielberg came to cast Schindler’s List, everyone was in hysterical convulsion: ‘Spielberg, Spielberg, Spielberg.’ Yes, it is all exciting, but frankly speaking, what is in it for me, personally? When you are offered the role of a German soldier in the ghetto who shouts, ‘Get out, get out, get out’ for $500, why would I want to do that?

“So, that was my mindset. I’m an extremely skeptical character, and when they gave me the whole screenplay, it was very heavy, with its title page written in Quentin Tarantino’s own hand, and from there you can get a feeling of the person who wrote it. I said to my agent in Germany, ‘Okay, what role do they want me to do?’ ‘Well, you should read for that Landa character,’ I was told.

“‘Okay, I’ll read Landa.’ Now, I was convinced that I was only reading this big part of Landa because they wanted me for a part that didn’t have enough text to actually show anything. It was just text material.”

Ushered into the kitchen, Waltz took a seat and waited his turn to enter the audition room.

This was the landscape of the time. If a big American film production came to Germany or Austria, local actors would be passed over, so television was where one made a living. It was, Waltz said, how it was.

“We, the natives, would be left with the shitty small parts, and usually the plum parts went to English actors who put in blue contact lenses, dyed their hair blond, and put on a phony accent which no one has. I was used to that. It was out of the question to think it would be any other way. So, when Quentin came, I was under the impression that what always happened would happen this time. They’re in town for the tax credit. They’ll do their search for local actors, hold up their hands that nobody suitable can be found, and an English-speaking actor will play the part. I had it pretty much sussed out how it worked. Not that I’m correct in everything, but experience told me that, you know, I did not need to get excited.”

He had been assured that this time was different. Waltz decided that, if nothing else, he would enjoy reading with Quentin Tarantino, and that would be that.

“Nice to meet with you, Mr. Tarantino. Nice to meet you, Mr. Bender.”

Sitting opposite the American director and producer, the first thing to strike the actor was the director asking him if he would mind reading the opening scene. “Would I mind?” he thought. “Are you kidding?”

“But of course,” said Waltz, nodding in agreement.

Waltz’s Colonel Hans Landa introduced himself to the French farm owner, Perrier LaPadite, on this occasion played by Quentin Tarantino.

“First of all, from the get-go, Lawrence and I kept looking over at each other, and our smiles just became bigger and bigger and bigger,” enthused Quentin.

“We were literally kicking each other under the table in excitement,” agreed Bender.

Waltz’s complete absorption into the scene was nearly broken when he thought to himself, “Why is Quentin sucking his thumb?” Instantly, he realized the banality of such questioning. Perrier LaPadite smokes a pipe. The man in front of him, the writer of the weighty screenplay, was pretending to smoke one.

The room was devoid of any noticeable cameras. Instead, the great Quentin Tarantino was encouraging him to play, like a kid. He had never felt so safe, so relaxed, in an audition room. Nobody was looking at him probingly, questioning how he had the temerity to even be in the room. His natural pessimism waited for the curveball that was sure to come.

Dig review: Amy Poehler and Parks and Recs Mike Schur have done it again

The first time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Parks and Recreation, a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love. The second time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Peacock's new series Dig, which is — you guessed it! — a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love.

Story-wise, the two shows are miles apart. Literally. The former takes place in the decidedly unglamorous town of Pawnee, Indiana, while the latter, based on Kate Myers' novel Excavations, transports us to a sun-bleached archaeological dig in Greece. Yet despite the flashy location and a new mystery twist, Dig retains the core formula that made Parks and Recreation so irresistible: investing deeply in a lovable new crew of unlikely friends.

Kicking off that group is Elise (Poehler), the lead archaeologist on a long-running dig in southern Greece. She's qualified and passionate, yet has a hard time connecting with the undergraduates (or UGs) participating in the dig. Because of her more guarded exterior, she's consistently second fiddle to longtime dig leader Neville (Hugh Laurie). Perpetually on the brink of retirement, Neville seems unable to cede control of the dig to Elise, both because of his love for archaeology and the ego boost his role gives him. He's the Lucy to Elise's Charlie Brown, constantly yanking the football away at the last second.

So, when Elise discovers a perfectly preserved statue of Hera at an older dig site, the last thing she wants to do is tell Neville and give him an opportunity to claim any shred of credit. However, hiding any cultural artifacts from Neville and the Grecian government at large is illegal, so Elise must assemble a crack team to help her cover up her covert dig of the Hera statue, whom they nickname "Pretty Woman." First up is Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), an always flaky wild card who has unfinished business at the dig. Joining her are Clare (Antonia Thomas), a prim and proper conservator born with a silver spoon in her mouth, and Patty (Fina Strazza), a sheltered farm girl with a deep passion for Greek mythology.

Like with most sitcoms, the characters begin as the broadest versions of themselves before revealing their deeper nuances. At first, Elise is prickly to a fault, allowing Poehler to sidestep any preconceived comparisons you might want to draw to Leslie Knope. Elsewhere, Dylan is the hottest of hot messes, Clare is a haughty queen bee, and Patty is an out-of-her-depth klutz. They border on parodies instead of full-bodied characters, making Dig's earlier episodes a bit lukewarm.

But as Dig's title suggests, this is a show that won't be satisfied by sticking at surface level. As the show delves into what makes its characters tick, it becomes a heartwarming tale of found family, not unlike many other beloved Schur shows. Suddenly, character traits that felt one-note to start are revealed to be defense mechanisms or misconceptions, adding entirely new dimensions to Dig's core four.

Dig works best when that core works together in earnest, which is another reason why the show's early entries feel like slower burns: They're doing the groundwork to loop every woman into the Pretty Woman dig. Once they're all on the same page, Dig gets off and running — even more so once Pretty Woman mysteriously vanishes, leaving Elise and her motley crew to do some boots-on-the-ground detective work. Thus begins a madcap caper involving stakeouts, undercover identities, and the black market.

Along the way, Elise, Dylan, Clare, and Patty are allowed to be wonderfully messy and vulnerable, all while staying refreshingly supportive of one another. For example, a love triangle burbles up between Clare, Dylan, and Clare's fiancé/Dylan's ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), yet the storyline never devolves into cat fights or possessiveness. Instead, Dig focuses more on what these relationships mean to Clare and Dylan beyond romance. For Clare, her engagement is a sign of stability and true adulthood, whereas Dylan is looking for closure, something she notoriously can never get.

Dig also dabbles in intergenerational conflict, highlighting the differences between each of its leads, all of whom are at very different stages in their lives. Again, Dig doesn't take a hackneyed "Gen Z versus millennials versus Gen X" route. It's more interested in showing the different wants that motivate us. Patty yearns for belonging and friendship, while Elise wants to cement her legacy and make sure she's done something big enough to call her own, no Neville required.

Once Dig's ensemble truly comes together, they mesh wonderfully. Poehler brings a weary exasperation to Elise that slowly gives way to warm leadership, a transformation that's a joy to witness. Thomas and Viswanathan are perfect foils, the former a picture of poise, the latter completely uninhibited. Watching them feed off each other offers up a perfect comedic contrast. Finally, there's Strazza, whose endearingly goofy Patty becomes the beating heart of the show. Laurie, too, is a joy, blending Neville's smarminess with a genial charm that makes him infuriatingly tough to hate.

In focusing on archaeology, Dig emphasizes the importance of excavating ancient objects and interrogating the stories of those who once used them. In many ways, Dig is doing the same thing with its characters: peeling back their layers to get at their core truths. Only instead of using trowels and brushes, Dig uses the power of the workplace sitcom. It's a power Schur and his collaborators know well, and the result is a show that will leave you feeling like you've just uncovered something great in a dig of your own — with far less dirt on your face and clothes, that is.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. I was afraid of not having enough or of being unable to find another job, and I just had to face that fear.

The AT forced me to confront my own limiting beliefs around money. In the years since that first thru-hike, I’ve gotten a little more relaxed about spending money on things I know I can afford without feeling guilty about it. I haven’t abandoned my frugal tendencies, but they don’t need to dictate every decision I make. I credit the trail for that growth.

“I wonder if I can get another 200 miles out of these shoes before I replace them. I wonder if I should get on a cheaper insurance plan next year. Do I have enough money to buy name-brand peanut butter in the next town? Should I get a different credit card? Should I go stoveless to save on fuel? “

I’ve written a few articles over the years about my peculiar hiking-related neuroses. As always, I want to clarify in the midst of all this doom and gloom that thru-hiking was and is one of the great joys of my life, despite my assorted pathologies and the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

Every thru-hiker will face struggles both external and internal; learning to deal with and overcome them is part of the experience, and this is just one more example. As always, do everything you can to prepare for your thru-hike, accept that you can’t plan for everything, and then enjoy the journey, hardships and all.

Keefe (1971), {the party seeking to suppress speech} "carries a heavy burden of showing justification for the imposition of such a restraint." …

The remedy for false or injurious statements lies in "actions under libel laws providing for redress and punishment, and not in proceedings to restrain the publication." [But t]he October 12 Order … does not impose consequences for particular statements adjudicated unlawful. It prohibits Mr. Hughes from speaking about entire categories of people in the future. That is a prior restraint in its most direct form which is presumed unconstitutional….

Test Masters Educ. Servs., Inc. v. Singh (5th Cir. 2005) … recognized that a court may enjoin "harassing communication" and "repeated invasions of privacy." But an injunction prohibiting all communication, rather than limiting itself to "threatening or harassing conduct," "swept too broadly" and constituted an unconstitutional restraint. Id.…

Plaintiffs cannot justify the restraint merely by characterizing Mr. Hughes's commentary as malicious, coercive, or injurious. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Keefe. There, the Court held that an individual's "interest in being free from public criticism of his business practices" did not justify an injunction against speech…. Plaintiffs may have remedies for unlawful conduct. But those remedies do not include an order forbidding Mr. Hughes from speaking about them at all….

Defendant also argues (again correctly, I think) that the order was unconstitutional because defendant wasn't given an opportunity to be heard before the order was entered:

The absence of notice presents a separate constitutional defect. In Carroll v. President & Commissioners of Princess Anne (1968), the Supreme Court set aside a short ex parte order restraining expression because of "a basic infirmity in the procedure by which it was obtained." The order "was issued ex parte, without notice to petitioners and without any effort, however informal, to invite or permit their participation in the proceedings."

There is a place in our jurisprudence for ex parte issuance, without notice, of temporary restraining orders of short duration; There is no place within the area of basic freedoms guaranteed by the First Amendment for such orders where no showing is made that it is impossible to serve or to notify the opposing parties and to give them an opportunity to participate.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that a later hearing could cure the problem. The availability of a subsequent opportunity to be heard "does not overcome the infirmity in the absence of a showing of justification for the ex parte nature of the proceedings."

The reason is straightforward. "The value of judicial decision-making … is substantially diluted where the process is ex parte, because the Court does not have available the fundamental instrument for judicial judgment: "an adversary proceeding in which both parties may participate." And where First Amendment freedoms are at stake, any order must be "couched in the narrowest terms that will accomplish the pin-pointed objective."

The October 12 Order fails both principles. It was entered without notice or an adversary hearing. It contains no finding that notifying Mr. Hughes was impossible. And far from identifying a narrow category of unlawful speech, it prohibits virtually any online commentary concerning the Plaintiffs, their families, or their counsel.

Carroll is directly controlling on the constitutional defect. An ex parte order restraining expression cannot stand without a showing that notice and participation were impossible. No such showing appears here….

Bigger armies will not stop climate change, and may make the world less safe

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Military emissions stay out of national climate tallies, and two BMJ authors say bigger defense budgets may leave people less safe, not more.

Many governments are raising their military budgets, and climate change is now one of the reasons they give for doing so. The thinking is that regions turning dangerously hot, wet, or dry will grow unstable and may go to war, so a bigger military is a precaution.

Two articles in a medical journal’s climate issue say that logic has it backwards.

War and the daily running of armies are themselves a large and mostly uncounted source of the gases heating the planet, their authors wrote. Spending more on them may leave people less safe, not more.

The articles are opinion pieces, not data studies, and they appeared in The BMJ on October 21. Neta Crawford, a professor of international relations at the University of St Andrews, is the author of the first. The journal commissioned it and didn’t send it for outside peer review.

Mohammed Abba-Aji of Washington University in St. Louis and Nason Maani of the University of Edinburgh are the authors of the second article.

In an interview with Earth.com, Abba-Aji described the comparison behind that article. “What struck me most was the contrast,” he said. The contrast is between what the world spends on weapons and what it gives away as aid.

Security officials describe climate change as a “threat multiplier,” Crawford wrote. The phrase means a warming climate could stress societies until they fight. The U.S. military, the U.K. Ministry of Defence, NATO, and the U.N. Security Council have all used it.

Crawford reversed the claim: war, she wrote, is a climate change multiplier.

The steadiest source of military emissions is the ordinary running of a military, from powering bases to the fuel burned in training and in moving troops. U.S. and NATO military doctrine stresses long-range air strikes, which produce more emissions than defensive doctrines do.

Everyday military emissions don’t appear in national greenhouse gas inventories. Crawford traced the gap to the Kyoto Protocol talks of the 1990s, when the U.S. Department of Defense pushed for a military exemption.

The department was the country’s largest petroleum user and feared being required to cut its emissions. The Pentagon is still the largest user, she wrote, and its annual emissions are larger than those of most countries.

Arms makers add to the total. BAE Systems, the biggest defense company in the U.K., reported about 377,000 tons (341,686 metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent for 2025 from its own operations and purchased energy.

Fighting raises the numbers fast. Crawford cited an accounting group’s estimate for the first four years of the war in Ukraine. Military operations alone produced about 126 million tons (114 million metric tons) of emissions in the form of carbon dioxide equivalent.

Destroyed forests and airliners rerouted around the war zone added more, and rebuilding will add more again. Those three sources come to roughly 190 million tons (170 million metric tons).

Strikes on oil and gas sites are worse still. The Nord Stream pipelines under the Baltic Sea were sabotaged in September 2022, and an estimated 513,000 tons (465,000 metric tons) of methane escaped, a 2025 study published in Nature found.

Crawford put that at about 15 million tons (13.85 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent, three times what Albania emitted during the whole of 2024.

Ukrainian drones hit Russian oil facilities this September, Crawford wrote, and strikes hit oil and gas infrastructure in the Middle East during the war with Iran this year.

The fuel shortages and price jumps were tracked. In contrast, the greenhouse gases released have gone essentially uncounted.

The second article is about the money. World military spending reached $2,887 billion in 2025, Abba-Aji told Earth.com. This is close to 2.5 percent of everything the world’s economies produced.

Official development assistance, the aid that richer governments send to poorer ones, fell to $174 billion the same year, the largest one-year drop on record.

Abba-Aji and Maani describe a cycle. Climate stress can make conflict more likely, through competition over resources or by making it easier for armed groups to recruit.

“Conflict in turn justifies more military spending, and that spending draws money away from the measures that could break the cycle,” Abba-Aji said.

Wealthier governments may stay out of the fighting, the two wrote, and still help create the conditions for it. They supply the weapons that keep a conflict going while cutting aid to the countries affected.

Crawford also objects to the idea that a hotter world leads to war on its own. The link between climate change and armed conflict is neither strong nor necessary, she wrote, citing a 2017 study that re-examined the Syrian civil war.

Governments can support their populations instead, she wrote, and neighboring governments can negotiate over shared resources. Those are political choices, not unavoidable consequences of climate change.

Climate change is already killing people without any war at all. In some regions, heatwaves, wildfires, storms, and floods made worse by warming now kill more people a year than armed conflict does, Crawford wrote.

By the Met Office‘s estimate, more than 1,100 deaths in England and Wales during the May and June heatwaves of 2026 were due to climate change.

The threat-multiplier frame has also changed where militaries operate. For years, Crawford wrote, armed forces have worried that a melting Arctic Ocean will open shipping lanes and a contest for minerals.

The U.S., NATO, Russia, and China have all increased their Arctic operations, at the same time as the region’s climate has become less stable and those operations harder to run.

Some armed forces present themselves as green, Crawford wrote. The U.S. Navy publicized a “great green fleet” in 2016, and the U.S. Army promoted its investments in green technology until mention of climate change was all but banned under the Trump administration.

Moving bases off coal does cut emissions, she wrote. But even forces that use fuel-efficient vehicles, batteries, solar power, and microgrids remain major emitters.

When spending grows, so do training, operations, and purchasing, and the emissions grow with them.

“Substantial emission reductions will occur only when armed forces reduce their overall size and footprint and transition to lower emitting doctrines, including those that do not require long range aircraft missions,” Crawford wrote.

Asked by Earth.com what governments should do first, Abba-Aji said they should “weigh the trade-offs between military and social spending for their health and climate consequences and set aside a share of security funding for climate adaptation, especially in the most vulnerable regions.”

Crawford’s own answer is to spend more on health, the environment, and the energy transition rather than take money from them.

“While some form of defense is required, the focus should shift to how to ensure effective defense at lower emission levels and reduced costs,” she wrote.

The size of the source that both articles describe is still unknown. Day-to-day military emissions are left out of national greenhouse gas inventories, and the gases released by this year’s strikes on oil and gas facilities haven’t been added up either.

Counting them would be the first step toward knowing its magnitude.

This '80s R-Rated Superhero Movie Is Unlike Anything Marvel Or DC Has Made

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We've been living in a world of mass entertainment dominated by superheroes and comic book characters for more than 25 years now, dating back to Marvel's earlier blockbuster hits like "X-Men" and "Spider-Man." Even small screen stuff like "The Walking Dead" has comic book roots. But decades before any of that, an indie film pioneer made a superhero movie unlike any other.

Lloyd Kaufman is independent film, in many ways, as the founder of Troma Entertainment. His media empire was built on the backs of low-budget schlock like "Surf Nazis Must Die" and "Pterodactyl Woman from Beverly Hills," among many others. But Kaufman's defining hit came in 1984 in the form of "The Toxic Avenger," an ultra violent, campy, R-rated superhero affair. It remains a distinct piece of punk rock cinema more than 40 years removed, unmatched in its insanity by anything Marvel or DC has done on screen.

The movie takes place in the fictional town of Tromaville, known as the toxic waste capital of America. A bullied young janitor in a popular gym falls into a vat of toxic waste and turns into a super strong but disfigured man. He begins cleaning up the streets from evildoers in an often funny yet violent fashion.

"The Toxic Avenger" has a long history on screen and is now a cult legend, but that legend began in '84, at a time when superheroes were far from bankable fare in the mainstream. Co-directed by Michael Herz and Kaufman himself, the resulting movie can't be accused of being high art, but it's one heck of a wild swing, and a testament to what the indie filmmaking spirit can accomplish.

At the time, Hollywood had only had the occasional superhero movie hit it big. "Superman: The Movie" in 1978 being the biggest one, while Batman didn't become a box office hero until 1989. Even that movie just led to the "Batman" franchise taking off rather than a broader trend of superheroes becoming big business. The '90s saw many try but few succeed, with stuff like "Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles" more the exception rather than the rule.

All of this to say, when Lloyd Kaufman decided to make a dark superhero comedy — an R-rated and super violent one no less — it wasn't exactly a recipe for success. At least not on paper. But decades before "Deadpool" broke the Marvel mold by going R-rated and injecting comedy into the genre, "The Toxic Avenger" dared to venture into that territory.

What's more, Kaufman and Co. did it on a shoestring budget of well under $1 million. "Deadpool" gets a lot of credit for being low-budget, but it still cost north of $50 million. What Kaufman pulled off with latex, old school special effects, and can-do indie spirit is nothing shy of impressive. Even those who are put off by the nature of this sort of exploitation film should be impressed by what was accomplished here with so little.

"The Toxic Avenger" has a demented sense of humor, and it doesn't give us a typical hero either. Toxie isn't a square-jawed, muscle-bound vigilante. He's a mutant who decides to clean up the streets with straight-up violence. The film has more in common with revenge driven horror/thrillers than it does any mainstream superhero movie. All the same, it became a cult hit and started a wildly unlikely franchise.

"No theater would play it," Lloyd Kaufman said of "The Toxic Avenger" to Parallax View in 2010. "We couldn't get one theater to play it anywhere and then we took it to Cannes and not one deal. Nothing! And then a few months later we got one movie theater in Greenwich Village, New York, and there was a line around the theater."

That single theater paved the way for more to follow. This was also at the dawn of the VHS home video craze, which is where the movie really thrived — so much so that Kaufman eventually produced a series of sequels. That includes 1989's "The Toxic Avenger Part II" and "The Toxic Avenger Part III: The Last Temptation of Toxie," also released in '89. Much later, "Citizen Toxie: The Toxic Avenger IV" would arrive in 2000. A gore-soaked remake of "The Toxic Avenger" starring Peter Dinklage was also released in 2025.

Kaufman's crown jewel is an elbow grease style, gory, raunchy, rubber-suit take on superheroes that doesn't resemble anything we've seen in the 2000s, really. Sure, "Clayface" brought body horror to the DC Universe, but it's completely without a sense of humor. It's also slick and relatively expensive (for a horror movie).

It was cutting edge then and it's impressively distinctive even now. More than that, "The Toxic Avenger" helped make the empire that is Troma. It's easy to write it all off as schlock, but Troma has been a haven for indie film for decades, with filmmakers like James Gunn ("Guardians of the Galaxy") cutting their teeth there. Without this movie, who knows if the empire as we know it even exists?

The U.S. on Tuesday lost its official designation as a measles-free nation, more than 25 years after it eliminated the highly contagious virus.

The Pan American Health Organization announced the decision, which came after an independent panel of eight scientific experts convened last week to consider the country's epidemiological evidence. The director of PAHO, the regional branch of the World Health Organization, made the final call that measles is endemic — or always present — in the U.S. following 12 months of continuous transmission.

In other words, measles is spreading regularly in the country again, with ongoing outbreaks sparking new ones.

"The commission concluded that endemic measles transmission has now been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico and the United States, and recommended that their measles elimination status be withdrawn — a recommendation which I have accepted," Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, PAHO director, said during a news briefing on Tuesday.

It's a devastating step backward for American public health, as declining childhood vaccination rates and vaccine hesitancy fuel outbreaks across the country that have sickened thousands of people and led to a tragic string of preventable deaths. Among the deaths where vaccination status was reported, all were unvaccinated.

More than 5,400 measles cases had been reported nationwide in 2026 as of mid-November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. States have confirmed 10 deaths associated with the disease so far in 2026: seven in Pennsylvania, one in New York and two in Wisconsin.

The commission found that out of 52 U.S. states and territories it tracked, 48 experienced measles outbreaks during the reporting period between January 2025 and January 2026, PAHO Assistant Director Dr. Rhonda Sealey-Thomas said during the briefing. But Barbosa said countries are already taking action to control outbreaks, such as strengthening vaccination, surveillance and outbreak response efforts.

The U.S. in particular has ramped up vaccination efforts to close immunity gaps, particularly in areas with low immunization coverage experiencing outbreaks, said Dr. Daniel Salas, executive manager of PAHO's Special Program for Comprehensive Immunization. He said PAHO expects the U.S. to present its corrective action plan in the coming weeks, outlining strategies to regain its designation.

In a Tuesday release, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said it will continue to ensure all communities have the resources to prevent and respond to outbreaks and is committed to working with states and other local partners to restore the U.S.'s measles elimination status.

CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz said in the release that the measles elimination designation "is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does." She said the agency has invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach communities where transmission is occurring.

Last week, Schwartz told Reuters that the agency is going to be "doing the same exact thing that we've been doing today" if the U.S. loses its measles-free status.

The decision also poses a new test for the Trump administration and its Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has taken steps to change recommendations for the childhood measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine and has a long history of questioning its safety without evidence.

CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases. The resurgence of measles exposes eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities.

Public health experts previously told CNBC that the return of measles ultimately boils down to fewer children getting vaccinated.

The MMR vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the CDC. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

The American Medical Association urged the public to get vaccinated after PAHO's announcement.

"We must treat the current measles outbreaks as an urgent call to rebuild trust in science-based recommendations and restore strong, fully-funded public health infrastructure to protect families and communities and prevent the return of diseases once eliminated," AMA President Dr. Willie Underwood said in a statement.

In a separate statement, the pharmaceutical industry's largest lobbying group called on policymakers to provide "clear, accurate information about the seriousness of measles infection and the effectiveness of vaccines against it, restore and sustain high vaccination coverage in every community, strengthen vaccination efforts, and invest in disease surveillance and rapid outbreak response."

"We have the tools to eliminate measles again and what we need now is the commitment to use them," said Dr. Michael Ybarra, chief medical officer of the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America.

Measles isn't just a public health threat — it's also a huge financial burden on the healthcare system, CNBC reported.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The U.S. now joins Canada, Spain and the U.K., which all recently lost their measles-free status after a review by experts at the World Health Organization. While the U.S. dropped out of the WHO, it remains a member of PAHO, its Americas branch.
