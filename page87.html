A Pediatrician, a Pastor, and a Paralympian Just Did Wonders for Kids’ Mental Health

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

— The midterm elections offer lessons in decency and difference

My son is 32. Early Wednesday morning he forwarded me a New York Times story about the new faces Tuesday's midterm elections sent to Washington and to statehouses.

He added a note: "Some themes for your next article, like the Knicks one. A message about being decent human beings. A message about how what makes us different brings us together." Then he quoted one line from the Times article: "There's a pediatrician, a pastor, a Paralympian and a former Navy helicopter pilot."

In June, I wrote that the Knicks' championship handed parents a teaching moment. My son was right: Tuesday handed us another.

Our kids and pediatric patients need the lesson. They have lived through a full campaign season. Attack ads. Comment sections. Adults yelling at screens. Before the last presidential election, 77% of American adults named the future of the nation a significant source of stress. Children absorb what we carry home. In the Trevor Project's 2024 national survey, 90% of LGBTQ+ young people said politics had hurt their well-being in the last year. Let's call that the un-teaching.

First, start with the line my son quoted. The pediatrician is Annie Andrews, MD, of South Carolina. She ran for the Senate on a platform focused on children's health after a 2025 measles outbreak sickened some 1,000 children in her state. Andrews, a pediatric hospitalist, saw harm to kids and asked for the job of stopping it.

The pastor is Adam Hamilton, who leads the largest United Methodist congregation in the country. In January he was a registered Republican. Now he is the first Democrat to win a New Hampshire Senate seat in Kansas since 1932. He campaigned on his faith and promised to work across the aisle.

The Paralympian is Josh Turek of Iowa. Two gold medals in wheelchair basketball. A working-class kid. A man who once voted for George W. Bush. He is headed to the Senate.

The helicopter pilot is Rebecca Bennett. She flew for the Navy, ran a healthcare business, and now heads to the House from New Jersey.

Then come the firsts. Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia will be the first Black woman ever elected governor in this country. Chris Pappas of New Hampshire will be the first openly gay man in the Senate; he told the Times he is living a life he could not have pictured as a boy. Abdul El-Sayed, MD, DPhil, of Michigan, a physician who ran Detroit's health department, will be the first Muslim senator. Melat Kiros, 29, becomes the first Gen Z woman elected to Congress. James Talarico, a former middle-school teacher who quotes Scripture in his stump speech, will be the youngest member of the Senate at 37.

Ask a child what these people share. Different faiths. Different bodies. Different races, ages, loves, hometowns. Each one walked into rooms full of strangers and asked for their trust. The strangers said yes. What makes each of us different brought them together.

Talk about Amy Acton, MD, MPH. A physician, Ohio's former health director, now its governor-elect. She has spoken openly about a traumatic childhood and sexual abuse at the hand of her stepfather. Late in the race, an ad from her opponent put Acton's estranged mother on camera to call her a liar. Acton held her ground. She said she told her story so other people might see a way out.

As a child psychiatrist, I treat children with histories like hers. Most of them believe the worst thing that happened to them is the truest thing about them. Let's tell these patients Acton's story.

Adolescents build an identity partly by borrowing one. They pick role models whose life stories connect to their own, and they gravitate toward role models who resemble them. For some kids that is a matter of life and death. LGBTQ+ youth who have an older LGBTQ+ role model report lower rates of considering and attempting suicide. In an earlier Trevor survey, more than 80% said positive public role models made them feel better about who they are. A gay teenager in Manchester watched a gay man win a New Hampshire Senate seat Tuesday night. A kid in a wheelchair in Des Moines watched a Paralympian win one in Iowa. That registers.

Difference, met up close, heals. Cross-group friendships rank among the strongest known antidotes to prejudice in childhood, and they support children's well-being. Civic life protects too. In a national study that followed more than 9,000 adolescents into adulthood, those who volunteered and voted went on to have better mental health and engage in fewer risky behaviors. Community engagement in adolescence predicted fewer depressive symptoms years later.

My son also pointed out another crucial point: Kids across the country just witnessed an incredible underdog story. Talarico and El-Sayed were up against hundreds of millions of dollars in opposition spending. Several of the winning candidates represent groups historically left out from Congress, including gays, Blacks, Hispanics, and women. There is something immensely powerful about marginalized candidates running campaigns built around taking care of their communities and defeating the wealthiest individuals in the country. It doesn't matter how powerful you are, compassion for others and belief in self is what can change the world. That's a story our young patients need to witness.

Of course, half of the country may have woken up disappointed Wednesday. But even for that half, the lesson holds. Hamilton was a Republican 10 months ago. Turek voted for Bush. A child can admire a person and still argue with the platform. Disagreement is healthy. Contempt is the toxin. After the last election, the head of the American Psychological Association warned that pulling away from people over politics can cost us the relationships we need most.

Decency. Service. Faith worn in the open. Grit in a wheelchair. Honesty about one's own pain. A hand across the aisle. Strangers saying yes to someone unlike themselves.

There's the message. Everyone in this country has a reason to bring it up this week, and a son or daughter or patient will be ready to hear it.

My son is 32. He still sends me lessons. That's how it's supposed to work. Don't let the moment pass.

Piroe warming up with Kanté and Mayers showed three changes at half time as Magassa, Morato and Taty departed.

Absolutely chaotic scenes in the away end as the incredulous claret and blue army couldn’t believe they’d witnessed a fightback from a team dead and buried at half time.

It could have been four as Trott stretched his fingers to Soucek’s header – agonisingly close as the minutes ticked by. Brilliant second half efforts by Piroe, by Bowen – the man of the match – and excellent impact from Ezra Mayers who might just have found himself a role in Parker’s side.

‘Godzilla Minus Zero’ Review: Oscar Winner Takashi Yamazaki’s Vision Of Japan’s Most Iconic Monster Is Back Bigger Than Ever Battling An Even Deadlier Foe

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

With 2023’s monster hit Godzilla Minus One, Takashi Yamazaki became only the second director after Stanley Kubrick and 2001: A Space Odyssey to take home the Visual Effects Academy Award, and his film became the only Godzilla movie in 70 years to win an Oscar.

Naturally, that all calls for a sequel, doesn’t it? The filmmaker wasn’t sure since he didn’t want to disappoint the legions of fans of the first film, a Japanese Kaiju cinema masterpiece which, unlike most of the Godzilla films, really succeeded in part because of the emotional human story it also had to tell. Well, it is nice to say he hasn’t forgotten that aspect, even as Godzilla Minus Zero, set two years after the first, has gone much bigger and was “filmed for IMAX”. The fabulous Scope visual palette is still there but adding the IMAX brand turns out to complement it rather than smother it.

The irony here is the titular star, Godzilla, is really just a supporting player in his own movie. This one, for much of the 135-minute running time, is more obsessed with another Kaiju, the regally named King Ghidorah (one of my favorite Godzilla foes ever). Ghidorah, in the wake of the apparent death of Godzilla in Minus One, is ruling the skies over Matsumoto, a truly terrifying creature who proves three heads are better than one. He seems invincible but the head of the Large Scale Disaster Response Bureau, Kenju Noda (Hidetake Yoshioka) is convinced he can be banished through electrocution and sets the plan in motion with associates, including Weather whiz and meteorologist Masami Nagasawa (Makoto Aoyama), and Professor Kanji Murakami (Min Tanaka), a survivor of unspeakable horrors including the atom bomb dropped on Hiroshima by the Americans to end WWII. The U.S. Army is around too, but with their own motivations, just as Murakami has his. The film’s first half is all about Ghidorah, and also the returning Shikishima family who so movingly figured in Minus One. Watch on Deadline

Koichi Shikishima (Ryunosuki Kamiki) is now married to Noriko (Minami Hamabe), sporting an eye patch and her own secrets, the parents of now five-year-old Akiko (played again by Sae Nagatani). Koichi is happy to put his past as a kamakazi pilot behind him, although still riddled with guilt over still being alive. Noriko’s connection to Godzilla will soon thrust this loving family into crisis as they are drawn into the new disaster when it becomes clear Godzilla is still around, and actually needed by the powers that be to destroy Ghidorah, a complicated plan concocted when all else fails. How the hell do they lure Godzilla out of hiding and to cooperate in this ambitious scheme. This all leads to the film’s truly spectacular Kaiju battle for the ages. Yamazaki had only 35 artisans on board for the equally thrilling fights in Godzilla Minus One, and here only really adds a handful, who all contribute to the remarkable visual effects on display, at an ounce of the cost of major studio productions on this scale. I smell another Oscar.

Still despite all the histrionics on display, it is the human element that makes this a step above the usual monster mashups. In a heartbreaking Solomon-style moment, circumstances force the parents to give up Akiko to an orphanage as their duty to country (and I won’t give the exact details of that but it is pretty persuasive) trumps duty to family. The separation scene is gut wrenching as Akiko fails to understand why she is being abandoned and gives one wail of a goodbye scene.

The craft of Godzilla Minus Zero is stunning to say the least and if ever there was a reason to head out to the local IMAX, this movie gives you one. The good news is this may not be the last of Yamazaki’s dance with Godzilla as the ending leaves room for a third edition, which considering the artistic and crowd- pleasing success of the first two is something to look forward to.

Producer is Kazuaki Kishida. In Japanese with English subtitles.

What Is Rhabdo? What Every Woman Should Know About the Rare Condition

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A woman’s death during the New York City Marathon has put the condition back in the spotlight.

At the New York City Marathon on Sunday, 24-year-old Rachel Apa of Lewisberry, Pennsylvania, collapsed two miles from the finish line. She later died at the hospital.

The medical examiner’s office is still investigating her cause of death. Her father, Steve Apa, told the New York Times that the cause was rhabdomyolysis, commonly known as rhabdo. The rare condition occurs when skeletal muscle breaks down, releasing its contents into the bloodstream, says Robert Glatter, MD, an emergency room doctor and an assistant professor of emergency medicine at Lenox Hill Hospital.

Apa’s death comes amid growing conversations about rhabdo on social media. People have shared their experiences with the condition after intense exercise, particularly at cycling classes. https://www.tiktok.com/@bryybell_/video/7693630991839153421?_r=1&_t=ZT-9Axae4rjSXu

But while these stories are alarming, rhabdo is still relatively rare. Yet they raise questions about the risks of intense exercise, how to recognize when something is wrong, and just how concerned the everyday exerciser should be. Here’s what experts want you to know.

Rhabdomyolysis happens when skeletal muscle breaks down and releases its contents into the bloodstream. The resulting spill can produce dangerous electrolyte imbalances and kidney injury, particularly when someone is dehydrated, Dr. Glatter says.

Although rhabdo is often associated with intense exercise, physical exertion is just one possible cause. “Rhabdomyolysis can also follow crush injuries, seizures, prolonged immobilization, heat stroke, severe infections, certain drugs or toxins, metabolic abnormalities and, more rarely, inherited muscle disorders,” Dr. Glatter says.

Exercise-related rhabdo typically happens when an individual suddenly performs an unusually difficult, repetitive workout involving large muscle groups—particularly when doing eccentric exercise, or when the muscle contracts while lengthening. The risk may be higher when the activity is unfamiliar or far more intense than the person is accustomed to. Some examples include high-repetition squats, lunges, downhill running, and intense cycling, Dr. Glatter says.

“The key issue is often not simply that the workout was ‘hard,’ but that its intensity or volume was far beyond what that person’s muscles were conditioned to tolerate,” he says.

Symptoms include muscle pain "dramatically out of proportion to ordinary post-workout ache,” Dr. Glatter describes, as well as dark-colored urine, swelling, weakness, and difficulty moving the affected muscles. Other possible symptoms include dizziness, fainting, confusion, persistent vomiting or feeling generally ill, he says. “Someone who develops severe or rapidly worsening muscle pain or swelling after a strenuous workout—especially together with marked weakness, dark urine or substantially decreased urination—should be evaluated urgently.”

It’s important to note that symptoms can start while someone is still exercising. An athlete may notice muscle pain that feels disproportionate to effort, unusual heaviness or weakness, severe fatigue, or a drop in pace or intensity that would normally be manageable, Dr. Glatter says. In severe cases, athletes can collapse, appear confused, or develop nausea, dizziness, and swelling.

Other risk factors also include dehydration, exercising in extreme heat or humidity, a recent viral illness, certain medications or drugs, and underlying genetic or metabolic disorders, such as sickle cell.

Although many recent social media accounts of exercise-related rhabdo have come from women, it’s important to know that an unaccustomed high-intensity workout can overwhelm muscles regardless of sex. Those stories don’t necessarily reflect who is most likely to develop the condition, and studies have shown generally higher rates in men.

It’s complicated. “It’s probably a combination of greater awareness and a genuine increase in some types of exertional rhabdomyolysis, but the evidence does not support describing rhabdo as a new epidemic,” Dr. Glatter says.

Rhabdo has been around for decades, and it’s difficult to determine overall incidence rates because mild cases may never be diagnosed. Still, some research suggests that exercise-related cases have increased in certain settings. A 2022 analysis published in the Physician and Sportsmedicine looked at emergency department data from 2000 through 2019. It estimated more than 40,000 cases of exertional rhabdomyolysis and found an increase over that period.

Dr. Glatter suggests this might be, in part, due to shifts in exercise culture. An increase in the availability of fitness classes such as high-intensity interval training, cycling, and CrossFit-style workouts can expose people to unfamiliar movement patterns at high intensities.

“These workouts are not inherently dangerous, and millions of people perform them safely. The risk arises when workload increases too quickly relative to conditioning, particularly when heat, dehydration, illness, medications or inadequate recovery are added to the equation,” Dr. Glatter says. “A highly motivated newcomer trying to keep pace with a class may continue well past the point at which her muscles are accustomed to working.”

It’s also important to note: Being well-trained does lower the risk of rhabdo, but it does not eliminate it. “In a trained athlete, rhabdomyolysis is often best understood as a ‘multiple-hit’ phenomenon, where several physiologic stresses occur at the same time,” Dr. Glatter says. Someone may be proportionally prepared for the activity, but factors such as unusually high intensity or duration, heat and humidity, dehydration, recent illness, inadequate recovery, electrolyte abnormalities, and certain medications or supplements can lower the threshold for injury. Rare underlying conditions, such as sickle cell trait or certain metabolic conditions, can also predispose a healthy athlete to rhabdo.

That doesn’t mean you should fear strenuous exercise. Starting at a manageable level, building up gradually, and staying hydrated can help reduce the risk.

“The body adapts extraordinarily well to exercise when intensity and volume are increased gradually,” Dr. Glatter says. “Going from relatively little training to an all-out, high-repetition workout involving large muscle groups is a very different physiological challenge.”

Exercise-related rhabdo is uncommon, but can happen when a workout places demands on muscles that are far beyond what they’re accustomed to. High-intensity exercise can be part of a healthy routine; the key is to build toward it gradually and pay attention to how your body responds. If you develop severe muscle pain or swelling, weakness, dark urine, or substantially decreased urination, seek urgent medical care.

That small hole on a ruler has a practical purpose and an unexpected history. Here's why it exists and how people use it.

Almost everyone has used a ruler at some point, whether it was for a school project, measuring something around the house, or drawing a straight line. But even after years of using one, there’s a small detail that’s easy to miss: the hole near the end.

Those holes in rulers aren’t just part of the design. They actually serve a purpose, and the reason they’re there goes back further than most people might expect. Here’s what they’re for, how they were used in the past, and a few other ways they come in handy.

The hole at the end of a ruler is mainly there for storage. It lets people hang the ruler on a hook, nail, or pegboard instead of leaving it in a drawer or on a workbench. This is particularly helpful with longer metal rulers that might not fit in a toolbox.

But the hole has been around for much longer than modern workshops. Years ago, before paper notebooks became common in schools, students practiced writing on small slate boards. They could wipe away their writing with a damp cloth and use the board again.

Some students tied that cloth to their rulers with a piece of string through the hole. That way, they always had something nearby to clean their slates. Schools eventually moved away from slate boards, but the holes remained. Today, they’re mostly there to make rulers easier to hang and store.

Hanging a ruler isn’t the only reason manufacturers add holes. Some rulers have several, and they can serve different purposes depending on where they’re placed. For example, certain school rulers have three holes spaced along one side. These line up with the rings in a standard three-ring binder, so students can keep their rulers with their other school supplies.

Some rulers also have holes that help with drawing circles. A pin through one hole can hold the ruler in place while a pencil in another hole traces a circle as the ruler turns. Not every ruler has holes designed for this, but specialty models like the Creative Grids Quick Trim Ruler do. A compass is still a better choice when exact measurements matter.

Some crafting rulers have holes for more specific jobs. Quilting rulers and templates may have openings that help mark circles or line up fabric before cutting. Others have slots or shaped edges that guide a rotary cutter, making it easier to cut certain shapes.

There are also special rulers for paper crafts and stitching. Some have rows of tiny, evenly spaced holes that help people mark where to make stitches or decorative patterns.

Of course, not every ruler has holes. Smaller plastic rulers often don’t need them, while longer metal versions may have one at each end. It mostly comes down to how the manufacturer expects people to use the ruler. And for everyday measuring, the holes don’t make much difference. A ruler without them can measure just as accurately as one with them.
