I finished collecting the bricks of cocaine and landed my bullet-ridden seaplane at the dock. It was time for birdwatching.

A few minutes after midnight on Thursday morning, I joined millions of other gamers for the release of Grand Theft Auto VI, the latest in the smash video game series from Rockstar Games. The new installment is already a worldwide sensation, earning more than $1.8 billion in revenue the day it was released.

Grand Theft Auto, or GTA, games are famous for their “open worlds,” which allow players to explore massive, intricately detailed versions of America. Much of that exploration is focused on mayhem and chaos: The story missions usher players through criminal underworlds, and players are at any time free to steal cars, rob stores and pedestrians, and initiate anarchy.

But GTA 6 has more going on than just criminality—it’s also a world filled with wildlife. Previous GTA games were set in fictionalized versions of New York City and Hollywood, but the new one returns to Vice City, its version of Miami, which was also the setting for 2002’s installment. Unlike that game, however, GTA 6’s South Florida is complete with dozens of species of birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, mammals, and other creatures. The in-game Progress menu tracks all kinds of items to find, including 338 vehicles, 205 weapons, and, of primary interest to me, at least 60 bird species.

I made it my mission to find them all as quickly as possible.

(Side note: Pre-release marketing for GTA 6 touted 62 bird species, but the official birds Progress screen only lists 60, with a handful of others listed on the hunting screen.)

You begin the game as Jason, a low-level criminal working to raise money to support his young family. You’re equipped with zoom binoculars before the very first mission—tracking drug runners through a swamp and escaping with the seaplane—and I used them immediately, stopping mid-lurk to scan some Double-crested Cormorants on a dock. (Distracted from the job at hand to look at birds—just like real life!) They looked like Double-crested Cormorants!

After the first mission the player is free to explore the entirety of the map, stealing cars, robbing stores, or just birding wherever they want. The game makes birding easy: Looking at any bird through the in-game binoculars gives players the option to hold a button to “study” it, thereby adding it to the Progress menu. Simply standing on the back deck of Jason’s first safehouse yields a bunch of species like Brown Pelican and Laughing Gull. It’s just like being in South Florida!

Looking south from the back deck, I spotted another bird perched on an adjacent dock, with a few people gathered at the end. Looking through my binoculars I spotted my first Herring Gull—and noticed that the people, a woman and three men, were fully and visibly naked. Jason apparently lives next to a nudist resort. It’s just like being in South Florida!

Two down, 58 to go. I found a few more species right in Jason’s backyard, including American Crow, House Sparrow, and South Florida-appropriate Monk Parakeets, before venturing out into the huge map. My strategy for finding birds in GTA 6 was the same as finding birds in real life: look in as many habitats as I could find. I started by “borrowing” a fanboat off a nearby dock and heading back to the swampy areas near the seaplane mission. There were tons of birds across the open water and in the marsh: Caspian Terns, Mottled Ducks, Sanderlings, Spotted Sandpipers, Great Blue Herons, Glossy and White Ibis, and … flocks of Whooping Cranes?

In fact, I found a number of avian inaccuracies in just my first few hours of playtime. Certain species like Whooping Crane and Red-cockaded Woodpecker are common throughout the map, despite being endangered and threatened, respectively, in real life. Golden Eagles are extremely rare in Florida, but I found one soaring over the Mt. Kalaga area (mountains, one may point out, aren’t found in Florida either). Some species have confusing naming quirks—the chickadee is Black-capped instead of Carolina, and there’s a “New World Oriole” instead of a Baltimore Oriole—but those changes are likely due to the fact that Carolina and Baltimore don’t exist in the GTA universe.

Though some of the inaccuracies may feel like a letdown in a game that is being hailed as the Greatest of All Time, I think it’s important to remember that it’s also game where you can get a ride from a dog driving a jet ski. Grand Theft Auto games are meant to be fun, not science. Plus, despite some of the misfires, the avifauna feels well thought-out. The map is true to the ecological boundaries of South Florida, with herons, gulls, and other waterbirds in the south while birds like Red-bellied Woodpecker, Wood Stork, and American Robin are found farther north. Birders know that certain exotic species have made their home in tropical South Florida, including Spot-breasted Oriole and Red Junglefowl, both in the game.

That geographic fidelity is the main tip for anyone looking to check off all the game’s birds: Find their habitat. The game doesn’t tell you what species to look for, so you’ll need to cover a lot of ground to find everything. Wading birds, shorebirds, and seabirds are common in the southern coastal part of the map. A number of smaller, open-country or forest birds are found up north. Owls come out at night. Birds in zoos and domestic farmyard birds count towards the 60, too, so don’t overlook those areas.

Birds and birding are presented quite similarly in both games, including the binocular “study” mechanic and many of the same species, but the experiences are totally different. RDR2 was set in the late 1800s, and players rode on horseback or locomotive through a comparatively wide-open and untrammeled version of America. There was mayhem in that game, too—outlaw gangs and marauders—but there were many peaceful moments, too, when a player could look out across wild landscape with nothing but nature as a soundtrack.

Not so in GTA 6. There’s plenty of birdsong on the soundtrack, but it competes with an endless racket of car radios, revving engines, swearing pedestrians, and blaring horns. There’s garbage almost everywhere, lapping the shorelines and blowing along the streets. There seems to be no inch of landscape in GTA 6 that hasn’t been lessened by the touch of humans. It is, I have to admit, a realistic depiction of birding in South Florida.

The eminent evolutionary biologist and modeller John Maynard Smith was once asked about Karl Popper and the philosophy of science. His answer was not flattering. His impression was that scientists who take the philosophy of science seriously, and allow their research to be influenced by philosophical preconceptions, are "far more likely to do themselves harm than good". He loved reading philosophy of science and was always ready to argue about it, he added, but he did not believe that one should allow oneself to be influenced by it when actually thinking about science.

This is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic. These are quite radical words, and though Maynard Smith's generalisation is untenable, there is an important insight to be extracted.

If modellers approach the philosophical literature on models with the goal of figuring out what models are and what a good model is, they might easily find themselves lured into naive monistic views of models that fail to represent the full epistemic richness of modelling practices, thereby turning them into worse scientists than they could have otherwise been.

This could happen in several ways. For instance, models with epistemic functions not recognised by narrow monist accounts might be discarded. Consider, for example, models with weak predictive power, but great generality and insight into complex systems.

Perhaps Maynard Smith's worry is most strongly illustrated in economics, where Milton Friedman and Karl Popper are taken to have the "last word" in any methodological debate, and are frequently invoked to both defend and criticise the work of modellers. They are perhaps paradigm cases of monist thinking. Friedman argued that the only relevant desideratum in science concerns predictive power, while Popper argued that what matters is falsifiability.

These accounts of science have received extensive criticism over the last decades. Indeed, the philosopher Alex Rosenberg argued that Friedman's naive picture single-handedly justified the existence of philosophy of economics as a distinct profession. Economists, however, are unlikely to read much if any work on methodology beyond Friedman and Popper. As the economist Dani Rodrik notes, economic methodology is relegated to specialised journals and, more importantly, is not really being taught in graduate school.

The result is that economists seem to believe that their science operates according to a simple form of falsificationism, a picture of science that, most contemporary philosophers of science would agree, both misrepresents scientific practice and provides the wrong ideal of how science ought to function.

The obvious counter to Maynard Smith's concern is that it is not an argument against the philosophy of science, but rather against bad philosophy of science, which limits, rather than fosters, insights into the richness of the epistemic tools available to scientists.

Philosophers are not innocent here. Some debates in the philosophy of science rest on such monist motivations to seek univocal accounts of laws, explanation, species, functions, preferences, and many more. As Peter Godfrey-Smith emphasised, one of the hazards of philosophy is the temptation to come up with theories that are "too broad and sweeping". This hazard has not been avoided in the philosophical literature on models. Many philosophers of science seem to be under the impression that once we figure out what "models" are and how they work, we can finally get a handle on all the other pressing questions in the philosophy of science. I argue that this way of thinking about models is grounded in a mistake.

A philosophy of science worth reading takes the plurality, diversity, and richness of models and modelling practices seriously. It does not hand scientists a single criterion for good science.

Economists, Rodrik argues, need a better story about the kind of science they practise, and some economists have recently engaged with philosophical questions themselves, seeking contact with philosophers of economics in the process. Philosophers of science, in turn, should turn more of their attention toward both the social sciences and the philosophy of the social sciences. Their lack of engagement with this rich literature is a historical omission that may have provided a misguided understanding of how science works.

Should scientists ignore philosophers? They should ignore those who tell them that there is only one way of doing science well.

Three Ancient Cosmic Collisions May Explain Why the Moon Has Two Faces

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have narrowed down when three massive collisions reshaped the Moon, bringing them closer to understanding why its Earth-facing side looks so different from its far side.

Billions of years ago, a collision powerful enough to melt enormous quantities of lunar rock created the Moon’s largest impact basin. Subsequent collisions broke apart and scattered some of that ancient material across the surface, leaving behind a geological record that scientists are now piecing together.

Located on the Moon’s far side, the South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin is one of the oldest and largest impact structures in the solar system. Understanding its history could help explain why the Moon’s near and far sides are so different, what lies beneath its surface, and when major collisions occurred during its early evolution.

An international research team led by Professor Guochun Zhao, Mok Sau-King, a professor in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (DEPS) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU), and Dr. Yuqi Qian, the study’s first author, has identified two types of magnesium-rich rocks with remarkably different origins. Published in Science Advances, the research also establishes a clearer timeline of the major impacts that shaped the region.

For decades, the basin remained largely unexplored. That changed in 2019, when Chang’e-4 completed the first successful landing on the Moon’s far side, followed in 2024 by Chang’e-6, which brought the first rock samples from this largely unexplored region back to Earth.

Using Chang’e-6 samples allocated to HKU DEPS and surface measurements from both missions, the researchers obtained the first direct geological evidence from the two landing sites for reconstructing the basin’s impact history. Satellite observations and numerical simulations helped them trace the origins of rocks across the wider basin.

One group of rocks, known as moderately magnesium-rich norites, formed in the aftermath of the original SPA collision. The impact generated a thick sheet of molten rock that gradually cooled, with different minerals crystallizing at different stages. Later collisions exposed these ancient rocks at the central peaks of large craters. Some were eventually blasted toward the Chang’e-4 landing site in Von Kármán crater by the impact that formed Finsen crater.

The second group contained considerably more magnesium and likely formed through a separate process called magnesium-suite magmatism, a type of magma activity associated with the original SPA impact. These rocks were exposed along the western ring of mountain peaks within the Apollo basin before the Chaffee S impact scattered some of them toward the Chang’e-6 landing site.

Earlier research by the HKU-led team had already uncovered evidence of prolonged volcanic activity and previously unrecognized magma activity in the region. Those studies also suggested that underground accumulations of titanium-rich minerals, known as ilmenite-bearing cumulates, helped account for the contrasting volcanic histories of the Moon’s two sides.

The latest findings suggest that the South Pole-Aitken basin formed approximately 4.34 billion years ago. Another major collision created the Apollo basin around 4.2 billion years ago, followed by the formation of the Orientale basin approximately 3.87 billion years ago. Together, these three events reveal how successive collisions reshaped the Moon’s largest impact basin over hundreds of millions of years.

Scientists Successfully Grow Potatoes in Simulated Moon Soil

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Successful potato harvests in simulated lunar soil suggest that future astronauts could recycle their food scraps to help grow their next meal.

A future in which humans live on the Moon and grow their own food may be a little closer to reality thanks to one of Earth’s most familiar crops. Researchers at Oregon State University have successfully grown edible potatoes in simulated lunar soil, achieving growth and yields comparable to those produced on Earth by adding compost made with the help of earthworms.

The findings support NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to establish a sustained human presence on the Moon. Producing food locally would reduce dependence on costly resupply missions from Earth and help make long-term lunar exploration more sustainable. Potatoes are particularly appealing because they provide substantial nutritional value and produce more calories per unit of land than many other crops.

“We found that potatoes can complete their entire life cycle in simulated lunar soil when supplemented with organic matter,” said Aymeric Goyer of Oregon State University’s Department of Botany and Plant Pathology. “That’s an important step toward developing reliable food production systems that could support long-term human exploration of the Moon.”

One of the biggest agricultural challenges on the Moon lies directly beneath astronauts’ feet. Most of the lunar surface is covered in regolith, a layer of loose rock and dust containing very little organic matter.

In a study published in npj Microgravity, Goyer and his colleagues tested whether adding different amounts of vermicompost, a nutrient-rich material produced when earthworms break down organic matter, could make simulated lunar soil suitable for growing potatoes.

Because the composition of lunar regolith varies across the Moon, the team tested five simulants representing both the Moon’s darker plains, known as lunar maria, and its highland regions. For comparison, they included soil from Hermiston, Oregon, a region known for producing some of the highest potato yields in the world.

Potato plants grown in simulated lunar soil without compost were severely stunted and developed poorly formed roots. Adding between 5% and 15% vermicompost led to only partial recovery. However, plants grown with 30% vermicompost matched the growth and development of those cultivated in Earth soils.

Lunar regolith also contains elevated levels of heavy metals that can damage plant roots and interfere with growth.

“We believe the compost helps balance some of the toxicity we see in lunar regolith by reducing plant exposure to harmful compounds,” said David Handy, a postdoctoral researcher in Goyer’s lab.

The researchers suspect that organic matter binds to heavy metals, making them less available to plants and limiting their harmful effects.

Growing potatoes successfully would mean little if astronauts could not safely eat them. Encouragingly, potatoes produced in the lunar soil simulants maintained their nutritional quality and showed no statistically significant increase in glycoalkaloids, naturally occurring potato toxins often associated with green discoloration beneath the skin. The findings indicate that the potatoes were safe to eat under the conditions tested.

Future lunar explorers could potentially bring a small supply of organic matter from Earth and gradually produce more by recycling food scraps and plant waste. Such a system could turn everyday waste into valuable growing material while reducing the supplies astronauts would need to transport from Earth.

The research team is now investigating which potatoes might be best suited for lunar agriculture. A forthcoming paper will report results from experiments involving 10 potato varieties grown in simulated lunar soil.

Reference: “Growth and molecular responses of potato to lunar regolith simulants” by David Handy, Anika Loeffler, Medora Knudson, Sydney Campbell, Pankaj Jaiswal, Jeffrey C. Anderson and Aymeric Goyer, 19 November 2026, npj Microgravity. DOI: 10.1038/s41526-026-00668-z

NASA Spacecraft Snaps Moody Photo of Earth on Its Way to Mars

NASA’s ESCAPADE spacecraft has captured an incredible photo of the Earth as it slingshotted past the planet on its way to Mars.

The images were taken by the spacecraft’s Visible and Infrared Observation System camera, developed by Northern Arizona University, at around 9 A.M. EST on November 9. This was approximately three hours after the spacecraft made its closest approach to Earth, when it was about 38,500 miles away and had a clear view of the Western Hemisphere.

The images were captured by the second of the two ESCAPADE spacecraft to perform a powered flyby of Earth this month. Both spacecraft used a combination of engine burns and Earth’s gravity to gain momentum for their 10-month journey to Mars.

Once they reach orbit around the Red Planet, the spacecraft will study how the solar wind — a stream of charged particles traveling at around a million miles per hour from the Sun — interacts with Mars’ environment and contributes to the loss of its atmosphere.

A commercial satellite captured images of the pair from approximately 3,000 miles away, while a member of the mission team photographed the spacecraft passing overhead from his home near Los Angeles (below).

Movement can change how quickly you learn from the same visual clues

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In a virtual reality study, swinging a lightsaber or throwing a punch helped volunteers learn visual patterns that required combining two features.

In the textbook account, learning happens in the head, and the hands do nothing more than report the answer.

Volunteers learned to sort striped discs more accurately when they answered by swinging a virtual lightsaber or punching a virtual target.

The advantage appeared when they had to judge the angle and thickness of the stripes together.

In the punching experiment, volunteers kept their lead when they switched to button presses immediately after training.

For sorting tasks that could be solved with explicit rules, bigger movements gave volunteers no clear advantage.

Luke Rosedahl is an assistant professor of biomedical engineering in Florida Atlantic University‘s College of Engineering and Computer Science.

Professor Rosedahl conducted the experiments at Brown University with Yuka Sasaki and Takeo Watanabe.

The 160 participants were young adults recruited around Brown, sorting striped discs in a headset, so how far the result reaches outside a lab is still open.

“We found that larger movements substantially improved learning when people had to recognize patterns by integrating multiple features, but provided no meaningful benefit when people learned categories using explicit rules,” Rosedahl said.

“Even more intriguing, the benefit occurred whether participants swung at the target or punched a separate response box. The movement itself appeared to matter, not whether it directly interacted with what they were learning.”

Some categories come with a rule. If it has whiskers and meows, it’s a cat. Psychologists call these rule-based categories, and people learn them by testing hypotheses, which is thought to lean on the front of the brain.

Other categories can’t be put into words. A radiologist’s sense that a lesion looks aggressive comes from weighing several features at once, and so does a driver’s read on whether a road is icy.

People learn these by comparing each new case with ones they’ve seen.

That kind of learning is thought to run through the striatum, a deep brain structure tied to movement and habits.

Both kinds were tested with the same material: gray discs striped at different angles and thicknesses, shown one at a time in a virtual reality hallway.

For the rule-based version, thickness and angle could be judged separately. For the other version, the dividing line ran diagonally through both, so no single feature gave the answer.

In the first experiment, 80 people each sorted 600 discs into two groups over 12 blocks, with the disc flashing green or red after every answer. Half answered by pulling a controller trigger.

The other half swung a virtual lightsaber and hit the disc.

On the hard, no-rule categories, the swingers pulled ahead by the second block and stayed ahead for most of the session.

On the rule-based categories, the swingers did very slightly worse, by an amount that could have been chance.

A swing takes longer than a button press, and it touches the thing being judged. Either could have been the real cause.

In a second experiment with 80 new volunteers, some punched a virtual response box separate from the disc, while others pulled a controller trigger.

Every disc stayed on screen for 2.5 seconds, however quickly they answered.

The rule-based task was also made harder, with two rules to discover instead of one, in case the first version had simply been too easy to show a difference.

Punching still helped on the no-rule categories and still did nothing for the rule-based ones. Slower answers went with worse accuracy, not better, so taking extra time wasn’t the reason.

In the final 100 training trials of the task that required combining both features, volunteers who punched got 90.7% of answers right, compared with 81.0% for those who pressed a button.

Then everyone switched. The punchers moved to buttons and scored 89.5%, almost unchanged. The button group moved to punching and scored 83.1%.

Whatever the big movements had built was still there when the movement stopped, which points to better learning rather than a boost at the moment of answering.

The authors can’t say what the arm is doing for the brain, and they said so. They offered two possibilities.

One possibility is that more effortful movements increase dopamine release in the striatum, which could help reinforce learning.

Effortful actions have also been shown to steer attention toward the features that matter. And movement has helped in other corners of learning, the authors wrote, such as writing by hand to learn to recognize letters.

The selectivity is the clue. If swinging had simply made people more engaged, Rosedahl’s team wrote, both kinds of categories should have improved.

The benefit appeared only when people had to combine visual features, a kind of learning thought to involve motor circuits.

The result also raises a question about decades of earlier work, nearly all of it done with button presses.

Those studies found that no-rule categories are learned slowly and that written instructions on how to perform them don’t help.

The authors suggested that the buttons themselves may have been holding that learning back.

The team didn’t record which hand participants favored, and controllers were handed out in no particular order, so the role of handedness and motor experience is unmeasured.

Other category types, such as rules with exceptions, may respond differently. And everything happened with participants seated and a headset on.

The team wrote that the next studies should test the effect in real training, in fields such as radiology and aviation.

There, the judgments that can’t be put into words are the ones that take years to learn. Rosedahl framed the stakes in one line.

“Incorporating movement could offer a simple way to improve learning in situations where conventional approaches fall short,” he said.

Whether a bigger swing of the arm can speed up a radiologist’s eye, and not only a student’s sorting of striped discs, is the test that comes next.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

Florida’s invasive pythons don’t always stay on the ground, and about 1 in 7 adult sightings were up in trees

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A USGS study found that invasive Burmese pythons in Florida sometimes climb trees, especially in winter, raising concerns for nesting birds.

Burmese pythons invading southern Florida are not primarily tree-dwellers, but some of them climb. Among 188 radio-tracked adults, about 14 percent, or roughly 1 in 7, were found in a tree or shrub at least once, according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS).

For 168 juveniles, the share was about 17 percent. Across more than 4,000 observations, however, fewer than 3 percent placed a python in a tree or shrub.

Both age groups were most likely to climb in December and January, the cooler months of southern Florida’s dry season.

Each observation came from locating a snake through radio telemetry, not from a chance sighting. Tracking ran from November 2017 to December 2024 in Big Cypress National Preserve.

Lisa McBride, a biologist at the USGS Fort Collins Science Center, is the study’s first author.

A public USGS data release lists 112 tracking events in trees or shrubs, involving 56 different pythons.

The first python was observed in the Everglades in 1979, but no more were seen until 1995. The species now breeds in the preserve and is spreading through south Florida.

Burmese pythons, one of Florida’s invasive reptiles, are notoriously difficult to find. By some estimates, the probability of detecting an individual python can be less than 5 percent.

Adults in trees were seen basking, resting, brooding eggs or gathering with other pythons. Juveniles rested, basked or moved between tree limbs.

Most adults found in trees during the peak climbing months were basking, a behavior that lets reptiles absorb heat from their surroundings.

The researchers suggest pythons may use trees and shrubs at that time of year to maintain a high body temperature.

In February 2022, one adult male basked atop a Brazilian peppertree about 16 feet (5 meters) above the ground. It then climbed to the crown of a cabbage palm, about 23 feet (7 meters) up.

A female brooded her eggs in a fallen, hollow cypress tree about 6.5 feet (2 meters) above the ground in May 2022.

A juvenile female rested on a pond apple tree about 5 feet (1.5 meters) up that same May.

A USGS video shows a juvenile female moving across the branches of a dahoon holly tree in the preserve.

The higher share among juveniles may reflect an ontogenetic shift, a change in habitat use as an animal grows, the researchers suggest.

The probability of finding a python in a tree generally fell as its weight rose. Smaller snakes may move through branches more easily than heavy adults.

Still, trees with strong branches, such as Brazilian peppertrees and live oaks, supported some of the largest pythons tracked.

Adults caught in Florida average 6 to 9 feet (1.8 to 2.7 meters), according to the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC). The species can reach 19 feet (5.8 meters), however.

Native from India to lower China, the Malay Peninsula and some islands of the East Indies, Burmese pythons are semi-aquatic and excellent climbers.

The exact route of their introduction is unknown. Pythons likely escaped from a breeding facility destroyed during Hurricane Andrew, and pet pythons have likely been released in and around the Everglades.

The months when adult pythons were most likely to be in trees overlap with the nesting season of several wading-bird species. Earlier studies documented pythons preying on nests of great egrets and white ibises in the Everglades.

No python was seen preying on birds in trees during the tracking. The USGS cautioned that the overlap does not mean tree use is causing bird declines.

Rather, it identifies another behavior that could bring an invasive predator into contact with vulnerable animals, and an area for continued research.

More than 25 percent of captured pythons have birds in their stomachs.

Before 2000, mammals were encountered frequently during nocturnal road surveys in Everglades National Park. Road surveys from 2003 to 2011 then recorded a 99.3 percent drop in raccoon observations as pythons spread.

Opossum observations fell by 98.9 percent and bobcat observations by 87.5 percent. The same surveys failed to detect rabbits.

Some reports put the south Florida python population anywhere from 5,000 to 180,000 snakes, though the best estimates so far place it closer to 30,000.

Almost 28,000 wild Burmese pythons have been removed from Florida’s environment and reported to the FWC since 2000. The 2026 Florida Python Challenge alone removed 280, with 907 participants from 30 U.S. states, Canada and Vietnam.

The South Florida Water Management District also pays agents an hourly rate to hunt pythons. They earn $50 for each snake up to 4 feet (1.2 meters), $25 for each additional foot (0.3 meters) and $200 for each verified active nest.

Seasonal data on tree use “could improve the effectiveness of removal efforts by management agencies,” the authors wrote.

Knowing when pythons climb could also help scientists understand how the snakes interact with the Greater Everglades ecosystem.

The study was published in the journal Journal of Herpetology.

Newborn stars are hiding in the red threads of a neighboring galaxy

Webb peers into N159, where dusty filaments shelter some of the youngest stars in a neighboring galaxy.

Today’s Image of the Day takes us inside a cloud where stars are being born in a neighboring galaxy.

In this view from the James Webb Space Telescope, pale blue gas fills broad cavities, orange pillars jut from their edges, and dark red threads wind across the middle.

Some of the youngest stars in the scene are still tucked inside those threads.

The cloud, known as N159, lies about 161,000 light-years away in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

Webb’s image spans about 230 light-years. Across that distance, some stars are still gathering the material they need to grow, while others already shine fiercely enough to change the shape of the cloud around them.

Start with the pale blue areas. They trace hydrogen gas exposed to intense ultraviolet light from massive stars.

That radiation can knock electrons loose from hydrogen atoms. As the electrons rejoin hydrogen nuclei, the gas gives off light of its own.

A bright patch can therefore reveal where massive stars are affecting their surroundings, even if the glow itself comes from the gas.

Those stars also send streams of particles into the cloud. Over time, these stellar winds can help hollow out cavities and sculpt the denser material along their edges.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has a smaller proportion of elements heavier than helium than the Milky Way does.

Studying a region like N159 gives astronomers a chance to see how stars form with a mix of ingredients closer in some respects to what was available long ago.

In another, dense gas still shelters stars that are just getting started. Given time, much of that gas will disperse, leaving the stars exposed.

For now, the thin red strands mark places where some of the newest stars in this corner of a neighboring galaxy are still taking shape.

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

For the last 60 years or so, we've been warned about stress and told to do everything we can to avoid it.

We've been taught how to meditate our way out of stress, down-regulate from it, "hack our cortisol," reduce our adrenaline, manage our vagus nerve, calm ourselves, numb ourselves, distract ourselves, and halt our stress responses in as many ways as we can.

We've also been taught to view many of the difficulties in our lives as always-negative exterior forces, or stressors, that act upon us without our knowledge or permission. Stress has been presented to us as a dangerously unwanted thing with no value whatsoever.

Thankfully, newer research into stressors and our stress response has found the opposite to be true.

In part 1 of this series, I focused on the weasel word of stress, because our stress response is an emotional response. In my work, I help people understand the emotions that form the foundation of our stress response. We learn how to name these emotions with precision and work directly with them to access the essential intelligence they contribute to us.

Three of the main emotions I see in our stress response are: anxiety (the emotion of motivation and task completion), anger (which helps us identify and uphold what we value), and panic (which steps forward when our lives are in danger).

Each of these emotions helps us meet stressors with intelligence and focus. When we know which emotions are supporting us, and we know how to work with those emotions (instead of against them), stressful situations can teach us who we are, what's important to us, and how we can meet life's challenges with focus, skills, and resilience.

But if we don't know how to identify or work with our emotions, we may feel overwhelmed as our bodies increase our activation to help us meet the necessary stressors we face. We may also learn two unhelpful lessons:

If we believe that stressors are foreign and that our emotions are problems, we'll create unnecessary suffering, not just in response to the stressors but because our skills are missing in action.

Psychological researcher Kelly McGonigal has been focusing on stress for many years, and in her 2016 book, The Upside of Stress: Why Stress Is Good for You, and How to Get Good at It, she shares information from decades of stress research. Her book is full of surprising facts that turn our understanding of stress on its ear. For instance:

If I told you that the price of tiger chow has gone up by 40 percent, you might have little to no reaction (assuming you aren't a tiger keeper). This is a big price increase, but because it has no meaning to you and does not impact any part of your life, your stress response will be quiet—as it should be; there's nothing for you to do about this, and your emotions don't need to be involved.

But if I told you that your share of the cost for your child's monthly doctor's visits has gone up by 40 percent, it's likely that your stress response would engage. Your emotions, most notably anxiety and anger, might intensify to bring you the energy and focus you need to work the problem. Your stress response would help you know that this situation is important, that it's worth your time and focus, and that you need its increased energy to help you make new plans to support your child's well-being.

Sadly, most stress management approaches would have you try to calm yourself or suppress your emotions (or merely observe them), as if the activation you feel is a problem in and of itself. But this old, tired idea is not only wrong; it's counterproductive, because the strength of your stress response is a key to your success and even mastery of the situation.

Of all the surprising things in stress research, this may be the most delightful: The strength of your stress response—your emotional intensity, your hyper-focus, your "nerves"—is directly connected to your ability to thrive. And this continues to be true even when you're well-acquainted with a stressor and no longer see it as surprising or shocking.

Researchers studied new and seasoned skydivers, certain that they would see a reduced stress response (reduced cortisol, adrenaline, and heart rate, less "stress") as the skydivers became accustomed to throwing themselves out of planes. Strangely, there wasn't any reduction in the markers of stress in the seasoned skydivers; in fact, the most skilled skydivers continued to exhibit very high stress responses, often higher than people jumping out of planes for the first time.

What they did find was that the seasoned skydivers had reframed their stress responses as excitement, readiness, stimulation, and even zest for life. This reframing helped them treat their activation as a necessary and fun part of skydiving, even though they might have experienced the exact same stress response as overwhelming the first time they leapt out of a plane.

This seemingly paradoxical relationship holds true for other high-performers such as athletes, surgeons, artists, actors, and musicians. A higher stress response does not predict failure; it is intimately connected to skill and mastery.

So, the key is not to avoid stressors or your stress response, and it's not to become an endlessly chilled-out and calm person.

The key to developing your resilience alongside your skills is to learn how to reframe your natural responses to stressors and welcome the stimulation, focus, and preparedness they provide.

Uranus and Neptune’s strange magnetic fields may be getting stranger

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

New simulations suggest Uranus and Neptune’s magnetic fields could shift dramatically within decades, driven by conditions deep inside the planets.

Earth’s magnetic field is fairly simple. It has one strong north pole and one strong south pole, both lined up close to the axis the planet spins on, and that’s why a compass points north.

The magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune are far messier.

Both planets’ magnetic fields are strongly tilted relative to their rotation axes.

A new study suggests the reason lies deep inside both planets, and that their fields may look quite different today from the last time a spacecraft measured them.

The study focuses on how well material deep inside the planets conducts electricity. In the computer models, conductivity increased with depth.

With that one change, the simulated fields matched the broad features measured during Voyager 2’s flybys in the 1980s.

The work was led by Chi Yan, a research associate at the Jackson School of Geosciences at The University of Texas at Austin.

Yan’s study is built on simulations, not new measurements. Each planet’s magnetic field has been measured up close only once.

NASA’s Voyager 2 flew past Uranus in 1986 and past Neptune in 1989. It’s still the only spacecraft to have visited either planet.

Earth’s magnetic field is tilted about 11 degrees from the axis it spins around. Voyager 2 found that Uranus’ field is tilted 59 degrees and Neptune’s is tilted 47 degrees.

On every other planet with a known magnetic field, the simple north-south pattern accounts for 74% to 98% of the magnetic energy at the surface.

For Uranus and Neptune, the figure is only 12%. The rest of their magnetic energy comes from smaller, more complicated patterns.

A planet makes a magnetic field when fluid that can carry electric current moves around deep inside it. Scientists call this process a dynamo.

Earth’s outer core is liquid iron, surrounded by a mostly solid layer of rock. As the molten iron moves, shaped by Earth’s spin, it produces a field with strong north and south poles.

Uranus and Neptune are about four times wider than Earth.

Scientists think they are made mostly of hydrogen, helium, water, ammonia, methane and rocky material, though the exact makeup is still unknown.

They’re called ice giants because water, ammonia and methane are classified as ices in planetary science.

Yan said the planets’ large size and chemistry could explain the tilted fields. Under enough pressure, water molecules can split into charged pieces called ions, which let electricity flow.

“Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, pressures and temperatures become extremely high,” said Yan.

“Under these conditions, which can cause water molecules to break apart into ions, the material may become increasingly electrically conductive with depth.”

The researchers simulated fluid moving inside an ice giant and tracked how the flow, the heat and the magnetic field changed together.

The team tested several interior layouts, with thicker and thinner layers of moving fluid.

In some runs, the fluid conducted electricity equally well at every depth. Those runs made strong, upright fields much like Earth’s, and the poles never flipped.

When the fluid conducted better with depth, the result changed in every layout the team tried. The fields became messy, with several poles, and they flipped direction at irregular times.

Near the top of the moving layer, where the fluid conducted less well, magnetic forces had less control over the flow. The motion there broke into smaller, more vigorous swirls.

The runs that looked most like Uranus and Neptune had a fairly thin layer of moving fluid. The authors wrote that the effect doesn’t need finely tuned conditions.

That flexibility matters because the two planets differ sharply in heat.

Uranus gives off little heat beyond the sunlight it absorbs. Neptune gives off nearly twice the energy it receives from the Sun.

In two runs, the simulated fields closely resembled those measured at Uranus and Neptune, then looked clearly different 32 simulated years later.

More than 30 years have passed since Voyager 2’s visits.

“Earth’s magnetic tilt drifts by only a degree or so over 50 years,” said Yan.

“Our models show the ice giants can behave very differently. In some stretches the tilt swings by tens of degrees within decades. In others it shifts only slightly over the same span.”

In one run that resembled Uranus, the tilt moved 84 degrees over 40 years. In another, it moved 13 degrees. Fast and slow change both showed up in runs resembling Uranus.

A 13-degree shift would fit what telescopes have seen at Uranus. Its auroras have appeared in roughly the same places as in 1986, give or take 10 to 20 degrees.

The slow periods didn’t last, though. In the 13-degree run, the field soon started changing faster.

Auroras are one way to check. They form when charged particles from the Sun interact with gases high in a planet’s atmosphere, and where they show up can hint at whether the planet’s magnetic tilt has moved.

Some recent telescope studies, including one that estimated Uranus’ rotation rate by tracking its auroras, assumed its magnetic field had remained unchanged since 1986.

At Neptune, the southern aurora appears to have moved about 30 degrees in latitude between 1989 and 2023.

Researchers aren’t sure yet whether that move reflects a change in the magnetic field.

They are now studying sharp infrared images of both planets’ auroras from the James Webb Space Telescope.

Tom Stallard, a professor at Northumbria University, led that observing campaign and reviewed the new paper for the journal.

Both planets also have an ionosphere, an electrically charged layer high in the atmosphere.

“We are busy working on what’s contained within these observations, but the shape and structure of the aurora, as well as other weird patterns in the ionosphere promises to provide us with a way of understanding how the magnetic fields have changed, an important stepping stone,” said Stallard.

Yan’s group plans to combine the Webb aurora images, Voyager 2’s magnetic readings and the simulations to estimate how the two fields are arranged today.

Uranus’ magnetic field could also turn out to change slowly over a long period.

If it does, the models suggest the planet’s deep interior carries electricity about 10 times better than standard estimates.

That could mean the inside of Uranus is hotter than scientists thought. Or it could mean the interior holds materials that carry electricity more easily.

Understanding the interiors properly will take new missions back to the ice giants, the researchers wrote.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Physics.

Following Repair, NASA’s NICER Improves Daytime Measurements

A NASA X-ray telescope on the International Space Station called NICER, or Neutron star Interior Composition Explorer, has regained additional daytime observation capabilities thanks to repairs completed during a spacewalk and a reconfiguration of its detectors.

In May 2023, NICER developed a light leak in which unwanted sunlight began entering the instrument. Photos taken from inside the space station revealed several small areas of damage to the telescope’s thin thermal shields, which block sunlight while allowing X-rays through to the detectors. Nighttime observations were unaffected, and with operational adjustments, the NICER team was able to recover about 20% of station daytime observations.

In January, NASA astronaut Nick Hague installed nine patches to cover the largest areas of damage during a spacewalk. After resuming science operations, the NICER team determined the overall level of sunlight inside NICER had substantially reduced. Still, it experienced more visible-light interference than expected.

Close-up, high-resolution photos from the spacewalk allowed the team to see additional small holes and cracks in the thermal shields that were not previously visible. These accounted for the remaining sunlight intrusion.

After further analysis, the NICER team developed a novel approach to regaining additional daytime data collection.

Each X-ray that hits a NICER detector generates electrical charge that is sensed by a measurement/power unit (MPU). After so many hits, the detector resets — like emptying a cup before it overflows.

Sunlight can also create charge that accumulates in the detector, adding water to the metaphorical cup. There was so much sunlight entering NICER that the detectors were filling up with charge and resetting thousands of times for every X-ray detection. It overwhelmed the MPU’s ability to process the valid X-ray events.

Hague’s repair in January reduced the amount of sunlight entering NICER, which enabled the team to reconfigure the MPUs to ignore the sunlight-generated resets. After initial testing on the ground, the team updated one MPU before switching all seven. The changeover was completed March 12.

In combination with the patches, the reconfiguration has allowed NICER to return to collecting observations during more than 70% of station daytime, as the telescope continues to help us better understand the X-ray universe, including neutron stars, black holes, and other energetic phenomena. The team continues to look for more opportunities to improve NICER’s operations.

Earth’s Ancient Oceans May Have Fooled Scientists About the Rise of Oxygen

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Some of Earth’s best-known oxygen milestones may belong to the oceans, not the atmosphere, reshaping our understanding of the conditions that allowed complex life to evolve.

For billions of years, Earth had almost no oxygen in its atmosphere, making it a vastly different world from the one that supports complex life today. New research suggests oxygen accumulated in three major stages, with the deep ocean taking hundreds of millions of years to catch up.

Two geochemists at Chengdu University of Technology argue in The Innovation that scientists may have misjudged the timing of Earth’s oxygenation by using ancient ocean chemistry to estimate atmospheric conditions. Ocean chemistry is also influenced by nutrients, biological activity, circulation, and basin shape, making it an imperfect record of the air.

Instead, the researchers examined an oxygen isotope signature preserved in sedimentary sulfate. Reactions involving oxygen, ozone, and carbon dioxide produce a distinctive shortage of oxygen-17 in the atmosphere. This signature enters sulfate when sulfide minerals weather, providing a record of atmospheric oxygen.

The first major increase, the Great Oxidation Event, occurred between 2.4 billion and 2.1 billion years ago, raising oxygen from less than one-millionth of today’s level to between 0.1% and 10%.

The next increases occurred between 1.3 billion and 1.0 billion years ago and between 440 million and 410 million years ago, earlier than traditionally thought. The better-known Neoproterozoic and Paleozoic oxygenation events, dated to 800 million to 500 million and 420 million to 360 million years ago, may instead reflect ocean responses that lagged behind atmospheric changes by roughly 200 million and 30 million years.

Oxygen nevertheless remained scarce. Models based on the isotope evidence place mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen at just 0.1% to 1% of modern levels. Charcoal, which requires roughly three-quarters of today’s oxygen concentration to form during burning, first appears around 420 million years ago.

Atmospheric and ocean oxygen generally increased together over billion-year timescales, although they could move in opposite directions over millions of years. When oxygen entered seawater containing accumulated organic carbon and sulfide, chemical reactions consumed the gas and could lower atmospheric levels. Some geological signals previously interpreted as oxygen increases may therefore indicate declines.

Oxygen also spread unevenly through the oceans. Photosynthetic microbes created localized oxygen-rich environments as early as 3 billion years ago, or earlier. These dominated during the Archean, before oxygen supplied from the atmosphere became increasingly important. The ocean interior did not become fully oxygenated until approximately 410 million years ago.

For life, the transition was gradual. An oxygen-free atmosphere severely limited organisms dependent on the gas, while fluctuating levels during the Proterozoic opened new habitats as animals evolved. After the third atmospheric increase, greater oxygen availability helped support larger bodies, active predation, and expansion into deeper waters.

The researchers caution that the isotope record has limited time resolution, oxygen concentrations after 1 billion years ago remain poorly constrained, and additional increases may have gone undetected. Their findings suggest that Earth’s transformation into an oxygen-rich world was shaped not only by rising atmospheric levels, but also by the long delay before oxygen reached the ocean depths.

Webb reveals a stellar nursery with secrets even infrared cannot reach

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Why does Sagittarius B2 produce so many stars? Webb’s infrared observations offer a closer look at an unusually active region near the Milky Way’s center.

Today’s Image of the Day reveals a crowded stretch of the Milky Way where bright stars and orange clouds surround patches of almost complete darkness.

Those dark areas may look like gaps in the scene, but they contain some of its most intriguing material: dense clouds concealing the birth of stars.

The James Webb Space Telescope captured this view of Sagittarius B2 with its Near-Infrared Camera, or NIRCam.

Released on September 24, 2025, the image offers a detailed look at a vast stellar nursery near our galaxy’s center.

Its brilliant points of light are only part of the story. Understanding what remains hidden could help astronomers explain why this particular cloud produces stars so much more actively than its surroundings.

Sagittarius B2 lies a few hundred light-years from Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the Milky Way’s heart.

This central neighborhood contains crowded star fields, thick clouds and complicated magnetic fields.

Yet an abundance of gas does not automatically translate into abundant star formation.

According to NASA’s release, Sagittarius B2 contains about 10 percent of the gas in the galactic center but accounts for roughly half of its star formation.

That imbalance makes it a valuable place to investigate what allows some clouds to become especially productive stellar nurseries.

Astronomers Adam Ginsburg and Nazar Budaiev of the University of Florida led the observing program.

The team used Webb to examine how massive stars emerge within this unusually dense environment.

Stars begin inside cold molecular clouds, where gas gathers into denser pockets.

As a clump gains material, its gravity strengthens. Eventually, gravity can pull it inward, beginning a collapse that heats the material and produces a developing star called a protostar.

At first, the object draws much of its energy from that collapse. Later, if its core becomes sufficiently hot and compressed, hydrogen nuclei begin combining to form helium.

This process, nuclear fusion, supplies the energy that allows a star to shine through the longest stage of its life.

The cloud surrounding a developing star can make that transition difficult to observe. Astronomers must examine stellar birth through the very material that makes it possible.

A bright, mature star may announce itself across space, while a much younger object remains buried in its original supply of gas and dust.

Webb’s instruments reveal different parts of this environment.

In the NIRCam image, stars crowd the field, interrupted by luminous clouds and dark structures.

Near-infrared light can pass through some of the obscuring dust, bringing objects into view that would be harder to distinguish in visible light.

A companion image from Webb’s Mid-Infrared Instrument, or MIRI, changes the emphasis.

Warm dust becomes much more prominent, while far fewer stars remain visible. Only the brightest stars emit strongly enough at these longer wavelengths to stand out through the clouds.

Together, the views show why one image cannot provide a complete inventory of a stellar nursery.

Changing the wavelengths changes which objects and structures dominate the scene. The same cloud can appear rich in stars in one view and rich in glowing dust in another.

Even Webb’s sensitivity has limits in Sagittarius B2.

The team’s observations used 14 filters covering wavelengths from 1.5 to 25 micrometers.

They revealed a layered cloud containing both exposed massive stars and deeply obscured populations.

The researchers also identified candidate regions of gas energized by massive stars that earlier radio observations had missed.

Elsewhere, MIRI detected radiation escaping from the embedded cluster Sagittarius B2 North along cavities associated with outflows.

These openings provide routes for light to emerge from an otherwise heavily concealed environment.

But many young stellar objects previously detected with the ALMA radio telescope remained invisible to Webb.

Their absence from the infrared images illustrates how effectively the surrounding material can conceal ongoing star formation.

The result gives today’s image a second way to be read. Its bright stars reveal what has emerged into view.

Its dark clouds mark places where the next part of the story is still unfolding beyond Webb’s sight.

Some cancer cells survive chemotherapy by turning into giants

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Cancer cells that survive chemo swell into giant senescent cells that summon immune repair, and drugs that block their IAP proteins kill them.

Chemotherapy can kill a cancer cell, but some survivors enter an unusual state. After a near-lethal dose, they stop dividing and swell to many times their normal size.

Then they start pumping out the chemical signals a wound uses to call in the immune system’s repair crew. These surviving cells may help tumors return.

A team in Dublin has identified a mechanism that keeps them alive after the damage, along with experimental drugs that kill them in laboratory tests.

Seamus Martin, the Smurfit Professor of Medical Genetics at Trinity College Dublin, led the work from his lab with postdoctoral fellow Gokhan Gunay at the university’s Smurfit Institute of Genetics.

Collaborators at the University of Cologne and the Olivia Newton-John Cancer Research Institute in Melbourne took part.

Nearly all of it was done in human cancer cells grown in the lab, with some experiments in mice, and none of it involved patients.

In an interview with Earth.com, Martin was asked what surprised him most. He pointed to the cells themselves.

“These are really bizarre-looking cells,” he said.

“They are truly gigantic when compared with normal cells (approx. 10X normal size) and this is highly unusual behavior for cells, even cancer cells.”

The team gave cervical, bowel and lung cancer cells a three-hour pulse of cisplatin or etoposide, two common chemotherapy drugs that work by damaging DNA.

The dose killed about 40% of the cells within two days. The rest stopped dividing and were still alive a week later, flattened and enormous.

Biologists call this state senescence, and cells in it are often nicknamed zombie cells. They stop dividing but remain alive, secreting a stream of inflammatory proteins.

In a tumor, those signals can attract immune cells that promote new blood vessel growth and help create conditions that support tumor growth.

Martin’s lab studies the machinery that makes cells self-destruct, and the team wanted to know why these cells hadn’t.

The chemotherapy had switched on the cells’ own self-destruct receptors.

Two of them, known as Fas and DR5, rose sharply on the surface of the damaged cells, in every cell type tested and in mouse tumors treated with cisplatin.

Inside the cells, those receptors had assembled the protein complex that normally triggers death. It was fully assembled, and something was holding it back.

One of the restraints turned out to be a family of proteins called inhibitors of apoptosis, or IAPs. The senescent cells were making more of one of them than untreated cells do.

“IAPs act as the antidote to the killing effects of Death Receptors that enable the senescent cells to survive,” Martin told Earth.com.

“However, this now leaves the senescent cells in a state of addiction to the IAPs for their continued survival, which now makes them extremely vulnerable to IAP antagonists.”

Drugs that destroy IAPs already exist, and several have entered clinical trials. The team tested two of them, BV6 and birinapant, which wiped out the IAP proteins inside cells within eight hours.

Given alongside chemotherapy, the drugs killed more cancer cells outright and left far fewer survivors to enter the zombie state.

Given afterward, to cells that had already turned senescent, they killed those cells at doses that did little to normal cells.

Cells engineered to lack the self-destruct receptors, or the enzyme they activate, were largely protected from the drugs, which is how the team knew the receptors were doing the killing.

A widely studied senolytic drug, navitoclax, kills senescent cells by a different route, and it worked just as well without those receptors.

The team also went the other way. Mice bred without caspase-8, the enzyme the receptors activate, built up senescent cells in the lining of the small intestine and showed more inflammatory signaling in the liver.

In cells, deleting the same enzyme let more survivors enter senescence and kept them alive longer.

Old mice told a similar story. In lung, kidney and heart tissue from 24-month-old animals, the two death receptors were higher than in three-month-olds.

There was one living test of the drugs. Mice with an inherited retinal disease, which produces leaky new blood vessels and senescent cells in the retina, got injections of BV6 into the eye.

Their lesions shrank, and the count of senescent cells in the retina fell.

Professor Martin described the strategy as a one-two punch.

The first punch is a standard DNA-damaging drug such as cisplatin, and the second, delivered days later, is a drug that kills the senescent cells the first punch created.

In the paper, the team suggested giving an IAP-blocking drug 5 to 7 days after each round of chemotherapy.

Given ahead of chemotherapy instead, it would push more cells toward death in the first place.

The timing is the hard part, Martin noted. The second punch has to land while the tumor is vulnerable, and he said several trials of IAP-blocking drugs have been run or are under way.

The one-two punch has not been tested against a tumor in an animal in this study.

The chemotherapy-plus-drug experiments were in dishes, and the tumor experiments in mice measured the receptors rather than survival.

The one animal test of the drugs’ zombie-killing power was in a diseased eye, not a cancer.

Whether a human tumor’s survivors depend on IAPs the way these lab cells did is the open question.

Answering it means the trial the team proposes: chemotherapy paired with an IAP-blocking drug, timed to the days when the giant cells appear.

Martin said getting that timing right may decide whether the second punch connects.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

Gut bacteria plays a huge role in vaccine side effects

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria that make a protein called flagellin keep low-grade inflammation going, and the people carrying more of them ran a fever.

For some people, a COVID shot means a night of chills and a headache. Others barely notice theirs, and the split holds, dose after dose. To some, this has always looked like luck.

Researchers have now found part of the reason, and it was in the gut before the needle went in. Certain bacteria there build whip-like tails out of a protein that the immune system reads as a warning.

People whose gut bacteria made more of that protein were the ones who ran a fever.

Kelsey Huus and her colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Biology Tübingen followed 171 healthy adults in Germany through an mRNA vaccination against SARS-CoV-2.

In people they can only show that gut microbes and fever go together, so they tested cause in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

If gut bacteria set the risk of a fever, that risk may be something a person can change, though that has not been shown in people.

In an interview with Earth.com, Ruth Ley, the study’s senior author, said the bigger idea isn’t really about vaccines at all.

“More broadly, I think the interesting idea coming from the study is that the gut microbiome helps set our baseline inflammatory ‘immune tone,'” she said. “That could potentially be relevant well beyond vaccination.”

Participants took their own temperature three times a day for two weeks, before and after their shot, and wrote every reading in a diary. The diaries came to 7,079 readings from 171 people.

The median was 97.7°F (36.5°C), which is below the 98.6°F (37°C) most of us were taught to expect.

Instead of using a clinical cutoff, the team compared each person against their own normal for that hour of the day.

Anyone whose temperature climbed 1.4°F (0.8°C) or more above their own baseline counted as fever-high. That line left 72 people above it and 99 below.

People who said earlier doses had given them a fever ran hot here too. The two groups also differed on a stool test for gut inflammation. This was higher in the fever-high group two weeks after the shot and tilted the same way beforehand.

Many gut bacteria grow whip-like tails to move around. Those tails are built from a protein the immune system treats as a sign that bacteria are on the move. The protein is called flagellin.

The team read the RNA in each stool sample to see which bacterial genes were switched on. In the fever-high group the flagellin genes were running harder, before the vaccine as well as after. Those gut microbiomes carried a wider variety of flagellins to start with.

One genus accounted for most of that. Waltera, a member of the Lachnospiraceae family, made 67.2 percent of the flagellins that were more active in fever-high people. The family as a whole made 76.3 percent.

That genus was also the only one reliably more common in the fever-high group.

The same thing turned up in a second group of people. Among 1,014 Canadian adults in the CHILD Cohort Study who answered questions about side effects after a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, the ones who reported any had more Waltera in their stool.

Measuring flagellin isn’t the same as showing it does something, so the team ran two experiments.

First they grew human colon cells in a dish and bathed them in stool extracts from the participants. Extracts from fever-high people pushed those cells to release more of an inflammatory signal, and the amount tracked how much active flagellin each sample held.

Then they transplanted human microbiomes into germ-free mice and vaccinated them. A mouse with an inflammatory reaction doesn’t run a fever. Its temperature drops instead.

Mice carrying a fever-high person’s gut bacteria dropped further, and how far they dropped tracked the fever that person had run.

Mice bred without the two receptors that detect flagellin still reacted, though less, so flagellin carries part of the signal and something else carries the rest.

The animal work was done at Georgia State University, and the gut cells and the additive cultures were handled with Institut Pasteur in Paris.

Diet changed the same thing. In culture, the common thickener carboxymethylcellulose helped Waltera grow, and raised the amount of inflammatory flagellin it made.

In tanks seeded with stool from two donors, that thickener on its own barely shifted those bacteria. A mix of it and 13 other additives of the kind that go into ultra-processed foods raised both the bacteria and the flagellin they made.

Mice fed a Western-style diet, high in fat and low in fiber, reacted harder to the vaccine than mice on standard chow. Their temperatures dropped further, and a day later they carried more of a second inflammatory signal.

Among the 171 people, 46 said they ate a plant-based diet. Temperatures rose less in that group than in the 125 omnivores.

The gap held even after the team adjusted for gender, age, body mass index, dose, fever medication, earlier fevers and starting antibody levels.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most, Ley pointed at how fast diet changed what the bacteria produced in the laboratory. “That gives us a plausible way in which diet could influence inflammatory immune tone through the microbiome,” she said.

None of this reached the part of the vaccine response that protects you. Fever-high participants carried somewhat higher antibody levels before and after their shot, but the booster lifted both groups by the same amount,. Diet made no difference either.

The mice ended up in the same place. Germ-free animals given fever-high or fever-low microbiomes finished with matching antibody levels, and the ones that cannot detect flagellin made antibodies anyway.

“This suggests that the mechanisms that make someone feel ill after vaccination are at least partly distinct from those responsible for generating protective antibodies,” Ley told Earth.com.

The human diet data are the thinnest part of the work. Participants picked from broad categories rather than filling in a food diary, and Ley said as much herself.

“But the dietary findings in people are much more preliminary: we only have broad, self-reported dietary categories, so we cannot conclude that diet caused the differences in fever that we observed,” she said.

The group was narrow by design. Everyone was a healthy adult between 18 and 40, with a median age of 25 years. Anyone immunocompromised, living with a gut disorder or recently on antibiotics was left out.

Huus and her co-authors wrote that their definitions of fever were built for research and may not meet the standard for a clinical diagnosis.

Ley said she wants to know first whether the same pattern shows up in other groups of people.

“One important next step is to determine whether the relationship between microbial flagellin and vaccine reactions holds across different populations, age groups and vaccines,” she said.

Her team also wants to know whether a person’s inflammatory tone can be shifted at all, which would take a controlled diet trial.

For now, a fever after a shot may say as much about the gut it arrives in as about the shot itself.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

Voyager 1 went silent in deep space right before reaching a historic milestone of one ‘light day’ away from Earth

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA briefly lost contact with Voyager 1 in October 2026. The signal returned just weeks before the spacecraft reaches a historic milestone.

On Oct. 18, 2026, the antennas of NASA’s Deep Space Network tried to pick up Voyager 1 during a scheduled contact and found nothing. A second attempt 24 hours later also failed.

The signal came back on Oct. 20, and the team at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California has kept in touch with the probe since then.

NASA schedules Nov. 18 as the day Voyager 1, already the farthest human-made object, reaches one light-day from Earth, according to JPL. They had not previously said what caused the October problems.

The Deep Space Network has large antennas at Goldstone in California, near Madrid in Spain and near Canberra in Australia. The largest dish at each site measures about 230 feet (70 meters) across.

Those antennas handled the two contact attempts in October. Mission controllers logged no detectable signal on Oct. 18 or Oct. 19.

Kareem Badaruddin, the Voyager mission manager at JPL, said the episode was new to his team. “This is the first time we’ve seen the spacecraft do something like this,” he said in the JPL statement.

He also said the mission has reached a stage where the team has “to expect the unexpected.” The probe has surprised its operators before, including the time it began talking through a radio system not used since 1981.

After contact returned, the team worked out that one group of thrusters on the spacecraft had gone offline and that a backup group had turned on.

The onboard computers carry instructions to make that swap by themselves when they detect a problem with the active thrusters.

JPL calls this kind of automatic reaction fault protection. It puts the probe in a safe, stable state and pauses normal operations so that engineers on the ground can study the problem.

In March 2025, engineers revived a set of roll thrusters that had stopped working in 2004.

Early in September, about six weeks before the probe dropped out of contact, JPL engineers sent the commands that turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and turned on three lower-power substitutes.

Inside the team, the job goes by the name Big Bang. The devices double as a source of warmth for the spacecraft, so JPL could not do them one at a time and all three changed at once.

Voyager 2 got the treatment first, and NASA reported on Aug. 4, 2026 that the swap there should keep three instruments powered for at least one more year. An instrument on Voyager 2 would otherwise have gone dark before the end of 2026.

Nothing in the Oct. 29 note ties the September commands to the thrusters.

Both probes run on radioisotope generators, which turn the heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. The output on each probe falls by about 4 watts a year and, with margins now thin, JPL has been shutting off whatever is not essential.

Ten science instruments went up on Voyager 1 in 1977. A NASA status page updated in April lists only two still running on it, the magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem.

Controllers switched off the cosmic ray subsystem on Feb. 25, 2025, in one of a series of shutdowns meant to stretch the remaining power. The low-energy charged particle instrument was next, on April 17, 2026.

Without the Big Bang swap, another Voyager 1 instrument would likely have been turned off as early as summer 2027, JPL said.

Voyager 1 launched on Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after its twin, passed Jupiter and Saturn, and entered interstellar space in August 2012. It is moving at about 38,000 miles per hour (61,155 kilometers per hour).

NASA’s light-day page sets the crossing on Nov. 18, when the probe will sit about 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) from Earth.

Light covers the distance to the Moon in 1.3 seconds. Artemis II astronauts needed about three days for the same trip.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory explains how far away Voyager 1 is when it reaches one light-day from Earth. Credit: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Voyager 2 took a different route so it could visit Uranus and Neptune, and NASA expects it to reach the same distance in November 2035. The nearest star system, Proxima Centauri, lies about 4.24 light-years away.

Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd described what the distance does to a conversation. “If you want to say ‘hello’ to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response,” she said in the JPL statement.

JPL’s Oct. 29 note does not say what knocked the thrusters offline, and engineers are still investigating. It also does not confirm that the thruster switch was what interrupted the signal.

The note does not name the three devices replaced in September or give the amount of power saved. It also says nothing about when the mission might end, although the team expects more surprises before summer 2027.

The light-day crossing itself will change nothing on board or around the spacecraft, JPL said. Its value lies in showing the length of the mission and the size of the distances involved.

At one light-day, a command sent from Goldstone, Madrid or Canberra needs a full day to reach Voyager 1. The answer needs another.

Expert Tips for Scouting, Scattering, and Tagging Fall Turkeys

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Fall turkey hunting requires matching your strategy to the specific flock type you're pursuing. Adult gobblers, hen-poult family groups, and second-year jakes all behave and vocalize differently. The spring yelp that pulled in a gobbler won't work in October. Identify the birds first, then dial in your calls and approach accordingly.

Turkeys typically only receive passing mentions when folks discuss their favorite fall pursuits. And with spring being a distant memory and other seasons in full swing, that’s understandable. And unfortunate. Fall turkeys offer a sporting experience unlike any other outdoors adventure. And with more than 40 states offering autumn turkey seasons—some of which open in September and others that run deep into winter—opportunities abound.

But the fall game plays out somewhat differently than the familiar spring ritual. Here’s a quick look at the types of turkeys you’ll likely encounter in the autumn woods and some tips on how to bring one home.

Many hunters kill fall turkeys incidentally while pursuing deer or small game, sometimes encountering flocks at harvested crop fields or mast-rich wooded areas. There’s nothing wrong with that, but true students of the autumn game dig far deeper into turkey behavior and learn to interact with each type of fall bird.

During fall, turkeys are in different modes than in spring, as they’re no longer concerned with breeding. Food and the struggle for survival dominate their existence. They typically fall into several distinct flock types: adult gobblers, hens without poults, and hens with broods of poults. Several subcategories also exist: Male birds in their second autumn—those that were jakes the previous spring—group together. These are sometimes called jake-and-a-halfs. Also, hen-poult groups often combine into larger family flocks, called crèches. The various groups sometimes intermingle, especially at prime feeding areas or large common roost sites. Often, however, you’ll encounter them separately.

As with spring, calling can be critical in autumn for finding turkeys and getting them close. However, turkeys—especially hens and poults—can be much more vocal in fall, and their lexicon expands to include sounds many spring hunters don’t hear much. Roost sites that attract family flocks can sound like a din of turkey chatter at dawn. Young turkeys often utter kee-kees (a series of three to four ascending whistles) and kee-kee runs, which are whistling series followed by a crude yelp or two. The vocalizations might sound slightly different depending on whether it’s uttered by a jake or jenny. Brood hens use long, pleading strings of assembly yelps to gather their offspring after being separated. Gobblers are typically less vocal in fall, communicating via slow, raspy gobbler yelps and popping clucks. Still, it’s not uncommon to hear them gobble at times.

Because the various types of turkeys act and vocalize differently in fall, general hunting approaches can fall flat. For example, the straightforward hen yelps that made a gobbler respond in April will likely be met with indifference from male turkeys in October. To boost your chances, you must know the kind of bird you’re hunting and then fine-tune your strategy.

Generally, fall hunts fall under two broad categories: 1) Hunters who call in an attempt to make turkeys come to them. 2) Hunters, or specially trained turkey dogs, who scatter groups of turkeys, then set up and try to call the birds back. Knowing the type of turkey you’re after is especially critical during scatter scenarios.

“You need to be the turkey that calls to you,” said Marlin Watkins, a noted Ohio call maker and expert fall turkey hunter. “A young gobbler is not coming to hen sounds. You need to be that bird, whether it’s a jenny hen, young gobbler, or adult hen. And if you produce those sounds, you will be far more successful.”

When his dogs scatter a flock, Watkins immediately tries to determine the group’s composition—hens, gobblers, or a family flock—as that will dictate his calling approach. “If I’ve scattered turkeys, I start with jenny hen kees and kee-kee runs, provided it’s a family flock,” he said. “As soon as first bird answers, I mimic that bird. A young gobbler is not coming to a jenny hen normally, so if a young gobbler kees and gobbler yelps, that’s what I do, even to the point of going note for note. As the bird approaches, I soften the calling, but I do not quit, or they will walk away. They can hear other birds calling that I can’t.”

Depending on the flock type, reassembling turkeys might present a shot opportunity within 20 or 30 minutes. However, adult hens and toms often take longer to get back together, so Watkins uses a different approach after flushing gangs of gobblers or jake-and-a-halfs. “We usually wait 30 minutes or more and start with gobbler yelping and clucking,” he said. “If we get no response in an hour or more, we will mix in gobbling. We start slow, only making a call or two every 10 or more minutes. After no response for that hour or more, we get a little more aggressive.”

That patient yet observant mindset can pay off, as it’s often unclear how birds will react. “I’ve broken gobblers one day and sat three hours, heard one cluck and then shot him,” Watkins said. “The very next day, I’ve broken a different flock, and they started gobbling in an hour and gobbled nonstop until we killed one. You just never know what they will do.”

When hunting alone, or in situations when you can’t scatter turkey flocks, calling strategies become more opportunistic. Brett Ladeau, a Vermont hunter and contest caller, often starts fall quests with a basic tactic. “I typically approach a fall hunt by finding roost sites and try to pattern either a family flock or mixed flock,” he said. “To locate birds, I scout much like I do in spring. I like to be close to birds while they are still on the roost, so finding roost areas is an important tactic I use to find fall birds. I scout for roosting areas and mark them on my mapping app for future hunts. If possible, I also like to watch or hear birds fly to the roost in the evening so I know where they will be in the morning.”

If a roost hunt doesn’t pan out, Ladeau tries to locate turkeys via calling, often taking a mobile approach. “I use my spring technique of riding from spot to spot where I know there are birds, and once I find a few spots that hold birds, I will cold-call at a few well-known turkey haunts,” he said.

And as with hunting scattered groups, Ladeau tries to match his calling to the vocalizations of responsive birds. “If I’m hunting a family group or mixed group, I will use the kee-kee a lot, along with assembly yelps and other calls,” he said. “If I’m hunting a mixed flock or a group of male birds, I will gobble- and jake-yelp to try to entice them into range. My setup is to try to get as close to the birds as possible and then start my calling sequence. If I’m close to a roost, I might not call much, especially if I know their routine, but if not, I call pretty aggressively.”

Get to know the flocks of autumn this year, and fine-tune your calling to bring them into shooting range. Ducks and big bucks can wait a bit as you enjoy the sights and sounds of the fall turkey woods.

Breakthrough cosmic hydrogen measurement could transform our view of the Universe

Advances in computing and astronomy have led to increasingly complex attempts to chart the large-scale structure of the Universe. For the past decade, scientists have been working to map one aspect in particular to see whether it matches observations of the cosmic web: cosmic hydrogen. For the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, neutral hydrogen was the only element in the Universe (known as the "Cosmic Dark Ages") and provided the feedstock for stars, galaxies, and the cosmic web.

Today, it remains the most abundant element in the Universe and can be found everywhere - the interstellar medium (ISM) and the intergalactic medium (IGM) - in abundance. For the past decade, scientists with the MeerKLASS Collaboration have been trying to measure how neutral hydrogen "clumps" in the distant Universe. In a study published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS), the team announced the first direct measurement of the large-scale structure of cosmic hydrogen.

The MeerKLASS (MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey) Collaboration is an international group of astronomers conducting cosmological surveys using the MeerKAT radio telescope. Their survey relies on a technique known as HI intensity (or 21-cm intensity) mapping to detect the combined emissions of galaxies within large segments of the sky. This enables the creation of 3D maps of the cosmos that show the distribution of matter (gas, dust, stars, galaxies, clusters, etc.) in the cosmic web.

Members of the MeerKLASS collaboration at their biannual 'Busy Week' meeting, hosted at the University of Torino in September 2025. Credit: SARAO

This is no easy task, since the 21-cm signal must be extracted from all the other emissions of the Universe (the "foreground noise"), which are about 10,000 times stronger than the hydrogen signal itself. To accomplish this, the team chose to operate each dish in the array as an independent telescope, rather than combining all 64 dishes into a single interferometer. This was an entirely new observation mode for MeerKAT that traded sharp angular resolution for very high survey speed.

For their survey, the team analyzed data collected by the MeerKAT array across ~250 square degrees of sky. This enabled the detection of a neutral hydrogen signal that originated 4.5 billion years ago (or 9 billion years after the Big Bang). The team's measurements produced many independent maps from different dishes and observing times. These were then cross-compared to suppress individual instrument effects and reinforce the structure of cosmic hydrogen.

Their results showed that cosmic hydrogen mirrors the large-scale structure of the cosmic web, advancing a technique that could transform how cosmologists map the Universe. Dr Steven Cunnington, a Senior Research Fellow at Purdue University's Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation, led the development of the analysis pipeline that enabled the mapping of cosmic hydrogen. Since 2015, he has also co-led one of the working groups within the MeerKLASS Collaboration.

About 416,000 years ago, the ground beneath Greenland’s Camp Century was exposed to the sky. A new University at Buffalo-led study estimates that summers there were 4.7 degrees Celsius (8.5 degrees Fahrenheit) warmer than today, offering a rare glimpse of the conditions that accompanied a major retreat of the ice sheet.

“It’s striking that a period of moderately warmer temperatures coincided with such a substantial retreat of the ice sheet,” says lead author John Michael Aguilar, a postdoctoral fellow at the UB RENEW Institute.

Published in Climate of the Past, the research also points to a crucial factor in Greenland’s future: how long higher temperatures persist.

Camp Century housed the U.S. Army’s failed Project Iceworm, a secret plan to hide nuclear missiles beneath Greenland’s ice. During drilling in the 1960s, researchers recovered sediment from nearly a mile (1.6 kilometers) below the surface. Those samples have since become an unlikely archive of Greenland’s climate.

Earlier work showed that the sediment last saw sunlight approximately 416,000 years ago. Exposing the ground at Camp Century would have required the ice sheet’s edge in that region to retreat more than 60 miles (97 kilometers) from its present position.

The new study goes beyond establishing that the ice disappeared. By examining fatty molecules produced by bacteria and preserved in the sediment, the team reconstructed the summer temperatures that accompanied that retreat.

“The types and proportions of these molecules change depending on the temperature,” Aguilar says. “By comparing them with similar molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, we were able to estimate how warm it was at Camp Century.”

The researchers believe their findings provide the first quantitative estimate of summer temperatures on land anywhere in Greenland during this ancient warm period. Such records are exceptionally difficult to recover because later advances of the ice sheet eroded most sediment from that time.

Surprisingly, Camp Century remained covered by ice even when Greenland experienced similarly warm summers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 years ago, after the last Ice Age.

One possible explanation is that the earlier warm period lasted much longer, potentially tens of thousands of years. Sustained warmth would have given the ice sheet more time to melt, suggesting that peak temperatures alone cannot explain how far it retreats.

“This work gives us a better understanding of how the ice sheet responds to sustained warming and what may be possible in the future,” says corresponding author Elizabeth Thomas, PhD, an associate professor in the Department of Earth Sciences in UB’s College of Arts and Sciences.

The findings arrive as scientists anticipate substantial Arctic warming. Depending on emissions, average annual Arctic temperatures could rise by 3.3 to 10 degrees Celsius (5.9 to 18 degrees Fahrenheit) by the end of this century, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

With that warming ahead, Thomas cautions against assuming that Greenland’s ice will respond as slowly as it did during the ancient warm period.

“Today’s human-caused warming is more intense than the warm period 400,000 years ago,” Thomas says. “And climate projections suggest that this warmth could persist for hundreds or thousands of years. Both the magnitude and duration of warming are important to understanding how the ice sheet will respond.”

The samples nearly slipped out of scientific view. Chester Langway, who led the Camp Century ice core drilling, brought them to UB in 1975. They remained in its Central Ice Core Storage Facility and Information Exchange until his retirement in the mid-1990s, then spent more than two decades largely forgotten at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark.

A chance freezer cleanout in 2018 brought the sediment back to researchers’ attention. Thomas has studied it with University of Vermont collaborators ever since, and its remarkable history became the subject of an Emmy Award-winning documentary.

For Thomas, the latest study brings years of work with Aguilar together. Measuring the molecules was familiar territory; translating them into a reliable comparison with modern temperatures was much harder.

“The type of molecular analysis we did in this study is something we do all the time in my lab, but interpreting the results was unusually challenging. We had to account for how the land’s elevation changed as the ice sheet grew and melted, which affects how temperatures can be compared across time,” Thomas says. “What may look like a routine analysis actually took years to figure out. It’s been quite a journey, not unlike the journey these sediment samples took to get here.”

Deep ocean oxygen kept rising and falling long after animals appeared

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Geologists found deep ocean oxygen built up and vanished again for over 100 million years after the Cambrian, complicating how animals evolved.

Researchers often explain the Cambrian Explosion, the burst of animal life half a billion years ago, with a lasting rise in ocean oxygen. In the deep ocean, though, the oxygen didn’t last..

Geologists studied mud that settled on the seafloor between about 485 million and 380 million years ago and later hardened into rock.

They found oxygen near the bottom building up for short stretches and then disappearing again. So animals may have evolved under less deep-sea oxygen than researchers once assumed.

Chadlin Ostrander, an assistant professor in the Geology & Geophysics department at the University of Utah, led the study. His team found no evidence of sustained oxygen in the deep ocean during that span.

The team concluded that oxygen didn’t settle into the deepest water for good until sometime after roughly 380 million years ago.

“Just a decade ago, it was thought that a deep ocean oxygenation switch was flipped around 540 million years ago. Our new dataset pushes that forward in time by at least ~160 million years,” he said.

The team didn’t measure oxygen directly. Instead, it read a chemical record in the rock that follows oxygen indirectly.

Researchers have invoked oxygen in the deep ocean to explain nearly every major step in early animal history. That includes the first animals and the Cambrian Explosion. It also includes a second burst of marine diversity from about 485 to 444 million years ago.

Other scientists have raised serious counterarguments to each. A common objection is that oxygen may have risen during these events, but not high enough, or for long enough, to fill the deep ocean.

Visitors to the Grand Canyon can see some of that early history. Its walls include about 1,600 feet (500 meters) of rock that settled in a Cambrian sea.

Ostrander’s samples start right after the Cambrian ended, about 485 million years ago, so the team didn’t sample that sea.

Geologists find tiny amounts of thallium, a heavy metal, in ordinary rock. It comes in two forms that differ by a hair in weight.

When a manganese mineral settles into seafloor mud, it pulls more of the heavier form out of seawater. That mineral survives in mud only where oxygen is steadily present in the water above it, and a few days without oxygen dissolve it again.

Mud formed in oxygen-poor places keeps a sample of the surrounding seawater, including its mix of the two forms. The team read a lower value, meaning more of the lighter form, as a sign that more seafloor lay under oxygen-rich water.

The team measured 82 shale samples from the Peel River in Canada’s Yukon. The river has cut into the rock, exposing fresh layers that stayed largely unweathered in the cold.

Erik Sperling of Stanford University and Justin Strauss of Dartmouth College led the expeditions that collected the Yukon mud.

Another 44 samples came from sites including Latvia, Libya, Wales and Montana.

For most of the record, the team found the same thallium mix as in ordinary continental rock. A seafloor with little oxygen above it should leave exactly that. In Yukon mud that formed between about 405 million and 386 million years ago, the readings dropped.

The lowest value came from a Marcellus Shale sample from the Appalachian Basin that formed about 388 million years ago. By the team’s calculations, that value means oxygen-rich water covered much more than 30% of the seafloor, well past the continental shelves and into the deep ocean.

Readings from the peak of the last ice age look similar, with surface oxygen reaching the deep ocean only slightly less effectively than it does now.

That high-oxygen stretch didn’t last. By about 378 million years ago, the readings returned to ordinary values and stayed there to the top of the rock. That could mean more of the deep ocean had lost its oxygen again.

Local conditions could have faked the dip, so the team checked. The 11 Yukon samples from the dip had the same chemistry as the other Yukon samples, and the Marcellus Shale, from a separate basin, dipped the same way.

By the time of those samples, Ostrander said, marine life was already large and doing energy-expensive work.

“You have some phenomenal changes in evolutionary biology happening during this time. And none of it seems to require substantial and sustained deep ocean oxygenation,” he said.

Animal life was changing fast before that window opened.

Paleontologists working at a fossil site in southwest China have found that many animal groups already existed before the Cambrian began. Researchers who analyzed fossil trails from about 545 million years ago reached a similar conclusion.

In rock about 520 million years old, researchers have found colonies of tiny filter-feeding animals and an early relative of octopuses and squid.

The deep ocean has had steady oxygen since then. The team can’t say when that began, only that it came after about 380 million years ago. Ostrander called that the study’s one disappointment.

Gaps remain. The team has only one rock sample for roughly every 450,000 years, so the real swings may have been even larger than the mud shows.

The team also couldn’t fully rule out that thallium cycled through seawater too fast for the mud to reflect the whole ocean. Its models and the match between basins make that unlikely.

The researchers wrote that long, closely spaced oxygen records from the late Paleozoic and the Mesozoic are still needed to learn when the deep ocean filled with oxygen for good.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
