Voyager 1 went silent in deep space right before reaching a historic milestone of one ‘light day’ away from Earth

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA briefly lost contact with Voyager 1 in October 2026. The signal returned just weeks before the spacecraft reaches a historic milestone.

On Oct. 18, 2026, the antennas of NASA’s Deep Space Network tried to pick up Voyager 1 during a scheduled contact and found nothing. A second attempt 24 hours later also failed.

The signal came back on Oct. 20, and the team at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California has kept in touch with the probe since then.

NASA schedules Nov. 18 as the day Voyager 1, already the farthest human-made object, reaches one light-day from Earth, according to JPL. They had not previously said what caused the October problems.

The Deep Space Network has large antennas at Goldstone in California, near Madrid in Spain and near Canberra in Australia. The largest dish at each site measures about 230 feet (70 meters) across.

Those antennas handled the two contact attempts in October. Mission controllers logged no detectable signal on Oct. 18 or Oct. 19.

Kareem Badaruddin, the Voyager mission manager at JPL, said the episode was new to his team. “This is the first time we’ve seen the spacecraft do something like this,” he said in the JPL statement.

He also said the mission has reached a stage where the team has “to expect the unexpected.” The probe has surprised its operators before, including the time it began talking through a radio system not used since 1981.

After contact returned, the team worked out that one group of thrusters on the spacecraft had gone offline and that a backup group had turned on.

The onboard computers carry instructions to make that swap by themselves when they detect a problem with the active thrusters.

JPL calls this kind of automatic reaction fault protection. It puts the probe in a safe, stable state and pauses normal operations so that engineers on the ground can study the problem.

In March 2025, engineers revived a set of roll thrusters that had stopped working in 2004.

Early in September, about six weeks before the probe dropped out of contact, JPL engineers sent the commands that turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and turned on three lower-power substitutes.

Inside the team, the job goes by the name Big Bang. The devices double as a source of warmth for the spacecraft, so JPL could not do them one at a time and all three changed at once.

Voyager 2 got the treatment first, and NASA reported on Aug. 4, 2026 that the swap there should keep three instruments powered for at least one more year. An instrument on Voyager 2 would otherwise have gone dark before the end of 2026.

Nothing in the Oct. 29 note ties the September commands to the thrusters.

Both probes run on radioisotope generators, which turn the heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. The output on each probe falls by about 4 watts a year and, with margins now thin, JPL has been shutting off whatever is not essential.

Ten science instruments went up on Voyager 1 in 1977. A NASA status page updated in April lists only two still running on it, the magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem.

Controllers switched off the cosmic ray subsystem on Feb. 25, 2025, in one of a series of shutdowns meant to stretch the remaining power. The low-energy charged particle instrument was next, on April 17, 2026.

Without the Big Bang swap, another Voyager 1 instrument would likely have been turned off as early as summer 2027, JPL said.

Voyager 1 launched on Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after its twin, passed Jupiter and Saturn, and entered interstellar space in August 2012. It is moving at about 38,000 miles per hour (61,155 kilometers per hour).

NASA’s light-day page sets the crossing on Nov. 18, when the probe will sit about 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) from Earth.

Light covers the distance to the Moon in 1.3 seconds. Artemis II astronauts needed about three days for the same trip.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory explains how far away Voyager 1 is when it reaches one light-day from Earth. Credit: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Voyager 2 took a different route so it could visit Uranus and Neptune, and NASA expects it to reach the same distance in November 2035. The nearest star system, Proxima Centauri, lies about 4.24 light-years away.

Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd described what the distance does to a conversation. “If you want to say ‘hello’ to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response,” she said in the JPL statement.

JPL’s Oct. 29 note does not say what knocked the thrusters offline, and engineers are still investigating. It also does not confirm that the thruster switch was what interrupted the signal.

The note does not name the three devices replaced in September or give the amount of power saved. It also says nothing about when the mission might end, although the team expects more surprises before summer 2027.

The light-day crossing itself will change nothing on board or around the spacecraft, JPL said. Its value lies in showing the length of the mission and the size of the distances involved.

At one light-day, a command sent from Goldstone, Madrid or Canberra needs a full day to reach Voyager 1. The answer needs another.

The information was obtained from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Disney's Legendary 218-Episode Sci-Fi Series Is the Perfect Anti-'Lanterns' Binge

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

All 218 episodes of this classic sci-fi mystery series are available to stream for free

The fate of Lanterns season 2, and the DC Universe in general, has never been murkier. With the sudden ousting of DC Studios' co-CEOs James Gunn and Peter Safran, the status of HBO's hit show series about two mismatched Green Lanterns who get paired together to solve an intergalactic mystery is up in the air like never before. Season 1 was a critical hit that captured the zeitgeist, and Lanterns co-creator Chris Mundy had already assembled a season 2 writers' room. But in a recent interview, Mundy said the odds of the series continuing were likely "50/50.”

That's not great, but thankfully, there's a perfect replacement waiting in the wings that flips the Lanterns formula on its head — and it's got an almost unlimited supply of episodes. That show is The X-Files, and even though it also features aliens and a pair of odd-couple cops, it couldn’t be more different.

Lanterns was a bold new take on DC Comics’ various Green Lantern heroes. While the character has most often been associated with grand, operatic space adventures, Lanterns was a grittier, more grounded affair. Hal Jordan (Kyle Chandler) is the cocky, go-with-your-gut veteran superhero who was selected to be Earth’s first Green Lantern, part of a universal protection force whose members use powerful green rings that can generate pretty much whatever the wearer can imagine out of green light.

Tired of Jordan’s rule-breaking behavior, the Guardians — the alien beings who oversee the Green Lantern Corps — select serious-minded ex-Marine John Stewart (Aaron Pierre) to become Hal’s partner, which is really just a ruse to have Stewart replace Jordan. Together, the two investigate a deadly incident in the small town of Rushville, Nebraska.

The X-Files also starts out with a bit of a ruse. The show is about a pair of FBI agents who investigate cases involving paranormal phenomena. The cases are referred to as “the X-Files” and they’ve become the obsession of Agent Fox Mulder (David Duchovny), who has a strong belief in aliens and other supernatural elements. Prior to the events of the series, Mulder had been a hotshot criminal profiler in the FBI, and the higher–ups at the agency are now looking to bring him back into the fold by giving him a partner who will debunk his work on the X-Files. They select Agent Dana Scully (Gillian Anderson), a trained medical doctor who believes in hard science, and is wildly skeptical about the wilder elements of Mulder's work.

For both Lanterns and The X-Files — and pretty much all media about mismatched cops — the two partners gain a begrudging respect for each other. In Lanterns, Jordan and Stewart come to admire one another's bravery and sense of honor. On The X-Files, Mulder learns to appreciate the tough questions Scully asks, while Scully comes to realize that Mulder’s theories and beliefs aren’t quite as wild as she’d originally thought. Mulder and Scully also develop a romantic relationship over time, but that takes several seasons to come to fruition and it’s done really organically.

While I realize that I’m pointing out a lot of the similarities between the two shows, they have vastly different viewing experiences. Lanterns is just eight episodes and they’re all dedicated to one case. In contrast, The X-Files ran for 11 seasons, totaling 218 episodes. While the show has a central mythology that extends through the series, the vast majority of episodes are procedural, with new standalone cases every week. The series also covers a wide variety of subject matter. Sometimes it’s about aliens, sometimes it’s about ghosts or urban legends or elements of the occult. There’s even an episode where Mulder is seduced by a vampire. (I recommend you skip that one, since it’s pretty terrible.)

The X-Files also has a very different tone. While Lanterns has a moodiness to it inspired by the likes of True Detective, The X-Files had a lot of variety. The show can be deadly serious in one episode, then lean into more of an action/thriller vibe for the next one. Some episodes are even quite funny. The genre-hopping all works really well too, thanks to the chemistry between Duchovny and Anderson. As wild, or as heavy, as the show gets, their partnership is the show’s anchor and that’s why the show lasted as long as it did and why it became a monster success in the 1990s.

In a way, The X-Files is kind of an inverted mirror of Lanterns. With The X-Files, the FBI agents are the most grounded thing about the show, while the cases themselves were more outlandish. In Lanterns, the Green Lanterns and their power set is the most fantastic element, whereas the case is somewhat more grounded. Even with the Manhunter component, Lanterns is interestingly low to the ground.

Plus, there are no power rings in The X-Files, which is a pretty big thing in Lanterns.

The X-Files is available to stream on Pluto TV, Hulu, and Disney Plus.

Expert Tips for Scouting, Scattering, and Tagging Fall Turkeys

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Fall turkey hunting requires matching your strategy to the specific flock type you're pursuing. Adult gobblers, hen-poult family groups, and second-year jakes all behave and vocalize differently. The spring yelp that pulled in a gobbler won't work in October. Identify the birds first, then dial in your calls and approach accordingly.

Turkeys typically only receive passing mentions when folks discuss their favorite fall pursuits. And with spring being a distant memory and other seasons in full swing, that’s understandable. And unfortunate. Fall turkeys offer a sporting experience unlike any other outdoors adventure. And with more than 40 states offering autumn turkey seasons—some of which open in September and others that run deep into winter—opportunities abound.

But the fall game plays out somewhat differently than the familiar spring ritual. Here’s a quick look at the types of turkeys you’ll likely encounter in the autumn woods and some tips on how to bring one home.

Many hunters kill fall turkeys incidentally while pursuing deer or small game, sometimes encountering flocks at harvested crop fields or mast-rich wooded areas. There’s nothing wrong with that, but true students of the autumn game dig far deeper into turkey behavior and learn to interact with each type of fall bird.

During fall, turkeys are in different modes than in spring, as they’re no longer concerned with breeding. Food and the struggle for survival dominate their existence. They typically fall into several distinct flock types: adult gobblers, hens without poults, and hens with broods of poults. Several subcategories also exist: Male birds in their second autumn—those that were jakes the previous spring—group together. These are sometimes called jake-and-a-halfs. Also, hen-poult groups often combine into larger family flocks, called crèches. The various groups sometimes intermingle, especially at prime feeding areas or large common roost sites. Often, however, you’ll encounter them separately.

As with spring, calling can be critical in autumn for finding turkeys and getting them close. However, turkeys—especially hens and poults—can be much more vocal in fall, and their lexicon expands to include sounds many spring hunters don’t hear much. Roost sites that attract family flocks can sound like a din of turkey chatter at dawn. Young turkeys often utter kee-kees (a series of three to four ascending whistles) and kee-kee runs, which are whistling series followed by a crude yelp or two. The vocalizations might sound slightly different depending on whether it’s uttered by a jake or jenny. Brood hens use long, pleading strings of assembly yelps to gather their offspring after being separated. Gobblers are typically less vocal in fall, communicating via slow, raspy gobbler yelps and popping clucks. Still, it’s not uncommon to hear them gobble at times.

Because the various types of turkeys act and vocalize differently in fall, general hunting approaches can fall flat. For example, the straightforward hen yelps that made a gobbler respond in April will likely be met with indifference from male turkeys in October. To boost your chances, you must know the kind of bird you’re hunting and then fine-tune your strategy.

Generally, fall hunts fall under two broad categories: 1) Hunters who call in an attempt to make turkeys come to them. 2) Hunters, or specially trained turkey dogs, who scatter groups of turkeys, then set up and try to call the birds back. Knowing the type of turkey you’re after is especially critical during scatter scenarios.

“You need to be the turkey that calls to you,” said Marlin Watkins, a noted Ohio call maker and expert fall turkey hunter. “A young gobbler is not coming to hen sounds. You need to be that bird, whether it’s a jenny hen, young gobbler, or adult hen. And if you produce those sounds, you will be far more successful.”

When his dogs scatter a flock, Watkins immediately tries to determine the group’s composition—hens, gobblers, or a family flock—as that will dictate his calling approach. “If I’ve scattered turkeys, I start with jenny hen kees and kee-kee runs, provided it’s a family flock,” he said. “As soon as first bird answers, I mimic that bird. A young gobbler is not coming to a jenny hen normally, so if a young gobbler kees and gobbler yelps, that’s what I do, even to the point of going note for note. As the bird approaches, I soften the calling, but I do not quit, or they will walk away. They can hear other birds calling that I can’t.”

Depending on the flock type, reassembling turkeys might present a shot opportunity within 20 or 30 minutes. However, adult hens and toms often take longer to get back together, so Watkins uses a different approach after flushing gangs of gobblers or jake-and-a-halfs. “We usually wait 30 minutes or more and start with gobbler yelping and clucking,” he said. “If we get no response in an hour or more, we will mix in gobbling. We start slow, only making a call or two every 10 or more minutes. After no response for that hour or more, we get a little more aggressive.”

That patient yet observant mindset can pay off, as it’s often unclear how birds will react. “I’ve broken gobblers one day and sat three hours, heard one cluck and then shot him,” Watkins said. “The very next day, I’ve broken a different flock, and they started gobbling in an hour and gobbled nonstop until we killed one. You just never know what they will do.”

When hunting alone, or in situations when you can’t scatter turkey flocks, calling strategies become more opportunistic. Brett Ladeau, a Vermont hunter and contest caller, often starts fall quests with a basic tactic. “I typically approach a fall hunt by finding roost sites and try to pattern either a family flock or mixed flock,” he said. “To locate birds, I scout much like I do in spring. I like to be close to birds while they are still on the roost, so finding roost areas is an important tactic I use to find fall birds. I scout for roosting areas and mark them on my mapping app for future hunts. If possible, I also like to watch or hear birds fly to the roost in the evening so I know where they will be in the morning.”

If a roost hunt doesn’t pan out, Ladeau tries to locate turkeys via calling, often taking a mobile approach. “I use my spring technique of riding from spot to spot where I know there are birds, and once I find a few spots that hold birds, I will cold-call at a few well-known turkey haunts,” he said.

And as with hunting scattered groups, Ladeau tries to match his calling to the vocalizations of responsive birds. “If I’m hunting a family group or mixed group, I will use the kee-kee a lot, along with assembly yelps and other calls,” he said. “If I’m hunting a mixed flock or a group of male birds, I will gobble- and jake-yelp to try to entice them into range. My setup is to try to get as close to the birds as possible and then start my calling sequence. If I’m close to a roost, I might not call much, especially if I know their routine, but if not, I call pretty aggressively.”

Get to know the flocks of autumn this year, and fine-tune your calling to bring them into shooting range. Ducks and big bucks can wait a bit as you enjoy the sights and sounds of the fall turkey woods.

Breakthrough cosmic hydrogen measurement could transform our view of the Universe

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Advances in computing and astronomy have led to increasingly complex attempts to chart the large-scale structure of the Universe. For the past decade, scientists have been working to map one aspect in particular to see whether it matches observations of the cosmic web: cosmic hydrogen. For the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, neutral hydrogen was the only element in the Universe (known as the "Cosmic Dark Ages") and provided the feedstock for stars, galaxies, and the cosmic web.

Today, it remains the most abundant element in the Universe and can be found everywhere - the interstellar medium (ISM) and the intergalactic medium (IGM) - in abundance. For the past decade, scientists with the MeerKLASS Collaboration have been trying to measure how neutral hydrogen "clumps" in the distant Universe. In a study published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS), the team announced the first direct measurement of the large-scale structure of cosmic hydrogen.

The MeerKLASS (MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey) Collaboration is an international group of astronomers conducting cosmological surveys using the MeerKAT radio telescope. Their survey relies on a technique known as HI intensity (or 21-cm intensity) mapping to detect the combined emissions of galaxies within large segments of the sky. This enables the creation of 3D maps of the cosmos that show the distribution of matter (gas, dust, stars, galaxies, clusters, etc.) in the cosmic web.

Members of the MeerKLASS collaboration at their biannual 'Busy Week' meeting, hosted at the University of Torino in September 2025. Credit: SARAO

This is no easy task, since the 21-cm signal must be extracted from all the other emissions of the Universe (the "foreground noise"), which are about 10,000 times stronger than the hydrogen signal itself. To accomplish this, the team chose to operate each dish in the array as an independent telescope, rather than combining all 64 dishes into a single interferometer. This was an entirely new observation mode for MeerKAT that traded sharp angular resolution for very high survey speed.

For their survey, the team analyzed data collected by the MeerKAT array across ~250 square degrees of sky. This enabled the detection of a neutral hydrogen signal that originated 4.5 billion years ago (or 9 billion years after the Big Bang). The team's measurements produced many independent maps from different dishes and observing times. These were then cross-compared to suppress individual instrument effects and reinforce the structure of cosmic hydrogen.

Their results showed that cosmic hydrogen mirrors the large-scale structure of the cosmic web, advancing a technique that could transform how cosmologists map the Universe. Dr Steven Cunnington, a Senior Research Fellow at Purdue University's Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation, led the development of the analysis pipeline that enabled the mapping of cosmic hydrogen. Since 2015, he has also co-led one of the working groups within the MeerKLASS Collaboration.

As he expressed in a University of Portsmouth release:

This result feels particularly special because it's the culmination of a journey that began when I started my PhD at Portsmouth over a decade ago. I am now fortunate to be part of a much wider international collaboration sharing this common goal. Many of the challenges addressed in this work have shaped my own research career, so seeing our team deliver this measurement is a remarkable milestone to witness.

This slice of the DESI data maps celestial objects from Earth (center) to billions of light-years away, showing nearby bright galaxies (yellow), luminous red galaxies (orange), emission-line galaxies (blue), and quasars (green). Credit: DESI Collaboration/DOE/KPNO/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA/C. Lamman

NASA Is Accepting Astronaut Applications for the First Time in 2 Years

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

If you have the right stuff, you could be on the next moon mission or end up on Mars.

This type of job posting happens once in a blue moon.  If you’ve always dreamed of becoming an astronaut, NASA just made space for those who meet its qualifications. On Saturday, the agency announced that it’s seeking the next class of astronauts who will journey to the moon, build the planned 2032 Moon Base and help create the systems that will enable the first Mars mission.

NASA last accepted applications in 2024. Over 8,000 people applied, but only 10 astronaut candidates were chosen. Since the 1960s, 370 people have been selected to train as astronaut candidates, and just 32 are currently in NASA’s astronaut corps.

If you want to journey into space with NASA, you have to be a US citizen, ideally with a master’s degree in a STEM field.

In addition to completing an application package that includes a long-form resume and five references, you will also have to pass NASA’s long-duration astronaut flight physical and show strong skills in communication, teamwork and leadership.

If you don’t have a master’s degree, you can meet that requirement with at least two years of work toward a doctoral program in a STEM field, completion of a medical degree or completion or enrollment in a nationally recognized Test Pilot School program.

You also must have at least three years of related professional experience. If Test Pilot School is your only advanced technical degree, you must also have a bachelor’s degree in a STEM field.

If you’re in residency training as a medical doctor, that could count toward the professional experience requirement, but training would have to be complete by July 2027.

If you’re a pilot, you could meet the professional experience requirement with 750 hours of pilot-in-command time in a high-performance jet aircraft; 1,500 total flight hours, with 750 pilot-in-command hours, in other fixed-wing aircraft; or 1,000 total flight hours, with 300 pilot-in-command hours, in a rotary-wing aircraft.

Yes, there is a job posting for becoming an astronaut. USAJOBS, the federal government’s official employment website, states that you’ll be reporting for duty at the Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center in Houston.

It also pays to become an astronaut: $164,395 per year.

Unsurprisingly, 76% or more travel is required — or what NASA calls “extensive travel.” Kind of the point of the job, though, no?

If you meet NASA’s requirements, you have until Dec. 3 at 11:59 p.m. ET to apply.

If accepted, you’ll spend about two years in training, learning robotics, leadership, lunar geology, spacewalking and more.

After completing training, you’ll become eligible for a spaceflight assignment. Until then, you’ll be an active astronaut corps member with astronaut office responsibilities. That’s more exciting than it sounds, as it could involve supporting fellow astronauts in space or helping develop a new spacecraft.

Democrats won big. Can they lower your grocery bill?

A supporter holds a sign during a campaign rally for Democratic Senate candidate Dr. Annie Andrews in Myrtle Beach, S.C., on Nov. 1. Andrews defeated her Republican opponent, Sen. Darline Graham. Bill Clark/CQ-Roll Call, Inc via Getty Images hide caption

It's been a feature of campaign ads and stump speeches for months: grocery prices are out of control.

Affordability concerns, including high food costs, were top of mind for voters heading into the midterms, as the Trump administration's tariffs and the war in Iran continued to drive up prices. That economic strain helped Democrats win control of both houses of Congress on Tuesday.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries said the incoming majority would make tackling high prices a priority come January. "It was a tremendous victory for the American people and a strong rebuke of MAGA extremism, which has made life more expensive for everyday Americans despite their promises to lower the high cost of living on day one," he told NPR on Friday.

The question is how Democrats will do that with President Trump still in the Oval Office, willing to wield his veto pen. Senate Democrats won't have a filibuster-proof majority, and Trump may have little appetite to roll back his signature tariffs or reverse course on the Iran war, two of the main causes of high food prices.

Those tariffs have not only raised the cost of certain foods and drinks imported to the U.S., but also created other pressures on the food industry by making it more expensive to import things like machine parts and packaging.

"I think the president would certainly want to find ways to reduce the cost of groceries. Democrats [also] see that in their interest," said Lehigh University political science professor Chris Borick. Several incoming Democratic senators also floated other proposals during their campaigns that aim to make groceries more affordable.

James Talarico of Texas said he would push back against "attacks" on the federal food assistance programs WIC and SNAP, the latter of which is undergoing a major overhaul by the Trump administration and has seen a precipitous drop in participation since last summer. Kansas' Adam Hamilton announced his support for a national ban on "dynamic pricing" in grocery stores. Incoming state governors have floated their own ideas for how to give residents relief in the grocery aisle.

In New Mexico, Deb Haaland said the state would explore a policy similar to one in Albuquerque that temporarily waives permitting fees for markets that sell healthy and affordable food in so-called "food deserts," the Albuquerque Journal reported.

However state and federal officials decide to go about lowering prices, Americans say they want to see cheaper groceries soon. According to a poll released by the Kitchen Table Project and Global Strategy Group in September, nearly half of respondents said their most important goal was seeing their grocery bills lowered within six months.

Scientists Discover Greenland’s Ice Sheet Once Retreated 60 Miles

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Sediment beneath a secret Cold War base reveals a time when warmer summers helped push Greenland’s ice more than 60 miles back from its current edge.

About 416,000 years ago, the ground beneath Greenland’s Camp Century was exposed to the sky. A new University at Buffalo-led study estimates that summers there were 4.7 degrees Celsius (8.5 degrees Fahrenheit) warmer than today, offering a rare glimpse of the conditions that accompanied a major retreat of the ice sheet.

“It’s striking that a period of moderately warmer temperatures coincided with such a substantial retreat of the ice sheet,” says lead author John Michael Aguilar, a postdoctoral fellow at the UB RENEW Institute.

Published in Climate of the Past, the research also points to a crucial factor in Greenland’s future: how long higher temperatures persist.

Camp Century housed the U.S. Army’s failed Project Iceworm, a secret plan to hide nuclear missiles beneath Greenland’s ice. During drilling in the 1960s, researchers recovered sediment from nearly a mile (1.6 kilometers) below the surface. Those samples have since become an unlikely archive of Greenland’s climate.

Earlier work showed that the sediment last saw sunlight approximately 416,000 years ago. Exposing the ground at Camp Century would have required the ice sheet’s edge in that region to retreat more than 60 miles (97 kilometers) from its present position.

The new study goes beyond establishing that the ice disappeared. By examining fatty molecules produced by bacteria and preserved in the sediment, the team reconstructed the summer temperatures that accompanied that retreat.

“The types and proportions of these molecules change depending on the temperature,” Aguilar says. “By comparing them with similar molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, we were able to estimate how warm it was at Camp Century.”

The researchers believe their findings provide the first quantitative estimate of summer temperatures on land anywhere in Greenland during this ancient warm period. Such records are exceptionally difficult to recover because later advances of the ice sheet eroded most sediment from that time.

Surprisingly, Camp Century remained covered by ice even when Greenland experienced similarly warm summers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 years ago, after the last Ice Age.

One possible explanation is that the earlier warm period lasted much longer, potentially tens of thousands of years. Sustained warmth would have given the ice sheet more time to melt, suggesting that peak temperatures alone cannot explain how far it retreats.

“This work gives us a better understanding of how the ice sheet responds to sustained warming and what may be possible in the future,” says corresponding author Elizabeth Thomas, PhD, an associate professor in the Department of Earth Sciences in UB’s College of Arts and Sciences.

The findings arrive as scientists anticipate substantial Arctic warming. Depending on emissions, average annual Arctic temperatures could rise by 3.3 to 10 degrees Celsius (5.9 to 18 degrees Fahrenheit) by the end of this century, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

With that warming ahead, Thomas cautions against assuming that Greenland’s ice will respond as slowly as it did during the ancient warm period.

“Today’s human-caused warming is more intense than the warm period 400,000 years ago,” Thomas says. “And climate projections suggest that this warmth could persist for hundreds or thousands of years. Both the magnitude and duration of warming are important to understanding how the ice sheet will respond.”

The samples nearly slipped out of scientific view. Chester Langway, who led the Camp Century ice core drilling, brought them to UB in 1975. They remained in its Central Ice Core Storage Facility and Information Exchange until his retirement in the mid-1990s, then spent more than two decades largely forgotten at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark.

A chance freezer cleanout in 2018 brought the sediment back to researchers’ attention. Thomas has studied it with University of Vermont collaborators ever since, and its remarkable history became the subject of an Emmy Award-winning documentary.

For Thomas, the latest study brings years of work with Aguilar together. Measuring the molecules was familiar territory; translating them into a reliable comparison with modern temperatures was much harder.

“The type of molecular analysis we did in this study is something we do all the time in my lab, but interpreting the results was unusually challenging. We had to account for how the land’s elevation changed as the ice sheet grew and melted, which affects how temperatures can be compared across time,” Thomas says. “What may look like a routine analysis actually took years to figure out. It’s been quite a journey, not unlike the journey these sediment samples took to get here.”

Reference: “Holocene-like summer climate during Marine Isotope Stage 11 in northwestern Greenland” by John Michael N. Aguilar, Elizabeth K. Thomas, Diana S. Aga, Paul R. Bierman, Jason P. Briner, Isla S. Castañeda and Andrew J. Christ, 28 September 2026, Climate of the Past. DOI: 10.5194/cp-22-1757-2026

Because this cult favorite has become a bona fide hit on Netflix.

If voters do take the plunge, they'll find a smart, terrifying series that flirts with the bleakness of Stephen King's legendary horror bummer "The Mist." A lot of people may not have the stomach for that, but Griffin and his writers adroitly plumb the darkness of our era, where days often feel like a purgatorial drag. But "From" keeps us watching because we need to believe there's a way out of this.

Deep ocean oxygen kept rising and falling long after animals appeared

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Geologists found deep ocean oxygen built up and vanished again for over 100 million years after the Cambrian, complicating how animals evolved.

Researchers often explain the Cambrian Explosion, the burst of animal life half a billion years ago, with a lasting rise in ocean oxygen. In the deep ocean, though, the oxygen didn’t last..

Geologists studied mud that settled on the seafloor between about 485 million and 380 million years ago and later hardened into rock.

They found oxygen near the bottom building up for short stretches and then disappearing again. So animals may have evolved under less deep-sea oxygen than researchers once assumed.

Chadlin Ostrander, an assistant professor in the Geology & Geophysics department at the University of Utah, led the study. His team found no evidence of sustained oxygen in the deep ocean during that span.

The team concluded that oxygen didn’t settle into the deepest water for good until sometime after roughly 380 million years ago.

“Just a decade ago, it was thought that a deep ocean oxygenation switch was flipped around 540 million years ago. Our new dataset pushes that forward in time by at least ~160 million years,” he said.

The team didn’t measure oxygen directly. Instead, it read a chemical record in the rock that follows oxygen indirectly.

Researchers have invoked oxygen in the deep ocean to explain nearly every major step in early animal history. That includes the first animals and the Cambrian Explosion. It also includes a second burst of marine diversity from about 485 to 444 million years ago.

Other scientists have raised serious counterarguments to each. A common objection is that oxygen may have risen during these events, but not high enough, or for long enough, to fill the deep ocean.

Visitors to the Grand Canyon can see some of that early history. Its walls include about 1,600 feet (500 meters) of rock that settled in a Cambrian sea.

Ostrander’s samples start right after the Cambrian ended, about 485 million years ago, so the team didn’t sample that sea.

Geologists find tiny amounts of thallium, a heavy metal, in ordinary rock. It comes in two forms that differ by a hair in weight.

When a manganese mineral settles into seafloor mud, it pulls more of the heavier form out of seawater. That mineral survives in mud only where oxygen is steadily present in the water above it, and a few days without oxygen dissolve it again.

Mud formed in oxygen-poor places keeps a sample of the surrounding seawater, including its mix of the two forms. The team read a lower value, meaning more of the lighter form, as a sign that more seafloor lay under oxygen-rich water.

The team measured 82 shale samples from the Peel River in Canada’s Yukon. The river has cut into the rock, exposing fresh layers that stayed largely unweathered in the cold.

Erik Sperling of Stanford University and Justin Strauss of Dartmouth College led the expeditions that collected the Yukon mud.

Another 44 samples came from sites including Latvia, Libya, Wales and Montana.

For most of the record, the team found the same thallium mix as in ordinary continental rock. A seafloor with little oxygen above it should leave exactly that. In Yukon mud that formed between about 405 million and 386 million years ago, the readings dropped.

The lowest value came from a Marcellus Shale sample from the Appalachian Basin that formed about 388 million years ago. By the team’s calculations, that value means oxygen-rich water covered much more than 30% of the seafloor, well past the continental shelves and into the deep ocean.

Readings from the peak of the last ice age look similar, with surface oxygen reaching the deep ocean only slightly less effectively than it does now.

That high-oxygen stretch didn’t last. By about 378 million years ago, the readings returned to ordinary values and stayed there to the top of the rock. That could mean more of the deep ocean had lost its oxygen again.

Local conditions could have faked the dip, so the team checked. The 11 Yukon samples from the dip had the same chemistry as the other Yukon samples, and the Marcellus Shale, from a separate basin, dipped the same way.

By the time of those samples, Ostrander said, marine life was already large and doing energy-expensive work.

“You have some phenomenal changes in evolutionary biology happening during this time. And none of it seems to require substantial and sustained deep ocean oxygenation,” he said.

Animal life was changing fast before that window opened.

Paleontologists working at a fossil site in southwest China have found that many animal groups already existed before the Cambrian began. Researchers who analyzed fossil trails from about 545 million years ago reached a similar conclusion.

In rock about 520 million years old, researchers have found colonies of tiny filter-feeding animals and an early relative of octopuses and squid.

The deep ocean has had steady oxygen since then. The team can’t say when that began, only that it came after about 380 million years ago. Ostrander called that the study’s one disappointment.

Gaps remain. The team has only one rock sample for roughly every 450,000 years, so the real swings may have been even larger than the mud shows.

The team also couldn’t fully rule out that thallium cycled through seawater too fast for the mud to reflect the whole ocean. Its models and the match between basins make that unlikely.

The researchers wrote that long, closely spaced oxygen records from the late Paleozoic and the Mesozoic are still needed to learn when the deep ocean filled with oxygen for good.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
