Dig review: Amy Poehler and Parks and Recs Mike Schur have done it again

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The first time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Parks and Recreation, a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love. The second time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Peacock's new series Dig, which is — you guessed it! — a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love.

Story-wise, the two shows are miles apart. Literally. The former takes place in the decidedly unglamorous town of Pawnee, Indiana, while the latter, based on Kate Myers' novel Excavations, transports us to a sun-bleached archaeological dig in Greece. Yet despite the flashy location and a new mystery twist, Dig retains the core formula that made Parks and Recreation so irresistible: investing deeply in a lovable new crew of unlikely friends.

Kicking off that group is Elise (Poehler), the lead archaeologist on a long-running dig in southern Greece. She's qualified and passionate, yet has a hard time connecting with the undergraduates (or UGs) participating in the dig. Because of her more guarded exterior, she's consistently second fiddle to longtime dig leader Neville (Hugh Laurie). Perpetually on the brink of retirement, Neville seems unable to cede control of the dig to Elise, both because of his love for archaeology and the ego boost his role gives him. He's the Lucy to Elise's Charlie Brown, constantly yanking the football away at the last second.

So, when Elise discovers a perfectly preserved statue of Hera at an older dig site, the last thing she wants to do is tell Neville and give him an opportunity to claim any shred of credit. However, hiding any cultural artifacts from Neville and the Grecian government at large is illegal, so Elise must assemble a crack team to help her cover up her covert dig of the Hera statue, whom they nickname "Pretty Woman." First up is Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), an always flaky wild card who has unfinished business at the dig. Joining her are Clare (Antonia Thomas), a prim and proper conservator born with a silver spoon in her mouth, and Patty (Fina Strazza), a sheltered farm girl with a deep passion for Greek mythology.

Like with most sitcoms, the characters begin as the broadest versions of themselves before revealing their deeper nuances. At first, Elise is prickly to a fault, allowing Poehler to sidestep any preconceived comparisons you might want to draw to Leslie Knope. Elsewhere, Dylan is the hottest of hot messes, Clare is a haughty queen bee, and Patty is an out-of-her-depth klutz. They border on parodies instead of full-bodied characters, making Dig's earlier episodes a bit lukewarm.

But as Dig's title suggests, this is a show that won't be satisfied by sticking at surface level. As the show delves into what makes its characters tick, it becomes a heartwarming tale of found family, not unlike many other beloved Schur shows. Suddenly, character traits that felt one-note to start are revealed to be defense mechanisms or misconceptions, adding entirely new dimensions to Dig's core four.

Dig works best when that core works together in earnest, which is another reason why the show's early entries feel like slower burns: They're doing the groundwork to loop every woman into the Pretty Woman dig. Once they're all on the same page, Dig gets off and running — even more so once Pretty Woman mysteriously vanishes, leaving Elise and her motley crew to do some boots-on-the-ground detective work. Thus begins a madcap caper involving stakeouts, undercover identities, and the black market.

Along the way, Elise, Dylan, Clare, and Patty are allowed to be wonderfully messy and vulnerable, all while staying refreshingly supportive of one another. For example, a love triangle burbles up between Clare, Dylan, and Clare's fiancé/Dylan's ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), yet the storyline never devolves into cat fights or possessiveness. Instead, Dig focuses more on what these relationships mean to Clare and Dylan beyond romance. For Clare, her engagement is a sign of stability and true adulthood, whereas Dylan is looking for closure, something she notoriously can never get.

Dig also dabbles in intergenerational conflict, highlighting the differences between each of its leads, all of whom are at very different stages in their lives. Again, Dig doesn't take a hackneyed "Gen Z versus millennials versus Gen X" route. It's more interested in showing the different wants that motivate us. Patty yearns for belonging and friendship, while Elise wants to cement her legacy and make sure she's done something big enough to call her own, no Neville required.

Once Dig's ensemble truly comes together, they mesh wonderfully. Poehler brings a weary exasperation to Elise that slowly gives way to warm leadership, a transformation that's a joy to witness. Thomas and Viswanathan are perfect foils, the former a picture of poise, the latter completely uninhibited. Watching them feed off each other offers up a perfect comedic contrast. Finally, there's Strazza, whose endearingly goofy Patty becomes the beating heart of the show. Laurie, too, is a joy, blending Neville's smarminess with a genial charm that makes him infuriatingly tough to hate.

In focusing on archaeology, Dig emphasizes the importance of excavating ancient objects and interrogating the stories of those who once used them. In many ways, Dig is doing the same thing with its characters: peeling back their layers to get at their core truths. Only instead of using trowels and brushes, Dig uses the power of the workplace sitcom. It's a power Schur and his collaborators know well, and the result is a show that will leave you feeling like you've just uncovered something great in a dig of your own — with far less dirt on your face and clothes, that is.

Former Alibaba exec’s Everful raises $48.5M to connect retailers with Chinese factories

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Everful, a San Francisco- and Singapore-based startup that connects smaller retailers and brands with Chinese factories and manages the process from order to delivery, has raised $48.5 million in Series A funding.

The round was led by Vertex Ventures Southeast Asia & India and January Capital, with participation from existing investors Square Peg and Lightspeed.

Everful is on track to reach $100 million in annualized revenue by the end of the year, about two and a half years after its launch, co-founder Sisi Song said. The company ships over 5 million items a month to customers in more than 60 countries and works with over 30,000 factory partners, she added. Its online wholesale catalog includes more than 20 million products.

Song, a former Alibaba executive, and serial entrepreneur Kevin Han, who sold a previous company to Amazon, co-founded Everful in 2024. The company began as a jewelry wholesale business before evolving into a multi-category, factory-direct sourcing platform.

Retailers and brands often rely on intermediaries, face high minimum order requirements, and experience long sourcing cycles, which tie up capital in inventory before sales trends are clear. Song noticed the growing gap between fast-changing consumer trends and traditional wholesale sourcing models while working as a venture investor at Bessemer Venture Partners, where she mostly focused on cross-border sourcing and meeting numerous startups in the sector. That same gap shaped Everful’s goal to simplify buying directly from factories through an AI-enabled platform.

The B2B sourcing industry is highly competitive. Big tech giant Alibaba connects international buyers with manufacturers and suppliers, while Faire, a San Francisco-based wholesale tech platform, enables retailers to purchase wholesale inventory directly from brands. According to Everful’s co-founders, its key differentiator is responsibility for the sourcing process, rather than simply facilitating transactions.

“We take care of everything,” Han said. “We handle sourcing, order consolidation, quality control, and logistics. That’s something the marketplaces don’t offer.”

Everful is built for smaller retailers and brands without dedicated sourcing teams, Song said, and it also verifies the factories it works with. While peers also offer services such as quality inspection, logistics, and AI-powered sourcing, Everful aims to integrate these capabilities into a more streamlined purchasing experience, creating an end-to-end process that reduces the work retailers must handle themselves.

Han added that Everful uses AI agents to automate labor-intensive sourcing tasks, including tracking consumer trends, identifying suppliers, coordinating orders across factories, and managing logistics.

Because Everful sources from Chinese factories, ongoing U.S.-China trade tensions and tariffs bear directly on its business. Song said the co-founders have been thinking about geopolitical issues since day one of building the company, and that this has shaped Everful’s strategy from the outset, leveraging her experience at Alibaba and Bessemer Venture Partners.

She mentioned the Trump administration’s suspension of the de minimis exemption, which had allowed eligible shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the U.S. duty-free, as one example of a shift in trade policy.

Song explained such changes could favor a model that combines overseas sourcing with local retail sales. “We’re empowering local retailers and brands,” she said. “Long-term cross-border commerce will be cross-border B2B sourcing plus local selling.”

Everful plans to use the Series A round to expand its presence in the U.S. and Europe, particularly the U.K., France, and Italy, while investing in AI to automate product customization and other sourcing workflows. It also aims to deepen integrations with e-commerce platforms, building on its Shopify integration as it works toward becoming an end-to-end infrastructure platform for global sourcing.

The startup employs about 70 full-time staff globally, including 45 in research and development. It has also established an AI operations team to connect engineering and business functions.

As widespread tech outages pile up, AI tools are in the spotlight again

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Inaccessible email inboxes. Banking delays. And a communications meltdown that grounded airplanes for hours, followed by waves of other travel disruptions.

In an increasingly interconnected world, some fear such disruptions could be the new normal.

Over the past week alone, problems that appeared to emerge from routine updates, security patches or other malfunctions surfaced at Microsoft, JP Morgan Chase and Apple, and even the U.S. government’s Federal Aviation Administration and National Security Agency. The Trump administration said there was “zero” indication the latest outages are linked, or caused by bad actors like cybercriminals.

The era of artificial intelligence has also raised the stakes for internet security overall. As AI tools uncover new vulnerabilities, many other companies and institutions are racing to patch them before they can be targeted by hackers, a cat-and-mouse game that can lead to a different set of glitches.

Social media platforms have been abuzz with a mixture of annoyance, confusion, conspiracy theories and jokes about the string of outages.

“To see several disruptions this close together, I think to a lot of people it’s made them realize how many parts of their lives they’ve handed over to these systems that we still, at this point, don’t fully understand,” Jake Smith, a content creator who regularly posts videos about politics on platforms like Instagram and YouTube, said in an interview. “I think it gives people a sense of helplessness to see all of it happening concurrently in a way.”

What’s gone offline recently

Outages and technology errors have piled up across sectors.

Late last week, a global Microsoft outage disrupted email and other workplace tools, plunging business and everyday services into disarray worldwide. Microsoft 365 services, including Outlook, Teams and AI assistant Copilot, were interrupted. Microsoft cited, in part, a failure during a recent upgrade to its servers, spanning from what the company later determined was a “latent defect” in a network fix it had begun deploying over the summer.

For air travel, a separate malfunction in the FAA’s network led to the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years on Friday. The stop lasted for more than four hours, and carriers cancelled and delayed swaths of flights as they worked to recover. Just days later, United Airlines saw its own system outage — forcing it to delay departing flights on Monday.

Online banking troubles also piled up on Monday. JPMorgan Chase said a software bug affected its internal hardware systems — causing transactional delays for its customers and others sending money to related accounts, sending jitters throughout the financial system before the problem was resolved.

And on Tuesday, Apple, Marriott and the NSA joined the growing list. Apple Music, the App Store and AppleCare services were temporarily disrupted after the company said an internal update caused outages. And Marriott said a technical issue also caused problems for sign-ins on its website and app.

Meanwhile, the NSA said an internal network it uses to communicate classified information was also offline Tuesday “during scheduled routine maintenance.”

The government — as well as Microsoft, United, JPMorgan Chase, Apple and Marriott — have again maintained there’s no evidence of these disruptions being linked or related to a cyberattack.

Microsoft also noted that its outage did not span from “AI / agents,” although it didn’t immediately specify the goal of the network fix it first deployed over the summer. Microsoft did not respond to an Associated Press request for further details, and the other companies did not provide additional comments Wednesday about their disruptions when contacted by the AP via email.

AI has helped identify vulnerabilities, but also adds to risks

AI has nonetheless come into the spotlight as experts evaluate these disruptions — both between heightened risks posed by the technology as well as its ability to detect security gaps.

For example, AI recently helped detect sweeping vulnerabilities for PHP, a general-purpose scripting language that powers websites around the world — leading to urgent calls for updates. And that security work has resulted in some limited outages and service delays.

President Donald Trump’s director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, pointed directly to patching those security gaps when discussing the NSA’s outage this week. Clayton told Fox News that the patching following last month’s PHP alert has caused some systems to “not come back up” as expected. “That is what has happened at the NSA today,” he said.

Meanwhile, also this week, cybersecurity startup Calif said it used AI to help uncover a software bug in Java, one of the world’s most widely used programming languages and crucial for many big companies. Oracle, which owns Java, later released a security update and U.S. cyber authorities issued an advisory on Wednesday urging organizations to install it.

At the same time, others warn that the AI boom could open the door to additional disruptions.

“As AI adoption accelerates, we are likely to witness more incidents of this nature,” Raj Rajarajan, director of the Institute for Cyber Security at City St George’s, University of London, said last week.

He noted a lot of today’s digital infrastructure wasn’t originally designed to handle the “computational demands of large-scale AI services.”

While recent outages haven’t been tied to hackers or a coordinated attack, he and others also pointed to cybersecurity risks more broadly.

“AI is absolutely making it way easier for attackers to go and launch cyber attacks. It is not quite as good at creating patches for bugs, like fixing problems, as it is exploiting them,” said Justin Cappos, a cybersecurity professor at New York University’s Tandon School of Engineering.

In a cybersecurity report this week, Google said hackers and cyber criminals are “moving beyond basic prompting” and using increasingly sophisticated AI techniques to devise and carry out their attacks. The report, which focused on nefarious activity rather than outages, said techniques include using large language models and “agentic frameworks” to reverse engineering security patches to uncover vulnerabilities and exploit them.

Unraveling one (of just a few) major players in our online lives

Regardless, the latest outages offer yet more examples of the cascading effects that can erupt when a central player in tech, banking, travel or even the government runs into system errors that upend services.

Democratic Sen. Mark Warner, co-chair of the U.S. Senate’s Cybersecurity Caucus, acknowledged the widespread concern — and again stressed what this means in the AI era.

“These disruptions should serve as a wake-up call. It feels like every day brings another reminder that AI is moving faster than our ability to manage its consequences,” Warner said Tuesday. He also argued that the Trump administration hadn’t shown enough interest in confronting these challenges.

Still, “based on what we know today, there is good reason to believe these disruptions are the unintended consequences of efforts to make our systems safer,” Warner added.

—Wyatte Grantham-Philips, Kelvin Chan and Kaitlyn Huamani, AP Business Writers

Anthropic’s IPO filing reveals a strategy to undercut its AI rivals

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Welcome to AI Decoded, Fast Company‘s weekly newsletter that breaks down the most important news in the world of AI. I’m Mark Sullivan, a senior writer at Fast Company, covering emerging tech, AI, and tech policy.

Sign up to receive this newsletter every week via email here. And if you have comments on this issue and/or ideas for future ones, drop me a line at sullivan@fastcompany.com, and follow me on X @thesullivan.

‘Compute multipliers’ and ‘Anthropic Direct’ may be the IPO’s secret sauce

As frontier models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google converge in capabilities, the companies selling them may end up competing for enterprise business less on model quality and more on the cost of the computing power needed to run them. In its IPO filing and investor roadshow video, Anthropic, which debuted on the Nasdaq on Thursday, suggested it may be preparing for that future with something called “compute multipliers” and a compute-sourcing model it calls Anthropic Direct.

In its S-1, filed publicly in October, Anthropic defines compute multipliers as “algorithmic and methodological” improvements in the way it trains and operates models, effectively increasing the computing power it can get from a given amount of capacity. And these multipliers are “stackable.” In its roadshow video, Anthropic says it measures “total effective compute,” which is its physical computing capacity multiplied by the multipliers it has discovered or expects to discover in the future. Its example: If a model takes 100 units of compute to train, a 4x multiplier lets Anthropic train the same model with 25 units, or use all 100 units to train a model that would previously have required 400.

The company also says the multipliers “stack,” with each new discovery building on previous ones, and that it expects Claude will eventually be able to discover multipliers on its own.

According to the filing, the gains apply to models used to serve customers as well as to model training. Anthropic says its researchers “have delivered meaningful compute multipliers for inference,” reducing the computing power needed to deliver a given level of model performance. The company says this “allows us to provide greater value for customers.”

Anthropic has already been cutting prices. It says price reductions tied to new model versions, along with contractual discounts, reduced revenue growth by about $600 million between the second and third quarters of 2026. It says Opus 5.5, launched in September, delivered “a lower cost per task” than earlier models. If Anthropic can get more usable compute out of each chip than its rivals can, it may be able to undercut other AI labs on price. It may also be able to undercut cloud providers that sell AI services built on their own models.

If compute multipliers let Anthropic do more with its physical computing power, Anthropic Direct could give the company greater control over where that power comes from. Right now, Anthropic buys almost all its computing power from cloud providers, including competitors like SpaceX, Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, and CoreWeave. The company says it has already committed to buying roughly $900.9 billion in compute from these providers over the coming years and decades. Those expenses also include short-term cloud compute purchases, mainly to cover peak usage periods, which come at a premium.

The S-1 says Anthropic is reducing its reliance on expensive cloud providers by developing its own data centers and leasing the chips that go inside them. Its first Anthropic Direct facility came online this year. The company says a “significant portion” of its planned compute spending will go toward building more Anthropic Direct capacity. Based on an analysis of contract values in the S-1 filing, PitchBook estimates that Direct data centers will supply about 1% of Anthropic’s available compute capacity this year, 48% in 2030, and 60% in 2035.

The S-1 says Anthropic’s total compute pool, including third-party cloud compute and Direct, will be used for its own research and model development, as well as customer workloads. Bringing more compute in-house could dramatically reduce the overhead costs of developing and serving Claude models, apps, and agents for enterprise and consumer customers. That, in turn, could allow Anthropic to lower per-token prices for enterprises and subscription rates for consumers.

But Anthropic Direct may end up being something more. The filing doesn’t specifically say Direct compute could be rented to enterprise customers. Still, it’s a possibility; Anthropic could be building an AI-native cloud where enterprises could store their proprietary data and run Anthropic’s models in the same secure environment, potentially at a lower cost than building the same setup within an Amazon, Google, or Microsoft cloud.

That would give the Anthropic Direct brand name a very different meaning. Instead of accessing Claude models through a cloud provider they already use, customers would come directly to Anthropic. That could turn computing from a major overhead expense into a service Anthropic controls and rents to others for profit—a significant change to the company’s business model.

Some big obstacles stand in the way of such an ambition. Google, Amazon, and Microsoft are currently Anthropic’s largest compute suppliers, as well as the main distribution channels through which enterprises buy Claude. As Power Demand Surges, Energy Is Moving Closer To The Edge

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Darren H. S. Tan. Chief Executive Officer at UNIGRID.

​Over the summer, Europe suffered a heat wave that strained its power grids, simultaneously increasing electricity demand and forcing some power plants to shut down.​

This isn’t the first time extreme temperatures have impacted the grid’s ability to deliver power. Many places around the world have experienced record-high or record-low temperatures in recent years, and the demand for heating or cooling reached levels that existing energy infrastructure wasn’t designed to handle. ​

The problem is being made worse by the construction of hyperscale AI data centers, which require massive amounts of electricity and further strain the grid. Completely rebuilding the grid to meet modern demands has proven fiscally impractical for many governments, forcing many places to solve the problem on the local level using distributed generation and storage.

Energy infrastructure across the U.S. and Europe was built over a century ago, when energy needs were much different. ​

Even so, studies have shown that we use only about 40% to 55% of the grid on average, and that’s by design, so it can handle spikes when they occur. But recent extreme weather events and data center demand are causing power needs to exceed even that generous buffer.​

For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which manages electricity in Texas, has said its interconnection queue currently includes over 1,800 projects totaling 474 gigawatts of power, over five times the state’s record peak electricity demand. Ninety percent of those requests come from data centers. ​

The cost to completely rebuild the grid to meet today’s needs is astronomical, with some estimates putting the total replacement cost in the trillions. Because of the potential cost and the fact that the aging grid is a chronic rather than an urgent problem, it receives patchwork upgrades as needed and as government budgets allow. ​

With a full rebuild out of the picture, several strategies are in play to work within the system we already have. When demand approaches available supply, energy companies offer rebates for reducing usage at peak times, implement peak pricing or, in more extreme cases, resort to rolling blackouts, which are planned outages to alleviate the demand during peak periods. ​

Beyond the grid itself, some areas are reducing their dependence on the centralized infrastructure by moving toward local power generation and storage.

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) can store electricity and discharge it when demand exceeds the grid’s capacity. ​

They are already showing promise in several states, such as California, where battery storage capacity has reached over 21,000 MW, equal to about one-third of the state’s peak demand. Eighty percent of that capacity is represented by grid-connected batteries, but smaller batteries are also enabling residents to increase their own energy storage capacity at home.​

As these batteries become cheaper and more reliable, it’s not out of the realm of possibility that these energy storage systems become a standard household appliance in the future, much like a refrigerator or washing machine is today. They can also play a role in energy needs for small businesses.

Being able to store energy and use it in an emergency is only half the equation. The other half is being able to generate electricity independently of the grid. ​

This isn’t a concept that should be unfamiliar to many, as many organizations and homes already have backup generators or solar panels. Although these methods can keep small businesses and homes powered up, they have their disadvantages. Generators are noisy, pollutive and can be dangerous, while solar has significant upfront installation costs. ​

Plug-in solar is emerging as another option for generating electricity locally that has already seen success in Europe and is starting to gain support in the U.S. as well. Instead of being installed on a rooftop, these smaller panels can be mounted on a balcony or a small section of a yard or business property and plugged into a wall outlet to help power homes and small businesses. ​

These systems cost significantly less than traditional solar and are portable, so people can move them around wherever they’re needed. These benefits open up power generation to a whole new segment of the country: commercial and residential renters alike who might never invest in a hardwired battery system or solar installation.

As energy demand continues to increase, expanding traditional grid infrastructure will remain important, but those projects can be expensive and take years to complete. Distributed generation and storage offer another way to increase resilience in the meantime. ​

By generating and storing more electricity closer to where it will be consumed, communities and business leaders can help reduce their reliance on the centralized grid during peak demand.​

We’re unlikely to solve growing power constraints with any single technology, but as local generation and storage become cheaper and more accessible, they can play a larger role in managing peak demand and making better use of the grid infrastructure we already have.​

The latest (reported) update? Ella Langley and Tucker Wetmore are rumored to be dating, but we really can’t talk about their maybe-relationship without discussing Tucker’s ex Bryana Ferringer...or Ella’s rumored ex Riley Green (who went on to maybe date Bryana).

Tucker posts a TikTok on August 20 with the caption “@ my ex,” sparking breakup rumors with his model girlfriend Bryana (who, btw, appeared in his music video for “Wind Up Missin’ You”).

Soon after, Bryana is spotted with Riley at his Red Rocks tour stop.

Ella and Tucker both attend the 2025 BMI Country Awards on November 18, though it’s unclear whether this is the first time they ever meet (or if they even interacted, despite standing right next to each other in this group photo):

A day later, after several reported sightings around Nashville, Riley brings Bryana as his date to the CMA Awards on November 19.

However! Riley later refers to Bryana as merely a “friend,” telling Backstage Country, “It’s gonna be tough to tiptoe out of having a date to an award show. Anthropic hits Nasdaq exchanges at $111, 33% above IPO price

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shares of Anthropic are now trading on the Nasdaq exchange, as the company successfully executes its initial public offering. In the early minutes of availability, shares were trading at $111.50, a 33% premium over the $83 IPO price.

Set on Wednesday, the IPO price was already higher than the projected range set by the company, which anticipated a price between $70 and $78 per share. All told, the offering will raise over $81 billion, directed to a combination of employees and the company’s own coffers.

The IPO funding, together with existing fundraising and new private offerings timed to coincide with the company’s public market debut, will leave Anthropic with an expected $220 billion in cash reserves. As one analyst told TechCrunch, “Anthropic’s going to have more cash on hand than any company has ever had.”

The share price continues to fluctuate, but the opening price would put the company’s market cap over $2 trillion, making it more valuable than Meta, Tesla, or Walmart. Only five companies boast a higher valuation by market cap: Apple, Amazon, Alphabet, Nvidia, and Microsoft.

In a speech before the opening bell, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei emphasized the company’s commitment to safety. “Today puts the company in a stronger financial position, and in doing so, only strengthens our resolve to do the right thing,” Amodei said. “If we can show it’s possible to make the right choices on safety and still build an incredibly successful company, others in our industry will be inspired to do the same. We call this a race to the top.”
