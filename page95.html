Charizard has been around since the beginning of the Pokémon series, and it quickly became a Pokémon that was beloved by everyone. Many of us were around to see Ash take in Charmander, and help it grow into a fiery beast.

However, there are many who consider Charizard to be a big red flag when choosing your favorite Pokémon. The reason for this is that you are far too basic if this is your choice.

Of course, there is a lot of context behind Charizard being a red flag. After all, it is the Pokémon that has received some of the most attention, with it getting multiple forms and promotions. It’s almost like Charizard is the mascot of Pokémon with how much attention it gets.

From the Eevee evolutions to Magikarp becoming something useful, here are the Pokémon in FireRed and LeafGreen that feel different after evolving.

The internet ruined Garbodor by turning it into a meme where if you like the Pokémon, you are considered dirty. On the other hand, many associate this Pokémon as a red flag because choosing it feels like someone is purposefully trying to go against the grain by choosing a less universally loved Pokémon.

Despite the leak never containing any canon information that made it into the games, and there being mistranslations, and other issues with it, Typhlosion has never really been able to escape the allegations, and so therefore, is still considered a red flag to many in the fan base.

When you bring together many people and give them the capability of being anonymous, as they can be on the internet, they have a habit of ruining everything you once loved. While there are multiple on this list, Lopunny is the first up.

Lopunny started off as Buneary - a rather adorable rabbit Pokémon - that gets an… interesting physique when it evolves into its final stage. Many saw this transformation, went online, and then proceeded to turn the Pokémon into a symbol for provocation.

Since then, it has been frowned upon whenever someone mentions that their favorite Pokémon is Lopunny, and the addition of Mega Lopunny did not help. Instead, it made things a bit worse.

Mr. Mime was an unsettling Pokémon when kids were introduced to it in the anime. Its human-like appearance mixed with a mime is something that is uncanny. However, it has only gotten worse over the years.

The other mindset people have is that Garchomp is associated with Cynthia from Diamond and Pearl. She dominated players in that era, and the idea is that those who like Garchomp haven’t gotten over that and want to inflict the same trauma on others.

The original Pokédex entries don’t make Drifloon out to be so bad, as the Dex says that it pulls on children to steal them away, but it ends up getting yanked around instead. However, this has gotten worse with time.

More recent entries specifically state that this Pokémon kidnaps children and whisks them off to the afterlife. Other entries talk about how it is specifically filled with souls and grows larger each time it steals one away. Let’s just leave this Pokémon out of our favorites list.

Morgan Stanley Says $300. Michael Burry Says Overvalued. Here's Who Is Right About SpaceX Stock.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Experts are split on where SpaceX stock will go next.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +8.62%) stock remains exceptionally volatile. Shares currently trade about 17% lower than their first-day closing price back in early June. At various times this year, shares have rapidly shed more than one-third of their value, only to surge higher again by more than 50%.

The market seemingly can't make up its mind about SpaceX's valuation. Neither can the experts.

By one metric, most of Wall Street is in agreement regarding SpaceX stock. Out of 35 analysts covering the space stock, 29 have "buy" ratings, six have "hold" ratings, and just one has a "sell" rating. The consensus price target is around $250, indicating more than 86% upside potential.

When digging deeper, however, it becomes clear how divided Wall Street is. Some bullish analysts have price targets under $200. Others have price targets as high as $500. Somewhere in the middle is Morgan Stanley, with a price target of $300, implying more than 124% upside over the next 12 months.

Legendary investor Michael Burry -- a hedge fund manager who made billions for investors by shorting the U.S. housing market during the 2008-09 financial crisis -- disagrees.

"Nothing in that S-1 suggests it is worth $1 trillion let alone $2 trillion," Burry warned this summer, referring to SpaceX's IPO prospectus. Burry became even more cautious after SpaceX's valuation shot higher following its IPO. "At $2.8 TRILLION market cap, SpaceX, which is fundamentally a small space company, a niche telecom, a bedeviled social media company, and a Coreweave-light, has less than $20 billion in total revenue," he highlighted. "Any move up will be on hype and technicals."

With these concerns in mind, why does Morgan Stanley remain so bullish? Morgan Stanley's $300 price target comes down to a few key assumptions.

Nearly every analyst who covers SpaceX stock and is bullish focuses on one key operating segment: AI (artificial intelligence). According to SpaceX's own IPO prospectus, AI represents more than 90% of the company's total addressable market. Any bullish stance on SpaceX stock, therefore, can be assumed to include bullish assumptions regarding the company's AI segment.

Morgan Stanley, however, isn't just bullish on SpaceX's AI segment. Instead, analysts at the bank's investment arm are optimistic about how SpaceX's other segments will create synergies with its AI efforts. "We see the company as one of the few platforms that can link real estate in orbit, global connectivity, and compute capacity into one infrastructure stack," Morgan Stanley analysts explain.

In another note, the firm stressed that SpaceX's AI business is "the part of the SPCX story that we believe holds the greatest growth potential." The firm added that rockets and satellites will prove key to generating AI revenue. That's because SpaceX's Starship rocket will prove key to getting satellites into orbit cheaply at scale.

Those satellites, meanwhile, can be used not only by SpaceX's Starlink division to provide more connectivity services -- currently, a high-cash-flow contributor to SpaceX's bottom line -- but also to support orbital data centers. Orbital data centers could give SpaceX a cost and availability edge over other big tech competitors, though questions remain about the economic viability, as well as the technological and physical constraints of data centers in space.

This is the major difference between how Burry and Morgan Stanley view SpaceX stock. Morgan Stanley believes in synergies among SpaceX's business segments and forecasts significant AI growth driven by them. Burry, meanwhile, is more skeptical.

Notably, Morgan Stanley analysts affirm the potential for Burry's pessimistic valuation in their bear case. "Our $75 bear case assumes Starship slips to 2029 and AI monetization and deployment speed miss expectations, leaving Space and Connectivity at roughly 90% of valuation," Morgan Stanley analysts explain. "Ability to secure necessary capital is one of the greatest risks to our forecasts."

Morgan Stanley's bull case relies on SpaceX tapping huge amounts of new capital throughout the rest of the decade. Burry's argument essentially rests on one thing: The simultaneous success and synergistic potential of SpaceX's three segments combined with continued affordable access to capital for years on end is a difficult bet to make. It's hard to disagree with that sentiment. Which is why, like Burry, I am not long or short SpaceX stock but simply on the sidelines.

Streaming vs. downloading: Which uses more battery and data?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Video and sound quality have an impact on battery and data usage.

Although some people are pushing back, a lot of us are dependent on streaming services for our movies and music. There's an obvious incentive — you get a massive library for a (relatively) cheap monthly fee and the ability to access that media from virtually any device with an internet connection. Conversely, owned downloads and physical media can be pricey and not terribly portable.

Many streaming services do offer a middle ground of sorts, letting you cache songs, videos or podcasts so you can play them with or without an internet connection. But let's say you're most concerned with efficiency and not the soundtrack to a remote camping expedition. Does it still make more sense to download in advance, or should you stream whenever possible?

The quick answer is that downloads (of all stripes) are usually the most efficient option when it comes to battery and usage. But there may be times when streaming is preferable, or possibly the only option.

For anything you enjoy on a regular basis, downloads win. While there's going to be an initial (and possibly intense) surge of battery and bandwidth consumption, that's vastly outweighed by the non-existent effort for subsequent plays. You should always cache over Wi-Fi, incidentally, since it's usually faster, and a way to avoid bumping up against the data caps that many cellular providers continue to impose on their plans.

It's only when you're talking about one-time plays that streaming is in any way efficient. If you hit Stop or Pause partway through a video or playlist with no intent of coming back, you may consume less power and data. We say "may" because all streaming apps perform a base amount of caching, whether or not you deliberately save anything. Depending on where you halt during playback, your device may already have a complete video or playlist in memory, which is quickly going to be wasted.

Really, the only reason to avoid downloading is storage space. Just a few cached playlists in a music app can consume several gigabytes, speaking from personal experience, and that's without factoring lossless options into the equation. In app settings, Spotify notes that its lossless downloads can consume up to 1 GB per hour of playback — which will probably make downloading your entire library impractical, if not impossible.

Perhaps the easiest way to rein things in is by adjusting media quality. Returning to the Spotify example, even scaling back from lossless quality to "Very high" (320 kbps) drops data consumption from 1 GB per hour to just 0.14 GB, according to the music streaming site. Lower data use naturally means reduced battery drain, since caching is faster. Similar principles apply to video apps, although they tend to manage quality via resolution options instead of bitrate. All else being equal, a 480p or 720p version of a video has a far smaller footprint than 1080p. Depending on your app and device, 4K downloads may not even be an option.

You should only scale things back so far. While lossless and 320 kbps are virtually indistinguishable in audio, you'll notice a lack of clarity by the time you drop to 160 kbps. Similarly, although you're probably not going to spot the difference between 1080p and 4K on something as small as a phone or tablet, 720p is below the sharpness of most displays.

There may be smarter ways of reducing battery and data use, such as lowering screen brightness, and being more selective over what you consume in general. Your life doesn't always need a soundtrack or video wallpaper. And if you limit the number of apps, podcasts and playlists on your device, there's less to update in the background.

The latest status symbol of the 1% is a third kid. ‘It has become rarer for everyone else,’ says an economist

For decades, the markers of extreme wealth were easy to spot: the second home, the fancy cars, the Ivy League education. New analysis of Federal Reserve data points to a subtler one: a third child.

Mike Konczal, vice president of policy and research at the Economic Security Project and a former White House economist, studied households headed by 45- to 64-year-olds in his analysis of the Fed’s Survey of Consumer Finances, pooling five surveys from 2013 through 2025. He found that 49% of top-1% earners have three or more children, compared with about 40% of the rest of the top 10%.

Families with four or more kids are about as common in both groups, so the gap comes down to the third child: 34% of the 1% stop at exactly three, versus 24% of the merely affluent.

What changed isn’t the rich. Since the 1990s, families with three or more kids have become less common overall and among the rest of the top 10%, while the 1%’s share barely moved. “What’s new is that it has become rarer for everyone else,” Konczal writes.

Big families thin out in the upper middle class. Rank families by wealth instead of income, and among those wealthier than about three-quarters of Americans their age, but well short of the top, only about a third have three or more kids.

That’s well below families further down the wealth ladder. In the top 1%, half do.

Many of these families earn too much for federal child care subsidies, which are capped at 85% of a state’s median income, but not enough to hire their way around the problem.

Elaine Shi and her husband once thought they might have four kids. They stopped at three. Asked how much cost factored in, Shi, the CEO and co-founder of body-composition scanning company BodySpec, said simply: “None.” What settled it was their third child’s “firecracker personality,” along with her health during each pregnancy.

Shi doesn’t see that divide among the people she knows. “Candidly, this has not been my experience,” she says. What stands out to her is timing: “Relatively few of my peers in our late 30s have chosen to have three children because they started having the first one relatively late in life.”

Big families used to be ordinary. In 1976, about two-thirds of mothers in their early 40s had three or more kids; by 2016, 38% did, according to Pew Research Center.

The U.S. fertility rate, close to two children per woman in 1995, hit a record low of 1.57 in 2025, by a Wall Street Journal calculation using federal data.

Americans still say they want more: 42% called three or more kids ideal in Gallup’s July 2025 poll. But more than 7 in 10 say that raising kids is unaffordable for most people, according to BYU’s 2025 American Family Survey.

Shi’s third child arrived at a hectic moment. Her older two, about two years apart, were starting school, and their needs were diverging.

“It didn’t seem fair to drag an infant along to every gymnastics and swim class, nor did it seem fair to forego those kinds of activities to stick to a baby’s nap schedule,” she says. She was also mid-fundraise and needed to get back to work fast. “So we ended up investing in multiple caregivers to better meet everyone’s needs.”

The biggest financial change from two kids to three, she says, was “definitely childcare.”

Most families can’t hire their way out. Full-day care for a child cost $6,552 to $15,600 a year in 2022, or 8.9% to 16% of median family income, according to the Labor Department. And that’s for one child.

By one LendingTree estimate, a two-child household needs to earn $400,000 a year for childcare to be affordable.

A third kid often means a bigger house, too. The typical existing single-family home sold for $412,500 in 2024, about five times the median household income, according to Harvard’s Joint Center for Housing Studies. In the 1990s, it was closer to three times.

The U.S. recorded just over half a million more births than deaths in 2025, and Census Bureau and Congressional Budget Office projections show that cushion ending in the next decade, the Wall Street Journal reported. After that, population growth would depend on immigration.

Economists warn that fewer births mean a smaller future workforce, slower growth and more strain on Social Security.

Konczal cautions that his numbers show a correlation, but it’s the one you’d expect if money solves the hard parts of raising kids. He argues that some of what money buys the rich, like stability and support, could be offered to families now, through policy aimed at the years when kids are young and budgets are tightest.

Shi’s own fix is more about time than money. She and her husband run BodySpec together, and their favorite workday, she says, is Saturday.

The office is empty, and one of the kids usually tags along, most often her son, the middle child. He gets to build a LEGO set without his sisters taking it apart, then has lunch alone with his mom.

“Nobody told us this counted as work-life balance, but it’s the closest thing we’ve found,” Shi says.

The 10 Most Perfect '90s Movie Movie Plot Twists, Ranked

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A New York native, Joe graduated from an avant-garde theater school upstate, where he was obsessed with sketch comedy. There, he and his lunatic friends founded The Sketchies — which still lives on at Skidmore College today. Living in New York City, a screenwriter friend of his then taught him how to write for film. This awakened a new passion in Joe. Since then his scripts have garnered over 200 festival selections, nominations, and wins all over the globe (including the BlueCat Best Feature award). Several of his screenplays have been made into feature films — some of them not that bad! Overall, he loves to create unique, hilarious, touching, and bizarre stories.

Oh, the 90s: what a time to be alive. The economy was booming, grunge music was flourishing, and just about every genre of film contributed at least one stunner with a glorious twist ending.

Obviously, this list is one giant string of spoilers — so, you are vehemently forewarned. Now, here are the most perfect movie plot twists of the 90s, a decade rife with intricately woven film devices. A lot of these flicks are in the thriller sphere, but there are also shocking gems that fall under drama, comedy, and action. Whichever way you classify these films, they all leave the viewer pleasantly head-spun.

Kicking off this list is a film that employs some major twists in a completely non-traditional way. This fantastically frenetic film begins with a very direct set-up, and then, almost out of nowhere, the action reverts, and the protagonist is given two different attempts to achieve her goal — all while German techno blasts, obviously.

Director/writer Tom Tykwer’s movie begins with the Berlin baddie Lola (Franka Potente) receiving a call from her distraught boyfriend, Manni (Moritz Bleibtreu). He’s lost 100,000 Deutsche Marks on the subway and if he doesn’t recover this loot in 20 minutes, his drug kingpin boss is going to kill him. So, Lola literally races around the city trying to somehow find this specific amount of cash. Her first attempt fails in dramatic fashion…and then the clock resets. In a full exploration of chaos theory, she starts her mad dash again, this time with an alternate plan. Again, massive fail. So, she gets one more try…which leads to the final twist. She’s finally successful in her third attempt, but when she meets Manni, in this timeline, he’s recovered the cash himself, and so the whole insane endeavor was for nothing. Ah, that’s German ingenuity for you.

Mm-hmm, redemption indeed. The Stephen King novella, Rita Hayworth and Shawshank Redemption, turned feature film is one of the best movies of the 90s, without question. Capturing not only the mood of the decades featured in the action (the 40s through the 60s), but also that of the prisoner mentality of the time, director Frank Darabont’s movie has lots of ups and downs, but no moment is as uplifting as the finale, which happens to contain quite the inventive twist.

Andy Dufresne (Tim Robbins) is an inmate in a penitentiary in Maine. He was wrongly accused of his wife’s murder and received a life sentence, but that doesn’t stop him from still trying to live his life to the fullest. His best buddy is the warmly wise Ellis Boyd 'Red' Redding (Morgan Freeman), and his nemesis is the Draconian, downright evil and corrupt Warden Norton (Bob Gunton). This man does everything in his power to suppress Andy’s seemingly indomitable spirit (along with those of all the other prisoners). Yet it’s Norton’s corruption that actually allows for Andy to have the last laugh. Andy systematically allocates the funds that Norton has pilfered and launders the cash — directing it to a bank account that only he can access under an alias (along with sending a newspaper a letter detailing all of Norton’s crimes against humanity). The ultimate twist comes when, after a routine cell inspection, it’s revealed that Andy is nowhere to be found. He spent the last twenty years chipping away at the wall of his cell (using a pin-up girl poster, of Rita Hayworth, to hide the hole) and digging a getaway tunnel to the sewer system — where he was able to eventually escape. The lesson, get busy livin’ or get busy dyin’, couldn’t be more heartfelt.

Writer-director Michael Haneke is known for being quite the provocateur. His viscerally brutal yet philosophically compelling film Funny Games vividly illustrates this point. While he remade it into a nearly identical American version, with the same title, the original German-language film was a bit sharper, all around.

Two teen boys, Peter (Frank Giering) and Paul (Arno Frisch), come to the vacation house of a small family: Georg (Ulrich Mühe), Anna (Susanne Lothar), and their little son, Schorschi (Stefan Clapczynski). The teens ask for some eggs, and it initially seems innocent enough. Then the one kid drops the eggs, repeatedly, and Anna’s and the audience’s Spidey-Senses begin tingling; something is rotten in the state of Austria. The teens then begin systematically breaking down this little family, injuring them and holding them hostage. When the unthinkable occurs (the kid is killed), the viewer now inherently believes that justice is coming, and the teens will pay, big time. Eventually, Anna frees herself and shoots Peter dead. Aha. Karmic retribution. Well, not quite. Paul takes the handy TV remote and — completely breaking the fourth wall — “rewinds” the action, so that Peter is still alive and now sees Anna’s move coming before she can do anything. What the what? The twist here is that nothing is real; it’s a movie, after all. Yet, Haneke raises a bigger question to the audience: you’re invested in the characters, but why? You know they are fictitious entities going into the viewing, so why should it shock you that they are given a bum deal/unfair outcome? No matter how one dissects this sick work of blasphemous art, it always leaves a nasty taste in one’s mouth…and that’s exactly the pleasantly putrid point.

While basically every decade since he hit the scene has been a good one for Brad Pitt, perhaps none showcased his range the ways the 90s did. One of his earliest, wildest roles takes place in the mind-bending Bruce Willis vehicle 12 Monkeys, directed by Terry Gilliam. While there aren’t any actual primates in the film, there is a lot of sci-fi monkey business going on, and the ending is truly bananas.

The plot of this film is crammed full of twisty turns, from the first act to the last. Set in 2035 (which eerily doesn’t seem that far off now…), most of humanity lives a subterranean life after a (man-made) virus has wiped out the majority of the world’s population. The good news is that some scientists have developed time travel; all they need is a test subject to go back in time to snag a pure sample of the early virus so they can create a cure, back (to the future) in the present. They pick a prisoner, James Cole (Willis), for the mission. What does he have to lose, right? It should be noted, the traumatized James has had a recurring dream since he was a mere boy; that of a man in an airport getting shot. Anyhoo, James goes back, appears to be a lunatic, links up with psychiatrist Dr. Kathryn Railly (Madeleine Stowe), and then ends up in a mental hospital where he encounters Jeffrey Goines (Pitt), an eco-warrior possibly responsible for the release of the nasty bug (he’s part of the “Army of 12 Monkeys,” which sounds less like an eco-terrorist group and more like an indie rock band). It’s not until the final sequence that it’s revealed that it was actually a deranged scientist, Dr. Peters (David Morse), who was responsible for the viral outbreak. James finds Peters in an airport, and before he can stop him, James is shot by airport police (he should’ve taken his shoes off before going through security), as a little boy watches this unfold — who turns out to be James himself (that’s where the dream came from). The swirly icing on this twisty cake is that once Peters boards a plane, en route to deploying the virus worldwide, the passenger next to him says her name is Jones (Carol Florence), and she’s “in insurance.” This final moment is a bit open-ended, but the ‘insurance’ quip seems to suggest she is there to get the pure virus sample, once and for all.

Most people that view this film are too busy trying to unpack all the metaphysical wackiness that occurred throughout the entire runtime to even let the bombshell ending twist really sit with them. While this hilarious Spike Jonze directed, Charlie Kaufman penned, work of sheer genius has wild turns throughout, its final mic drop of an ending is perhaps the film’s peak moment.

Craig Schwartz (John Cusack) is an out-of-work puppeteer, desperate to find work in his field. This is futile, even with all the support from his loving wife, Lotte (Cameron Diaz). Craig nabs a job at an odd company on the “7 and a half” floor of a building in NYC (in a nod to surrealist playwright Alfred Jarry, who lived in a “half floor” apartment in Paris, and also scribed the play Ubu Roi that was performed with puppets). Craig accidentally discovers an odd, fleshy hole behind a cabinet — that is a portal into the mind of the actor John Malkovich. So, Craig and his snarky co-worker Maxine Lund (Catherine Keener) open a covert business selling “rides” into Malko’s consciousness. Eventually, it’s revealed that Craig’s elderly boss, Dr. Lester (Orson Bean), has been readying himself and some senior citizen friends to enter the portal together and take over Malkovich’s body forever. There’s a lot more to this, but, basically, Craig wants to use Malkovich’s fame and money to lock down Maxine and enters the portal too late. He’s diverted to the subconscious of Maxine’s new baby — who Maxine raises with Lotte — where Craig is a silent prisoner, forever. It’s all quite heady.

Some games are fun. Some games are exciting. And then there’s The Game, which may or may not cause the player to intentionally plummet to their apparent demise. Master of the shadowy shot, David Fincher, directs this masterwork in manipulative mind games and, wow, is it ever decadently dark.

Nicholas Van Orton (Michael Douglas) is a rich dude who is so accustomed to having everything in life that basically nothing excites him anymore. His freewheeling brother, Conrad (Sean Penn), gets him a gift for his birthday, simply called “The Game.” Before Nicholas even realizes it, he’s playing. It’s just, this game is so exhilarating and disruptive, it seems that it’s veered off course and Nicholas’s life is actually in grave danger. After a series of incidents that are beyond harrowing, Nick finds himself on the roof of a very tall building, his entire fortune having been wiped clean, apparently being hunted by a vast network of high criminals — with seemingly no way out. He opts to jump, ostensibly ending his life…only there’s a big suicide prevention balloon underneath him. This was all part of the game, as Conrad and other birthday party guests pop out and congratulate Nicholas on a well-played adventure. Hmm. Fun.

“What’s in the box?” Ah, a question for the ages. David Fincher’s pitch black, bleak, awful thriller is one of the most satisfyingly perverse films ever produced. With a dark mystery insidiously pulsing through the whole film, this wonderfully waterlogged movie is thrilling throughout, and culminates in a twist of pure, heartless beauty.

Detective Mills (Brad Pitt) is a newbie cop in town, whereas Detective Somerset (Morgan Freeman) is a veteran, on the verge of retirement. When a string of sick murders/assaults occur which appear to be under the religious umbrella of the seven deadly sins, the two gumshoes know they’re in for a wild ride of a case. As they try to home in on the culprit (before the seventh crime is committed/enacted), the alleged perpetrator, John Doe (Kevin Spacey, at his creepiest), turns himself in. It’s a baffling move, but he calmly croons that he will lead the detectives to the final crime site if they accompany him to a yet undisclosed location, alone. They comply, and that’s when it gets really interesting. A clueless delivery dude shows up with a box, meeting John, Mills, and Somerset, in a remote part of the Californian desert. Well, the box contains the head of Mill’s wife, Tracy (Gwyneth Paltrow). John explains that his own personal sin was “envy,” as he envied Mill’s nice, normal, wife-having life. The final sin left to commit is wrath…which Mills, between bitter tears, executes, putting a bullet in John’s head. It’s an ironic ending; John Doe, the villain, wins — even though his brains get blown out. All in all, a mind-blowing experience…for the character and the audience.

Who is Keyser Söze? That’s the main question at the heart of director Bryan Singer’s crime-action-thiller-mystery The Usual Suspects. With one of the most well-crafted scripts of the decade, not to mention searingly stellar performances, brilliant direction, sharp cinematography, and crisp editing, this film hits all the right notes throughout its symphonic runtime. And, naturally, it concludes with one of the most jaw-dropping, and coolest, twists in movie history.

The film is framed with a very nifty device; a low-level career criminal, Verbal (Kevin Spacey, having a banner year), is interrogated by the bullish, blowhard detective Dave Kujan (Chazz Palminteri), in a precinct room. The loquacious Verbal, who has a disability that impedes his arm and leg from functioning normally, reluctantly provides Kujan with info surrounding a boat heist gone wrong. Verbal and a deliciously diverse cadre of crime types, including the clownish McManus (Stephen Baldwin), the wisecracking Hockney (Kevin Pollak), and mumbling Fenster (Benicio Del Toro), were given faulty information and most of them ended up dead during the botched robbery. Kujan presses Verbal about the de facto leader of the group, Keaton (Gabriel Byrne), accusing him of being the notorious Söze. Eventually, Verbal is released, and that’s when it hits Kujan: Verbal was giving an Upright Citizen’s Brigade-level improv class, as he took random words, names, and phrases from around the office and spun them into a long-winded yarn about the whole criminal endeavor. As Verbal loses his limp and lights a cig, we collectively realize that he was the lethal rapscallion Keyser all along. Mind. Blown.

The first rule of Fight Club may be that you don’t talk about Fight Club, but here, we simply must. The phenomenal Chuck Palahniuk novel turned David Fincher film begins with the explosion of the unnamed protagonist’s condo, and never lets its filmic foot off the gas. Yes, this movie is the physical manifestation of octane fuel mixed with 100-proof testosterone, but there’s oh so much more to it than just boys masquerading as manly men.

The Narrator (Edward Norton) is a milquetoast dude, working a boring job, living an Ikea-inspired life. He has chronic insomnia that eventually becomes a major factor in the twist ending. He begins going to nighttime support groups (for afflictions that he doesn’t have). Here he meets Marla Singer (Helena Bonham Carter), a tsunami of disaster in cool sunglasses. Also, during his travels, he encounters what can only be described as his polar opposite: Tyler Durden (Brad Pitt), the badass artisanal soap entrepreneur who is everything the Narrator wishes he could be. After starting an underground fight club, which blossoms into a national movement of chaos and calculated destruction, the Narrator eventually comes to a disturbing conclusion: he is Tyler. The real fly in the ointment is that Tyler wants him dead, and seems pretty intent on making this happen (along with blowing up all the financial institutions in the city). Probably the most entertaining examination of multiple personality disorder caught on celluloid, Fincher’s darkly comedic thriller has one of the coolest twist endings ever — accompanied by the perfect Pixies song.

Unless you’ve been living under a rock, that’s buried under a boulder, that’s housed in an end-of-the-world bunker, you probably already know the brain-exploding twist that drops at the end of The Sixth Sense. If not, whoo-hoo, are you in for one sensationally spooky spoiler (even comic Nate Bargatze made fun of people who don’t know the end of this movie by now). Seriously, if you don’t know what the big reveal is, stop reading this article immediately, find auteur creator M. Night Shyamalan’s supernatural mystery streaming somewhere, and watch the heck out of it.

Cole Sear (Haley Joel Osment) is a boy with a unique gift that actually is much more of a curse: he sees dead people (as the now infamous lines goes). He connects with a child therapist, Malcolm Crowe (Bruce Willis), who has a knack for helping disturbed children, even if his track record is not exactly perfect (one of his former patients, Vincent Gray (Donnie Wahlberg), shot him). So, Malcolm spends the majority of the film trying to help Cole navigate the unsettling world of the undead…only to finally realize that he is a ghost as well. It’s probably the most perfectly executed twist in cinema history, but hey, that’s just the opinion…of basically everyone.

Now that I know exactly what directing is and how complicated it is and how these movies specifically are so hard, you really need to know what you're doing. It can't be your first movie.
