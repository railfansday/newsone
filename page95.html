Scientists Discover Greenland’s Ice Sheet Once Retreated 60 Miles

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Sediment beneath a secret Cold War base reveals a time when warmer summers helped push Greenland’s ice more than 60 miles back from its current edge.

About 416,000 years ago, the ground beneath Greenland’s Camp Century was exposed to the sky. A new University at Buffalo-led study estimates that summers there were 4.7 degrees Celsius (8.5 degrees Fahrenheit) warmer than today, offering a rare glimpse of the conditions that accompanied a major retreat of the ice sheet.

“It’s striking that a period of moderately warmer temperatures coincided with such a substantial retreat of the ice sheet,” says lead author John Michael Aguilar, a postdoctoral fellow at the UB RENEW Institute.

Published in Climate of the Past, the research also points to a crucial factor in Greenland’s future: how long higher temperatures persist.

Camp Century housed the U.S. Army’s failed Project Iceworm, a secret plan to hide nuclear missiles beneath Greenland’s ice. During drilling in the 1960s, researchers recovered sediment from nearly a mile (1.6 kilometers) below the surface. Those samples have since become an unlikely archive of Greenland’s climate.

Earlier work showed that the sediment last saw sunlight approximately 416,000 years ago. Exposing the ground at Camp Century would have required the ice sheet’s edge in that region to retreat more than 60 miles (97 kilometers) from its present position.

The new study goes beyond establishing that the ice disappeared. By examining fatty molecules produced by bacteria and preserved in the sediment, the team reconstructed the summer temperatures that accompanied that retreat.

“The types and proportions of these molecules change depending on the temperature,” Aguilar says. “By comparing them with similar molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, we were able to estimate how warm it was at Camp Century.”

The researchers believe their findings provide the first quantitative estimate of summer temperatures on land anywhere in Greenland during this ancient warm period. Such records are exceptionally difficult to recover because later advances of the ice sheet eroded most sediment from that time.

Surprisingly, Camp Century remained covered by ice even when Greenland experienced similarly warm summers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 years ago, after the last Ice Age.

One possible explanation is that the earlier warm period lasted much longer, potentially tens of thousands of years. Sustained warmth would have given the ice sheet more time to melt, suggesting that peak temperatures alone cannot explain how far it retreats.

“This work gives us a better understanding of how the ice sheet responds to sustained warming and what may be possible in the future,” says corresponding author Elizabeth Thomas, PhD, an associate professor in the Department of Earth Sciences in UB’s College of Arts and Sciences.

The findings arrive as scientists anticipate substantial Arctic warming. Depending on emissions, average annual Arctic temperatures could rise by 3.3 to 10 degrees Celsius (5.9 to 18 degrees Fahrenheit) by the end of this century, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

With that warming ahead, Thomas cautions against assuming that Greenland’s ice will respond as slowly as it did during the ancient warm period.

“Today’s human-caused warming is more intense than the warm period 400,000 years ago,” Thomas says. “And climate projections suggest that this warmth could persist for hundreds or thousands of years. Both the magnitude and duration of warming are important to understanding how the ice sheet will respond.”

The samples nearly slipped out of scientific view. Chester Langway, who led the Camp Century ice core drilling, brought them to UB in 1975. They remained in its Central Ice Core Storage Facility and Information Exchange until his retirement in the mid-1990s, then spent more than two decades largely forgotten at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark.

A chance freezer cleanout in 2018 brought the sediment back to researchers’ attention. Thomas has studied it with University of Vermont collaborators ever since, and its remarkable history became the subject of an Emmy Award-winning documentary.

For Thomas, the latest study brings years of work with Aguilar together. Measuring the molecules was familiar territory; translating them into a reliable comparison with modern temperatures was much harder.

“The type of molecular analysis we did in this study is something we do all the time in my lab, but interpreting the results was unusually challenging. We had to account for how the land’s elevation changed as the ice sheet grew and melted, which affects how temperatures can be compared across time,” Thomas says. “What may look like a routine analysis actually took years to figure out. It’s been quite a journey, not unlike the journey these sediment samples took to get here.”

Reference: “Holocene-like summer climate during Marine Isotope Stage 11 in northwestern Greenland” by John Michael N. Aguilar, Elizabeth K. Thomas, Diana S. Aga, Paul R. Bierman, Jason P. Briner, Isla S. Castañeda and Andrew J. Christ, 28 September 2026, Climate of the Past. DOI: 10.5194/cp-22-1757-2026

One of Star Trek's Best Characters Almost Didn't Exist Because of Gene Roddenberry

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Even though hundreds of notable actors have graced Star Trek with their presence, only one cast member can claim the distinguished title of "most prolific Trek contributor." With 288 onscreen appearances to his name, Michael Dorn's franchise résumé eclipses even his equally legendary Star Trek: The Next Generation co-stars and their Star Trek: The Original Series predecessors. One exquisite Season 3 episode heralds a crucial turning point for both Lieutenant Worf's evolution and Trek's approach to the Klingons — even though Dorn's record-setting, career-defining portrayal faced behind-the-scenes interference from the start.

Despite Worf's immediate intrigue as the first main Klingon character and the lone Klingon to join Starfleet, he spends Season 1 as an isolated background figure — a symbol of the Federation's peaceful alliance with their former antagonists. Once Worf replaces Tasha Yar (Denise Crosby) as the Enterprise's chief of security, he assumes a pivotal role and demonstrates perhaps the most substantial growth out of the entire ensemble. Fond of prune juice, Klingon opera, romantic poetry, and cosplaying American Westerns, Worf survives The Next Generation's seven-season run and its quartet of feature films, joins Star Trek: Deep Space Nine, and reunites with his original crew in Star Trek: Picard's final season as an elite Starfleet Intelligence agent — complete with white hair and a new propensity for witty retorts, meditation, and imbibing tea.

Yet Star Trek's architect, Gene Roddenberry, almost prevented this austere, enthralling, and multifaceted fan favorite from walking onto the Enterprise bridge. According to interviews collected by Mark A. Altman and Edward Gross for their book The 50-Year Mission: The Next 25 Years, Roddenberry wasn't interested in developing the Klingons beyond their restrictive origins as recurring foils whose aggressive nature employs racially-coded stereotypes. This sentiment led Roddenberry, in the words of creative consultant David Gerrold, to "adamantly" resist "[my suggestion of a Klingon first officer]. Gene vetoed it for four months." Even after Roddenberry conceded, executive producer Rick Berman revealed that "Michael Dorn wasn’t even guaranteed all episodes in the first season."

Six decades after The Original Series premiered, the Klingons are regarded as one of the canon's most prominent, recognizable, and sublimely realized alien species. Before The Next Generation Season 3 — the first installment to hit its creative stride — the spin-off had already opened the door for a more sensitive, if still imperfect, depiction. The character beats and plot threads established in the third season's seventeenth episode, "Sins of the Father," written by Ronald D. Moore and W. Reed Moran from a story by Drew Deighan, reverberate for years to come.

Moore displayed an early aptitude for crafting engrossing Klingon-oriented stories. Moore's collective influence and his collaboration with Moran on "Sins of the Father" expands the Klingon Empire into a culture thrumming with vibrant life. Their spiritual practices, feudalistic government, home planet (seen for the first time in "Sins of the Father"), language, and the immense value they place on battle prowess and warrior honor code surpass archetypal words.

Can a new live-action series maintain fans' passion for Star Trek, or will it be another disappointment?

Likewise, "Sins of the Father" grants Worf's underutilized potential a narrative purpose beyond the metaphorical. His upbringing as an orphan separated from his civilization and adopted by doting human parents exacerbates his conflicted duality. Worf strives to prove his worth by honoring his biological heritage and fulfilling his duty to Starfleet, and refuses to accept that he can't unify that contradictory schism. His Enterprise colleagues gradually accept him, while the Qo'noS-born Klingons dismiss him as a weakling who willingly betrayed his birthright.

In "Sins of the Father," Kurn (Tony Todd), the younger brother Worf believes had perished alongside their parents during the infamous Khitomar Massacre, visits the Enterprise to disclose their connection and employ his sibling's aid. Duras (Patrick Massett), the leader of a rival house and a prominent member of the Klingon High Council, has accused the brothers' late father, Mogh, of forming a treasonous alliance with the Romulans. What befalls an ancestor taints their entire bloodline, so Worf vows to prove his father's innocence. If his claim fails, then the sons of Mogh will both die by execution.

Assisted in person by an unwavering Captain Picard (Patrick Stewart) and from afar by the loyal, evidence-scouring Enterprise crew, Worf uncovers a nefarious conspiracy. Duras is framing Mogh to deflect suspicion from his own father, the real traitor, and Chancellor K’mpec (Charles Cooper), the High Council’s leader, has advanced the lie to avoid sparking a civil war. Worf's challenge was doomed long before he issued it.

Worf can’t fathom betraying his familial responsibility, let alone a major legal body condoning such injustice. Nevertheless, he understands the preventative reason behind their cover-up and accepts discommendation (a non-lethal ceremony where individuals endure public dishonor) as long as the Council spares Kurn's life. As Worf's fellow Klingons declare him a coward and turn their backs, leaving him abandoned and shunned, the outlier who studied his culture from afar models a far deeper comprehension of honor, sacrifice, integrity, and courage than the rulers who spout such values but fail to walk the talk.

Between the meaty material and Les Landau's direction, Dorn's magnetism, gravitas, and pathos enjoy their richest opportunity to shine to date. Roddenberry gave Dorn leeway to develop Worf as he saw fit, and the incisive actor-character synergy that elevates Worf into a series-stealing standout is on full display through Worf's dignified resolve, his steely determination, the strength of his principles, and Dorn's extensive range. Since Worf tends to be a man of few words, the ones he does utter carry lethally significant weight.

Meanwhile, his wordless micro-expressions carry enough gravity to sustain multiple planets. Dorn carries Worf through an array of demandingly complex emotions: repressing his fury over Kurn's needling comments, grappling with the appearance of his lone living relative, solemnly yet proudly shouldering the weight of his parents' legacy, his profound gratitude and admiration for Picard, and reconciling with how the Empire's corrupt leadership has shattered the idealized vision of his people Worf carries.

History repeated itself when The Next Generation's production group defended Season 4's "Redemption," a two-part finale following up on "Sins of the Father." In Moore's 2021 interview with the Hollywood Reporter, he shared: "[Gene] didn’t really see Worf as a primary character. [...] And this was going to be the series' 100th episode on top of it. So, we had to fight somewhat to get the episode going." In true warrior fashion, the team's commitment ensured Worf's victory against every obstacle in his path. "Sins of the Father" guides Worf toward his endgame – an assured and complete identity — as an ethical soldier, a diplomatic ambassador, a father, a devout friend, and a widower. A defining moment in Star Trek history, it's a focus for Worf and Dorn that was long overdue.

A little-known protein could be a major culprit in type 2 diabetes

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Researchers have identified a protein that may play an important role in Type 2 diabetes by interfering with the body's ability to respond to insulin. The discovery could provide a promising new target for treatments designed to improve blood sugar control.

The protein, known as Miro1, was found at higher levels in the skeletal muscles of people with Type 2 diabetes. In preclinical experiments, scientists discovered that removing Miro1 restored the muscles' response to insulin. They also found that exercise lowered Miro1 levels in people with the disease, offering new insight into how physical activity improves insulin sensitivity.

The findings were published in Nature Communications and could help researchers better understand the biological processes behind insulin resistance, a central feature of Type 2 diabetes.

How a Hidden Muscle Protein Affects Insulin Resistance

Miro1 (Mitochondrial Rho GTPase1) is located on the outer membrane of mitochondria, the tiny structures inside cells responsible for producing energy. Researchers believe that excessive amounts of this protein may interfere with how muscles respond to insulin, making it more difficult for the body to regulate blood sugar effectively.

To investigate this connection, the research team studied Miro1 in laboratory cells, preclinical models, and people living with Type 2 diabetes. By examining the protein across these different settings, they were able to explore its effects on muscle function and determine whether the findings could be relevant to human disease.

The results showed that Miro1 buildup impaired the muscles' response to insulin. When researchers removed the protein in preclinical models, insulin responsiveness returned, providing evidence that Miro1 contributes directly to this process.

Exercise Reduces a Protein Linked to Type 2 Diabetes

The researchers also investigated whether exercise could influence Miro1 levels in people with diabetes. In a randomized controlled trial involving participants with obesity and Type 2 diabetes, they compared the effects of aerobic exercise with those of standard medical care.

People assigned to the exercise group experienced a decline in Miro1 levels in their muscles. These changes occurred alongside improvements in insulin sensitivity and the muscles' capacity to produce energy.

The findings suggest that reducing Miro1 may be one of the ways exercise helps the body respond more effectively to insulin. They also raise the possibility that treatments designed to target the protein could eventually provide additional benefits alongside physical activity.

"Insulin resistance in muscle is at the heart of Type 2 diabetes, and its molecular causes have remained frustratingly unclear. This study, the product of years of work, identifies Miro1 as a key part of the answer. By tracing Miro1 from cells to preclinical models to patients, we show that Miro1 accumulation impairs the muscle's response to insulin, that removing it causally restores that response in our models, and that exercise reverses its buildup in people. For the field, this is a new therapeutic target. For patients, it deepens our understanding of why exercise is so effective and opens a path toward treatments that could one day complement it," said Dr. John P. Kirwan, the study's senior author.

A Promising New Direction for Diabetes Treatment

Kirwan recently became executive director of the OU Health Harold Hamm Diabetes Center at the University of Oklahoma. He carried out the research while working at LSU's Pennington Biomedical Research Center and has since brought his internationally recognized translational research program to the University of Oklahoma.

He also serves as Vice Provost for Diabetes Programs at the university and holds the Chickasaw Nation Endowed Chair in Diabetes Research.

The study's first author, Elizabeth Heintz, is a doctoral candidate who worked in the Integrated Physiology and Molecular Medicine Laboratory at Pennington Biomedical. Christopher Axelrod, the study's co-corresponding author, has also moved to the Harold Hamm Diabetes Center, joining Kirwan in continuing this line of research.

Dr. Jennifer Rood, Interim Senior Vice Chancellor and Executive Director of Pennington Biomedical, emphasized the importance of connecting laboratory discoveries with research involving patients and broader populations.

"This research exemplifies the breadth of scientific discovery at Pennington Biomedical, where research spans all levels, from basic science that reveals the biological mechanisms underlying disease to clinical studies and population-based research that informs public health," said Dr. Jennifer Rood, Interim Senior Vice Chancellor and Executive Director of Pennington Biomedical. "Dr. Kirwan and his team's findings on Miro1 demonstrate the value of this integrated approach, connecting fundamental discoveries about muscle function with insights into insulin resistance and the benefits of exercise in people with Type 2 diabetes. This work is advancing our understanding of diabetes and helping lay the foundation for future approaches to prevention and treatment."

Could Targeting Miro1 Lead to New Diabetes Therapies?

Although the results are encouraging, scientists still need to establish whether directly targeting Miro1 can produce safe and effective treatments for people with Type 2 diabetes. The restoration of insulin responsiveness was demonstrated in preclinical models, while the human trial showed that exercise reduced Miro1 levels alongside improvements in insulin sensitivity.

Kirwan plans to continue investigating these findings at the Harold Hamm Diabetes Center. His research will combine laboratory experiments with studies involving people to uncover more of the biological mechanisms responsible for diabetes and explore new strategies for preventing and treating the condition.

"Type 2 diabetes is an affliction in Oklahoma and nationally," he said. "Ultimately, we are looking for cures, and we hope these recent findings could be part of the solution."

The study received funding from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, the National Institute on Aging, the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, and the National Institute of General Medical Sciences.

Pope Leo urges Argentina's Milei to put people before the economy

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pope Leo XIV has urged Argentina to protect democracy and ensure economic growth benefits the poor, delivering a message to President Javier Milei over austerity, social welfare and political divisions.

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Argentina on Sunday, urging to protect democracy and ensure economic growth benefits the poor.

The pope's remarks were widely watched for criticism of President Javier Milei's harsh austerity programs and cuts affecting social services.

While Leo did not address the government's policies directly, he said "the economy must be at the service of the people, rather than people being at the service of the economy."

Talking to Milei and other government officials, he pointed to the plight of those who, "despite hard work, nonetheless struggle to meet their household's needs."

With regards to democracy, he said it was "part of Argentina's heritage that needs to be safeguarded."

The visit to Buenos Aires marks the second leg of the pope's South America tour and is the first papal visit to Argentina in nearly 40 years.

The 71-year old pontiff, who underwent surgery for lung cancer less than a month ago, arrived from Uruguay and is scheduled to visit several cities in the Catholic-majority country before continuing to Peru on Wednesday.

Milei's policies have in the past drawn strong criticism from Argentina's Catholic Church, particularly cuts affecting social services, pensions, disability assistance and public universities.

The Argentine leader, a close ally of US President Donald Trump, has defended his aggressive spending cuts as necessary to stabilize the country's ailing economy and curb inflation.

Annual inflation has fallen from more than 200% to approximately 33% under his administration, but households continue to face financial pressure, and there's been anger over public-sector layoffs and business closures.

In the past, he agressively dismissed criticsm from the Catholic Church, going as far as calling Leo's predecessor, Pope Francis, an "imbecile." He had later though softened his tone and met with Francis in Rome.

On Sunday, Milei struck a significantly more conciliatory note, praising Francis as an "exceptional figure" and the "most important Argentine in history."

Pope Leo also stressed the need to safeguard democracy in the country.

"Those who exercise authority have a duty to act with prudence, to acknowledge the limits of the power entrusted to them, and to direct their decisions toward the good of the entire community."

The Pope's words carry particular significance ahead of Argentina's presidential election in 2027, in which Milei has said he will seek another term.

The president's aggressive rhetoric and frequent attacks on journalists and political opponents are widely seen as part of an increasing political polarization in the country.

Leo told Milei that strengthening democracy required a "willingness to listen" and the "careful use of words."

His visit marks the first time a pope has traveled to Argentina since John Paul II's trip in 1987. It also brings renewed attention to the legacy of Francis, the country's first pope, who died in April 2025 without returning to his homeland during his 12-year papacy.
