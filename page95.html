Ways to tell if someone blocked your phone number on iPhone

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A missing read receipt or an unanswered FaceTime call could mean a block, or it could just be a bad signal.

Let's be very clear about this — if someone has blocked your phone number, whether on iPhone or Android, that's their right and you shouldn't disregard it. They probably don't want to talk to you, and deliberately trying to circumvent that could constitute harassment, maybe even at the criminal level. It's better to just move on.

That said, there's no inherent harm in trying to determine if you've been blocked. It may even be crucial if you have an honest reason to think it was a mistake. While blocking a number by accident is unlikely, it's not impossible.

Since you're using an iPhone, the clues are mostly in Apple's Phone, Messages, and FaceTime apps. But you may want to expand your sleuthing to third-party messaging platforms, depending on how you previously communicated with someone.

Your biggest clue is likely to be a call in the Phone app that goes straight to voicemail after one ring or no ring at all. You can only be sure after multiple calls at varying hours, however. That's because with a single call, or several calls within a short timeframe, there may be alternative explanations. Perhaps the person has Do Not Disturb or another Focus mode on. It could also be that their cellular or Wi-Fi signal is bad, that their phone is off or even that they're in the middle of an iOS or Android update, which requires a lengthy reboot.

A faster way of confirming things is by calling from a second number if you have one, such as Google Voice or a work phone. Exercise caution here, since this could be seen as harassment. Passive options are always preferable. Besides, if someone knows (or knew) you well enough, there's a good chance they've blocked all your normal contact options.

Further signs that you've been blocked could include FaceTime calls failing to connect, or absent read receipts in the Messages app even though it's been hours or days since your last text. Once again, there may be other issues at play. Apart from bad signal quality or a phone being turned off, if they're an iPhone owner, it could be that they've disabled iMessage, or that Apple's servers are temporarily offline. Android phones won't provide receipts at all unless they're using RCS instead of SMS.

While third-party apps like WhatsApp, Signal and Telegram are often linked to your phone number, blocking someone via iOS settings doesn't affect those apps, for the simple reason that they rely on frameworks beyond Apple's control. Missing read receipts or unanswered calls could be evidence that you've been blocked across multiple platforms. However, they might mean something else, such as Meta or Telegram experiencing technical difficulties.

You also can't read too much into automated replies, regardless of the app. They can, however, be used as a kind of "soft" block, discouraging you from calling or texting without seeming so harsh. If you're encountering an iPhone's automatic call screening when it wasn't there before, the other person may have deleted you from their Contacts app, which could make it the most obvious sign that you should stop.

Unfortunately, that's about for your options. Apple and your carrier can't tell you that you're blocked. That would be unethical anyway, since it would violate the privacy of the blocker. You're just going to have to rely on all the clues we've suggested so far.

The Fed’s first close look at the AI buildout finds $3 trillion of AI commitments that aren’t on big tech’s balance sheet as debt

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The first time the Federal Reserve took a close look at how the AI buildout is being financed, it found more than $3 trillion of promises that don’t show up as debt.

The Fed’s November Financial Stability Report includes a new section, “Leverage in the Data Center Buildout.” None of the report’s five previous editions, going back to 2024, had one. The closest, in November 2025, was about AI and algorithmic trading.

The new section counts more than $1 trillion in leases that are signed but haven’t started yet, plus more than $2 trillion in take-or-pay contracts and residual value guarantees, at Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, and Oracle.

Citing the report in a speech Monday in Milan, Fed Governor Michael Barr put the “potential maximum nominal value” of those obligations above $3 trillion. He said the seven largest AI firms’ on-balance-sheet debt “is expected to exceed $800 billion before year’s end.” The Fed notes the off-balance-sheet figures are undiscounted and “not directly comparable” with debt.

Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle didn’t immediately respond to Fortune’s requests for comment.

It raises the question of who takes the hit if AI demand comes in short. The report says the arrangements let companies with strong credit support weaker-credit projects “without having an immediate, direct effect on the balance sheets” of the big firms. Data-center developers use those leases to finance construction, and the riskier slices of the resulting debt are held by high-yield bond funds, life insurers, business development companies, and other private debt vehicles. The largest U.S. bank holding companies have “close to $1 trillion of exposure” to large technology firms, data-center developers, AI-related special purpose entities, private credit vehicles, and utilities, according to the Fed.

Barr warned the opacity “can exacerbate selloffs when investor sentiment shifts, as it is hard to know where risk resides.” Separately, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said Monday Board staff have started modeling AI scenarios, from a growth boom to an AI disappointment.

Debt issuance tied to advanced computing rose to more than $680 billion by October, the Fed found, about five times the $130 billion issued in all of 2023. Advanced computing’s share of nonfinancial investment-grade corporate debt issuance went from less than 6% in 2023 to about 31% this year. The Fed estimates outstanding public debt tied to advanced computing at more than $1.1 trillion and says private credit deals make the full total harder to measure.

Market players are worried, too. The Fed surveyed 23 contacts at broker-dealers, banks, investment funds, and advisory firms in September and October, and 87% named AI as a salient risk to the financial system, up from 50% in the May survey. A broad correction in risk assets came next, at 52%.

The report reflects data through Oct. 28, two weeks before Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned.

Huang resigned Nov. 12 as president, CEO, and board member of Nvidia, and CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO. Nvidia reports third-quarter results after the close today. Analysts expect revenue of about $108 billion, CoinDesk reported, and said Kress “faces questions about demand, financing commitments, and China exposure.”

Nvidia’s filings show the kind of commitments the Fed is counting. In August, the chipmaker agreed to residual value guarantees with SB Energy, the landlord for an AI campus in Pike County, Ohio, where an OpenAI affiliate will be the tenant on about 4.25 gigawatts of IT load. If OpenAI goes insolvent and defaults on a lease, or fails to make payments, Nvidia generally covers the gap between the lease’s guaranteed minimum value and what’s recovered. Its total obligation is “cumulatively capped at $105 billion” for the initial commitment, subject to conditions that include the sites being ready for service, expected beginning in 2028. OpenAI has agreed to reimburse Nvidia for anything it pays. Separately, Nvidia’s latest quarterly filing lists $3.5 billion in guarantees on AI cloud partners’ data center leases as of July 26, against which those partners have put $712 million in escrow. Nvidia, SB Energy and OpenAI didn’t immediately respond to requests for comment.

Barr said such guarantees “represent the degree to which hyperscalers and GPU producers might absorb losses” if borrowers default.

Telecom equipment makers tried a smaller version of this during the dot-com boom. Lucent committed $5.7 billion in credit to customers in 2000, eWeek reported at the time. One of those customers, Winstar, had a $2 billion vendor financing deal with Lucent. It filed for bankruptcy in April 2001 and sued Lucent for $10 billion. Four years later, a bankruptcy judge ordered Lucent to pay $244 million.

Arista takes aim at the next AI network battleground: distributed inference

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

Awareness of the network’s role in AI has steadily increased, with much of the focus on the back end – larger clusters, faster ports, and scale-across fabrics that stitch GPUs together across data centers. Arista Networks Inc. has seen continued, accelerated growth and has been one of the biggest winners of that buildout.

This week at SC26, Arista announced a shift in focus to the other side of the network. The company also announced a new set of Etherlink R4 routing platforms designed for what it calls distributed inferencing, along with a pluggable optical line system for data center interconnect. The lineup includes:

All are based on Broadcom’s Jericho and Qumran silicon, designed to deliver up to 60% lower power consumption than competing 7nm and 16nm platforms. Everything is available now, except the 7020HR4, which ships in early 2027. The speeds and feeds always make headlines, but the implications of the launch point to where AI infrastructure is heading.

During a pre-briefing, Brendan Gibbs, Arista’s AVP of product line management, argued that the forces driving training across multiple sites will have the same effect on inference. Gibbs also said that roughly 30% of Arista’s AI business is now scale-across, indicating that distributed AI is rapidly becoming commonplace.

Inference will be distributed for three reinforcing reasons:

The implication is that the front-end network, long treated as plumbing, now needs back-end attributes: deep buffers, traffic engineering, multi-tenancy, and encryption, because traffic now leaves the building.

This launch is the opposite of the descriptors we typically associate with Arista. Recent announcements focused on dense 800G, 1.6T, and liquid cooling. The products launched at SC26 are smaller and less expensive. Arista dominates big iron and is now rounding out its portfolio. Arista’s customers range from hyperscalers to neoclouds to enterprises building local AI. The front end needs rich features, not massive bandwidth, and Arista hasn’t had a viable option for that tier until now.

These routers are designed as entry-level but ship with 400G uplinks, which may seem like overkill today but offer investment protection. Gibbs said the goal is a 10-year life cycle, with customers swapping optics rather than chassis. No one knows what agents will do to traffic volumes, and I suspect companies that consider this overkill today will think differently in three or four years. Headroom is cheaper than a forklift upgrade at a remote site. Also notable: Arista co-designed the 7280R4 with a hyperscaler seeking a gray-to-coherent bridge, a sign that it’s building with its biggest customers.

The most intriguing product is the hardened 7020HR4, built for 5G and AI-RAN cell sites. Telcos have largely sat out the AI buildout, yet they own the last mile and real estate near users, exactly what low-latency inference requires. This gives a carrier the backbone to offer a managed AI factory.

My view is that this performs better outside the U.S., where enterprises don’t trust carriers beyond connectivity. In Europe and Asia-Pacific, managed services are far more common, and providers such as BT, NTT, and Telstra have the footprint to make it work. With sovereignty requirements, the opportunity for telcos to move up the stack has never been better. Telcos have never been a core market for Arista, so any growth from that audience is additive to their already massive TAM.

Since 2019, Arista has reported a far lower CVE count than competitors, even after the surge in AI-assisted discovery following Anthropic’s Mythos. I’m skeptical of CVE comparisons because counts depend on testing rigor, installed base, and reporting practices, and some vendors now bundle multiple issues into a single CVE.

The more relevant benefit is operational. Customers often don’t apply patches because upgrades cause downtime and break things. If they trust the software, they patch faster, and the risk window shrinks. With Mythos accelerating the retirement of end-of-support gear, quality and longevity become buying criteria, not nice-to-haves. On the call, Gibbs agreed it’s hard to draw conclusions from CVE counts but explained that CVE counts were a measure of Arista’s software quality. This has been Arista’s calling card since it was a startup. While CVEs provide one metric, the bigger proof point is its historical track record of quality software.

Also, wire-speed MACsec and IPsec on every port is a differentiator because AI brings different security requirements. However, integrated security does not replace firewalls. I asked Gibbs about this, and he said that Arista has no intention of replacing firewalls and expects Palo Alto Networks to continue to sit behind it in the line. I’d like to see that formalized into validated designs for distributed inference, like what Nvidia-validated designs did for the back end.

The optical line system is a similar simplification play. Arista isn’t an optical company and is reselling these products, but offering dark fiber customers a turnkey, EOS-integrated way to move 25.6 Tbps across a metro network removes a real headache.

Customers have responded positively to Arista’s entry into this market. Douglas Fast Net CTO Jon Vradenburg said the company needs “a compact platform that supports the services our business and carrier customers rely on.” Takayuki Sakou of Japan’s STNet cited “higher throughput and improved power efficiency while maintaining the quality of the EOS software we trust.”

The back end of AI networking is largely a solved architecture at this point. The front end is not, and that’s where the next round of competition will play out. With R4, Arista is betting that its routing credibility, built quietly within hyperscaler backbones, can make it the default there as well. Given how much traffic agentic AI is likely to generate, that’s a bet that, like most Arista bets, will likely pay off.

Zeus Kerravala is a principal analyst at ZK Research, a division of Kerravala Consulting. He wrote this article for SiliconANGLE.
