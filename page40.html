5 Ian Evatt replacements Blackpool must consider ASAP

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Blackpool have confirmed the sacking of Ian Evatt following a year in charge at Bloomfield Road, with the Tangerines now intent on reviving their fortunes under fresh management and avoiding a battle against relegation from League One.

Evatt had led the side to a respectable 13th-placed finish during the previous campaign after taking over from Steve Bruce, though the former Bolton Wanderers boss would struggle to get going once again at Bloomfield Road this term.

Last Saturday's 1-0 defeat away to fellow strugglers AFC Wimbledon marked the sixth in just ten games for Blackpool, who now find themselves only positioned above Doncaster Rovers in 21st by virtue of goal difference at this moment in time.

Blackpool's key decision-makers eventually listened to intensified sack calls from supporters by relieving Evatt of his duties on Thursday afternoon, and whilst the relegation-threatened third-tier outfit have confirmed that Steve Thompson will be in charge for this weekend's clash with Wigan Athletic, the search for a replacement has already begun.

With that in mind, FLW has taken a close look at FIVE managers who Blackpool should be seriously considering as potential successors to Evatt.

One manager who should surely feature within Blackpool's thinking is Andy Woodman who, remarkably enough, currently finds himself out of work following a controversial EFL sacking for the ages from League One rivals Bromley.

Woodman had, of course, inspired the plucky Ravens' unprecedented rise into the EFL pyramid for the first time in their history before winning the League Two title against all odds last season, although matters both on and off the field in that particular corner of South London have not been plain-sailing as of late.

Nonetheless, it was a huge surprise when the 52-year-old's historic 289-match reign at Bromley came to an abrupt end last month, and one suspects he won't be sat around unattached for too long.

We rank all 24 League One stadiums by their ground's distance from the city or town, starting with the longest distance.

Woodman's resourceful ability to get the very best out of what was at his disposal with the Ravens certainly reads well, and he frequently elevated and identified talent with great success.

His strong body of work, albeit at a lower level, means that he should be entering the thinking of the Tangerines hierarchy in the coming days.

A previous specialist of the firefighting genre, Gary Rowett's knack in that field has faded in recent times and he may need to re-attempt his hand in a lower division.

As such, Rowett could represent the most outstanding realistic candidate available to Blackpool right now, with the former Derby County, Millwall and Stoke City boss having been linked with the vacancy at promotion hopefuls Bradford City prior to Michael Duff's appointment.

The last few years haven't reflected too kindly on Rowett, who led both Birmingham City and Leicester to relegations while setting Oxford United up for the same fate in the same season that he was in charge of the Foxes' demotion last time out.

Therefore, the wily 52-year-old may not score favourably in the consideration of Championship decision-makers and could instead drop to League One for the first time in his managerial career.

Previously, of course, Rowett has excelled in firing teams towards safety and, though much of his fresher work has been conducted on a short-term basis, there's no reason why he couldn't provide a stable, steady and longer-term presence with the Seasiders.

Matt Bloomfield, meanwhile, having reportedly held talks with Blackpool following the aforementioned Bruce's departure in October of last year, is only likely to re-emerge as a contender for the Tangerines' top job in the near future.

Bloomfield was ultimately unable to prevent Oxford's relegation to League One after succeeding Rowett, yet many believed his subsequent sacking from the Kassam Stadium to represent a surprise and even harsh call - albeit Aaron Ramsey has since vindicated the Yellows' change in direction.

Still aged just 42, Bloomfield's stock has taken a few hits and he's perhaps yet to recover from an ill-fated spell in charge of Luton Town, in which he fell narrowly short of ensuring survival on the 2024/25 second-tier term's final-day before failing to inspire a tangible promotion push in League One.

He has, however, previously excelled at this level, with Wycombe Wanderers having been situated just two points behind eventual-runaway champions Birmingham at the time of his departure to Luton in January 2025.

Bloomfield may now require a sustained spell back in League One leading a side with more modest expectations such as the Chairboys, where he was able to work his magic - and Blackpool can provide just that.

In contrast to Rowett and Bloomfield, Stuart Maynard lacks pedigree above League Two level, but that shouldn't rule the ex-Notts County and York City boss out of contention.

Indeed, at the age of 46 and with an obvious appetite to prove his managerial mettle higher up the pyramid, Maynard could be an ideal candidate for Blackpool in their efforts to not only survive, but begin laying the foundational blueprint for longer-term stability and success.

Much like Woodman at Bromley, Maynard's dismissal from York in the wake of promotion back to League Two following a decade-long exile as National League champions with no fewer than 108 points was scarcely believable. The difference, of course, is that Maynard did not even receive the opportunity to lead the Ministermen into the current campaign, and their loss could turn out to be Blackpool's gain.

Maynard has a solid body of work to boast of from his time at Notts County, where he failed to deliver promotion but did take the Magpies into the fourth-tier play-offs during their second season back at the level and ultimately put the foundations in place for Martin Paterson to go one better with the Meadow Lane outfit.

Blackpool fans, having sat through an overly-patient and slow-paced possession-based style under Evatt, would likely warm to a more dynamic, attack-oriented approach through Maynard, who would tick many boxes for the League One strugglers.

A more left-field option with greater risk but no shortage of potential upside, Ryan Mason remains available following his departure from West Bromwich Albion at the start of the year and represents an interesting candidates for lower-ranked EFL outfits to consider.

Mason was highly-thought-of during his time coaching Tottenham Hotspur and even taking occasional interim charge of the Lilywhites, which meant that many were surprised when the one-cap ex-England international failed to get going at West Brom.

He would take just nine wins from 27 matches across all competitions with the Baggies before being dismissed ahead of a relegation battle in the deeper stages of the previous campaign, with James Morrison going on to prove just how much his two predecessors in Mason and Eric Ramsey had underperformed at the Hawthorns.

It was, however, Mason's first senior, full-time gig, and it would be harsh to judge him entirely from that alone.

Blackpool fans, was it the right time to sack Ian Evatt? Who would be your number-one replacement choice, and what do you make of FLW's selections? Let us know your thoughts in the comments below!

Mason showed plenty of signs at West Brom that suggest he'd be a more-than-capable candidate somewhat lower down the ladder, with his progressive style, experience with young talent and connections within the game all representing healthy selling points for a club in Blackpool's position.

It's essentially a case of risk versus reward, and only Blackpool's key boardroom figures can decide whether it's a gamble worth taking amid the threat of relegation to League Two.

They do have a history of appointing older managers with their best years very much in the past, though, and there are worse options than going for a change of tact and rolling the dice on Mason.

Bitcoin fell as low as $62k late Saturday, its sixth straight weekly decline. Meanwhile, tokenized stocks crossed $4 billion in onchain value for the first time on Nov 11. Tokenized stocks are now roughly five times their value on January 1.

Tokenized stocks track equity prices, not crypto, so a falling BTC doesn’t directly pull their prices down. What stands out, however, is that crypto users appear to keep buying them through the selloff. Demand for 24/7 trading and onchain access to US equities appears to have found strong product-market fit as they are holding up even as the rest of the crypto market weakens.

In September, rising share prices drove most of the growth in value of tokenized stocks. Since mid-October, new minting has driven the growth in value even as share prices dropped. Over the eleven weeks to Nov 13, minting added $1.12 billion while price moves took away $339 million. Binance’s bStocks accounted for about four fifths of that minting, led by NVIDIA, SpaceX and Circle.

Market value has stalled over the past two weeks. We expect minting to pick back up again with the launch of OKXICE on Nov 23 opening large-cap tokenized stocks to US retail.

Securitize reported $11.5 million in tokenization revenue in Q3, its highest on record and 28% more than Q3 2025. Securitize's average tokenized assets reached a record $5 billion as of Q3 as BlackRock, Ark Invest and Neuberger brought new products onchain.

Tokenization revenue is more variable because it rises and falls with issuance, while asset servicing has settled around $7 million a quarter. Securitize's acquisition of River One could help smooth out those revenue swings and diversify its top line. Lending against tokenized assets adds recurring income on top of issuance. We expect credit to show up as Securitize's third revenue line within a year of the deal closing.

Most of the roughly $4 billion of tokenized stock sits in wrappers that US investors can't buy. Only about 10% is open to American investors, and almost all of that is crypto companies including Securitize, Figure, Exodus and Forward Industries. NVIDIA, SpaceX and the other large names are effectively offshore only.

That could change on Nov 23, when OKXICE opens to US customers who have onboarded through its CeDeFi Wallet. It’s the first venue built for American retail at scale, so the onshore share should grow post-launch. We expect issuer-backed listings to drive it, with 67 companies having opted out.

MegaRobo raises nearly $200M to let AI run real lab experiments

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Oman's sovereign wealth fund joins a round for robot labs that test what AI designs.

Chinese lab automation company MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M in a round joined by the Oman Investment Authority and CMB International. It builds systems that let AI design experiments, run them in robotic labs and learn from the results, and plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

China’s MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M to build labs where AI can run its own experiments, the company announced on Thursday. The Oman Investment Authority, the country’s sovereign wealth fund, joined the round, along with new investor CMB International.

Existing backers Asia Investment Capital, Granite Asia and Sinovation Ventures also put in more money. MegaRobo did not disclose its valuation.

AI can read papers and predict molecules, but science still needs real tests. MegaRobo links AI models to robots and lab tools. A model can plan a test, run it and use the results to pick the next one.

“That shift changes AI from a system that interprets the world into one that can help create new knowledge, materials, processes and products,” said founder and CEO Daniel Huang.

MegaRobo says it has more than 500 customers, mostly drug makers, biotech firms and research labs. Its Megalaxy Lab lets scientists and AI agents send tests to robot labs, and has served more than 200 of them.

It has also launched Poreva Open, a protein design contest with Tencent Cloud and DNA maker Twist Bioscience. Megalaxy Lab will test more than 10,000 AI-designed proteins in a real lab, to set a shared benchmark built on actual results. Anthropic, for one, had outside labs check Claude’s protein designs in August.

Money is pouring into AI drug design. Alphabet’s Isomorphic Labs raised $5B on Tuesday, and Chai Discovery raised $400M in July. In Berlin, Dunia Innovations is building a €280M robotic lab to test what AI designs.

MegaRobo says it has raised about $600M in total, including a $300M Series C backed by Goldman Sachs in 2022. It plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia, and to use the same technology beyond science, much like Jeff Bezos’ physical AI lab.

‘Birthright,’ About Differing Attitudes on Israel, Set For Broadway

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The play follows seven friends through 18 years, starting just after their Birthright trip to Israel.

Birthright, a play by Tony Award winner Jonathan Spector examining differing views about Israel among Jewish-American friends, will move to Broadway this spring.

The play is set for a 20-week run at Broadway’s Hudson Theatre starting Feb. 23, 2027, ahead of a March 15 opening night. It moves to Broadway after a sold-out and critically acclaimed run at the Off-Broadway MCC Theater this past summer. Teddy Bergman directs, making his Broadway debut after helming the Off-Broadway run.

Casting for the Broadway run has not yet been announced.

The play follows seven friends through 18 years, starting just after their Birthright trip to Israel in 2006, tracking their differing and changing attitudes on Israel as well as changes within their own lives. Spector returns to Broadway after last season’s Eureka Day, which won the Tony Award for best revival of a play, and centered on a school board dealing with select parents who did not want to vaccinate their kids.

“Just over two years ago, I was racing to finish a first draft of Birthright before starting rehearsals for Eureka Day at MTC. I could never have fathomed that such a short time later we’d be bringing it to Broadway, when the questions the play is wrestling with still feel so close to our present moment. Writing this was the scariest thing I’ve ever attempted to do, and (perhaps not coincidentally), has become the most meaningful and rewarding. I’m beyond thrilled that a wider audience will have a chance to encounter the play and see the work of our brilliant artistic collaborators,” Spector said.

iOS 27 gave AirPods Pro a convenient new setting, here’s how to use it

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

iOS 27 gave AirPods Pro a variety of new features, but there’s another addition that’s easy to miss: a convenient new setting in Control Center for Adaptive Audio.

Several AirPods models offer excellent Active Noise Cancellation and Transparency modes—especially AirPods Pro 3 (now only $179).

But Adaptive Audio is another option you may or may not have tried. The feature aims to blend the strengths of both ANC and Transparency in a single mode.

And in iOS 27, Apple added a new setting to let you conveniently fine-tune Adaptive Audio to your preferences.

While wearing AirPods, open Control Center on your iPhone and tap the volume slider, then hit ‘Listening Mode’ in the bottom-left corner. Here you’ll see the standard listening mode options.

New in iOS 27 though, when ‘Adaptive’ is selected, you can now use a Liquid Glass slider to adjust whether the mode lets in more or less external noise.

Slide it to the left and Adaptive Audio will more closely resemble Transparency, while sliding it to the right makes it cancel out more noise.

You can also move the new slider incrementally to achieve exactly your ideal balance of Transparency and Noise Cancellation. It’s not limited to just a few preset options.

This Control Center slider is available on all AirPods models that support Adaptive Audio.

AI features could be turned off with a simple toggle, Canva proves

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI has taken over everything in the past few years, with AI features bombarding virtually every app or service available. If you’ve ever wanted to just turn off AI features, Canva proves that it could be a simple toggle, if companies actually wanted to give you a choice.

Canva today announced “AI Controls,” a new toggle that completely turns off generative AI features within the editing app.

Generative AI can be quite helpful in working on posters, editing images, and more, but it’s not everyone’s preference. Canva puts this well:

For some people, AI is the most exciting creative tool in a generation, while others prefer bringing their ideas to life without it. We believe the future of creativity should give everyone the freedom to choose their own path, whether that’s creating with AI, without it, or somewhere in between.

To give its users the choice, Canva’s Permissions menu now has a simple toggle for “Enable Generative AI features.” Toggling it off disables features such as Magic Write and Magic Edit. There’s also an option to limit how much AI-generated imagery appears in the Canva library.

AI has its place, but everyone should have a choice like this, especially when it comes to apps and services used for artistry in the same vein as Canva. Yet, the tech industry continues to go in the opposite direction, shoving AI into every corner whether it’s actually useful or not. Canva proves, though, that it doesn’t have to be that way.

A vanished bird from Haiti may help solve an ancient Pacific mystery

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DNA reveals how snipes and woodcocks reached distant islands, stopped migrating, changed physically, and became vulnerable to extinction.

The sandpiper family includes some of the most extreme long-distance migrants among birds.

Two branches of that family, the snipes and the woodcocks, have behaved quite differently. These birds reached islands again and again, and migration was then abandoned.

Nearly every living species was examined in a new DNA study.

The results suggest that island settlement was accompanied by changes in body size and flying ability. Island species later proved vulnerable when people arrived with rats and cats.

The work was led by Min Zhao, then a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. Colleagues at Virginia Tech and the Field Museum in Chicago also took part.

According to Zhao, the whole project grew out of one extinct bird from Hispaniola. That Caribbean island is shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic.

The dates reported in the study were estimated from family trees built with DNA. The uncertainty attached to them can run to millions of years.

Florida Museum researchers dug the bird’s bones out of a cave in Haiti in 1984.

In 2015, those bones were described by two scientists as a new species of woodcock. Woodcocks are long-billed birds that probe soft soil for food.

One of the two scientists was David Steadman, who is now an emeritus curator at the museum. He is also listed as a co-author of the new study.

Zhao’s team extracted DNA from two of the fossil specimens. The researchers then read the mitochondrial genome, a small set of genes passed from mother to offspring.

Both specimens were placed with the snipes, which form the sister group of the woodcocks. Their closest living relative turned out to be the giant snipe of lowland South America.

The analysis suggested a South American origin for the Haitian bird’s ancestors. The two lineages are thought to have split between 5 and 12 million years ago.

Why, then, did the bones resemble those of a woodcock? The authors think the bird’s build may have been altered by island living, which could explain the mix-up.

The outer wing bone of the Haitian bird was unusually short when compared with its upper wing bone. The authors wrote that it could fly but probably not well.

The team sequenced 45 samples in total. Two were the Haitian fossils and 19 were toe pads taken from old museum specimens. The remaining 24 were fresh tissue samples.

All but one of the 29 living species of snipes and woodcocks were covered by these samples. Specimens were supplied by ten museums and research collections.

A family tree was built from the data, and both groups were traced back to Eurasia.

The ancestors of today’s American birds crossed from the Eastern Hemisphere to the Western Hemisphere four separate times.

One of those crossings took place roughly 20 million years ago. It was then that the ancestor of the American woodcock reached North America.

The researchers could not tell whether the bird arrived across the Atlantic or by way of Alaska.

The Austral snipes are found on the outlying islands of New Zealand. Scientists have long placed these small brown birds in a group of their own.

Some snipes from Eurasia spend each winter in Australia and on nearby islands. The DNA showed that those visitors are not close relatives of the Austral snipes.

Instead, the Austral snipes share a branch with several large South American snipes and the extinct Haitian bird.

Two DNA datasets placed the split between the Austral snipes and their relatives at about 14 million and nearly 18 million years ago, respectively.

The southern continents had broken apart long before either estimate. The snipes’ ancestor most likely crossed the Pacific.

How the crossing happened remains an open question. The authors suggested that birds straying far outside their normal range may have played a role. Such straying was perhaps caused by extreme weather.

The authors also raised a route through Antarctica. Patches of treeless tundra are thought to have lasted there until at least 12.8 million years ago.

Several living snipe species still breed on tundra in the Arctic.

The analysis indicated that the ancestors of both snipes and woodcocks were migrants. Their island descendants are not.

Ten of the 29 living species are found only on islands, and none of those ten migrates.

The habit was lost several times. Woodcocks gave it up when they settled the Malay Archipelago in Southeast Asia. Snipes did the same when they settled South America and Madagascar.

Body size changed as well, although not in a single direction.

The Amami woodcock of southern Japan is about 45% heavier than the Eurasian woodcock, its closest mainland relative. Madagascar’s snipe is about 91% heavier than the common snipe of Eurasia.

The opposite change is seen in the Austral snipes. Their average weight is 3.4 ounces (96 grams), which is about the weight of a deck of cards.

South American snipes on the same branch average 8.6 ounces (243.5 grams). That is more than twice as much.

The smaller size may be explained by scarce food, the authors suggested. Competition from kiwis and other birds that probe the soil for worms was also considered possible.

Ten species of snipes and woodcocks are known to have died out in the recent geologic past. Nine of them lived on islands.

The Austral snipes suffered the heaviest losses. Three of nine known species survive. All three are ranked as Vulnerable or Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List.

The South Island snipe died out in 1964, and introduced black rats are regarded as the likely cause.

No resident snipe or woodcock is left in the Caribbean. At least three species have been lost there.

The youngest dated bones of the Haitian bird are about 600 years old. Rats and feral cats brought by people are suspected of causing its extinction.

Snipes nest on the ground, where they can be reached by those predators. Island species had given up migration, and in some cases strong flight as well.

The authors wrote that these changes made such species especially vulnerable to human impacts.

Overhunting was named as one of those impacts.

Jessica Oswald, a co-author, also worked on the study at the Florida Museum.

“People will eat anything,” said Oswald. She said a crew of hungry explorers or whalers could wipe out an island’s birds in a single weekend.

Island woodcocks in Japan and Southeast Asia appear to have fared better. They live in mountain forests, which may be harder for people to reach.

New Zealand may have been reached first by the Austral snipes.

From there they could have spread to Norfolk Island, New Caledonia and Fiji. The opposite path cannot be ruled out, because no genetic data exist for those islands’ extinct snipes.

More snipe species may once have lived in the Pacific and are now extinct. In that case, today’s birds would be the last survivors of a larger group. Direct fossil evidence of that link across the Pacific is still missing.

The Haitian bird’s place on the tree rests on mitochondrial genes from two specimens. A possible close relative from Puerto Rico is also extinct and has not been tested genetically.

The authors suggest that DNA from fossils yet to be found on Pacific islands could help trace how snipes spread across the region.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
