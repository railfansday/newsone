Agent instructions: in the repo or in the tool?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are two places to put the standing rules you want a coding agent to follow: in a file in the repo, or built into the tool that runs the agent. I've ended up with both, and they trade off differently.

In the repo, the rules are versioned. They show up in pull requests, the whole team sees them, and they travel with a clone. The cost is that you write and maintain them per project, and they're only as good as the last person who edited them.

In the tool, they work in every project and for every agent with no setup. The cost is that they're invisible: nobody reviews a diff of them, and they can clash with how a particular repo does things.

Styx does the second kind. It builds in guidance from a few public repos: Ponytail for coding, Impeccable for interfaces, design-critique for reviewing a design, and architecture-designer for system design. Only the core rules are included, each applies only to that kind of work, and your project's own design system and rules come first.

That last part is the rule I'd defend: when the two disagree, the repo wins, because it knows the project and the tool doesn't.

Your Mac has been keeping a file on your family

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since macOS 13 shipped in 2022, every Mac has run two daemons whose only job is to build a graph about you. Who you are, who you know, how those people relate to you and to each other, how you communicate, where you go, what you do and when. The daemons are called knowledgeconstructiond and intelligenceplatformd, and the closest thing Apple has ever documented about them is a two-sentence man page.

A security duo going by The Bishop and The Knight took the system apart in October and published the anatomy. The graph lives in ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform, fifty-one plaintext SQLite databases protected by macOS's Full Disk Access permission. The core one, graph.db, is a triple store: 181 entity classes, 278 predicates, and a confidence score on every row. What goes into it is basically everything the OS can see. Contacts. Photos, with faces. Calendar titles and attendees. Family Sharing, including the age of every member. The emergency contact in your Medical ID. Events and people Siri Suggestions digs out of your mail and messages. Apps donated through App Intents. Beside the graph sit tables for who appears with whom in your photos, a communication log without content, location and movement, and a stream of device signals: screen locked, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging, ambient light.

Then there is the part the HN headline picked up: a pair of XGBoost classifiers, shipped as CoreML models, that decide who counts as family. They read 144 features and output a probability for seventeen relationship columns per person, recomputed every day. Twenty of the features are identity: same last name, shared home address, an iCloud Family flag, age difference. Forty are communication patterns. Eighty-four come from photographs.

One shipped feature name is worth reading twice: photosPersonPosteriorProbabilityGivenSaturdayNight. A real input to a real classifier, and its job is deciding whether someone is your sister.

The scores look mundane until you look at what produced them. A mother scored 0.960, the ceiling of that column. Nobody typed it. The post counts nine independent signals that land on the same person: the partner column, a composite score, a household entity, the Family Sharing token, an iCloud flag, top twelve-month contact, a Photos face cluster, a Contacts record, a reachable handle. The model joined them and concluded "partner" for someone the owner had never labeled.

The joins are where it stops being about you. From one store, the authors worked out that there is a child in the household and roughly how old, which daycare the child attends along with both phone numbers, where the family will be and between which hours on a given Thursday, and which password manager is installed. A relationship is intelligence one level above the rows it was inferred from.

Deletion does not fully retract any of it. The live graph drops a contact, but a second table, expired_stable_graph, keeps the retracted rows with their confidence and sources, about forty-eight hours by the authors' measurement, longer on their own machines. The scorecard still scored two people a day after the graph had dropped them, people it knew only from a calendar invitation.

The authors also run a thought experiment that applies to any personalized system: what happens when this file ends up in the wrong hands, paired with a local model that has had its guardrails stripped. They call the graph a privacy single point of failure, and they play the scenario out in a second post.

To be fair to Apple: everything happens on device. The databases sit behind the Full Disk Access permission, so other apps cannot read them without the user approving it. Apple's researchers published a paper on the graph techniques back in 2023. And the same store exists on iOS, where a forensics tool called iLEAPP has been reading it since September.

The fair criticism is narrower and harder to wave off. The depth of the inference is undocumented. The kinship classifier is mentioned nowhere. And the graph does not just hold your data; it holds data about everyone who has ever emailed you, appeared in your photos, or been invited to a calendar event, and none of those people were asked.

The authors open-sourced a dashboard called iKnow that reads your own graph locally, if you want to see the file your Mac keeps on you.

Pydio Cells access needs a PAT, workspace, and public-share inventory

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pydio Cells combines identity, file storage, workspaces, OAuth clients, and public sharing. A person who reaches the login page may be blocked before seeing any files, while a leaked personal access token or public link may bypass the browser flow entirely. An exposure assessment that only records the login page leaves the important paths out.

On 2026-11-08, ZoomEye count-only observations returned 0 matches for app="Pydio Cells", 1,470 for title="Pydio Cells", and 2,397 for http.body="Pydio Cells". The query scope was sub_type=all, page 1, and page size 1. The title and body counts are close enough to be useful leads, but neither proves that a host is configured with public shares or weak tokens.

Pydio documents personal access tokens as an alternative to the standard OAuth flow. A token can replace a bearer access token when calling the REST API. It can also be restricted to a specific node with a scope in the form node:NODE_UUID:PERMISSION, where the permission can include read or write.

That scope is useful only when it is applied to the right node and the underlying user still has access to that node. The token should not be treated as an independent grant of filesystem authority. If the user is removed from a workspace or loses a role, the token should lose the effective access as well. Review both the token's scope and the roles that determine the user's workspace ACLs.

Workspaces define which data a user can see through the application. Public links and shares can expose selected data to people who do not have a Cells account. The sharing model may include link permissions and visibility settings. Each share is a capability that should have an owner, a purpose, an expiration where supported, and a revocation path.

Inventory links that are reachable without authentication, including links that are unlisted rather than indexed. A link that does not appear in search results is still reachable by anyone who receives it. Check whether the link grants read only, write, or upload permission and whether the underlying workspace changed after the link was created.

Start from the public host and identify the OAuth, API, and share routes. Confirm that anonymous users can reach only the intended login or share surfaces. Test an API token in a separate test account and verify that it cannot read a workspace the account is not assigned to.

Review user roles, group membership, workspace ACLs, and administrator roles. Then list active personal access tokens by owner and creation date. Revoke tokens that have no owner or no documented consumer. Rotate secrets that were stored in CI logs, browser storage, or public repositories.

For shares, record the creator, target node, permission, and expiration. Test a link after removing the creator's access. Decide whether the link should continue as an independent capability or fail with the user. That policy should be explicit.

Pydio's value comes from flexible delegation. The security work is to inventory every delegation separately: the OAuth client, the personal token, the workspace ACL, and the public share.

Virginia holds fifth place in the current national layoff picture. California and Washington together hold roughly 60% of that total.

This is a November refresh of Virginia's numbers, last spotlighted on October 24. Here is where the state stands now, what changed, and how to read the numbers.

Virginia's full imported archive now holds 2,195 non-cancelled WARN notices reporting 255,368 workers affected. In the last 365 days, the archive contains 74 eligible official filings reporting 8,917 workers, with no worker counts unavailable. The newest official notice date is October 30, 2026. So far in 2026, Virginia has 67 notices reporting 8,394 workers. That works out to about 125 workers per filing on average. The state's 2026 total is already close to its full-year 2024 total of 63 notices and 8,706 workers, and it sits below 2025, when Virginia recorded 112 notices and 12,067 workers. With almost two months of the year left, 2026 is on course to land between those two years.

The mix of sources behind Virginia's numbers is nearly even: 1,138 direct government records and 1,057 attributed secondary records appear in the full archive. Recent official event types are layoffs and closures.

Compared with the October 24 snapshot, Virginia's 2026 figures have grown. Notices for the year rose from 61 to 67, and reported workers rose from 7,735 to 8,394 — an increase of 659 workers in about two weeks. The archive total moved from 2,189 notices and 254,709 workers to 2,195 and 255,368. Official filings in the last 365 days rose from 71 to 74, and their worker total from 8,388 to 8,917. Future effective dates increased from 13 to 16, and the newest official notice date moved from October 7 to October 30.

Virginia's position in the national ranking has also firmed up. On November 3, the state entered the top five with 2,373 workers in the 90-day window. Five days later that figure is 2,546, a gain of 173 workers, and fifth place has firmed up. Virginia's 90-day total is small next to California's 21,514 and Washington's 17,411, but it leads every state outside the top four.

Virginia's recent history shows how uneven layoff years can be. The pandemic year of 2020 dominates the archive: 453 notices and 58,089 workers, nearly five times the worker total of any year since. The years after that fell back sharply — 74 notices and 7,852 workers in 2021, then 60 and 5,220 in 2022. 2023 brought 112 notices and 9,742 workers, 2024 brought 63 and 8,706, and 2025 brought 112 and 12,067.

Further back, totals ran higher: 132 notices and 17,968 workers in 2019, 188 and 19,216 in 2018, 121 and 12,261 in 2017, and 118 and 15,058 in 2016. By that longer view, 2026 is a quiet year for Virginia — below every year from 2012 through 2019, and below 2023 and 2025 as well.

Two Virginia filings stand out in the recent record. The largest is The Washington Post's 118-worker filing for its Springfield, Virginia operation, with notice given on October 7, 2026 and an effective date of December 31, 2026. It is the single biggest Virginia filing in the current window.

The newest is Haynes Furniture's 83-worker filing, with notice on October 30, 2026 and an effective date of November 2, 2026 — a notice gap of just three days, far shorter than the 60 days the WARN Act generally requires. That short gap is the kind of detail worth watching: the law allows shorter notice only in narrow circumstances, such as unforeseeable business circumstances or a faltering company seeking capital.

Both filings sit inside a national calendar whose heaviest week ahead is November 15, with 7,887 workers scheduled, followed by November 22 with 6,614. The newest week, December 13, carries 5,307. Virginia's 16 future effective dates are part of that pipeline, including the Washington Post filing effective on the last day of the year.

Three caveats matter. First, these are reported plans, not confirmed job losses. A WARN notice records what an employer told the state it intends to do; some planned layoffs shrink, some are averted, and some workers are rehired. Second, Virginia's archive blends 1,138 direct government records with 1,057 secondary records, so coverage depends on what sources report and what the registry has imported. A quiet feed is not proof of zero layoffs. Third, the 90-day window shows the current pace, while the full archive shows the state's longer history.

What the numbers do show is a state near the top of the national ranking on a moderate 2026 pace by its own history, with small recent filings and a modest pipeline of 16 future effective dates. The story to watch is whether the heavy November and December weeks bring new Virginia filings with them.

That also switches off the protection. The check is there because of a real 2022 vulnerability, and the situation it guards against is the one you build when you give an agent its own user or container.

On a Linux host, file ownership is a number. A bind mount shows the container the same number the host has. Your repo is owned by your uid, 1001 in the example above, and git inside the container checks it against its own. The quickest way to see a mismatch needs no custom user at all: Docker runs containers as root (uid 0) by default, and 0 isn't 1001, so git stops.

I haven't run this exact command myself. What git checks is in its source: is_path_owned_by_current_uid calls lstat on the path and compares the file's owner with geteuid(). When git runs as root it also accepts the uid in SUDO_UID, which sudo sets and docker run does not. Docker Desktop handles bind mounts its own way, so on macOS or Windows you may not see the error at all.

The non-root user from the opening fails the same way: node at 1000 inside the container, files owned by 1001 on the host, same message.

The check comes from CVE-2022-24765. When you run git in a folder, it walks up the parent directories looking for a .git. On a shared machine, someone else could create one above you. The Git 2.30.3 release notes give the examples: a repository in C:\.git, on a mounted network drive, or in a scratch space.

That matters because some config values run programs. GitHub's announcement names core.fsmonitor, which makes git execute a command. Plant a C:\.git\config with that value, and every user who runs git in a folder under C:\ that isn't its own repo runs your command. They don't even have to type git. The release notes point out that a Git-aware shell prompt running git status, or opening the folder in VS Code or Atom, was enough.

CVE-2022-24765: a .git planted in a parent folder could run its owner's commands when you ran git below it.

Vendor docs followed. Atlassian's article lists both wordings. GitHub's actions/checkout gained a set-safe-directory input, on by default, which runs git config --global --add safe.directory for the one workspace path.

The git-config docs put it plainly: "To completely opt-out of this security check, set safe.directory to the string *." Every repository on the machine is then treated as safe, whoever owns it.

Two details in the same docs matter for agents. safe.directory is only read from protected configuration, meaning system, global and command scope, so a repo can't vouch for itself. And a value ending in /* allows every repository under that directory, which is a middle ground but still broader than one path.

In a throwaway container that only ever mounts one repo, * costs you little. There is no second user inside to plant anything. The damage comes when it leaks out of there: into your host ~/.gitconfig because the error showed up once, into a base image the whole team builds on, or onto a shared CI runner.

A second OS user for the agent, a shared box you reach over SSH, a container user writing into a folder your host user also works in: each is the multi-user machine the CVE was written for. The less-trusted user is the agent, and Anthropic's own docs warn that, run with --dangerously-skip-permissions, a dev container doesn't stop a malicious project from exfiltrating anything accessible inside it. If the agent's user can create a .git with core.fsmonitor in a directory above where you later run git, the ownership check is what keeps that command from running as you. With * in your global config, nothing does.

If someone already put safe.directory=* in the system config, the git-config docs say to start your own list with an empty value. That resets it, and the paths you add after it are the only ones trusted.

Tools that run agents in parallel, Hermes included, often give each task its own git worktree. A linked worktree has a .git file, not a directory, and the git-worktree docs say it points back to the main repository's .git/worktrees/ folder. By default that pointer is an absolute host path. Mount only the worktree into a container and the path it names doesn't exist in there.

Two ways around it. Mount the main repository too, at the same path it has on the host. Or link worktrees with relative paths: Git 2.48 added this, set with worktree.useRelativePaths or --relative-paths. The docs warn it enables the extensions.relativeWorktrees extension, which older Git versions can't read. Remember the July 2022 fix as well: the ownership check now covers the worktree, the .git directory and the .git file, so all three have to belong to the container's user, or the worktree path goes in safe.directory.

I build Hermes IDE, so weigh this part with that in mind. Hermes starts each task on its own hermes/ branch in its own worktree, and every agent runs in a real terminal on your machine, as you. The agent's git and your git are the same user there, so the check never fires. The boundary comes in when you add one: an SSH session to a shared box (Hermes supports those), or a container you start from a plain shell session in the task's worktree. That's when the three fixes above apply.

Before you add anything, see where a * might already be hiding. git config --show-origin --get-all safe.directory prints every entry with the file it came from. Entries in a repo's local config show up in that list too, but git ignores them for this check, per the git-config docs. A * in your global or system file is the one to replace with a path.

8 Masterpiece Detective Shows That Are Better Than 'Sherlock'

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Most people around the globe agree that Sherlock Holmes is the greatest fictional detective of all time, with a legacy that has endured for over a century across literature, cinema, games, and countless other forms of art and entertainment. Among the many film and television projects made about Sherlock Holmes over the years, the BBC’s Sherlock is unarguably one of the most beloved. A modern reimagining of Arthur Conan Doyle’s darling detective, the show remains a television landmark of the 21st century. However, the world of detective fiction is exhaustive and forever expanding, with endless new stories that deserve your attention, including some that arguably overshadow this popular icon.

These shows are not necessarily bigger in scale, but they bring a new narrative style to the genre, explore conventions from a new perspective, or feature more complex, mind-bending mysteries that can leave fans in shock, awe, and, in some cases, inspired. With every detective an intriguing character, each of these shows is a must-watch for genre fans. So, without further ado, here’s our selection of detective shows that make Sherlock look small.

A psychological crime thriller written and created by Neil Cross, Luther revolves around the titular detective, John Luther, who is famed for taking down notorious criminals but also infamous for getting consumed by his cases, often resulting in violent and obsessive behavior. The series follows his relentless mission to thwart crime while navigating his personal demons, but mostly his arch-nemesis, Alice Morgan. Idris Elba stars as Luther and Ruth Wilson as Alice, with Steven Mackintosh, Saskia Reeves, Warren Brown, and Dermot Crowley in other notable roles.

A highly acclaimed and popular police procedural of its time, Luther brings a darker, graver tone to the genre, with more emphasis on the psychological narrative of its characters. Idris Elba is widely acclaimed for his powerhouse performance as the troubled detective, as is Ruth Wilson for her impeccable turn as Alice. During its original five-season run, Luther earned sweeping accolades and was widely acclaimed for reviving the form of hard-boiled detective noir with morally grey themes and a contemporary setting.

Created by Joe Penhall, Netflix’s Mindhunter is based on the non-fiction book Mindhunter: Inside the FBI's Elite Serial Crime Unit by FBI agent John E. Douglas and Mark Olshaker. Dramatizing the founding of the agency’s Behavioral Science Unit in the 1970s, the series follows agents Holden Ford and Bill Tench, as well as psychologist Wendy Carr, as they interview imprisoned serial killers and profile them, hoping to use the data to solve ongoing cases. Jonathan Groff, Anna Torv, Holt McCallany, Cotter Smith, and Hannah Gross star in key roles.

Mindhunter puts a deep analytical spin on detective fiction with strong elements of the psychological horror, crime thriller, and murder mystery genres. Through fictional interviews with some of the most notorious killers in American history, like Charles Manson, Jerry Brudos, and Son of Sam, it provides chilling psychological background to their infamous crimes. With its near-perfect storytelling inspired by major real-life crimes and compelling performances that leave a lasting impression, Mindhunter has been widely praised as one of the best true crime shows on Netflix.

Created by Robert Doherty, Elementary is a contemporary reimagination of Arthur Conan Doyle’s Sherlock Holmes, but with very different stories and characterizations, transporting the British detective from London to New York City as a recovering addict. Starring Jonny Lee Miller as Sherlock Holmes and Lucy Liu as Dr. Watson, the CBS drama follows the duo as they help the NYPD with complex cases, using Sherlock’s genius deduction methods and Watson’s investigative skills. Aidan Quinn, Natalie Dormer, Rhys Ifans, Jon Michael Hill, and John Noble round out the main cast.

Elementary never feels like a pastiche of a literary icon; rather, it simply takes the classic characters and themes and invents new stories, modern crimes, and character backstories in a high-energy, modern procedural. Even the case-of-the-week stories are not adapted from Doyle’s originals, making this ABC hit series intriguingly original. Sherlock fans are sure to appreciate the reinvented Watson, Mycroft, and Moriarty, but most importantly Lee Miller’s Sherlock, which is considered one of the best portrayals of Holmes on television.

A great detective is really just a personality with a method attached, which is why we keep remaking the same four of them. Here is yours, what it says about you, and the case for them being the best in the business.

You let people think you are not a threat and then you simply never go away. Columbo is the only major detective show that tells you who did it in the first ten minutes, because the pleasure was never the puzzle, it was watching a rich man slowly realise he is in trouble.

You are the most interesting person in the room and you know exactly how to use it. Rian Johnson built Blanc as a gentleman detective who is also an outsider everywhere he goes, which lets him be charming at people who would never otherwise talk to him.

Everybody tells you everything, and you have never once asked them to. Jessica Fletcher is a crime novelist from a small town in Maine who is so warm and so obviously trustworthy that suspects confess to her essentially as a social courtesy.

You expect to be lied to and you are almost never disappointed. Chandler wrote Marlowe as a man with a rigid private code in a city that has none, which is why he takes the case, solves it, gets nothing out of it and takes the next one.

An anthology crime thriller series created by Nic Pizzolatto, True Detective explores various stories of uncanny crimes and the local police’s attempts to seek truth and deliver justice, while also navigating their personal demons. Each season follows a new homicide case revolving around a disparate set of characters, with the settings spanning Alaska, Arkansas, California, and Louisiana. The show’s revolving ensemble cast features Matthew McConaughey, Woody Harrelson, Colin Farrell, Rachel McAdams, Jodie Foster, and more across seasons.

True Detective is diverse in its storytelling, with styles spanning neo-noir to Southern Gothic, true-crime, and Nordic noir, making every season feel like an expansive new show. But all seasons are bound by the underlying philosophical themes, unsettling local legends, and deeply flawed detectives, which makes True Detective way darker, more complex, and layered than Sherlock. Since its premiere over a decade ago, True Detective has remained highly respected and has won major accolades, including several Emmys and a BAFTA.

Rian Johnson’s first major TV show as a creator, Poker Face is a crime comedy series that uses a case-of-the-week format, centering on Charlie Cale, a casino employee in Nevada who happens to be a bona fide human lie detector. After getting on the wrong side of her mobster boss, Charlie goes on the run, traveling across the country and solving murders using her unique ability and remarkable social skills. Natasha Lyonne stars as Charlie, with Benjamin Bratt, Ron Perlman, Rhea Perlman, Simon Helberg, Patti Harrison, and Steve Buscemi in recurring roles, and several famous guest stars appearing in individual episodes, including Giancarlo Esposito, Katie Holmes, and more.

Unlike most contemporary detective shows or case-of-the-week procedurals, Poker Face is a “howcatchem” that takes inspiration from classic detective series like Columbo and Magnum, P.I. Johnson brings his signature brand of mystery storytelling to this colorful ride with a quirky nomad who entirely changes the perspective of the genre. Driven largely by Natasha Lyonne’s live-wire performance, often compared to Peter Falk’s, Poker Face is a fantastic detective show with mature themes beneath its fun exterior.

A British sci-fi mystery thriller created by Paul Tomalin, Bodies is a Netflix adaptation of Si Spencer’s DC Vertigo comic book series. The show follows a murder mystery that spans generations, time, and space. The plot follows four different detectives who try to solve the same mystery of a dead body found in a London back alley in four different timelines. The series stars Stephen Graham, Amaka Okafor, Kyle Soller, Jacob Fortune-Lloyd, Shira Haas, and Greta Scacchi in key roles.

A unique, genre-blending, and mind-bending sci-fi show, Bodies is detective noir, high-concept sci-fi, and period drama, all rolled into one. Highly suspenseful, with unexpected outcomes in every episode, Bodies keeps the audience constantly on the edge, confounded by the story’s movement and engrossed in the convincing performances. This Bitcoin Developer’s Idea Would Make Quantum Attacks a Losing Bet

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This Bitcoin Developer’s Idea Would Make Quantum Attacks a Losing Bet

Bitcoin developer Cory Fields has an unusual idea for dealing with future quantum thieves: Make them risk their own Bitcoin every time they try to steal someone else’s.

In a November 4 post to the Bitcoin development mailing list, Fields outlined “quantum landmines” and two types of auctions designed to turn attacks on vulnerable wallets into expensive gambles. He called the ideas “thought experiments,” not a completed Bitcoin upgrade.

Bitcoin owners use a secret private key to authorize transactions. A matching public key lets the network check their signatures without learning the secret.

If the whole idea seems too hard to understand, we used AI to generate a video to help you grasp it:

Today’s computers cannot realistically calculate that private key from the public one. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could use a mathematical technique called Shor’s algorithm to do it, potentially letting a thief spend coins as if they owned them.

Some older Bitcoin outputs have exposed public keys, and certain newer formats reveal them too. Other addresses hide the key until someone spends, so the risk is not identical for every wallet.

Researchers call the hypothetical arrival of a computer capable of these attacks “Q-Day.” No publicly demonstrated machine can steal Bitcoin this way today, and nobody knows exactly when one might exist.

Imagine someone owns 1 BTC in an old, potentially vulnerable wallet. Before Q-Day, this person creates a tiny, separate quantum-safe Bitcoin output containing a hidden proof that they controlled the original private key while quantum theft was still impossible.

Think of it as a sealed ownership receipt. Outsiders can see the small output, but not the evidence tucked inside it, or even that it is a trap.

Now imagine a future thief uses a quantum computer to recover the user’s old private key. Under Fields’s suggested rules, the thief cannot simply take her 1 BTC: They must add 1 BTC of their own from a quantum-safe wallet as collateral.

Both coins enter a restricted output worth 2 BTC. The thief must wait through a challenge period—30 days in Fields’s example—before spending it freely.

The wallet owner notices the transaction and reveals the pre-Q-Day proof. The network then allows the owner to move the full 2 BTC to the quantum-safe address they committed to earlier: their original coin, plus the thief’s collateral.

The attacker has no way to know in advance which old wallets contain these hidden receipts. Even a successful quantum key recovery could become a 1 BTC loss.

The wallet owner would also need matching collateral to initiate a normal post-Q-Day move of her own legacy coin. If they have no spare BTC, the same rule intended to hurt thieves could make moving her savings difficult.

Landmines only work when an owner prepares one before Q-Day and reveals it in time. They cannot protect coins whose keys are already lost, including some of the early Bitcoin holdings widely believed to be inaccessible.

Fields proposes a second mechanism called a staked auction. Before Q-Day, someone could lock up quantum-safe Bitcoin and secretly register a bid against a specific old wallet they expect thieves to target.

Suppose an apparently abandoned wallet holds 50 BTC. A future attacker tries to move it and posts another 50 BTC as collateral; during the auction period, someone who committed a bid in advance can challenge the attempted theft.

In Fields’s example, a successful challenger claims part of the attacker’s collateral while the original vulnerable coins are destroyed rather than awarded to the bidder. The point is to deprive thieves of a reliable payday, not return the coins to a missing owner.

His third idea, a sats auction, requires no advance stake. Instead, a vulnerable-coin spend would trigger a contest in which the highest bidder gets the coins minus their bid, while the runner-up pays their bid and gets nothing.

That resembles a notorious auction game where two bidders keep raising their offers because neither wants to be the person who loses money for nothing. A thief might avoid starting a contest that could cost more than the coins they hoped to steal.

One proposed response to quantum risk is to restrict spending from outdated wallets after a migration deadline. That might prevent theft, but could also permanently lock out legitimate owners who never moved their coins.

Fields is exploring a different trade-off: Keep old coins potentially spendable, but make attempted transfers costly and open to challenges. Bitcoin cannot tell an honest owner from a quantum thief if both can produce the same valid signature.

The trade-off is severe, considering that honest owners might need large collateral deposits, inactive holders could miss their chance to object, and auction rules might burn coins belonging to people who are still alive and simply offline.

Bitcoin would first need a quantum-safe way to hold and spend funds. Wallets would then need to support hidden ownership commitments, while Bitcoin’s consensus rules would have to enforce collateral, waiting periods, special recovery transactions, and auctions.

Developers would also need to agree on when the new rules activate, how much collateral is required, and what happens to losing bids or leftover funds. Fields believes some mechanics could be introduced through a soft fork, a type of Bitcoin rule change, but has not supplied the detailed design.

None of these protections exists in Bitcoin today. Fields has published no implementation or activation schedule, and his original post leaves the auction payouts and even the desired outcome of an auction unresolved.

At Target Malaria, an international research consortium headquartered in London working on genetic technologies to control malaria, scientists are using a groundbreaking gene-editing method to produce sterile female Anopheles gambiae mosquitoes, the primary vector for the transmission of human malaria. Diminishing the species’ population—especially in Africa, which accounts for 95 percent of malaria deaths, mostly in children under 5—could combat the deadly disease. However, some skeptics are concerned that this may open a door that can’t be shut.

To do this, researchers are using CRISPR-Cas9, which allows them to selectively modify DNA at precise locations in the genome, to induce something called a gene drive. This technology essentially bypasses the standard Mendelian system of inheritance—think back to your high school biology lecture on pea plants.

Like humans, mosquitoes have two copies of every gene. Some traits are dominant, which means they’re expressed even if an individual only has one copy of the gene. Others are recessive, which means both copies of the gene must be present for the gene to express itself. The gene drive skews the usual rules of inheritance so that a targeted trait—in this case, the gene related to infertility—is inherited at a higher rate. “If you disrupt only one copy of the gene, the mosquitoes are still fertile. But when you target both copies, the mosquitoes become sterile,” explains Alekos Simoni, PhD, a molecular biologist and technical support specialist at Target Malaria.

The thought is that, once researchers have enough mosquitoes with single copies of the gene drive, they’ll release the insects into the wild. Then, these mosquitoes will mate and pass on the mutation. This process will repeat with each generation, eventually causing a snowball effect.

“It’s a two-step process,” Simoni says. “So you first spread the gene drive, and when there are enough mosquitoes with the gene drive mating with each other, you produce sterile mosquitoes.” Eventually, “the population progressively declines,” he continues.

It seems like a straightforward plan in theory—but genetically engineering mosquitoes is a painstaking process. First, the researchers use a tiny needle to introduce the mutation in a mosquito embryo under a microscope. The scientists also include a fluorescent marker within the introduced genetic material that makes the modified mosquitoes glow in the dark, allowing them to easily track the insects. Then, they breed the modified mosquitoes and keep them in carefully controlled temperature, humidity, and light conditions.

“Then, we run a lot of different studies,” Simoni says. Researchers analyze how the gene drive transmits to the progeny, whether it impacts their behavior, and what the implications of releasing them into the wild might be.

Using sterilization to control insect populations is nothing new. A method called the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) has been used since the 1950s to control populations of pests such as fruit flies and screwworm flies. In recent decades, it has been used in the United States to control Aedes aegypti mosquitoes, which can spread dengue, Zika, and chikungunya. SIT doesn’t involve any genetic engineering—it uses radiation to sterilize male mosquitoes that are then released into the wild. When these mosquitoes mate with a female, she will not produce any viable offspring.

Scaling SIT, however, is difficult. “You need to release 10 to 20 times as many males as the target population you want to control,” says Simoni, “and it also works very poorly in rural areas where there is very little infrastructure.” A gene drive, on the other hand, is based on releasing a relatively small number of mosquitoes, because it’s a process that spreads from generation to generation.

The latter method is still in its early stages—no gene-drive mosquitoes have been released anywhere yet. The first field trials will probably take place on islands or small areas, Simoni says, and will involve the release of mosquitoes on the order of thousands. Target Malaria previously had an approved release scheduled in the West African country of Burkina Faso, but the national authorities halted the effort.

Still, the work shows promise. In a 2024 study published in Nature Communications, researchers used a model to simulate the release of gene-drive mosquitoes in 16 areas across West Africa, each roughly 4,630 square miles in size. (For reference, Connecticut has a land area of roughly 4,842 square miles.) The team found that these releases could reduce the abundance of disease-carrying mosquitoes by 72 to 98 percent. Meanwhile, other unrelated teams are also using gene drives to engineer mosquitoes that are resistant to malaria parasites, which are what infect the insect in the first place.

Some skeptics have raised concerns over the potential use of gene-drive mosquitoes. After all, once you release a gene drive into the wild, you can’t easily reverse it. We don’t know how local ecosystems and food webs will respond to a decline in mosquito populations. Accidentally spreading organisms that carry the gene drive is also a major risk, according to a proposed code of ethics published in The CRISPR Journal. The technology also raises a more philosophical question: Is it morally acceptable for humans to potentially eradicate a species because it causes us harm?

Simoni thinks about this differently. He believes that it’s also an ethical concern to not intervene when there are tools available to solve a threat as deadly as malaria. “It’s always a matter of risks and benefits,” he says. When we’re thinking about a health problem that kills hundreds of thousands of people each year, “the ethical questions are: How can we stop it? If we have something that could potentially make an impact, why not use it?”

At the same time, researchers working on gene drives know that their work must be conducted responsibly. For instance, the team that wrote the aforementioned code of ethics recommends proactively incorporating ecological risk assessment into gene drive research. They also emphasize transparency, like conducting replicable studies and partnering with outside experts to mitigate any adverse effects of the work.

Simoni also stresses that gene-drive mosquitoes won’t be a silver bullet against vector-borne diseases. “A gene drive is something very promising,” Simoni says. “It’s a novel technology that has a lot of potential to make an impact, but not alone. It has to be used in combination with new vaccines, bed nets, or insecticides. But it can really make a difference.”

Would You Rather: Cadillac Escalade IQ vs. Escalade-V

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For about $160,000, are you taking the 682-hp supercharged gas Escalade or the 750-hp electric one?

A lot of companies build EV models that have an internal-combustion analogue, with the guiding principle of, “the car you liked before, but electric.” There’s a tight thematic cohesiveness to that approach—a BMW i3 and M340i each endeavor to arrive at their optimized level of BMW 3-ness despite the differing powertrains—but the Cadillac Escalade is not like that. Yes, there are gas and electric versions, but they’re similar only inasmuch as they each avidly pursue the high-level Escalade goals of cosseting bigness and cocky braggadocio. When Tony Soprano got an Escalade in season 5 of The Sopranos, the only question around that casting decision was, what took so long? Today Tony would have more choices in his pursuit of the maximum expression of GM SUV, as do any of us with about $160,000 to spend. Namely, would you rather have a supercharged V-8 Escalade-V or an electric Escalade IQ?

Cadillac designed the Escalade-V’s exhaust to be extra boisterous, adding a 360-degree loop to the passenger’s-side pipe to equalize the length with the driver’s-side counterpart, for maximum sonic awesomeness. The IQ does not have equal-length dual exhaust. Whatever exhaust it does produce will get you into a tedious and possibly confrontational discussion of well-to-wheel emissions with your uncle at Thanksgiving. However, you’ll never have to worry about thieves stealing your catalytic converters, so I’ll call that one a draw.

Looking at the specs, I’m surprised to see that the Escalade-V weighs eight pounds more than the IQ. Hold on, that doesn’t sound right—let me check my notes—sorry, my curb-weight figure for the Escalade-V inadvertently included a Toyota GR86 parked on its roof. Remove that extra car, and the supercharged Escalade is quite a bit lighter: 6290 pounds for the V, and a pavement-crushing 9120 pounds for the IQ.

Despite the weight disparity, the two Slades are evenly matched in a drag race, with the 750-hp IQ trailing the 682-hp V by a couple tenths of a second to 60 mph (4.5 seconds to 4.3). On a rolling start from 5 mph, they’re tied at 4.7 seconds, and passing a dawdler will be a quicker task in the IQ: 50 to 70 mph in 2.6 seconds, versus 3.1 seconds for the loud Caddy.

Now, I’m old enough to remember the days when a Ferrari 458 Italia felt like it had pretty decent passing power, but the Escalade IQ’s 2.1-second 30-to-50-mph time beats the Ferrari by a tenth. If the IQ had been part of our Chariots of the Gods comparison test in 2011, its 50-to-70-mph time would have slotted it a tenth of a second behind the 458 and two-tenths ahead of the McLaren MP4-12C. Neither of those, when accelerating, heave their hoods skyward like a 70-foot Viking sportfish climbing on a plane, but the Escalade IQ certainly does. And they say EVs don’t bring any drama to the experience—behind the wheel, the IQ accelerating like my favorite Ferrari of all time feels as improbable as it actually is, like the vehicle itself is struggling to cope with its superpowers. An Escalade-V drives more like a mutant CT5-V Blackwing, and it’s definitely the Escalade where the driver’s seat is more desirable than one of the chauffeur chairs in back.

If we accept that one of the primary goals for an Escalade is to project brash confidence, and that hugeness is fundamental to that project, the Escalade IQ wins going away. It reminds me of a scaled-up Rolls-Royce Cullinan in the side profile, and there’s a lot of side to behold: the IQ is more than a foot longer than the gas-powered Escalade. It’s also just newer, and I think it helps the premium aura not to have a bunch of Chevys and GMCs out there copping your style with very similar bodies. Also: rear-axle steering is unequivocally a flex, and the internal-combustion Escalade doesn’t have it.

Inside, both Escalades offer huge screens, lots of leather, and power-operated doors. However, only the IQ is eligible for the Curated by Cadillac program, wherein buyers fork over an additional $54,995 and get to choose from a huge palette of colors, inside and out. With 44 interior color choices, you can certainly spec a one-of-one IQ, while the Escalade-V sticks with “Yukon Denali, but fancier” as a guiding principle. Advantage, IQ.

When we’re discussing EVs versus internal combustion, range is usually an easy win for the gas-powered candidate. But not here—the Escalade-V has an EPA-rated 312 miles of range, while the IQ gets a 465-mile rating. Yes, the V will refuel a lot quicker, but you’ll be stopping way more often. And if your gas pump stops at $100, you might technically need to fill twice to top off the 24-gallon tank with premium.

Another factor I have to consider is rap music references, and on that front the Escalade-V is the easy winner, if only because the IQ is too new—most Escalade lyrics concern gas-powered models by default. Cadillac did hire Big Boi from Outkast to rap about the IQ, but that doesn’t count. (“Escalade IQ, this is the intro to, the all-electric Cadillac perfection ...”) I’m sure we’ll get there eventually: “Rolling in the IQ to the house with a view, IQ got the roof see-through, park the IQ next to the other ’cause I just bought two.”

Finally, I think part of the Escalade mystique has always hinged on leadership. This is a boss truck, from Sopranos to Succession. And where are the luxury winds blowing? It seems to me that in this space, which is to say “not a V-12 Ferrari,” the market is amenable to electrification and the technology is progressing fast. GM just announced that its forthcoming battery chemistry will improve range while being 20 percent more compact. There will come a time, maybe not far off, when EVs just make more sense objectively (they already do, depending on use case) and internal combustion will be the nostalgia play. So in this Would You Rather, I’m embracing that future and going for the Escalade IQ. The V is charming, but for $160,000, the IQ better fulfills the role of flagship SUV—quick, cocky, luxurious, and incontrovertibly baller.

Nov. 8, 1656: The birth of Edmond Halley

It’s been said that the general public knows three astronomical objects: the Moon, the rings of Saturn, and Halley’s Comet. English astronomer Edmond Halley, born Nov. 8, 1656, didn’t discover the object named for him. In fact, he never even saw it — but his mathematical prediction of its return in 1758 guaranteed his place in history.

Halley was a friend of Isaac Newton and used Newton’s theory of gravity and to calculate “his” comet’s orbit. In fact, Halley published at his own expense the book Newton wrote explaining the theory, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica. In 1676, Halley sailed to the island of St. Helena in the South Atlantic Ocean and spent a year measuring stellar positions, the result of which was the first catalog of the southern sky.

Disney Tried To Resurrect A Groundbreaking '60s Sitcom With A Movie That Bombed

In 1999, Disney decided it was time to bring back a 1960s sitcom about a lovable alien who shacks up with a newspaper reporter. It wasn't the most convincing reboot idea the studio ever had, but Disney pushed ahead with "My Favorite Martian" anyway. Unfortunately, the movie made just over half its budget back and has — like the sitcom on which it was based — been mostly forgotten.

"My Favorite Martian" became America's first fantasy sci-fi sitcom when it debuted in 1963, beating more well-known examples of the format such as "Bewitched" and "I Dream of Jeannie." "Mister Ed" had arrived two years prior and introduced the concept of a fantastical character being thrown into an otherwise standard sitcom setup (i.e. a talking horse living with an architect in his suburban home). But "My Favorite Martian" was the first sitcom to feature a human-looking being with fantastical powers. As such, it remains one of the most significant and quietly influential sitcoms in TV history.

The movie version, however, was neither significant nor influential. Disney brought in Christopher Lloyd and Jeff Daniels to lead this ill-advised reboot, which debuted in February 1999 and ended up making back just $36.8 million against a $65 million budget. What was the plan here? Well, "My Favorite Martian" was conceivably one of several '60s sci-fi shows that deserved a modern reboot. The problem was that the film wasn't any good.

The original version of "My Favorite Martian" is one of the best sci-fi sitcoms that takes place on Earth. It stars Bill Bixby as Los Angeles Sun reporter Tim O'Hara, who discovers the crashed spaceship of a 450-year-old Martian anthropologist (Ray Walston). With the alien's ship completely wrecked, Tim invites him to stay in his garage apartment, where the Martian quickly establishes himself as the wacky yet genial figure of "Uncle Martin." The pair agree to keep Uncle Martin's identity a secret while he tries to repair his ship, but they end up forming a bond that ultimately lasts for three seasons and 107 episodes.

"My Favorite Martian" episodes revolved around Uncle Martin's lack of familiarity with human customs, his incredible powers, and outlandish adventures that saw Tim and his alien pal using time machines and anti-gravity wristbands. The show also featured some esteemed guest stars, including a young Tom Skerritt who appeared in the 1963 episode "Mrs. Jekyll and Hyde." "My Favorite Martian" also welcomed "Gilligan's Island" star Alan Hale Jr., who appeared on an episode of the show the same year he set sail on his fateful voyage on the S.S. Minnow.

The sitcom came to an end in 1966, and 33 years later Disney decided it was time to give it another go. This time, however, the studio tried to reinvent the property as a family friendly big-screen adventure along the lines of "Flubber," which was itself based on a 1960s sci-fi comedy and had been distributed by Disney's Buena Vista Pictures in 1997. "My Favorite Martian," however, was nowhere near as successful as that Robin Williams-led comedy.

"My Favorite Martian" the movie is directed by Donald Petrie of "Mystic Pizza" and "Miss Congeniality" fame. It stars Jeff Daniels as Tim O'Hara, a news producer who, like in the original show, encounters a downed spacecraft while driving. In this version, however, the recently-fired O'Hara sees this discovery as a way to make it back into the news business. Christopher Lloyd plays The Martian, who follows Tim home and ends up staying. While Tim initially plans to expose his new friend and regain his job, he grows fond of Uncle Martin. When SETI starts hunting the pair, they have to work together to evade capture and get Uncle Martin back to his home planet.

Original Uncle Martin actor Ray Walston plays Armitan/Neenert in the film, a fellow Martian who became stranded on Earth years prior. This was a nice little full circle moment as it marked Walston's last theatrical film role prior to his death in 2001. Unfortunately, the film wasn't quite the tribute it could have been.

Aside from barely making half its budget back, "My Favorite Martian" earned a 14% critic score on Rotten Tomatoes. Kevin Thomas of the Los Angeles Times praised Walston for his "crisp wit and blithe sense of whimsy," which according to the critic was "otherwise lacking in this loser." Roger Ebert was slightly kinder, calling the movie "clever in its visuals," but "labored in its audios, and noisy enough to entertain kids up to a certain age." Otherwise the reviews were pretty damning, with Derek Adams of Time Out calling the film simply "Dire." Needless to say this was not one of the best sci-fi movies of 1999.
