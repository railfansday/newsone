Have we been thinking about franchise QBs all wrong? CJ Carr, Darian Mensah and the Brock Purdy predicament

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

CJ Carr running Notre Dame's two-minute offense against Miami to end the first half is all you need to know about who he is.

It's as surgical a series of plays as you'll see this season; he's calm before the snap, unpanicked after, even when he's avoiding the rush, always has his eyes downfield, and throws with the accuracy and precision that will make you a first-rounder, even if you lack the "prototypical physical tools" NFL teams covet in their franchise QBs. When you look like you've been doing this for a decade, no one cares if you can throw the ball 80 yards or run a 4.3 forty.

Heck of a 2 Min Drive from CJ Carr! pic.twitter.com/HxMYEJksnu

That drive ended with an 18-yard laser to Micah Gilbert to give the Fighting Irish a 17-3 halftime lead. And while they didn't cruise to a blowout win – credit to Darian Mensah and the Canes for battling back – it did reinforce a question I've been thinking about as we work our way through another "can't-miss" quarterback class that has, once again, missed:

Have we been evaluating the position backward?

Every fall, NFL teams (and folks in the media too) fall in love with the big, athletic, strong-armed quarterbacks who, if you squint enough, start to look like Justin Herbert or Josh Allen. And there's a reason for that. When it all comes together, it can be special. When it doesn't … well, it can be the 2026 Los Angeles Chargers.

It's why we annually watch evaluators excuse bad decision-making, chronic inaccuracy and poor processing because a QB gets off the bus looking like Will Levis or Drew Allar or if we're talking the '27 class, names like Arch Manning and LaNorris Sellers. Meanwhile, Brock Purdy, Mr. Irrelevant in 2022, and Kirk Cousins, a fourth-rounder a decade earlier, lacked those eye-popping physical traits but proved they could play quarterback at a high level in the NFL.

I'd even throw Mac Jones into the group. He put up huge numbers at Alabama and made the Pro Bowl as a rookie in New England, but his career went sideways after the Patriots decided Matt Patricia and Joe Judge should lead the offense. Jones had a pit stop in Jacksonville before finding his way to Kyle Shanahan and San Francisco, and all he did was play so well in Purdy's absence early last season that there were questions about whether he should keep the job. The point, in part, is what a smart, accurate quarterback with average physical tools can accomplish when the system plays to his strengths.

To be clear, this is less about arm strength or athleticism than how much inconsistency evaluators are willing to put up with for the upside, no matter how unlikely that realized upside might be. Put another way: are we undervaluing quarterbacks who win with anticipation, accuracy, processing and average NFL arms?

Which brings us to CJ Carr and Darian Mensah. Both should come in 6-foot-2 or 6-foot-3, so we're not talking about Bryce Young or Kyler Murray. But neither has a Patrick Mahomes fastball in their locker, either. They see the field, throw on time and with anticipation, and consistently understand where the football needs to go. Saturday gave us two very different looks at those skills.

Carr was clinical from the start. He set protections, got the ball out quickly and accurately, and once he started feeling it, was damn-near unstoppable in the way Purdy can be when he's in his bag. Again, I'd refer you to Carr running that two-minute offense as all the evidence you need.

Mensah spent much of the first half running for his life before settling down in the third quarter and drawing the Hurricanes to within seven points after one of his bread-and-butter back-shoulder throws:

Mensah —> BarkateThat 2025 Duke team… I’ll never forget pic.twitter.com/qa8pn75KKn https://t.co/rG4KgrMZZe

Notre Dame gave Miami's offensive line fits for much of the night, and on the Hurricanes' very next drive, Mensah threw just his second interception of the season.

NOTRE DAME INTERCEPTS DARIAN MENSAH!📺 Notre Dame Football on NBC & Peacock pic.twitter.com/QA0UH9tXao

It's undoubtedly a bad pick, but this is what NFL evaluators will note – and what shows up all over Mensah's tape: he's one of the best anticipation passers in the class. And sometimes, when you throw with anticipation on a speed out and miss inside, you're playing with fire. On this play, Mensah got burned.

It also didn't help that Malachi Toney, the best player on the planet not named Jeremiah Smith, was out with an ankle injury and exactly zero of the remaining Miami receiving options scared the Fighting Irish secondary.

But excuses, as they say, are for losers.

And don't get it confused: this wasn't Caleb Williams going into South Bend in 2023 and throwing three first-half interceptions, repeatedly making bad decisions under pressure as Notre Dame dismantled USC. Mensah was under siege often, and despite that pick, he kept competing, made big-time throws down the stretch and gave Miami a chance to tie the game in the closing minutes.

Mensah finds Lofton but Luke Talich knocks it out of his hands 😱 pic.twitter.com/oA1bN4io4a

He didn't win, and he wasn't perfect, but he bounced back from a slow start and an early second-half interception to give Miami a chance to tie it late, on the road against one of the best defenses he'll face all season. And he did it without abandoning the traits that made him my QB1 in the first place (and Malachi Toney).

It's worth remembering that neither Mensah nor Carr entered the season as sure-fire first-round picks. Back in August, I had both with second-round grades and a lot to prove. And while there was a lot to like, there were also some legitimate concerns.

During his 2025 season at Duke, Mensah appeared to have the bigger arm, but his footwork was all over the place, and his decision-making was at times spotty. Carr threw with anticipation, consistently knew where to go with the football and showed impressive touch on second-level throws. But he could also let pressure affect him, and an average arm raised questions about how his game would translate to the NFL.

The numbers told a different story. In August, we introduced FRED, a model that compares college quarterbacks with previous first-round picks based on their production and experience. Mensah scored a 39.4, putting him within striking distance of 50, a mark every eligible quarterback in our historical sample had reached before eventually becoming a first-round pick. Carr scored just 10.6, although he had considerably less starting experience. I liked Carr more than the model did, but Mensah's numbers suggested he might be closer to first-round territory than his 2025 tape suggested.

Fast-forward to present day, and Mensah is my QB1, Oklahoma State's Drew Mestemaker is QB1a, and Carr is QB3. Both Mensah and Carr have made impressive strides, and both have looked the part of future first-round picks.

Both had dominated stretches of the season, but neither had faced many opponents capable of truly testing them. The Miami-Notre Dame matchup offered a chance to change that.

For Mensah, the biggest improvement this season has been his consistency. Coming into Saturday's game, he had completed 77.4% of his passes, averaged 10.3 yards per attempt and thrown 25 touchdowns and just one interception (which is now two). But here's what really stands out: When he throws in rhythm, he's completing 83.1% of his passes and averaging 11.4 yards per attempt, both among the best marks in college football. It's a reflection of what shows up every week on tape: He sees the field, throws with anticipation and rarely passes up an easy completion to force something downfield.

Carr has made significant strides too, in part because he has addressed one of my biggest preseason concerns. A year ago, he took sacks on nearly 21% of the plays in which he was pressured. This season, that number is now 6.7%, and he's been sacked just twice. His completion percentage under pressure has also improved from 26.1% to 51.9%. Notre Dame's outstanding offensive line deserves some credit, certainly, but Carr looks like a different quarterback when things break down; he's more decisive, better identifies pressure and seldom looks rattled.

The ability to avoid costly mistakes separates both quarterbacks from some of their peers. Mestemaker has put the ball in harm's way seven times in 54 opportunities under pressure, compared with one apiece for Mensah and Carr. Neither has been immune to bad decisions, but both have generally understood when to test a defense and when to take what it's giving them. And as we've seen with many highly drafted quarterbacks, that matters.

How top 2027 quarterback prospects handled pressure entering Week 10

There's also this idea that Carr's average arm means he's Captain Checkdown. Fun fact: Nearly a quarter of his passes this season have traveled at least 20 yards, almost twice Mensah's rate. Heading into Week 10, Carr had eight touchdowns on those attempts and only one turnover-worthy play. Mensah was far more accurate on deep throws, completing 58.1% compared with Carr's 37%, but Carr had 23 more attempts. He wins with timing and touch, even if his accuracy can suffer when he's late on an intermediate route or forced off his spot.

There's also some context to Mensah's remarkable efficiency. A quarter of his attempts have come behind the line of scrimmage, generating 553 yards, compared with just 12.6% of Carr's throws. Miami does a terrific job creating easy completions, and Mensah takes advantage of them. But he's also shown he can make the difficult throws, and his ability to generate offense without regularly risking turnovers puts him in rarefied company, even when compared to previous first-rounders.

None of this means the next Brock Purdy should be auto-drafted ahead of the next Josh Allen. Allen is proof that betting on rare talent can pay off (even if those bets rarely work out, the allure is often too irresistible), and Bryce Young shows NFL teams will invest the No. 1 pick in a quarterback with more modest physical tools if the college tape is exceptional. And Carr and Mensah are making strong cases that they belong in that category.

Processing and anticipation don't replace physical talent. They are talents themselves, and Carr's and Mensah's performances were a reminder of how much they matter.

The question for NFL teams isn't whether either can make NFL throws. They've done that repeatedly. It's whether we've been too quick to forgive the quarterbacks who look the part, and too slow to appreciate the ones who consistently play the part.

This Is The Only Road In The US That Closes For Snakes

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Street takeovers are a menace. Heck, the disruptive crime has jurisdictions like Compton installing new medians to prevent takeovers. This, however, isn't that kind of takeover. Twice a year, thousands of snakes take over a small road in Illinois. It's the sort of thing that would be enough to make Indiana Jones ditch his motorcycle, drop his whip, and head for the hills. Now, before you conjure mental images of "Raiders of the Lost Ark" and a writhing tangle of venomous vipers, this is all part of nature. Though there are still thousands of slithery critters on the so-called "Snake Road," a few of which are venomous.

It's enough to shut down the road that runs through part of the Shawnee National Forest in southern Illinois. Every year, the little road closes to vehicle traffic due to the influx of scaly travelers. Not once, but twice every trip around the sun. In fact, it's the only known road in the United States to shut down in observance of a reptile migration. The shutdown isn't meant to protect the national forest's human visitors, either – it's to make sure the road-going snakes can complete their journey unharmed.

Shawnee National Forest is a biodiverse landscape spanning 289,000 acres between the Mississippi River and the Ohio River. It's home to around 35 migratory species of snakes and 23 species of migrating amphibians. Twice a year, those critters make the trek between the national forest's limestone bluffs and the LaRue Swamp. In the spring, the reptiles and amphibians travel from the bluffs to the swamp. In the fall, the ectothermic excursionists do the trip in reverse order, returning to the bluffs from the LaRue Swamp. Be it a slither, crawl, or hop, these reptiles and amphibians are on a mission.

The adventure takes the animals right across Forest Service Road 345, also known as LaRue Road. "We call it Snake Road," Forest Service Enforcement Officer Anderson Harris told CBS Evening News. It's a fitting name, too. The Forest Service closes the road because of snake, reptile, and amphibian migrations from March 15 to May 15 and September 1 to October 30 every year. "That protects animals from being incidentally killed by vehicles running over them," Officer Harris added. "It's the only road in the U.S., to my knowledge, that we close for migrations of the amphibians and reptiles."

At first, locals weren't so pleased with the idea of closing the road for four months of the year. Some of the more cruel motorists made a game of killing as many snakes as they could on the road. Eventually, they came around to the idea of closing the road. Best to leave the reptilian travelers in peace, even if you're curious.

Before you ask, yes, you can go check out the cold-blooded crossers for yourself. While the road is closed to cars, it's open to visitors on foot. That said, you might want to pay close attention to where you step. Of the snakes that migrate through Shawnee National Forest's Snake Road, at least three are venomous. For starters, there's the timber rattlesnake, a pit viper with a maraca for a tail. There's also the cottonmouth, also known as the water moccasin. Finally, the eastern copperhead, which can average up to three feet in length.

Again, you might want to steer clear of Snake Road if you have a bad case of ophidiophobia. If herpetoculture is your hobby of choice, it might be worth checking out. That said, collecting the snakes as they cross the road is against the law. Of course, if you're looking for something viper-esque, a clean Ram SRT-10 might be a charming enough snake. Frankly, you'd probably rather a series of snakes choose to shut down a little service road in Illinois than find a giant snake nestled in your engine bay. That is, unless Steve Irwin is your kindred spirit or something. If you're more like Indiana Jones, you'd be more likely to curse, "Why did it have to be snakes?"

“He’s Got The Yips” Mads Hermansen’s West Ham Nightmare Goes Beyond Bad Form

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There is a phenomenon in sport known as the yips. It’s a condition which seems to affect previously capable sportsmen, psychologically blocking them from performing even the simplest of tasks. In darts, it can become difficult to release the dart from the hand, while in golf it can make putting almost impossible.

In essence, you’re frozen, seemingly unable to perform a task which should have become second nature. And it was during Blackburn Rovers’ second goal against West Ham yesterday, when I watched Mads Hermansen fail to dive, that I found myself wondering whether this goalkeeper has developed the footballing equivalent of the yips.

It wasn’t just that goal, of course. The first was equally concerning, when Ezra Mayers was beaten down the flank and the ball was crossed into the penalty area. The situation required some kind of intervention from Hermansen, but there was none. No punch, no parry, no save. Just indecision and a complete lack of intervention.

Unfortunately, this apparent hesitation has permeated Hermansen’s game recently and it’s hard not to conclude that he’s got the yips. There have been occasions when he has looked like little more than a bystander as goals have flown past him, sometimes appearing rooted to the spot or simply falling over rather than making a convincing attempt to save the ball.

The statistics are equally worrying. Based on the match figures we’ve examined, Hermansen has saved just 20% of the shots on target he has faced over West Ham’s last three Championship matches, conceding eight goals from ten attempts on target.

That’s an extraordinary return for a goalkeeper at any level, let alone one playing for a club with West Ham’s promotion ambitions. And while defenders must accept their share of the responsibility, the goalkeeper ultimately has to provide some resistance when opponents get a shot away.

The warning signs were already there before Cardiff, and his latest performance has only increased my concerns. I don’t know whether Hermansen is suffering from anything resembling the yips, but from watching him, I do wonder whether his confidence has deteriorated to the point where instinctive reactions are being replaced by hesitation.

This has become a huge problem, and one Scott Parker needs to address immediately. As things stand, I would have more confidence in either Alphonse Areola or Finlay Herrick than a goalkeeper who currently appears unable to make the saves West Ham desperately need.

Areola is an experienced international goalkeeper whom Parker already knows from their time together at Fulham. I thought he deserved his opportunity against Blackburn, and nothing I witnessed yesterday has changed my mind.

Hermansen’s performance was among the biggest disappointments in our Blackburn player ratings, and continuing to select him simply because he was previously considered first choice doesn’t make much sense when his performances have deteriorated so badly.

Clearly, there is a bigger issue to address in January, when West Ham will have another opportunity to examine their goalkeeping options. But we’ve only just entered November, and there are still two months and a hell of a lot of fixtures to negotiate before the transfer window opens.

That’s an awful lot of potential points to lose while waiting for a goalkeeper to rediscover his confidence. Parker has an international break in which to make some important decisions, and I would suggest the identity of his first-choice goalkeeper should be very near the top of that list.

A celebration of peak Joe Cole: ‘A special player, full of fantasy’

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every English football fan of a certain age should have a special place in their hearts for Joe Cole. Once upon a time, he was the nation’s greatest hope.

No matter how many people warn against heaping too much pressure on young players’ shoulders, in England we just can’t help it. The country is constantly looking for a new hero.

Even in the digital age of constant hyperbole, however, few players have had to handle the intense fever which surrounded Joe Cole’s embryonic days as a burgeoning professional footballer.

At the age of 15, Cole was earning rave reviews in the national press thanks to his performances for England schoolboys, attracting interest from the likes of Arsenal and Manchester United.

By 17, he was being invited by Kevin Keegan to train with the senior England squad after inspiring West Ham to a 9-0 thrashing of Coventry City over two legs in the final of the FA Youth Cup.

And just to temper expectations, he was one of the first teenagers to be dubbed ‘The Next Gazza‘.

When Cole was paraded at Upton Park during half-time of a league match against Chelsea to sign his first pro contract, the stadium announcer declared: “You can tell your grandchildren you were here when Joe Cole signed.”

Cole’s career since the end of the noughties created the perception that he failed to live up to his immense potential, but that’s unfair. If not necessarily ever the best English footballer, for a period between 2004-09, Cole was undoubtedly one of the most fun. That alone makes him worthy of celebration.

One of the things which always made Cole so alluring is that he was so obviously mad about football. He always seemed most at home on the pitch, with the ball at his feet and a defender stood in his way, primed to be embarrassed.

By the time he had broken into West Ham’s first team, Cole was already asking Harry Redknapp if he could avoid giving interviews. The boy from Camden only ever had one thing on his mind: football.

He went on to make 150 appearances for the Hammers and became renowned for his flair and trickery. Those sort of players are few and far between in England, and Cole was a welcome breath of fresh air; a footballer seemingly born to entertain.

As a child he had paid particularly close attention to Italian and African football, making a note of the latest piece of skill and honing it himself at the next available opportunity.

Rather than be overawed by the seriousness of top-flight football, Cole carried the same attitude against his senior pros, with the advice of his coach at Camden and Islington Boys, Terry Howard, ringing in his ears: “Do what you like from now on, son.”

“I’ve been coaching for 20 years and have never seen such a combination of flair, pace and commitment,” Howard said of his first impressions of the youngster in an interview with The Independent in 1999. “He was also the most modest.”

Come 2002-03, and still at the tender age of 21, Cole was made West Ham captain by Glenn Roeder and flourished with the added responsibility.

West Ham may have been unable to avoid relegation, but Cole’s valiant displays in midfield earned him the club’s Player of the Year award and a £6.6million move to Chelsea, where he was labelled the “new Gianfranco Zola” by Claudio Ranieri. The unrelenting expectation continued to increase.

However, handed the No.10 shirt, his debut season with the Blues was somewhat underwhelming. While he made 50 appearances in all competitions, exactly half of those were from the bench, and he did not score in the league until April as he struggled to find a suitable role in the team.

It would be a theme which continued throughout his career. Cole played at a time when English football transitioned from 4-4-2 to 4-3-3, neither formation particularly allowing for the indulgence of a free-spirited No.10.

It was the arrival of Jose Mourinho at Chelsea which prompted the best football of Cole’s career. If the game was unwilling to adapt to his talents, he could at least adapt himself under the guidance of one of the most tactically astute managers in modern football.

Two things helped Cole rediscover his verve at Chelsea.

First, John Terry, who Cole has described as “like an older brother” at the time, asked one of the video analysts to pull together a compilation of all the midfielder’s highlights to remind him of his unique ability.

But the main turning point came in October 2004. Cole walked off the pitch at Stamford Bridge having scored an excellent winner against Liverpool, only to be publicly criticised by Mourinho for a lack of defensive application.

“He has two faces – one beautiful and one I don’t like,” Mourinho said. “He must keep one and change the other one.”

﻿

Rather than take the criticism to heart, Cole had the belief in his own game to go out and prove his manager wrong. After all, even though he was still only 22, it was all part and parcel of being Joe Cole.

“We are on the same page,” he said. “He wants Chelsea to win games and I want Chelsea to win games. He wants Joe Cole to be a better player than he already is – and so do I. I have always taken criticism well and it wasn’t massive criticism in my eyes.

“We both want to make me a better player for Chelsea and if that is the end result then that is all that matters. It hurt when people said I was showing off. All I like to do is put a smile on fans’ faces as part of the side.

“What people must appreciate is I have had this attention since I was 16 at West Ham. The expectancy was enormous then and hasn’t changed.”

From that moment on, Cole established himself as one of Mourinho’s go-to men on the wing, starting the League Cup final victory over Liverpool and both legs of the Champions League semi-final defeat to the same opponents.

He ended the season with nine goals in 46 appearances, with only 12 of those outings coming from the bench. Most importantly, he also had a Premier League winner’s medal.

The following season continued in much the same manner. With the fully-earned trust of Mourinho, Cole went one better in terms of both appearances and goals, despite the added competition from Shaun Wright-Phillips alongside Damien Duff and Arjen Robben.

And at the end of that 2005-06 campaign, Cole produced one of his finest moments in a blue shirt.

With Manchester United arriving at Stamford Bridge in April, Chelsea had the chance to clinch the title against their nearest rivals. Fittingly, it was Cole who produced the piece of skill worthy of winning a championship.

Receiving the ball with his back to goal and three United defenders around him just outside the box, Cole somehow left all three for dead with the deception of the world’s greatest conman before firing the ball home with an emphatic finish.

In one moment Cole showed both the absurd talent which first attracted him so much hype at West Ham and the refinement of his game at Chelsea which made him the country’s best attacking midfielder.

Later that summer, Cole also produced the highlight of his England career with his stunning, dipping volley against Sweden at the World Cup.

It was another example of him attempting something most other players wouldn’t even dream of.

“Joe Cole has always been a special player, full of fantasy,” Sven-Goran Eriksson had commented four years previous. Finally, he was proving it on a regular basis.

In the following three seasons Cole cemented his status as one of English football’s favourite sons, collecting the FA Cup twice, another League Cup and coming a John Terry slip away from winning the Champions League.

But in January 2009, Cole ruptured his anterior cruciate ligament playing in an FA Cup replay at Southend, and his career would never be the same.

Upon his return, nine months later, he never fully established himself in what was then Carlo Ancelotti’s side before his contract expired in the summer.

“Ever since I did my knee at Chelsea, it was an uphill struggle,” he told the Daily Mail in 2017.

Cole’s last appearance for Chelsea came in the FA Cup final victory over Portsmouth which secured the club’s first ever Double, and there were still enough flashes of inspiration – namely his back-heeled opener in a win at Old Trafford – to convince Fabio Capello to name him in England’s squad for the 2010 World Cup.

It speaks volumes about how he was regarded that Terry tried to lead a mutiny against the manager to ensure Cole was selected after a dismal display against Algeria devoid of any spark.

“Listen, Joe is one of the best players in our country,” he said. “He has come back from an injury and showed great form for Chelsea. It still amazes me how the club have come to the conclusion of letting Joe go.”

But Cole was only granted two substitute appearances, and his club career similarly failed to take off thereafter.

A move to Liverpool never recovered after a red card on his debut and a missed penalty in his second appearance.

Though he did impress at Lille alongside Dimitri Payet and Eden Hazard – we’re not ashamed to admit that resembles something close to our wet dream – a return to West Ham and short spell at Aston Villa also fell flat.

To his credit, Cole continued to battle against his body, and dropped down two divisions to rekindle his love for the game at Coventry City before heading west for an Indian Summer with the Tampa Bay Rowdies.

While many are tempted to label Cole a waste of potential, that would be unfair. He is one of only 11 Englishmen to have won three Premier League titles, three FA Cups and played in three World Cups.

“Once you stop learning, there’s no point playing,” he told The Guardian in 2011. “You’d have mastered it. And no one’s mastered football yet.”

Joe Cole shouldn’t be used as a warning to young footballers, he should be used as an example.

In addition to his journalist work, Mewis is also the author of four football history books that include times on Leeds United and the England national team. Now working as a digital marketing coordinator at Harrogate Town, too, Mewis counts some of his best career moments as being in the iconic Spygate press conference under Marcelo Bielsa and seeing his beloved Leeds lift the Championship trophy during lockdown.
