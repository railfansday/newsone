I've accepted that Arnold Schwarzenegger can probably do things at 79 that would put me in physical therapy. Still, watching him take time from his busy 2026 movie schedule to hoist an enormous Christmas tree overhead while dressed as Santa Claus made me do a double take. The moment comes from a new Santa Schwarzenegger holiday ad, which follows the action icon through New York City in a Santa suit as he dodges the paparazzi. Somehow, he winds up helping a family load a tree onto their car. I suspect some movie magic made that lift possible, but seeing Arnold casually handle an entire evergreen is pretty funny. It also has me wondering: What's it like doing stunts at his age?

The Santa clip landed on Arnold's Instagram just days after the former governor got candid about what action scenes feel like at his age. During his November 11 appearance on Jimmy Kimmel Live!, Schwarzenegger discussed the possibility of returning to Predator, Commando, and Conan the Barbarian. Kimmel brought up King Conan, which would follow his famous warrior as an older ruler whose reign is threatened. When the conversation turned to the demands of filming that kind of movie, Arnold admitted the years have made a difference:

I tell you one thing, to do all this action and the sword fighting and horseback riding, and all this stuff is not as easy anymore today than it was 40 years ago…. Well, you get older and you slow down a little bit, right?

Four decades ago, Schwarzenegger swung swords and ran through jungles. I'd imagine climbing onto a horse in full costume feels a little different at 79. Of course, he wasn't about to let a serious observation about aging ruin a perfectly good joke. He quickly followed up with a line that had me laughing:

No, not very much. But still, I'm now at an age where it takes me an hour and a half to watch 60 Minutes. Unbelievable, I think. Things are really slowing down.

That's a pretty fantastic old-man joke. The timing is even better when you remember this is the same guy currently showing up in Amazon’s holiday commercial looking capable of bench-pressing a Christmas tree. Kimmel also commented on how muscular Schwarzenegger still is, which led to some playful back-and-forth between the two. Even the Terminator is still perfectly comfortable being the butt of his own jokes.

A post shared by Arnold Schwarzenegger (@schwarzenegger)

Schwarzenegger isn't pretending he can simply pick up where he left off in 1986, either. He's spoken about how a potential King Conan would acknowledge the character's age, with Conan having spent decades on the throne before facing a threat to his kingdom. That's an idea I can get behind. CinemaBlend has already covered his updates about possible Predator and Commando sequels, although he told Kimmel he was still waiting to see finalized scripts.

Meanwhile, the Christmas tree is a nice bit of promotion for The Man with the Bag, Schwarzenegger's new Prime Video holiday movie with Reacher star Alan Ritchson and Awkwafina. In it, he plays Santa, who recruits a thief to recover his stolen bag of presents. The film arrives December 2 for anyone with a Prime Video subscription, marking his return to Christmas comedy 30 years after Jingle All the Way.

As for that tree lift, I'd love to see some behind-the-scenes footage before giving Arnold full credit for the feat. But the man seems to be having a blast playing Santa, and I hope he keeps finding excuses to do action scenes. Maybe someone can make him a really comfortable saddle for King Conan.

Corsair is continuing the 2026 trend of brands pushing out new and innovative mouse technologies. In this case, it's all in the 'PureVector' part of the name. In partnership with AimLabs, Corsair has made a mouse that can adjust sensitivity in different directions to tailor it to your movements and help you aim better.

That's the claim, anyway. In practice, I've found that PureVector hasn't improved my aim, both subjectively while gaming and objectively based on multiple AimLabs tests. Some pro players that Corsair highlights claim it's helped them, so your mileage may vary.

Apart from PureVector, though, the mouse itself deserves some attention, as it's still a very performant, lightweight mouse for competitive gaming. It has some upgrades compared to its predecessor, the Sabre V2 Pro, too.

The aim-assisting PureVector technology in this mouse promises a lot, but in my case it fails to deliver, as my aim doesn't improve from turning it on. It's a solid, lightweight, competitive mouse regardless, but it's too expensive for what it is without delivering on the PureVector promise.

Replacement skates, USB-A to USB-C cable, wireless dongle, AimLabs PureVector software integration

In most ways, the Sabre V3 Pro PureVector Ergo is your standard competitive gaming mouse: lightweight, few frills, simple design, good sensor. It is lighter than most competitive mice with a solid shell, though a little heavier than its predecessor, the Sabre V2 Pro.

Setting aside the PureVector aiming shenanigans for a moment, probably the biggest improvement compared to the Sabre V2 Pro is its battery life, which has been doubled. Apart from that, you're getting a 42,000 DPI sensor, optical clicks, and some bigger replacement skates included in the box.

Now we come to the main attraction: PureVector. Corsair has designed the mouse's internals and firmware to permit different sensitivities in different directions. So, for instance, it can move slightly slower horizontally than vertically.

Corsair has worked with AimLabs to let you test your aiming in the latter software and then have the mouse adjust your directional sensitivity for you. The PureVector software within AimLabs has been made with the help of a bunch of aiming data (over 4 billion data points, apparently) and machine learning. The end result is, in theory, a mouse that can tailor its sensitivity in different directions to stop you over-shooting or under-shooting your target.

Again, in theory, this should also save you time when you aim, and Corsair claims it can save more time than reducing the latency of clicks—the latter is, for example, what we see the Logitech G Pro X2 Superstrike and X3 Superstrike focusing on.

It's worth noting that Corsair highlights a number of pro players who say they've benefitted from this new tech. However, for my part, I haven't noticed a difference.

For reference, I play Counter-Strike 2 at about FaceIt Level 7 or Premier 18–20,000 rating (approx. top 25%). I'm far from a pro, but I'm far from the worst. In-game, I didn't notice any difference between my aim with PureVector set up and with it disabled.

I also repeatedly tested my aim in the AimLabs SpiderShot Precision test, and I actually had slightly better results using another mouse without PureVector (the SteelSeries Sensei Pro), and the latter mouse my hand was less familiar with.

More than this, though, I can't help but feel that it's a bit of a band-aid solution to poor aim. If you're overshooting your shots when you aim left, for instance, you could get PureVector to lower your sensitivity in that direction. But why not instead work on training your hand not to overshoot? That way, having good aim won't be dependent on using this one mouse, and you can get used to aiming consistently in different directions.

PureVector aside, the Sabre V3 Pro performs well. The sensor is great and feels very responsive. Ditto the new optical mouse clicks. I have no complaints about its performance as a general mouse, I just don't see the use in PureVector myself. Of course, your mileage may vary, as it seems to have for some pro players.

One area where the Sabre V3 Pro does particularly shine, just like the Sabre V2 Pro, is in its light weight. This latest iteration feels even sturdier than the previous one and still manages to be lighter than most competitive gaming mice.

Battery life is a definite selling point of the Sabre V3 Pro PureVector. It's a lot better than I was expecting. The Sabre V2 Pro can only manage about 70 hours at 1 kHz, and this new one doubles that to 140 hours.

I think Corsair has struck a great balance here, because it's not as light as the V2—which was 36 g—but 41 g is still incredibly light, and I'd much rather have the bigger battery than shave off those few extra grams. You're not getting any fancy wireless charging or hot-swappable batteries here, but 140 hours of battery life is great.

The Corsair Sabre V3 Pro PureVector Ergo is, as its excruciatingly long name suggests, pretty ergonomic. It's not the biggest ergo mouse I've used—the Zowie EC2-DW and SteelSeries Rival Pro both feel a bit bigger in-hand—but it still feels comfy under my medium-sized palm.

There's also a non-ergo, symmetrical version for the same price, and you can get carbon fiber or magnesium versions for each, if you're willing to spend even more ($200 and $230, respectively).

I like the lightweight ergonomic version, though. Its slightly smaller size, combined with its light weight, also means holding it in a fingertip or claw grip doesn't feel too bad, which isn't something you can say about many ergonomic mice. Palm grip is still the main intention here, though. Playing Counter-Strike on low sensitivity feels great.

Build quality is decent, too, which is a bit of a relief because the previous version, the Sabre V2 Pro, sacrificed some on this front to achieve its 'ultralight' status. There's no creak or flex along the sides, and only a slight bit of flex on the underside. The side buttons feel great, with no pre-travel, and the scroll wheel has well-defined steps.

There is some pre-travel to the left- and right-mouse buttons, however, and they don't feel as satisfying to press as some other mice like the Razer Viper V4 Pro or SteelSeries Rival Pro. They're far from bad, just not quite up to the standard of some other expensive gaming mice I've tried.

The version I've got here, the 'Ergo' one, is pretty plain and looks quite similar to its predecessor, the Sabre V2 Pro. It's plain white, with only some very subtle grey accents. I'm fine with that, as the focus is on performance and comfort.

Any extra aesthetic fancies are likely to add to the weight, which isn't worth the sacrifice for this kind of mouse. As with most ultralight mice, you know what you're getting aesthetically, and it's minimal.

This mouse uses the same software as the previous V2 Pro. What's noteworthy is the software is browser-based—there's no locally-installable app to use. That's mainly because you're expected to just set it and go, without returning to adjust things.

You can set up profiles to toggle between DPI using a mouse button combination, however. And the web software can be used offline if you install it as an app in your browser, so internet outages shouldn't cause any problems.

The web hub is basic but gets the job done. The battery indicator isn't very precise, only showing a tiny icon and no percentage numbers, but that's my only complaint.

The AimLabs integration is perfectly fine, too. Assuming you've got it installed on Steam, you can allow the web hub to boot it up for you from there, or boot it up yourself. Once in, you can navigate to PureVector and let it run you through the aim training to adjust your sensitivity in different sweep directions. It's intuitive, and the directional charts it shows are clear enough.

At $180/£160, the Sabre V3 Pro PureVector Ergo is an expensive mouse. It joins the likes of the G Pro X2/X3 Superstrike, Viper V4 Pro, DeathAdder V4 Pro, SteelSeries Rival Pro, and other high-end, competitive gaming mice.

Can it compete with these? In my opinion, not quite. These other gaming mice each have extra features and new technologies that go some way towards justifying the very high price tags. The Superstrikes have analogue clicks, the Viper V4 Pro has FrameSync, the Rival Pro has hot-swappable batteries and UWB connectivity.

The DeathAdder V4 Pro doesn't have anything too fancy like that, but is a very solid gaming mouse. I'd class the Sabre V3 Pro PureVector in this category, alongside the DeathAdder. That's because while the PureVector technology is indeed very new, I haven't found it to have much of an impact, and so I'm left considering its merits as a general competitive gaming mouse, setting aside this new aim assist tech.

There are merits to it that justify a price tag above $100: light weight, comfy ergonomic shape, optical clicks, responsive scroll wheel, great battery life. But not enough to justify $180—not without something extra, which PureVector promises but, in my case, fails to deliver. Unless, that is, you're set on getting a mouse as light as this one is that still has a sturdy shell.

You could instead, for instance, go for something like the Mchose K7 V2 Ultra+ for about $90. Or a Pulsar mouse like the Pulsar X2 CrazyLight for $130, which sometimes drops to about $100 on sale.

If you fancy spending the extra to try it out for yourself and see if you can get the kinds of benefits the pros that Corsair highlights espouse, go for it. But for my money, I'd be opting for a cheaper lightweight mouse, or one with better build quality like the Viper V4 Pro.

NAND prices tipped to fall as Chinese cloud giants cut SSD orders

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TrendForce sees supply outpacing demand in 2027, though cheaper flash won't fix the DRAM squeeze

NAND flash prices could start falling next year as Chinese cloud operators cut enterprise SSD orders, helping push the market toward oversupply.

Taiwan-based market intelligence firm TrendForce says Chinese cloud service providers are grappling with GPU shortages, high inventories, and fierce competition over AI token pricing.

As a result, they have reduced their total enterprise SSD orders for 2027 by a substantial amount. Although TrendForce doesn't specify any figures, it says the cutbacks in China are large enough to outweigh the additional demand coming from US-based cloud operators.

But don't get too excited: TrendForce forecasts overall NAND flash contract prices falling by 0-5 percent quarter on quarter in early 2027, with all product categories entering a downturn from the second quarter.

Falling orders are only half the story. Manufacturers are also increasing output, while weaker consumer electronics sales could further depress demand.

Strong demand for high-performance storage in the US continues to drive manufacturers to expand capacity and upgrade to newer process technologies. At the same time, the rising adoption of quad-level cell (QLC) technology and increasing number of layers in the NAND chips will further boost bit output, TrendForce observes.

Some suppliers may go slower on their expansion plans in response to sluggish consumer demand, but the firm still expects global NAND flash output, measured in storage capacity, to grow by 25 to 30 percent in 2027.

US restrictions on access to advanced AI chips could put Chinese cloud operators under further pressure. The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has introduced new restrictions on sales of GPUs, and on remote access to them via the cloud.

The new restrictions "set everyone back in China," as The Register reported.

TrendForce adds the caveat to its forecast that its market outlook depends on factors such as the pace of inventory reduction among Chinese CSPs, further changes to the restrictions on overseas GPU procurement, and whether device makers continue reducing storage capacities in 2028.

Sadly, cheaper NAND wouldn't resolve the separate squeeze on DRAM supplies. The situation, caused by memory makers opting to produce high-margin products for the AI market rather than mainstream chips, has already increased prices of PCs in Europe by double-digit percentages, leading to a fall in shipments as buyers balk at the cost. ®

Parks and Rec's Spiritual Sequel Reveals a Dark Truth About Sitcoms and Streaming

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Not even sitcom genius Mike Schur can make it work in the streaming era

Parks and Recreation wouldn’t have succeeded in the streaming era. While the beloved sitcom is now considered one of the best in TV history, its first season is mostly an uneven slog with ill-defined characters and a bizarre romance subplot that ultimately goes nowhere. Back in 2009, NBC was willing to give the show another shot by soft-rebooting it with Adam Scott and Rob Lowe. In the 2020s, Netflix probably would have pulled the plug after six episodes like it was Blockbuster.

So when Peacock announced a new comedy series from Parks and Rec creator Mike Schur and its star, Amy Poehler, my initial response was unbridled optimism. If anyone could prove the genre still works, it's the team behind one of the best sitcoms ever, right?

Unfortunately, Schur and Poehler had other ideas. Their new eight-episode serialized comedy, Dig, couldn't be more different from Parks and Rec. In fact, the only thing the two shows have in common is that, like Parks and Rec in its first season, Dig is desperately trying to figure itself out — and mostly failing.

Adapted from Kate Myers' 2023 novel Excavations, Dig takes place over the course of one summer at an archaeological site in Greece. An overworked staff that includes Poehler, Geraldine Viswanathan (Thunderbolts, Blockers), and Hugh Laurie (Veep, House) manages a team of horny undergrads who help excavate ancient artifacts under the hot Mediterranean sun. That sounds like a decent enough premise for a Mike Schur workplace comedy, but watching Dig, it's hard to believe this was created by the same guy who made The Good Place and worked on The Office.

Dig's biggest sin is simply that it's serialized, not episodic. The charm of settling into a 20-minute story that resolves when the credits roll is gone, replaced by a series of cliffhanger endings designed to keep you clicking "Next Episode" in the Peacock app. To be fair, eight episodes isn't exactly enough to establish a large cast of characters and send them off on a series of self-contained adventures. Then again, FX's Adults pulled it off, so my expectations were high.

It doesn’t help that Dig’s story isn't particularly interesting. The plot revolves around Poehler's character, Elise, a grumpy archeologist who's sick of being second-in-command. (Extremely important side note: Who decided it was a good idea to make Leslie freaking Knope play an unlikable protagonist?!) After her boss Neville (Laurie) refuses to retire yet again at the start of the summer, Elise storms off, only to accidentally discover an ancient statue in one of the old, abandoned dig sites. She decides to keep it a secret and claim the glory for herself. Later, when the statue disappears under suspicious circumstances, Dig transforms into a whodunit​​​​​​​ as Elise and her friends race to recover the artifact before it can disappear into the black market.

If the plot I just described sounds more like an action-adventure movie or a TV drama, you're not wrong. Schur and his writers manage to find very little that's particularly funny about the world of archeology. Instead, Dig oscillates between contrived caper and an ensemble comedy where most of the ensemble are poorly defined background actors. That said, there are a few bright spots in this eclectic cast.

Viswanathan is genuinely hilarious as Elise's hot-mess of a friend who's failed at everything in her life and shows up at the dig site seeking a second chance with her ex-boyfriend. The actor brings a chaotic energy to what is otherwise a weirdly buttoned-up comedy​​​​​​​ to the point that I almost wonder if some of her best lines were improvised. Another highlight is Fina Strazza (Paper Girls, Fear Street: Prom Queen) as Patty, an undergrad student with frizzy hair, a bizarre personality, and a tendency to talk about her isolated childhood growing up on a farm. (She's basically Dwight from The Office if he was a cute, awkward young woman instead of a psychotic middle-aged man.) As a diehard Parks fan, I wish I had anything positive to report on what Poehler brings to the table.

Dig doesn't have the deep bench of supporting characters that made Parks and Rec so great, but there are a few decent recurring faces. Kayvan Novak (Nandor in What We Do in the Shadows) is charming as the very Greek mayor of the small town where the show takes place, who has a devastating crush on Elise​​​​​​​. Meanwhile, Dropout's Brennan Lee Mulligan has a hilarious guest role as the bumbling fail-son of the rich old man who's been bankrolling the entire dig site. Mulligan's episode in particular, in which the archeologists have to placate an idiotic millionaire in the hopes of getting more funding for their work, feels like a vision of what the show could have been in another timeline where it wasn't bogged down by its serialized drama.

There's a bizarre streak of sadness running through Dig. But unlike the darkly comedic realities of local government bureaucracy that defined Parks and Rec, this story feels grimmer. Everyone in the show realizes to some extent that their life's work is dying. As Elise and her boss Neville repeat over and over, funding is drying up for digs like theirs and the world at large no longer seems to care about ancient Greek pottery. (There are echoes of The Office​​​​​​​ and the existential state of small-town paper sellers in the face of big-box stores and online retailers, but it all feels much less relatable against the sunny backdrop of Greece). I have no idea if this is true and the field of archeology really is in crisis, or if it's something Mike Schur came up with to inject some extra stakes into his story. Either way, I have a feeling that Schur is talking about more than just archeology.

Like the archeology field in Dig, the sitcom industry seems to be drying up too. Hollywood is struggling, writers' room jobs are dwindling, and AI is… also bad. There's a nagging sense that the type of entry-level job that gave Schur a pathway to creating shows like Parks and Rec may not exist for much longer. I'm not saying Schur set out to tell a depressing story about the death of his industry, but in a sense that's exactly what happened.

And unfortunately, that doesn't exactly make for great TV comedy.

People with higher levels of vitamin D tend to carry less fat around their internal organs.

This deeper layer of belly fat, known as visceral fat, surrounds organs such as the liver and intestines.

It matters because people with more visceral fat tend to have greater difficulty using insulin to control their blood sugar.

The link with vitamin D became weaker when the researchers took body mass index into account, suggesting that overall body size could explain part of the difference.

One possibility is that vitamin D is spread through a larger volume of tissue in people with more body fat, leaving a lower concentration in the blood.

The researchers reviewed records from nearly 1,300 adults with type 2 diabetes who attended a hospital in Quzhou, China, during a two-and-a-half-year period.

Each person had a blood test for vitamin D and a measurement to estimate their visceral fat.

The researchers divided patients into four groups according to their vitamin D levels and compared the estimated area of visceral fat in each group.

The clearest difference was between the groups with the highest and lowest vitamin D levels, with the middle groups showing a less consistent pattern.

After accounting for BMI and other differences between patients, the researchers estimated that the group with the highest vitamin D levels had about 10 square centimetres less visceral fat area than the group with the lowest levels.

Earlier research using genetic data suggested that a higher BMI can contribute to lower vitamin D levels, with weaker evidence for an effect in the other direction (Vimaleswaran et al., 2013).

As well as being distributed through a larger volume of tissue, some vitamin D may be stored in fat tissue, which could affect how much circulates in the blood.

Everyday habits could also help explain why the two measures vary together.

Someone who exercises outdoors may get more sunlight and carry less belly fat, two differences that could help explain the link.

The hospital records did not capture how much sunlight participants got, how active they were or whether they took vitamin D supplements.

Since vitamin D and belly fat were assessed at the same visit, it remains unclear which changed first or whether both were influenced by other factors.

The study was published in the Journal of International Medical Research (Zhu et al., 2026).

As down as he was, this made perfect sense to Quentin. Universal would start financing the movie on Friday, so he had just days. If any plug was going to be pulled, it had to happen by Friday.

“Lawrence was 100 percent right. He said, ‘We have got to call the Weinsteins, Universal, Mike, and Carlos, and tell them the situation—and, most importantly, Simone. We have to tell her that for the rest of the week, it is Landa, Landa, Landa, Landa. Nobody else. We ain’t got time to fuck around. We’ve got to find Landa by end of business Thursday night, or we’ve got to shut the film down. It is just that fucking simple. We’ve gotta tell Simone what time it is. It is just that fucking simple. It’s just what we are doing.’ I needed the best of Lawrence, and that was him there and then,” said Quentin.

Casting director Alexandra Montag offered her recollections of the “Finding Hans Landa” casting meeting.

“Simone explained that we had worked to the criteria that Quentin had expressed, and that was that every German-speaking actor speak not only German but also English, French, and Italian authentically. There were very few actors in Germany who had this range, and we agreed that although the actors he had seen were very successful actors, there was something missing. We had seen the tapes.

“But it is also true that not every actor was available on that first day, and he had yet to see an actor Simone believed just right for the role. His name was Christoph Waltz. ‘Please wait for Christoph—Christoph will come,’ she told Quentin. It was her belief that he had all the charm, charisma, and cynicism that fit with Hans Landa.

“I, however, picked up Quentin’s fear as well, and when they had left, I said, ‘Oh shit, shit, shit, what are we going to do?’ But Simone was completely calm and always said, ‘Just wait for Christoph Waltz.’ She was completely confident.”

Making his way to meet with Quentin Tarantino, Christoph Waltz had very little reason to feel optimistic. He had been in his chosen profession since the ’70s, and since leaving the theater world to concentrate on television and film he had become, to a degree, a respected working actor. “Everybody knew who I was; I had played some good parts, of course, I had to do some crap also. But in all those years, Simone Bär had never once cast me in anything. It was odd, but there it was.”

So, not convinced that Bär believed in his talent, Waltz pushed open the door and entered her casting office. He was confident in his own abilities and ready to play the game, like any other actor.

Montag recalled Bär never doubting Waltz’s talent. It was more “because she never found the role for him in the way that this one fit him, the charm, the cynicism, which he could play so well. She knew how good he was. She knew in Landa she had the perfect role for him.”

Waltz, however, was unaware of any such confidence. It was, he felt, another audition he did not need to get excited about.

“We had had it happen many times before: an international filmmaker coming in to cast their film. When Spielberg came to cast Schindler’s List, everyone was in hysterical convulsion: ‘Spielberg, Spielberg, Spielberg.’ Yes, it is all exciting, but frankly speaking, what is in it for me, personally? When you are offered the role of a German soldier in the ghetto who shouts, ‘Get out, get out, get out’ for $500, why would I want to do that?

“So, that was my mindset. I’m an extremely skeptical character, and when they gave me the whole screenplay, it was very heavy, with its title page written in Quentin Tarantino’s own hand, and from there you can get a feeling of the person who wrote it. I said to my agent in Germany, ‘Okay, what role do they want me to do?’ ‘Well, you should read for that Landa character,’ I was told.

“‘Okay, I’ll read Landa.’ Now, I was convinced that I was only reading this big part of Landa because they wanted me for a part that didn’t have enough text to actually show anything. It was just text material.”

Ushered into the kitchen, Waltz took a seat and waited his turn to enter the audition room.

This was the landscape of the time. If a big American film production came to Germany or Austria, local actors would be passed over, so television was where one made a living. It was, Waltz said, how it was.

“We, the natives, would be left with the shitty small parts, and usually the plum parts went to English actors who put in blue contact lenses, dyed their hair blond, and put on a phony accent which no one has. I was used to that. It was out of the question to think it would be any other way. So, when Quentin came, I was under the impression that what always happened would happen this time. They’re in town for the tax credit. They’ll do their search for local actors, hold up their hands that nobody suitable can be found, and an English-speaking actor will play the part. I had it pretty much sussed out how it worked. Not that I’m correct in everything, but experience told me that, you know, I did not need to get excited.”

He had been assured that this time was different. Waltz decided that, if nothing else, he would enjoy reading with Quentin Tarantino, and that would be that.

“Nice to meet with you, Mr. Tarantino. Nice to meet you, Mr. Bender.”

Sitting opposite the American director and producer, the first thing to strike the actor was the director asking him if he would mind reading the opening scene. “Would I mind?” he thought. “Are you kidding?”

“But of course,” said Waltz, nodding in agreement.

Waltz’s Colonel Hans Landa introduced himself to the French farm owner, Perrier LaPadite, on this occasion played by Quentin Tarantino.

“First of all, from the get-go, Lawrence and I kept looking over at each other, and our smiles just became bigger and bigger and bigger,” enthused Quentin.

“We were literally kicking each other under the table in excitement,” agreed Bender.

Waltz’s complete absorption into the scene was nearly broken when he thought to himself, “Why is Quentin sucking his thumb?” Instantly, he realized the banality of such questioning. Perrier LaPadite smokes a pipe. The man in front of him, the writer of the weighty screenplay, was pretending to smoke one.

The room was devoid of any noticeable cameras. Instead, the great Quentin Tarantino was encouraging him to play, like a kid. He had never felt so safe, so relaxed, in an audition room. Nobody was looking at him probingly, questioning how he had the temerity to even be in the room. His natural pessimism waited for the curveball that was sure to come.

Quentin, on the other hand, never witnessed anything other than devotion to what he had written from the actor who sat in front of him. “It was amazing. It wasn’t as though none of the other actors had done the scene well. Some had done it well, but they didn’t own the role, especially that scene. The scene was an incredibly dangerous mountain that they managed to scale and survive, but they didn’t own the mountain—they just got to the top. Waltz owned the mountain.

“By the time we finished reading the scene, we knew that he had the role. He had saved the movie; he absolutely saved the film. So it is over with, and Lawrence and I are absolutely losing our fucking minds. We are just so effusive, and Christoph is just being very casual about it. He is looking at us as if we are just a couple of gregarious Americans. I pick up that he is thinking we have said this to everybody, and we are blowing a little sunshine up his ass. So, I make it plain: ‘No, no, you understand this character. You’re fantastic, you’re great.’

“To which Christoph replied, ‘Well, thank you very much. That is very nice to hear. However, I’m of the belief—and sometimes I run afoul of my colleagues in this regard—that an actor is only as capable as the role that he is playing. If you want an actor to be great, you need to give him great material. So, when you are effusively telling me how magnificent I am, in fact, you are just applauding your own material. Because, yes, I’m quite good playing this role, but that is because it is a damned good role, but ultimately, you are just complimenting yourself.’

“To which I reply, ‘Well, thank you very much. But be that as it may, you cannot deny that when it comes to this role, you have an inner compass.’

“He thinks about it for a second and responds by saying, ‘Okay, possibly I have this compass of which you speak. Nevertheless’—he points at the script—‘the North Pole is the North Pole.’

“Ha! ‘Well,’ I say, ‘you have just done the scene in English, and you obviously speak German?’

“‘Of course I speak German—I’m from Austria.’

“‘Can you do a little bit of it in German for us?’

“He picks up the script, and away he goes. It is perfect. ‘There are also parts written in French. Could you give us a little of it in French?’

“Picking up the screenplay written in English, he translates it into French, and he is doing it perfect. So, I ask him, ‘There is also a little in Italian—do you speak Italian?’

“Of course, he fucking nailed it! We found our guy!”

Quentin called his friend Tom Tykwer [director of Run Lola Run and who had been tasked with translating Quentin’s script into German]

“I found him, I found him, I found him! I found Landa!”

A bomb went off in Tykwer’s head. “Of course you did! Why did I not think of Christoph?”

Peacock's New Workplace Sitcom 'Dig' Unearths Amy Poehler's Best TV Role in Years | Review

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amy Poehler hasn't led a TV comedy in over a decade, and the return vehicle she's chosen for herself is, of all things, an archaeological workplace comedy set in Greece. Dig, Peacock's new 10-episode half-hour series from Poehler, Mike Schur, and J.J. Philbin, based on Kate Myers' bestselling novel Excavations, reunites the Parks and Recreation brain trust, adding in a dash of Hugh Laurie for good measure.

It’s a strong starting lineup, and yet, Dig spends its early episodes trying to be a workplace comedy, leaning on dig-site hierarchies and academic turf wars, when what it actually turns out to be good at is something else entirely. Stick with it. The show finds itself once everyone stops bickering over chain-of-command nonsense and starts working together to solve a mystery (and maybe excavate their own long-buried issues).

Poehler plays Elise DeBellnick, a career archaeologist who has spent what feels like her entire adult life serving as the right hand to Dr. Neville Wycombe (Laurie), a legendary, pompous, impossibly charming British professor who keeps almost retiring every summer. She dreams of leading her own dig in Scotland, if the gods of academic funding allow it, but every season Wycombe announces his grand departure and then just . . . stays. The students clearly love him. What they don’t love is Elise's management style, which is blunt and humorless and entirely too by-the-book for a group of twenty-somethings spending their sun-drenched holiday on the Aegean coast.

The women surrounding Elise are where the show loads most of its more interesting character work, though it takes a while for any of them to land. Geraldine Viswanathan plays Dylan, a former dig favorite who ghosted Elise and the site years ago under circumstances the show treats as a big reveal but lands with a thud. Antonia Thomas is Clare, the hyper-competent lab manager who is roughly a decade younger than Elise and stuck in a Cold War with her that the show gestures at without ever making you feel the weight of. Dylan and Clare have history too, through Clare's fiancé Gary (Jon Pointing), who used to date Dylan. The love triangle, or whatever geometric shape this is, doesn't generate nearly as much tension as the show seems to think it should, mostly because we're never given enough about these people to understand why they'd care about each other this deeply, or at all. Their backstories are a bit too fill-in-the-blank, even for this modern age of television.

Then there's Patty Borkman. Fina Strazza, in a performance that should have casting directors rearranging their call sheets, plays Wycombe's twitchy, overeager assistant like a cartoon mouse who somehow earned a research fellowship. She's the character the show doesn't seem to realize it needs the most. Where the other women arrive preloaded with backstory the writers expect you to care about without earning it, Patty is a blank page, and Strazza fills her in with a goofy, wide-eyed sincerity that feels completely unmanufactured. She endears without trying. She's odd in a way the show badly needs more of, a little left of center in every scene she's in, and her chemistry with Poehler sneaks up on them both.

Kayvan Novak is a highlight as Stavros, the local Greek mayor who is ridiculous and openly besotted with Elise. The show also sprinkles in cameos from familiar Parks and Rec faces like Mo Collins, which land as fun surprises without being asked to carry too much weight. Instead, the focus is on the setting and job it houses.

The central mystery involves the discovery of a rare artifact the team dubs "Pretty Woman," and this is where Dig starts losing me. The discovery itself feels contrived, too perfectly timed to kick the plot into gear right when it needs kicking. It doesn't help that the artifact looks like it was unwrapped from museum gift shop packaging an hour before they shot the scene. For a show about people who spend their lives sifting through centuries of grime and sediment, nothing about this find has any texture. It's too clean, too white, too pristine. (Someone in the props department apparently decided that ancient Greek statues should look like they were 3D-printed yesterday.) It robs the discovery of the weight it needs to carry the back half of the season, and the resulting whodunit, while competently plotted, never feels like much is actually at stake.

But you push past it because what Poehler is trying to do with her characters is genuinely worthwhile. Schur, Philbin, and the team clearly want Dig to be an archaeology Hacks, but the early episodes lean almost too hard into the workplace comedy template with departmental rivalries played for laughs and the whole "dysfunctional team at a quirky job" structure that Schur has built a career on. It's fine. Some of it is sharp. But it's also where the show is weakest, because the dig site never quite feels like a real place with real stakes, and the workplace dynamics can't generate friction when we don't understand why half these people showed up in the first place. Poehler is doing something interesting with Elise even in these early stretches, though, playing a woman whose competence has fossilized into rigidity, whose commitment to one man's project has started to look less like loyalty and more like a failure of nerve. The show just isn't giving her enough to push against yet.

Tina and Amy are the peanut butter and jelly of Hollywood.

Where Dig transforms is around the midpoint, once the mystery forces the four women into actual collaboration. The workplace comedy scaffolding falls away, and what replaces it is a caper driven by friendship. The shift isn't subtle, and it's not entirely smooth, but it works because the actors have been building chemistry in the margins all along. Part of what helps it along is the generational math. Poehler has talked about how Elise sees herself at 19, 29, and 39 in the women around her, and when the show leans into that, it shines. Patty's wide-eyed intensity reminds Elise of who she was before she got cautious. Dylan's restlessness mirrors the version of herself that almost left. Clare's desire to be appreciated for her talents is what Elise feels now. They shouldn't get along, and for most of the early episodes they don't, but the mystery strips away the professional pecking order and forces them to actually see each other.

What saves Dig from collapsing under its own structural wobbliness is the thing you'd expect: the people in it are really, really funny. Laurie, who studied archaeology at Cambridge in real life, starts the season doing what amounts to a prestige comedy cameo, with his kerchiefs and copious Eton references. But somewhere around the midpoint, he unlocks something warmer and more vulnerable, and his scenes with Poehler in the back half of the season become some of the show's strongest. Strazza is the other revelation. She's a dark horse in a cast of heavy hitters, and she commits to Patty's weirdness with a fearlessness that most young actors would sand down into something safer. Instead, she lets Patty be strange and awkward and a little too intense, and it works because Strazza never signals to the audience that she's in on the joke.

Dig has the bones (sorry) of something great, and by its final stretch, it's closer to great than the first few episodes would have you guess. The setting is gorgeous when the show ventures off the lot and actually shoots in Greece and southern Italy. Poehler and Laurie are as good a comedy pairing as you'll find on television right now. The worldbuilding never fully recovers from the early stumbles, and Dylan's backstory stays paper-thin no matter how many scenes Viswanathan throws at it. Once the four women lock in and the show stops trying to be an office comedy with shovels, the gap between what Dig is and what it could be narrows considerably. But Amy Poehler on a television set, doing what she does best with people who know how to write for her, is something we’ve been missing for too long. We’re glad Peacock unearthed her talents again for this one.

All 10 episodes of Dig drop November 23 on Peacock.
