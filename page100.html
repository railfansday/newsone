The ballot for the BBWAA portion of the 2027 Baseball Hall of Fame vote was revealed on Monday, featuring 13 holdovers and 14 newcomers.

The full ballot, in alphabetical order, is Bobby Abreu, Jake Arrieta, Jay Bruce, Mark Buehrle, Asdrúbal Cabrera, Starlin Castro, Wade Davis, Brett Gardner, Cole Hamels, Félix Hernández, Torii Hunter, Jon Lester, Andrew Miller, Dustin Pedroia, Andy Pettitte, Buster Posey, Alex Rodriguez, Francisco Rodríguez, Jimmy Rollins, Pablo Sandoval, Ervin Santana, Kyle Seager, Joakim Soria, Chase Utley, Omar Vizquel, David Wright and Ryan Zimmerman. Newcomers to the ballot are in bold.

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

We'll dive further into this group as we get closer to the Dec. 6 vote.

Giants legend Buster Posey joins the ballot, and he's one of those cases where I don't think a bunch of people need to delve deeply into his numbers. More than statistics, sometimes we get "feel" guys, as in, you just know he's a Hall of Famer. Posey qualifies there, I would think, for 75% of the electorate. We'll obviously do a deeper dive in the coming weeks, but even without the longevity component (Posey only played in parts of 12 MLB seasons), I think he's in.

As for the rest of the first-timers, the best case would be Jon Lester. Given the way a lot of similar starting pitchers have been treated in voting in recent years, my hunch is he'll be able to hang around on the ballot, but he has a major uphill battle in order to get in.

Then you're looking at Brett Gardner, Ryan Zimmerman, Kyle Seager, the short peak of Jake Arrieta and relief work of Andrew Miller. Good players worth mentioning, of course, but they won't sniff the Hall and it seems likely all behind Posey and Lester are one-and-dones on this ballot, as they'll fall short of 5% of the vote.

This is the sixth year for Alex Rodriguez on the ballot, which means it's the sixth time that I point out that we don't really need much time to go over his case. His numbers say he's one of the 10 best position players of all time, but his ties to PEDs are keeping him out to this point.

He did rise from 37.1% of the vote in 2025 to 40% last time, a new high-water mark. The progress is too modest to believe it's trending toward him getting in, but the number this year is certainly worth watching. If he somehow makes a leap to the 50% range, a deeper conversation might be in order.

Medical personnel surrounded Jackson, draped a towel over his hand and brought him down the tunnel, off the field.

What more was there to say? Lamar Jackson, one of the defining players not just of the era but of NFL history, was hurt. Again. And it was serious. Again.

Jackson officially landed on IR with a thumb injury that will require surgery. Josh Allen and Patrick Mahomes have both had hundreds of similar plays and, with the exception of Mahomes' torn ACL last year, avoided injury for the most part. Thomas Frank succeeds Pierre Sage as Crystal Palace manager, signing a two-and-a-half-year deal with the south-east London club.

The Dane will be in the dugout for Palace’s trip to Coventry on Saturday, returning to management for the first time since leaving Tottenham in February.

Frank was on the Eagles’ shortlist to take over from Oliver Glasner this summer before they opted for Sage, and the former Spurs and Brentford boss is said to have been keen on a return to the Premier League.

After a difficult spell in north London, with the club sitting narrowly above the relegation zone when he left, Frank took time away from management to learn from leaders inside and outside football, as well as spending time with friends and family.

His disastrous stint at the Tottenham Hotspur Stadium has inevitably left some scars, but Frank is still widely revered for his work in the west of the capital, where he got the Bees promoted to the Premier League, stabilising them as a top-flight club on one of the division’s lowest budgets.

Frank will undoubtedly have learnt some lessons from his seven-month spell at Spurs.

Here, Sam Tabuteau identifies the three biggest mishaps of Frank’s Tottenham tenure…

Part of what made Frank such an engaging character at Brentford was his ability to talk openly and honestly about the club’s culture, tactics and wider societal issues while maintaining an endearingly British sense of humour.

At times self-deprecating and sarcastic, Frank earned plenty of admirers for the way in which he humanised what can often be a cut-throat and emotionally vacant industry.

After nine years at Brentford, nearly seven as head coach, Frank had earned the luxury of being able to talk so candidly. He was comfortable in his surroundings, and safe in the knowledge that Brentford had not sacked a manager since 2015.

However, at Tottenham, he was starting from scratch at a club whose ambitions don’t leave much room for home truths.

Coming off the back of their Europa League triumph, Spurs supporters wanted to hear how the club was going to push forward.

Yet in his first press conference as Spurs manager, Frank joked that if there was one thing he could promise, it was that they would lose games.

His ill-timed quip did not go down well, regardless of the spirit in which it was intended, and from that point on Frank was fighting a losing battle to convince supporters he understood the culture of the club.

Losing supporters after tame Chelsea defeat

Given how it ended, Frank’s tenure started surprisingly positively, with Tottenham third in the Premier League table after nine games.

The mood quickly changed, though, after a dispiriting 1-0 home defeat to London rivals Chelsea, in which Spurs managed just one shot on target.

Frank’s pragmatic style worked well at Brentford because they had one of the most efficient frontlines in the division and were not expected to dominate possession.

At Spurs, however, Frank’s risk-averse style combined with an attacking unit missing Dejan Kulusevski and James Maddison meant they relied heavily on set-piece goals and struggled to create chances in open play.

Frank’s inability to evolve his style, coupled with Spurs’ shortcomings in attack, culminated in a performance against Chelsea that left many supporters disillusioned and would come to symbolise his time in charge.

Another example of Frank’s naivety came during January’s stoppage-time defeat to Bournemouth, when the 53-year-old was pictured pre-match walking around the pitch at the Vitality Stadium with an Arsenal-branded coffee cup.

Seemingly none the wiser, Frank stood with the cup in hand while some staff members could not hide their shock, and the resulting pictures went viral as Spurs slumped to an excruciating defeat.

After the game, Frank’s blunder took centre stage, and his mystified reaction just about summed up the growing rift between him and Spurs’ supporters.

While Spurs fans vented their anger and embarrassment online and in the stands, Frank could not begin to understand how an innocent mistake could be blown so far out of proportion.

From that point on, it was clear that the narrative was quickly beginning to slip from Frank’s grasp and Spurs would lose their next five league games before he was relieved of his duties.
