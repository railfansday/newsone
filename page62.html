Hims & Hers inks deal with Eli Lilly to offer branded weight loss drugs" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Hims & Hers struck a deal with Eli Lilly to offer the drugmaker’s branded obesity treatments through its online health and wellness platform.

Under the agreement, Eli Lilly will give Hims & Hers pharmacies direct access to its U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved GLP-1 medications at self-pay prices. Eligible patients on the Hims & Hers platform can be prescribed and order Eli Lilly's injectable Zepbound (tirzepatide) and daily oral medication Foundayo (orforglipron) without leaving the company's platform.

Prior to this agreement, Lilly drugs were available to patients on Hims & Hers only if they left the site and went to Lilly's direct-to-consumer platform, LillyDirect.

Shares of Hims & Hers jumped 8% Friday following news of the Eli Lilly partnership.

The agreement brings together Lilly’s obesity medicines and Hims & Hers’ consumer platform to expand consumers' access to FDA-approved weight loss drugs, the companies said.

"Patients can complete a consultation, receive a prescription if their provider determines it is appropriate and have an FDA-approved Lilly medicine shipped directly to their home, all in one seamless experience," the companies said in a press release.

Self-pay pricing starts at $149 per month for Foundayo and $299 per month for Zepbound.

"At Lilly, we are always looking for new ways to help more people get the medicines they need, and that means meeting patients where they already are," said Ilya Yuffa, executive vice president and president, Lilly USA and global customer capabilities, Eli Lilly and Company, in a statement. "Millions of people turn to Hims & Hers for their care, and through this agreement, those patients now have a simple, more direct way to get FDA-approved Lilly medicine at an affordable price if prescribed."

Hims & Hers' technology platform supports provider outreach, follow-up care and treatment planning. Weight loss patients on Hims & Hers receive a membership experience that includes 24/7 access to a care team, nutrition guidance, regular clinical check-ins and access to the Hims & Hers Weight Loss community, according to the company.

The agreement with Eli Lilly mirrors Hims & Hers' partnership with Novo Nordisk, which has been selling its branded weight loss drugs Wegovy and Ozempic through Hims & Hers since March. Novo had previously sued the telehealth company for offering compounded versions of its medications. Hims & Hers' agreement with Novo marked a shift in its obesity strategy toward FDA-approved branded GLP-1 treatments and away from cheaper compounded GLP-1 drugs.

Earlier this year, Hims & Hers took heat from the pharmaceutical manufacturer and federal regulators after announcing plans to launch a knockoff of Novo's oral pill. It backed down from those plans under pressure.

Hims & Hers stated that it never offered access to compounded copies of Lilly’s GLP-1 drugs and has committed not to do so in the future.

As part of the agreement, the companies have committed to protecting independent clinical decision-making, so that providers on the Hims & Hers platform continue to prescribe the treatment they determine is right for each patient.

“Lilly is one of the most innovative pharmaceutical companies in history, and I’m incredibly proud that Hims & Hers is a part of making sure more people can access the medicines they’ve created,” said Andrew Dudum, co-founder and CEO of Hims & Hers, in a statement. “By working together, we’re not just reaching more people with obesity with seamless, personal access to FDA-approved Lilly medicine. We’re showing the industry what innovators can achieve for customers everywhere when we work together to make the world well.”

Hims & Hers has faced financial pressure as it shifts from high-margin compounded GLP-1s to lower-margin branded weight-loss drugs. The company posted a $92 million loss in Q1, including $33 million in restructuring costs, largely from write-downs of its compounded GLP-1 supply chain, which executives said faced “risk of obsolescence.”

In Q2, Hims & Hers reported an $86 million loss, compared with a $43 million profit a year earlier. The quarter included $81 million in one-time costs, including expenses tied to its exit from compounded GLP-1s.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/pharma/cms-makes-it-official-codifying-medicare-drug-price-negotiations-rolled-out-under-ira" hreflang="en">CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Against a backdrop of repeated industry challenges in court, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) is locking in the drug price negotiation framework established under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act (IRA).

In turn, the process under which CMS selects certain high-spend Medicare Part B or Part D drugs and negotiates lower prices with manufacturers will carry on into 2029 and beyond. The rule codifies and expands policies previously established through guidance for the initial 2026-28 period.

The final rule is slated to go into effect Dec. 5, CMS said in a fact sheet accompanying Thursday’s announcement. By Feb. 1 of next year, CMS will select up to 20 drugs covered under Medicare Part B or Part D for price negotiations taking effect in 2029, with the potential to also select medicines included in previous rounds for renegotiation.

That upcoming group of drugs will make up the fourth cycle of pricing negotiations, with negotiated prices taking effect in 2029. CMS asserted that the rule gives both patients and drugmakers “a clear path forward as the agency builds on the program’s results and continues negotiating prices for high-cost drugs.”

The initial rounds of price negotiations have already included a slew of prominent brands, including Novo's semaglutide franchise of Wegovy and Ozempic and cancer meds like Bristol Myers Squibb’s Pomalyst, Pfizer’s Ibrance and AstraZeneca’s Calquence.

Meanwhile, the latest tranche of drugs selected earlier this year for negotiation, whose new prices would go into effect in 2028, includes notable entrants like Gilead’s HIV treatment Biktarvy, AbbVie’s Botox and Eli Lilly’s diabetes drug Trulicity, to name just a few.

Beyond codifying the framework for price negotiations in 2029 and subsequent years, CMS has also taken steps to fine-tune several elements of the program.

Namely, the rule finalizes a policy on how CMS classifies certain fixed-combination drugs, with the aim of addressing “potential program integrity concerns” that could encourage drugmakers to evade negotiated prices by steering patients toward new product formulations.

The rule also includes a provision establishing a temporary “small biotech floor” that prevents CMS from offering or accepting a counteroffer below a specified price floor for certain eligible small biotech drugs in 2029 and 2030. It also codifies two policies related to Medicare Part D intended to ensure plan coverage of medicines with a maximum fair price in effect and limit the prices plans pay pharmacies and other dispensing entities.

The IRA and the Medicare price negotiations it empowered have been frequent targets of the biopharma industry, which has broadly alleged that the policy is unconstitutional and that the pricing talks aren’t as voluntary as advertised. But attempts by major drugmakers and powerful trade organizations like the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) to push back on Medicare price negotiations have repeatedly stalled out.

In the interim, the U.S. government has made a separate and more recent push to rein in high prescription drug costs by leaning on President Donald Trump’s “most favored nation” (MFN) strategy.

“President Trump directed us to lower drug costs for Americans using every tool at our disposal,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a statement announcing the new rule. “This rule provides additional tools to help CMS negotiate better deals, achieve greater savings for taxpayers and beneficiaries, and improve health outcomes for seniors who rely on the Medicare Prescription Drug Program.”

MFN, for its part, has yielded at times, controversial deals with more than two dozen drugmakers, generally involving commitments to price new medicines in line with prices in high-income comparator countries and offer MFN pricing on certain existing drugs through Medicaid.

Through those deals, which have also included investment commitments in the U.S., drugmakers have been able to secure temporary exemptions from the Trump administration’s threatened pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Around this week’s midterm elections, several Big Pharma executives have signaled that they expect little immediate change to their thinking around MFN. Nevertheless, the lack of transparency surrounding the deals has already drawn scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers. With Democrats set to control both chambers of Congress beginning in January, the party may soon press harder to uncover the details of those agreements.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/ai-and-machine-learning/equiti-acquires-lexi-ai-combine-ai-human-medical-interpretation" hreflang="en">Equiti acquires Lexi AI to combine AI, human medical interpretation</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Equiti acquires Lexi AI to combine AI, human medical interpretation" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Equiti is acquiring Lexi AI to build a hybrid language-access model that combines AI language support with human medical interpreters.

Equiti, which sells medical interpretation services to hospitals and health systems through its Martti business, provides 24/7 access to qualified medical interpreters across more than 250 languages and dialects. Lexi provides multilingual, AI-powered medical interpretation. The acquisition deal merges Lexi's immediate AI language support, covering over 35 languages and dialects, with Martti and its network of qualified medical interpreters to help healthcare organizations expand language access.

The acquisition was announced October 28. Financial details of the deal were not disclosed.

With this acquisition, Equiti is building on Martti’s work pioneering video remote interpretation in healthcare more than two decades ago, according to executives. The use of AI can expand where and when language services can be offered, particularly for everyday interactions that may not currently receive interpretation support, Equiti executives said.

Martti was built alongside healthcare organizations to make qualified interpreters available by video. Lexi, in turn, has been developed with healthcare customers to extend immediate language support into more workflows. The acquisition brings those capabilities together, with human-in-the-loop design, patient safety and patient experience at its center, Equiti executives said.

The company saw an opportunity to combine its services with Lexi's AI expertise rather than build a healthcare AI interpretation product internally, Maureen Huber, Equiti CEO, told Fierce Healthcare.

"Lexi brings specialized expertise in AI, engineering and healthcare product design, along with experience putting that technology into the hands of care teams. That combination takes time to build," Huber said. "For more than 20 years, our work through Martti has been about making qualified human interpretation accessible within healthcare. We understand the service, the clinical environment and what it takes to support healthcare organizations every day. Developing AI and machine learning technology calls for additional, specialized expertise. We wanted people who had already been doing that work with healthcare in mind."

She added, "We spent time evaluating the category and learning from customers. Lexi brought together the people, product and approach we were seeking. Combining their expertise with ours gives us a stronger foundation to expand language access responsibly."

Combining AI and human interpretation makes language support more accessible across the patient journey, helping organizations improve care delivery while making better use of their resources, according to Equiti executives.

Healthcare organizations are increasingly interested in AI interpretation capabilities and are looking for guidance, Huber noted.

"Customers were asking: 'Where could AI help? How do we evaluate it? What is appropriate for our patients?' Healthcare leaders see the same advances in consumer AI that everyone else sees. They also must think about what happens when those tools enter a care environment, sometimes before an organization has decided how they should be used. They want a partner who understands both the possibilities and the responsibilities," she said.

She added, "I would describe that as inquiry and evaluation. There is real curiosity about extending language support to more of the patient experience, particularly before and after a visit. Our job is to help customers identify those opportunities while keeping qualified human interpretation central to care delivery."

Equiti calls this approach Modality Matters, a connected model that allows healthcare organizations to match each encounter with the appropriate modality — immediate, healthcare-purpose-built AI support for approved use cases and qualified medical interpretation where clinical complexity, regulatory requirements, organizational policy or patient preference calls for a human interpreter. With this approach, organizations have the flexibility to shift between modalities as clinical context and communication needs change in real time.

The combined portfolio of Equiti and Lexi's capabilities will extend language access into everyday interactions that often go unsupported, Huber noted.

Equiti is focusing Lexi's AI-powered medical interpretation on routine patient engagement, including before and after care, such as appointment communications, scheduling and nonclinical follow-up logistics.

"We see a significant opportunity for Lexi in that communication before and after care: helping patients exchange messages, arrange appointments and stay connected in their preferred language. Language access must extend beyond the clinical encounter. Think about what happens after a patient leaves the clinic. They receive a message about a follow-up appointment, but they need to ask whether it is at the same location, how to reschedule, or whom to contact. Those may be simple questions for the organization, but the answers determine whether the patient can take the next step," she said.

Qualified human interpreters remain the foundation for care delivery, Huber noted. "In conversations such as those about a diagnosis, informed consent, treatment or palliative care, the ability to clarify meaning, recognize cultural context and respond with empathy is essential. A medically qualified interpreter helps people understand one another through conversations that can be complex and deeply personal," she said.

Equiti said it can work with customers to define appropriate uses within their policies and applicable requirements, preserving patient choice and access to a qualified human interpreter.

Language access is also a strategic performance driver for healthcare organizations, as effective communication influences patient understanding and experience, clinical outcomes, workforce efficiency and financial performance.

Equiti's customers include Wellstar Health System, Corewell Health, NYU Langone and Lee Health. Lexi works with a growing pipeline of federally qualified health centers and community providers including Lowell Community Health Center, which serves one of the most multilingual communities in Massachusetts.

As general-purpose AI gets better at translation, a healthcare-specific interpretation platform has distinct advantages, Huber asserted.

"Healthcare communication involves clinical meaning, cultural context, jargon and acronyms during situations where people may be frightened, vulnerable or unsure how to ask a question. Our job is to remove those communication barriers, and that requires understanding the care environment, fitting into clinical workflows and knowing when a qualified human interpreter needs to be involved," she said. "That is where Equiti’s experience matters. We bring the relationships, service infrastructure and human expertise developed through more than two decades of healthcare interpretation, alongside Lexi’s technology and team."

Huber said she envisions broader uses of Lexi's AI language support in healthcare workflows. "A patient’s need to communicate continues after the conversation with a clinician ends. Lexi already supports text-based messaging, including translation of text provided by a care team, and can even leave voicemail messages for patients. That creates opportunities to help patients access information and communicate between visits. Helping someone navigate follow-up care is a natural direction for that work, like understanding the next step, connecting with the right office or arranging an appointment. The value is in making it easier for patients to act on the care plan they have been given and ask for help along the way," she said.

"We built Lexi to fit the way healthcare teams actually work, bringing immediate language support into the devices and workflows they already use,” Siddharth UR, co-founder and chief product officer of Lexi, said in a statement. “As part of Equiti, we can extend that experience across the patient journey and connect it to a broader language access model built around the needs of each encounter.”

Lexi will operate alongside Martti under Equiti, with the teams working together on implementation and integration across healthcare workflows. Equiti will work with each customer to determine how the solutions fit within its language access program, clinical environment, and organizational policies.

Melissa Joan Hart Explains It All: Hot Flashes, Menopause, and Turning 50

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Actor Melissa Joan Hart has a vivid memory of her mother standing in front of their open freezer to cool off. As a child, she didn’t understand why. Now, as a 50-year-old woman, Hart knows that her mom was experiencing hot flashes.

“[She’s] only 20 years older than me, so maybe I missed the part where she was going through [menopause], but there was no conversation about it,” Hart told Healthline. “I feel like it’s just becoming a hot topic with women, where…the taboo is going away from it now.”

Her own perimenopausal symptoms started with night sweats.

“[It] was really jarring, and the first few times, you’re not really sure what that was…do I need to turn the air down or what’s going on here?” Hart said.

Over time, she learned that she was experiencing moderate to severe hot flashes, which affect up to 80% of women during the menopause transition and can persist for 7 to 10 years.

Doctors categorize hot flashes as mild, moderate, or severe, said Diana Bitner, MD, OB-GYN, at Corewell Health.

“[So] if they’re mild, it’s a sense of warmth (maybe in the chest, or the neck, or the face). A moderate hot flash is where there’s associated sweating, and a severe hot flash is something that stops you in your tracks, wakes you up from the middle of the night,” Bitner told Healthline.

Hart has mostly experienced hot flashes while home or at her three sons’ activities, and they have yet to interfere with her work. However, according to research, one in three women report moderate to severe challenges in managing their work due to symptoms related to menopause.

“It’s just so sad that about 10% of women leave the workforce due to menopause,” said Bitner. “And so it can really affect our ability to perform at these key years of not only institutional memory, but also of earning power, and it can really affect the whole community.”

Bitner said women should feel empowered to bring up their menopausal symptoms or questions with their doctor.

“[This] is something that was taboo for so long…women didn’t really talk about this, whether it was because of embarrassment, or [they] weren’t sure if anyone was experiencing it,” she said. “[There’s] no reason, in this day and age, we need to suffer through it…it’s something that there are treatment options for.”

In fact, Bitner said providing specific details about their symptoms, such as how many mild, moderate, or severe hot flashes they’ve experienced over the past week, can help doctors better assess patients’ needs.

Hart plans to discuss treatment options for her hot flashes with her doctor soon. In the last few years, she said she has put emphasis on her midlife health.

“I just was sick of waking up with aches and pains in general [thinking], ‘is this the rest of my life?’” she said.

She started listening to self-help books in the car while driving her kids around and found ways to put herself first.

“My kids are a little bit older, so I kind of have that freedom to be able to do that,” she said. “[But] along with getting older, comes the menopause, comes that different time in life. And I think it’s a good time to reevaluate [how] our bodies can’t handle what [they] used to handle, and let’s figure it out.”

Hart has partnered with pharmaceutical company Bayer on its “Life Doesn’t Stop for a Hot Flash” campaign, which encourages women to discuss menopause symptoms with their doctors and promotes Lynkuet, a hormone-free prescription medication for moderate to severe hot flashes associated with menopause.

“It’s called ‘Life Doesn’t Stop for a Hot Flash’ campaign because it shouldn’t, because we should be able to push through, and this should be a time in our lives when our kids are getting older, and we’re able to kind of reinvent ourselves,” said Hart. “And I think it’s an exciting time, and it shouldn’t be something that we’re afraid of.”

She is embracing speaking openly about midlife health.

“I hit 50 this year, and it was, like, a huge milestone, and I think that’s something to be proud of, and not something to shy away from,” she said.

Hart also hopes to challenge the longstanding barriers women face in Hollywood as they age, including fewer opportunities for leading roles.

“[I] think we’re seeing that the generation before, and my generation, it feels like we’re pushing those boundaries…and we’re saying, you know, that women at this age have a lot to give, have a lot of information to share, have a lot of stories to tell and experiences and adventures still ahead of us, and, you know, we’re not ready to stop,” Hart said.

In everyday life, Bitner said that while some patients avoid talking about midlife issues like menopause with their doctors, she is seeing more patients who are confident being forthcoming.

As more women talk openly about menopause, she hopes to include men in the conversation, too. In fact, she invites her patients to bring their partners to appointments, so they can gain an understanding of menopause and the experiences their partners face, such as hot flashes, sleep issues, vaginal dryness, and discomfort or pain during intercourse.

“[Often], there’s, you know, comments that come up [from men who] say, ‘How do I talk about this without getting in trouble?’” said Bitner. “[We] want everyone to join this conversation…it doesn’t have to be just her issue because we know it’s not.”

And once you’re there, you still need to work out what to do next. As people need to review more and more pull requests, time spent scrolling adds up. This new food pyramid emphasizes plant-based protein sources, cooking with plant-based oils, and healthier sugars and carbohydrates.

It also echoes the traditional food pyramid used in the United States since 1992.

The new pyramid breaks away from the Trump administration’s updated nutritional guidelines, which are part of Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s Make America Health Again (MAHA) agenda and prioritize red meat over plants.

The new pyramid was designed by a research team led by Brigham Young University professor Karen Della Corte. The researchers noted that this is the first international food pyramid that is designed to reflect scientific agreement across multiple countries and cultures.

“I find the new international food pyramid a more practical and aligned approach for the prevention of chronic disease,” said Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the creation of the food pyramid.

“The new international food pyramid places greater emphasis on food quality and the importance of building meals around nutrient-dense plant foods, while still allowing room for lean animal proteins such as fish, poultry, eggs, and yogurt,” she added.

Routhenstein told Healthline that research consistently shows that plant-forward eating patterns, including the Mediterranean and DASH diets, are associated with better cardiovascular health and a lower risk of heart disease.

This isn't a bug. It's the default.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at randomchaos.us

A machine running OpenAI's Codex wrote about 37 TB to its main SSD in 21 days of uptime. Process and file-level checks pinned the continuous writer to one place: Codex's local SQLite feedback log at ~/.codex/logs_2.sqlite. At that rate the drive takes roughly 640 TB a year, about 640 full-drive writes on a 1 TB SSD. Many consumer drives are rated near 600 TBW, so Codex alone can burn a drive's warranted write endurance in under a year.

The database looks small. It retains about half a million rows and sits around 1.2 GiB. The SQLite AUTOINCREMENT counter tells the real story: it has advanced past 5.5 billion ids, a roughly 10,000x gap between rows kept and rows ever inserted. In one 15-second sample about 36,211 rows went in while the retained count never moved. Codex inserts a batch, indexes it, writes it to the WAL, then prunes inside the same transaction. The file size stays flat and the disk keeps taking writes, which is why drive wear rather than database growth is the first visible signal.

The cause is a single line. The persistent SQLite sink is attached with a global TRACE default, Targets::new().with_default(Level::TRACE), in both the app-server and the TUI. Every target gets persisted at TRACE, so the database fills with logs no one chose to keep. The default log level, not a stray code path, is what writes the terabytes, and every install ships this way.

The high-frequency rows are dependency noise and raw protocol traffic: inotify events (128,764 OPEN events for ld.so.cache, 23,843 for passwd, more for nsswitch.conf and locale.alias), thousands of tokio-tungstenite poll_read and with_context entries, websocket frame fields, and repeated OpenTelemetry mirror rows from codex_otel.log_only and codex_otel.trace_safe. TRACE alone is 70.7% of retained bytes; the two OTel mirror targets add another 25.3%. Dropping those categories removes about 96% of retained log bytes without turning feedback logging off. Raw websocket and SSE payload bodies are also persisted, and the reporter left them out of the public sample because they can contain private conversation content.

A large logs DB does more than wear the SSD. On startup the app-server's SQLite pool can time out (#27741), and a 419 MB logs DB alongside session state has produced 1-to-5-minute cold starts on WSL2 (#28166). One commenter reports that Codex in /goal mode will delete files and folders trying to free space when the disk is full, so a full drive becomes data loss, and a Linux box rebooted while full can fail to log in.

Three PRs merged by late June 2026 and, per the reporter's own Codex, cut persisted logs by about 85%. #29432 stops logging every Responses websocket event and #29457 filters noisy targets, both in 0.142.0; #29599 stops persisting bridged log events in 0.143.0. OpenAI says the series of fixes is in the latest shipping desktop app, CLI, and IDE extension.

Not all of it has shipped. A later comment notes 0.144.1 is missing #31789 through #31792, which appear in 0.145.0-alpha.4 and presumably 0.145.0. A separate root cause, tracked in #32496, has Codex continually rewriting models_cache.json and scope_v3.json for roughly another 1.3 TB a year, well under the logging figure but still idle I/O for nothing. Testing with GPT 5.6 Sol High on macOS, one commenter measured unmitigated churn falling from 33.1 to 7.36 inserted ids per second after the upstream fixes, with a local filter taking it to 0.45.

Before and after slider in React, accessible and draggable

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at facadeui.com

Roofers, painters, landscapers and remodelers all have the same proof: a photo from before the job and a photo from after. Put the two side by side and the eye wanders. Put them in one frame with a handle the visitor drags and the eye follows the handle. That is why the comparison slider earns a spot on every home services site I build, and why I spent more time on the Before / after slider in Facade than its size suggests. Most of that time went into things you cannot see in a demo: touch, keyboard and screen readers.

The first version of every comparison slider on the internet listens to mousedown, mousemove and mouseup. Then someone opens it on a phone and nothing happens, so touchstart and touchmove get bolted on, and now there are two code paths that drift apart.

Pointer events replace both. onPointerDown, onPointerMove and onPointerUp fire for mouse, touch and pen with the same event shape, and clientX means the same thing in all three. The one extra call that makes dragging reliable is setPointerCapture: once the visitor presses down, the element keeps receiving move events even when the finger or cursor leaves its box. Without it, a fast drag that exits the frame freezes the handle halfway.

fromClientX reads the container's bounding rect and converts the pointer position into a percentage between 0 and 100. That percentage is the only piece of state. Everything else, the clip, the handle position, the ARIA attributes, derives from it.

Listen on the whole frame, not just the handle. Visitors tap anywhere on the photo and expect the handle to jump there. A 24 pixel handle as the only hit target is a usability bug on touch screens.

The after photo fills the frame. The before photo sits on top of it in an absolutely positioned layer, and clip-path: inset(0 X% 0 0) cuts that layer off on the right at the handle position. Dragging changes X and the before photo reveals or hides the after photo underneath.

Clip-path is the right tool because the two images never move or resize. A common alternative is to change the width of the top layer, which stretches the image unless you also counter-scale it, and that path leads to subpixel jitter during a drag. With clip-path both images stay pixel aligned and the browser only repaints the clipped region.

In the Facade component the value runs through a useSpring from motion/react and useTransform turns the spring into the clip string. The trick is in one effect: while dragging, the spring jumps straight to the value so the handle follows the finger one to one; when the visitor releases or taps somewhere, the spring eases to the new position. Reduced motion users get the jump in both cases.

A slider that only works with a pointer is a slider half the audience cannot use. Keyboard support is a single handler on the focusable handle, and it follows the conventions people already know from range inputs:

Call preventDefault on the keys you handle so the page does not scroll when someone presses Down with the handle focused. Do not handle keys you do not use; Tab must keep working.

The two images are not decorative. When you pass beforeSrc and afterSrc, each layer renders an with the beforeAlt and afterAlt text, because "Roof before replacement, missing shingles on the south side" is information a sighted visitor gets from the photo. The illustrated fallback that renders without URLs uses role="img" with the same labels.

Three things break comparison sliders on phones, and all three are fixable with CSS and one attribute.

First, scrolling. A horizontal drag on a touch screen is also a scroll gesture, and the browser will happily scroll the page while you try to move the handle. touch-action: none on the frame tells the browser that this element owns its touches. Only set it on the slider, never on the page.

Second, selection. A long press selects the image or the labels. user-select: none on the frame prevents it.

Third, the handle size. The visual handle can be slim, but the hit area is the entire frame thanks to the pointer handlers above, so a thumb never has to land on a two pixel line. The handle itself still gets a 44 pixel circle so it reads as something to grab.

Online Course Tutor Retrieval Architecture: Designing Fallbacks That Keep Answers Grounded

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use a staged retrieval design with explicit collections, bounded queries, and traceable source context; make each fallback narrower and more conservative than the previous stage.

An online course tutor is a small production system with an awkward failure mode: a plausible answer backed by the wrong lesson is harder to notice than an obvious error. I have been paged for missed jobs and duplicate deliveries, so I treat retrieval the same way I treat queue work: every stage needs a contract, a timeout, and an idempotent handoff.

The contract starts with the answer the student should see. A question about “the gradient descent lab” should return a lesson section, its course and tenant, and a citation anchor. If the first pass cannot satisfy those fields, it must say so and move to a defined fallback. Silent broadening is how cross-course answers leak into a tutor.

Keep ingestion, querying, and citation as separate observable stages. Ingestion normalizes a representative set of lessons, preserves tenant and access-control metadata on every indexed item, and records a stable document identifier. Querying searches the course collection first, with a bounded top-k and a deadline. Citation assembly checks that returned identifiers still belong to the requesting tenant before the language model sees them.

Fallback one can widen within the same course: a lexical match over titles and headings catches an unusual term that embeddings missed. Fallback two can search a curated, tenant-wide collection, but it should require a stronger confidence threshold and return fewer passages. The final fallback is an explicit “I could not find this in the course material” response, with a path for the instructor to add or correct a source.

That last step matters. A tutor that always produces text has no useful failure signal.

I don't let a timeout silently promote a broader search. In one runbook review, the dangerous path was a retry that reused the old broad query after the course index had recovered; the answer looked fine, but its citation belonged to a neighboring course. The fix was a stage token carried through the trace, a maximum of three retrieval attempts, and a final authorization check against the enrollment snapshot. That sequence takes more lines than a single search call, yet it makes a duplicate delivery and a late response converge on the same decision. The tutor either has an approved source or returns no answer.

I model each retrieval result as a record, not a loose chunk:

The check is deliberately boring. A score is not authorization. A high-scoring chunk from another tenant is still unusable, and a low-scoring chunk should not be promoted merely because the first query timed out.

Give every stage a budget and a reason code. For example, course_vector_timeout, course_low_recall, and tenant_lexical_hit are more useful in a runbook than one boolean called retrieval_failed. The application can then alert on a rising fallback rate without paging on a single hard question.

Evaluation needs representative documents and failure cases: short definitions, long lab instructions, duplicated slide text, misspelled terms, and questions that intentionally have no answer. Measure recall for finding the right section and precision for keeping irrelevant sections out. Also test a query whose only matching passage has a revoked enrollment; the expected result is no citation, not a clever guess.

A practical trace records the collection, stage, query hash, result IDs, scores, elapsed time, and the final citation IDs. Do not log raw student prompts by default. Retain enough context to replay a miss while respecting the tenant's data policy.

The write path should be equally explicit. A collection is created before ingestion, vectors are upserted with the metadata needed for authorization, and deletion removes both the item and its collection membership when a course is retired. These are separate state transitions, so a retry worker can safely repeat a transition keyed by the document ID.

The choice is less about a leaderboard score than about who owns the operational boundary. Pinecone is a managed vector service with a focused API. Weaviate offers an open-source and managed path with a broader schema layer. Postgres with pgvector keeps vectors beside transactional course and enrollment data, which can simplify joins and policy checks. Infrai is another option when a team wants one plain REST API and no SDK to install; its discovery surface is public and self-describing, and its wider backend surface can keep adjacent services behind one key.

The catch is that a broad interface does not remove retrieval design work. Use a direct specialist when you need a feature your evaluation depends on, such as a particular hybrid-ranking implementation, tight database transactions, or a self-hosted deployment mandate. Stick with Postgres when the authorization join is the hard part. Choose a managed vector service when your team cannot own index capacity and recovery. Your mileage may vary because corpus shape and enrollment rules dominate the result.
