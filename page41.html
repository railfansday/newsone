Dollar heads for best day since September after inflation beat forecasts

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Hotter U.S. consumer prices lifted bets on a December Fed rate increase, with a small chance priced in for a half-point move

The dollar climbed on Tuesday against every major counterpart as U.S. consumer prices that ran hotter than economists projected strengthened wagers that the Federal Reserve will raise rates in December. The Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index gained 0.6%, putting it on pace for its biggest daily gain since September, according to Bloomberg.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from September, while core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3%. A closely watched gauge of consumer prices came in above forecasts for the third month running, according to Bloomberg.

The Norwegian krone, Australian dollar and Swedish krona each lost over 1% versus the dollar, while the yen declined by up to 0.6%. The dollar is up 0.5% in November.

Traders began to assign a slim probability to a half-point increase in December, and they also bet on additional tightening in 2027, according to Bloomberg. Before the data, markets had priced in a quarter-point increase for next month.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller backed a December rate increase in remarks before the data release and said a half-point move should be considered if October core inflation came in around 0.3% or higher. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan called for a minimum of two more quarter-point increases when she spoke once the inflation figures were out, according to Bloomberg.

"A 50-basis-point hike is now being considered as a real possibility, and I don't think anyone was really considering that before," said Andrew Hazlett, a foreign-exchange trader at Monex, according to Bloomberg.

Overall consumer prices rose 0.6% in September before the 0.4% gain in October. The energy index rose 0.7% in October after a 5.2% jump in September, and the shelter index rose 0.3%.

The two-year Treasury yield climbed to 4.69%, a gain of more than a tenth of a percentage point.

The Fed meets Dec. 8-9. The bureau will publish the November CPI report on Dec. 10 at 8:30 a.m. ET.

5 Expert Habits to Find Inner Peace from Best-Selling Author Yung Pueblo

This is Expert Habits, a regular column in which thinkers, leaders, and other high performers talk about the habits and routines that help them live a better life.

Yung Pueblo wants to be clear on something: “I’m not a monk.” Sure, in his lifetime he’s meditated for over 13,000 hours, but he still enjoys the drama and chaos of Love Island UK like the rest of us. The best-selling author’s philosophical thoughts on self-awareness, emotional healing, and mindfulness have reached over 3.5 million followers since he first started posting to Instagram in 2013. His digital presence has since grown into published works of poetry and prose, including the New York Times bestsellers Inward, Clarity & Connection, and The Way Forward. Now Pueblo, whose real name is Diego Perez, is releasing a new book: Journey: The Year That Changes Everything (Penguin Random House), a 365-day guide to finding inner peace.

What exactly is inner peace to Perez? “It means that you have a good relationship with your emotions,” he says. “It’s that you’re able to feel your emotions without necessarily fueling the heavy ones..”

Inner peace, per Perez, isn’t a final destination. It’s about recognizing negative feelings and using tools you’ve learned to acknowledge that they’re happening without allowing them to take over your life. That’s what Journey—which debuts on November 10—is about. The following habits are just a few of the techniques he’s developed to do exactly that. Below, Perez explains the creative benefits of walking, the value of daily gratitude, and why you should protect your own peace even when others around you are chaotic—the latter of which makes keeping up with reality TV that much more enjoyable.

Yung Pueblo: “Oftentimes we have a relationship where we’re afraid of change and we need to realize that the universe that we live in, it’s totally founded on change at every level of existence—the atomic level, the biological level, the cosmological level. It’s because of change that we even have the opportunity to experience things. If our universe was static, we wouldn’t be having this conversation right now. So changing our relationship with impermanence and making it one where we actually get inspired to be more present. When we’re with our friends, with our family, with your mom, with your dad, you know that they’re not always going to be around. So the times when you are with them, to be ever more present, because you know change will happen at one point or another. Being able to have that presence just makes you have this ability to really enjoy some of the most beautiful parts of life.

“When it’s a sad moment, you have to be like, ‘OK, this is not going to last forever.’ But also in the precious moments, this isn’t going to last forever, so let me be here in the moment.

“Another way to look at it is that it’s defined by your relationship with change. So the more that you’re able to embrace change and flow with change, then your mind’s going to have less stress. But it’s probably one of the biggest challenges any human being can accept— willingly trying to change their relationship with change.”

“So much of our impulsiveness just comes from these lightning-quick reactions that we think are our nature. We think that they’re unchangeable, but the reality is that we can train ourselves to slow down to be able to ask: OK, is this me having an impulsive reaction right now? and then asking ourselves: Is this actually useful? Should I act this out or instead should I actually choose something that’s different than what I would’ve done in the past? And in the act of slowing down and being intentional, you just end up finding way better results. This is a real habit. You can learn that from meditating, you can learn that from therapy, but you can also just teach yourself the raw skill of feeling impulsiveness coming up and then challenging yourself to slow down.

“I’m not going to jump to conclusions. I’m not going to say anything right now. Let me just check in with how I feel, what actually feels true to me, and then put forward something that I think is actually going to be helpful as opposed to just something that I later regret.

“So if a friend of yours is telling you about something that they’re really mad about, they’re offering an invitation for you to be angry with them on their behalf. But instead I found that I don’t necessarily want to be angry, but I want to be able to hear my friends and offer support or a listening ear or whatever the situation is needed. At the same time, I can remain in my own energy while offering support to this individual. We get sucked into the energy of other people really, really quickly, but instead of getting sucked into someone’s chaos, for the past two years, what I’ve been working on is: Can I live in my peace and offer my peace as an invitation? They can join me in my peace too, if they want to, instead of me just getting sucked into their drama.”

“We fall into self-analysis when we don’t feel good. It’s just the worst time for you to be analyzing yourself about what you’ve done right, what you’ve done wrong. Negative self-talk isn’t productive. It’s good for you to be honest with yourself, but honesty also can come with gentleness and with self-acceptance. But when your mood is low, it’s going to be really hard for you to be gentle with yourself. So one thing I realize is when I don’t feel good or I’m over-exhausted from too much work or too much travel or whatnot, that’s the time for me to just suspend self-analysis and just treat myself gently—be mindful of what I’m saying to other people too, and just let it pass.

“And in the act of letting it pass, then you can do some more proper self-assessment because your best assessment is going to be when your mind is calm, not when it’s heavy.”

“Life is a long series of ups and downs, and there’s nothing wrong with that. But in the down moments, we tend to look at everything with such darkness that we forget that we still have access to clean water, that we still have access to food, that we have a house over our head. A lot of people in the world don’t have that. Having gratitude for the basics and the fundamentals really helps reset the balance where if something went wrong at work, you’re going to be okay, you’re going to be fine.”

“This is my recent favorite and it’s the newest habit that I picked up. You got to walk. You got to go for walks. Walking supports clarity, and I think it really supports creativity. I find that whenever I feel a little stuck with writing, just going on a 20- or 40-minute walk really helps to regenerate and create this new sense of clarity.”

Colorado nonprofit launches plug-in solar program ahead of new state law

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Marie DeFreitas, Contributor | November 10, 2026

A new Colorado nonprofit is looking to bring distributed solar to households that have traditionally been left out of the residential solar market.

Solar Access Colorado, which bills itself as the state’s first organization dedicated to plug-in and balcony solar systems, has launched with pre-orders for small-scale solar kits ahead of the implementation of Colorado House Bill 26-1007, also known as the Improve Customer Use of Distributed Resources bill. The legislation establishes consumer protections for qualifying portable-scale solar devices, including provisions intended to make adoption easier for renters and homeowners living in communities governed by homeowners associations.

The Denver-based organization is positioning plug-in solar as an alternative for residents who cannot install traditional rooftop systems because they rent, live in multifamily buildings or lack suitable roof space.

“Colorado has taken an important step toward expanding access to distributed solar energy,” said Adam Harris, founder of Solar Access Colorado. “We’re launching Solar Access Colorado to help renters, apartment residents and homeowners participate in the clean energy transition with practical, accessible solar solutions.”

HB26-1007 creates a framework for personal, portable-scale solar generation devices. Beginning Jan. 1, 2027, the law will protect qualifying systems from unreasonable restrictions on their installation, use and operation.

The legislation also addresses HOA restrictions by treating qualifying portable-scale solar devices as energy efficiency measures under Colorado’s common-interest-community laws. This could help expand access to solar for residents who previously faced restrictions on making property modifications.

However, the law does not eliminate all installation requirements. Reasonable safety-related restrictions remain permissible, including provisions addressing secure mounting, fire prevention, electrical overload and potential property damage.

Solar Access Colorado plans to help consumers understand the new regulatory landscape and navigate product requirements and installation considerations as the law takes effect. Its initial product offering is designed for balconies, patios, fences and other outdoor spaces, giving residents additional options for generating electricity without committing to a conventional rooftop installation.

Beyond product sales, Solar Access Colorado plans to work with municipalities, housing organizations, property managers and community groups to broaden access to plug-in solar. The nonprofit also intends to prioritize education and outreach to low-income communities and multifamily households.

As states explore policies supporting smaller, customer-sited generation systems, plug-in solar could become another entry point for residential clean energy adoption. Colorado’s upcoming legislation is providing a framework for that market to develop.

Meta’s Faiss 1.16.0 Adds E-Means for Faster Large-Scale Clustering

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta’s Faiss maintainers released version 1.16.0 on November 10, 2026, packaging FAIR at Meta’s e-means clustering algorithm with CPU, GPU and distributed implementations. The researchers report beating Lloyd k-means’ loss after 25 epochs in 1.4 to 2.6 epochs, with 10–45× GPU wall-clock speedups against the compared implementations.

The software release follows the research preprint’s arXiv submission on November 6, 2026, rather than marking the algorithm’s first public disclosure. The work comes from researchers at FAIR at Meta and École Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées.

Version 1.16.0 adds reference e-means implementations, distributed and centroid-sharded training, Triton and CuTe assignment backends, reduced-precision assignment and benchmarks for reproducing the research results. It also includes DINO demonstrations that keep data resident or stream it from SSDs.

K-means seeks centroids that minimize the mean squared distance between vectors and their nearest centroid, supporting retrieval, quantization and data curation. Lloyd’s algorithm assigns every point to its nearest centroid and rebuilds each cluster’s sum and count from the full dataset. e-means instead updates exponentially decayed sums and counts from mini-batches, so older assignments gradually lose influence; each centroid remains the ratio of its running vector sum to its running count. Using the entire dataset as one batch with a step of 1 reproduces one Lloyd iteration.

Two fitted scaling laws derive batch size and initial step size from dataset size, cluster count and epoch budget, removing the separate hyperparameter sweep required by earlier mini-batch approaches. A cosine schedule reduces the step to one-tenth of its starting value. The released class defaults to five training passes and accepts fractional passes.

The authors fitted the laws using 75,000 optimization runs on DEEP configurations. They report median held-out-dataset regret, meaning the excess error relative to the best grid setting, of 0.07% for the step law and 0.19% for the default batch law. The recipe also revives underused clusters by splitting a donor cluster, then finishes with each cluster’s exact mean over last-epoch assignments. A separate slow-moving-average finalization instead targets out-of-sample error.

The full paper describes validation across embedding and synthetic datasets, with its principal GPU comparisons on H100 80GB GPUs and CPU experiments on a 64-core Xeon Platinum 8462Y+. Across 11 VIBE embedding tests, the authors report that e-means required 1.6 to 2.6 epochs to reach Lloyd’s 25-epoch error, with speedups of 8.0× to 22.8× against Flash-KMeans on one H100 and 9.0× to 15.0× against Faiss on 64 CPU cores, using 20 seeds per test.

At the largest scale, the authors report partitioning DINO10B, containing 10 billion 1,024-dimensional DINO patch embeddings, into approximately 16.7 million clusters in 2.5 epochs with exact assignment, reaching slightly lower test error than the 25-epoch Lloyd baseline. That run used 779 GPU-hours across 32 machines. A separate run using approximate nearest-centroid search through an inverted-file (IVF) index crossed the baseline after three epochs using 23.2 GPU-hours on one eight-GPU machine. The IVF approach searches selected centroid buckets rather than comparing every point with every centroid.

The paper reports that IVF assignment is 34× cheaper than exact assignment at the ten-billion-vector scale but brings no gain at 100 million vectors. That difference shows why the epoch count alone does not determine infrastructure requirements: the implementation and assignment method decide how much hardware a run needs.

The official user guide provides NumpyEmeans for NumPy data and Emeans for PyTorch tensors. Faiss alone supports CPU execution; GPU execution uses CUDA-enabled PyTorch, with CuTe kernels available on H100 and H200 and Triton on other supported recent NVIDIA GPUs. Only nearest-centroid assignment uses reduced precision on GPU, while centroids, running sums and returned distances remain FP32. Replicated- and sharded-centroid classes support multi-GPU training; the paper notes that FP32 sums for 16 million centroids occupy 64 GiB, motivating sharding at that scale.

The scaling laws were tuned for relatively high-dimensional data and are not optimal for very low-dimensional or tiny datasets. The released defaults consequently choose the quality batch law at 16 dimensions or fewer and disable revival at two dimensions or fewer. The guide also cautions that returned training labels come from the last pass and are not necessarily assignments to the final centroids, and that the GPU path does not check inputs for NaN or infinite values.

For AI pipelines that cluster large embedding collections, the release makes the trade-off concrete: fewer training passes are available in a maintained library, while the largest reductions in GPU consumption depend on scale, hardware and whether approximate assignment fits the workload.

Earlier this year, Ripple introduced a multi-phase roadmap to prepare the XRP Ledger (XRPL) for a post-quantum future, with a target for full readiness by 2028. As described in Google’s quantum computing research paper, there’s a few key risks the crypto sphere has to handle. The most quoted one is the exposable public key risks when users transact within the blockchain, something that quantum-computing based hackers could take serious advantage of.

There’s a more subtle risk too, one elliptic curve researchers call “harvest now, decrypt later.” Simply put, bad actors can collect publicly visible cryptographic data from the blockchain today and hold onto it. This raises the chances of the account eventually being broken into.

Pixel Tag, Google’s $30 Android-ready answer to Apple’s AirTag, is now available

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google’s answer to Apple’s AirTag, the Google Pixel Tag, goes on sale starting Tuesday, allowing Android users to track items like their keys, wallet, or bag using their smartphone’s built-in Find Hub.

First announced in August, the $29.99 device takes cues from the AirTag and other lost-item finders by offering connectivity via Bluetooth and ultra-wideband for precision finding. This lets Android device owners, including Pixel Watch wearers, locate their missing items using a combination of visual distance and directional guidance.

Plus, the tag has a built-in speaker to play a chime that helps you locate your item if nearby. (Or, if you’ve lost your phone, you can press the button on the Pixel Tag twice to ring it, too.) You can choose between three different levels of loudness in the settings within the Find Hub, with the maximum volume around 80 decibels.

Though the Pixel Tag serves the same purpose as an AirTag, Google has opted for a different design. Instead of a small, round device, like Apple’s, the Pixel Tag is slimmer and more elongated.

Spec-by-spec, the Pixel Tag is 1.1-inches wide, compared to the AirTag’s 1.26 inches. As the Pixel Tag is oval-shaped, it’s 1.8-inches high from top to bottom. Thickness-wise, the Pixel Tag clocks in at a depth of 0.2 inches, compared with the AirTag’s 0.31 inches (which Apple calls its height). The slimness of the device might make it more appealing for some, particularly if they’re adding the tag to a small item, like a keychain.

The device weighs just 0.4 oz, with the battery included.

Unlike Apple, Google opted for a physical button you can press on the tag, which makes it more obvious how it’s meant to work.

However, the Pixel Tag doesn’t include NFC, which Apple’s AirTag uses to aid the people who find lost items, as they can tap the side of the tag to launch a webpage that displays the owner’s contact information.

Instead, for lost items that are not nearby, the Pixel Tag relies on Google’s crowdsourced network of over a billion Android devices globally. The network, similar to competitive offerings from Apple and Tile, taps into the connectivity provided by other Android devices, which can identify and share the location of the lost item back to its owner. This location information is displayed on a map inside the Find Hub app.

While the person who found the item can’t tap it, as with NFC-enabled devices, Pixel Tags that are put into “Lost” mode will be able to share the owner’s email, number, and a short message so the person who finds the tag can contact you. When the tag comes back into range of the user’s phone, the item is automatically marked as found.

The Pixel Tag’s location can also be shared with up to 10 people in the Find Hub app, which could be useful for shared items like the family car keys, which multiple people might rely on.

Like AirTag, the Pixel Tag is also powered by a user-replaceable coin cell battery (the CR2032), which makes the device reusable for the long term. (Google claims the device has up to one year of battery life to start.)

Meanwhile, the device itself is made from stainless steel and features IP67 water and dust resistance (maximum depth of 1 meter up to 30 minutes). It pairs with devices running Android 9 or newer via Fast Pair technology.

The Pixel Tag comes in two shades: silver and a pale green, and can be bought individually or in a four-pack for $99.99.

Alongside the launch, the company Bellroy is debuting its own keychain cases in a variety of colors and styles, which will also be sold on the Google Store.

New U.S. vehicle prices hit a record high of $51,109, Kelley Blue Book said

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The October average rose 1.3% from September and 2.1% from a year earlier, below the 3.6% typical annual gain for the month

Kelley Blue Book, a Cox Automotive brand, reported a record average transaction price of $51,109 for a new U.S. vehicle in October. The figure rose $649, or 1.3%, from a revised September average of $50,460 and was 2.1% above October 2025.

The year-over-year gain fell below the 3.6% longer-run average for October, according to Kelley Blue Book. The average manufacturer's suggested retail price rose to $52,933, up 1.4% from September and 2.4% from a year earlier.

Kelley Blue Book said price inflation and strong sales of midsize and full-size SUVs and full-size pickup trucks helped push the average higher. Incentives averaged $3,490, or 6.8% of the average transaction price, up from 6.7% in September and 6.5% a year earlier. Kelley Blue Book said that level remains below long-term averages.

Midsize SUVs became the top-selling segment in October, moving ahead of compact SUVs. The midsize SUV average transaction price was $50,570, up 1.9% from a year earlier. The full-size pickup average was $67,912, up 1.4%. The five highest-volume segments made up 64.4% of sales.

"The average is telling us as much about product mix as it is about affordability," Erin Keating, an executive analyst at Cox Automotive, said. She said three of the five highest-volume segments sold well below the $51,109 average, and vehicles priced from about $28,000 to $38,000 gained 9.6% in volume from a year earlier.

Electric vehicle prices also rose. The average transaction price for a new EV was $56,220, up 1% from September and down 2.7% from a year earlier. EV incentives averaged 11.3% of the price, up from 11.2% in September and 10.3% a year earlier.

The previous record was set in December 2025, according to NPR. Tariffs and buyer demand for larger, higher-priced vehicles have contributed to rising prices.

NASA last accepted applications in 2024. Over 8,000 people applied, but only 10 astronaut candidates were chosen. Since the 1960s, 370 people have been selected to train as astronaut candidates, and just 32 are currently in NASA’s astronaut corps.

If you want to journey into space with NASA, you have to be a US citizen, ideally with a master’s degree in a STEM field.

In addition to completing an application package that includes a long-form resume and five references, you will also have to pass NASA’s long-duration astronaut flight physical and show strong skills in communication, teamwork and leadership.

If you don’t have a master’s degree, you can meet that requirement with at least two years of work toward a doctoral program in a STEM field, completion of a medical degree or completion or enrollment in a nationally recognized Test Pilot School program.

The Lighthouse for the Blind is adding braille displays to its computer lab. Friends of the Issaquah Salmon Hatchery is taking its lessons to low-income schools. And Lake Washington High School’s band boosters are replacing marching capes that date to the MS-DOS era.

Those are among 25 King County nonprofits that received $20,000 apiece from Microsoft in grants announced this week. It’s the first round of the company’s Community-First Grants, a $1 million program launched in September.

Microsoft said it received 813 applications.

“Boy, I think we hit a nerve,” said Jane Broom, Microsoft’s senior director of Community Affairs, during an event Monday recognizing the nonprofits on the company’s Redmond campus, featuring Mariners catcher Cal Raleigh and the team’s mascots.

The grants are meant for immediate needs, such as replacing a broken-down van or a failed freezer, Broom explained. Nonprofits that already received or were invited to receive Microsoft corporate grants in 2026 weren’t eligible.

Funding and the tax debate: Nonprofits are dealing with rising costs and tighter budgets. As a group, their largest funding source is the state government, which is in the middle of an ongoing debate with members of the business community over taxes and economic competitiveness amid a budget crunch.

Microsoft is among the companies asserting that current and proposed taxes are hurting the business climate. It’s also the second-largest funder of the state’s nonprofits, said Brad Smith, the company’s president and vice chair.

“In my view, a healthy nonprofit community actually has aspects of support from more than one source,” Smith said in an interview after the event, noting that state grants are often at risk when a recession forces budget cuts.

Where the money goes: Demand is climbing for many of the grant recipients. At Community Lunch on Capitol Hill, which serves free hot meals, it’s up 40% this year, and the diaper bank at Byrd Barr Place has grown from 720 families in 2021 to 1,600, Microsoft said at the event.

“If they can’t bring the school to the hatchery, we will go out to the school,” said Clemens, a former Scientific American art director, who was creative director of the Modernist Cuisine cookbook project before taking over the nonprofit in 2023.

One bonus for the group, which goes by the clever acronym of FISH, was the appearance of the Mariners’ salmon mascots, which joined the group onstage for a photo op when Clemens and his colleagues accepted its grant.

The Lighthouse for the Blind, a Seattle-based nonprofit that employs blind people in manufacturing, retail and other jobs, will use its grant to upgrade its computer training lab with Windows 11 PCs, braille displays and wireless keyboards, said Antonio Rozier, the nonprofit’s IT director.

The goal is to keep employees current on screen readers and other tools so they can work alongside sighted colleagues, Rozier said. The Lighthouse also has computer labs at its three manufacturing plants, and employees can use them for personal tasks, since some don’t have that equipment at home.

Rozier called the upgraded lab “a game changer for the future.”

The Lake Washington High School Band and Jazz Boosters will buy 150 uniforms for the school’s marching band, which returned in 2025 after a 40-year absence and has been performing in capes from 1981.

Microsoft’s company culture: Most of the company’s local giving is directed by its employees. Microsoft matches their donations and adds $25 for every hour they volunteer, up to a combined $15,000 a year per person, Smith said.

That program accounts for most of the $1.9 billion that Microsoft and its employees have given to about 13,000 Washington nonprofits over the past 30 years, he said.

“We let our people decide where the money goes,” Smith said.

Microsoft’s employee base is going through a period of major change. After layoffs in July and September, the company offered voluntary buyouts last month to about 60,000 people, roughly a quarter of its global workforce. Employees have described mounting pressure to use AI, stagnant pay and a growing sense of job insecurity, The New York Times reported.

Asked about the buyouts, Smith said it’s a good time for Microsoft to keep investing in the employee experience, and he described the giving program as part of that.

Silicon Valley companies may offer more free food as a perk, he said, but Microsoft matches employee donations, giving workers who often move to the region from elsewhere a way to get involved in the community.

This year’s Microsoft Give campaign in October, the company’s biggest month for employee donations, was again a success, Smith said, showing that “employee participation and employee giving is alive and well.”

Microsoft plans to open a second round of applications for the Community-First Grants in the spring of 2027.

This actually technically represents the reopening of a lounge, as the global lounge operator had such a facility until 2019. At that point it closed due to an expansion project at the airport. Once open, it will be the only arrivals lounge at the airport. Cathay Pacific used to have an arrivals lounge at its hub, but it closed in 2018, and the airline has no plans to reopen such a facility.

The exact entry requirements of the Plaza Premium arrivals lounge in Hong Kong remain to be seen, but like most Plaza Premium lounges, the expectation is that entry will be possible through select credit cards and Priority Pass, and access can also be purchased directly.

Generally we think of an arrivals lounge in terms of its functional purpose, which is to shower, have breakfast, and get some work done, before heading into the city. Many arrivals lounges are only open in the morning, since that’s when there’s the most value. In the coming weeks, Hong Kong Airport will once again have an arrivals lounge, thanks to the opening of a facility by Plaza Premium. Arrivals lounges are usually pretty basic, and are about showers and breakfast. AI Coding Agents Leak Thousands of Secrets to GitHub

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A command in Claude Code inadvertently saves files with credentials and other sensitive data to public Git repositories, creating security gaps for organizations.

AI coding assistants may inadvertently be leaking thousands of secrets to GitHub public repositories, making what seem like routine interactions between developers and these tools a gaping enterprise security risk.

Research from security firm Apiiro found 2,539 credentials across nearly 2,000 public GitHub repositories, with secrets such as database passwords, cloud keys, API tokens, and SSH root logins among the exposed data, according to a recent report published online. Apiiro tracked some of the leaked data to Claude Code, which was, unbeknownst to users, copying secrets to a hidden file and committing them with the rest of the developer's code.

Among specific secrets leaked were "a database vendor's own admin-level cloud key, a production Azure Postgres password, a Square production token, and the owner password of a medical-records database," Apiiro researchers Shmulik Cohen and Matan Giladi wrote in the post.

Related:AI Gave Your Incident Archive a Second Job

Most of the secrets belong to individual developers across 63 organizations, "from student groups to startups, software agencies, and established vendors," they wrote.

The issue stems from how coding assistants — Claude Code, in a specific case that the researchers examined — carry out certain commands. "Ask Claude Code to check a database or call an API, and the command it proposes carries the secret from your config file," the researchers explained.

The tool will ask the user before running the command and, if the choice is "Yes, and don't ask again," then "it saves that exact command, secret included, to .claude/settings.local.json inside the project folder," according to the report.

"Nothing in the repo marks that file as private, so it gets committed with the rest of the code," the researchers explained.

The fact that a routine development task creates a security exposure is dangerous for organizations, especially because these AI coding tools are supposed to be helpful, not harmful, notes Ryan McCurdy, vice president of marketing for database security firm Liquibase.

“A developer approves a command to save time, the AI agent retains credentials as part of that approval, and those credentials can eventually end up in a public repository," he observes via email. "Nobody intended to expose them, but the workflow made it possible."

In the case of Claude Code, Apiiro examined 104,185 publicly accessible copies of .claude/settings.local.json on GitHub, finding that 1,122 files contained real secrets. The researchers identified 1,379 distinct secrets in those files alone, including 636 provider-specific keys and 743 passwords or tokens associated with public hosts.

Related:What We Missed: Knicks Phishing Test Goes Out of Bounds

Claude Code is not being overtly careless; it does try to protect files when it creates its .claude/settings.local.json file inside a Git repository, the researchers noted. The tool does this by adding the file to the developer's global Git ignore list, which tells Git to ignore the file on that particular developer's machine. This prevents it from being included in routine commits.

However, the protection is not built into the development project itself, and the "ignore" rule does not travel with the repository when it is cloned onto another machine, the researchers noted. "The rule never goes into the repo's own .gitignore, so nothing travels with the project to protect it," they wrote.

Moreover, Claude Code itself was sometimes responsible for committing the exposed files. In 539 of the 1,105 repositories containing real secrets that the researchers investigated, a commit co-authored by Claude Code had modified the affected settings file.

The action that exposes these secrets while using Claude Code appears to be a "dead zone" of sorts for developers using AI coding assistants, so they likely have no idea that they've just exposed a secret, observes Seemant Sehgal, founder and CEO of AI penetration testing firm BreachLock.

Related:9 Cyber Awareness Tips That Actually Match Today's Maze of Threats

"A 'don't ask again' prompt is a decision to write a credential into a file, and many developers clicking it likely don't know that file exists," he says. "Once it lands in a repository, it sits in a place many secret scanners were never built to check, which appears to be why some of these leaks slipped through."

Apiiro contacted Anthropic about the issue before publishing the findings. After their publication, Boris Cherny, Anthropic's head of Claude Code, said in public comments that although Claude Code historically has not done secrets scrubbing, the company is looking into what it can do to prevent the secrets exposure described by Apiiro, according to Apiiro's report.

Those approaches may be to make sure auto mode catches the exposure, or to build in basic secrets scanning, among other ideas that Anthropic will prototype "to understand the tradeoffs" before shipping something with its next version of Claude Code, Cherny said.

Indeed, the leakage exposes how Claude Code's protection relies heavily on a developer-specific Git setting rather than a safeguard built into the project or the AI coding tool itself, exposing a gap that can turn an ordinary command approval into a persistent credential leak.

To avoid this, Apiiro recommends that developers rotate every credential a file has ever held, and open all fields and delete every rule containing a secret, even if a file was never committed to a project. Meanwhile, security teams should treat all AI agent folders as secret-bearing on laptops as well as in repos, and add them to pre-commit and secret scanning, including history.

"As AI agents become more involved in software development, organizations need to recognize that source code isn't the only thing they need to secure," McCurdy observes. "Agent configuration files, transcripts, and execution histories can contain sensitive information, and traditional security checks may not catch everything."
