How Each Music Listening Platform Is Treating AI Music (Updating)

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From Bandcamp's outright ban of AI music to Spotify's "AI Personas" approach, here's how all the music platforms are dealing with the flood of AI-generated content.

One of the most pressing questions in the music industry right now is how streaming services should be treating the deluge of AI-generated music on their platforms. Should AI music be demonetized, or even removed from platforms? Should it be labeled or flagged so that users know what they are listening to? Or, for those optimistic about AI’s creative possibilities, should it all just be left alone?

It’s hard to know the exact scale of AI music on streaming services today, but what is clear is that there’s a lot of it — and the amount of it is growing fast. Deezer reports the count of daily AI track uploads at over 50% at “peak level” in June 2026, and Apple Music told Billboard in April that “more than a third” of monthly uploads were 100% AI-generated. Qobuz also noted in January that 34% of new uploads each day are AI-generated. These metrics, however, may still represent an undercount of all the material that uses AI in some part of the creative process, given partially AI generated music is much more challenging to detect. Plus, it’s impossible to determine when AI is used to write lyrics or melodies that are then recorded by human musicians, unless a user offered up their prompting history.

When considering the challenges of actually implementing regulations on streaming services, the question of what to do about AI music becomes incredibly complex. There’s a risk of false positives with AI detection tools, but requiring self-disclosure from rights holders will likely lead to outright deception at worst, or innocent omission at best, given how prevalent AI elements can be at any point in the recording process.

Still, lines are being drawn, and until some sort of consensus emerges, those lines are different at different digital service providers. To learn more about what each music listening platform is doing about AI-generated music, Billboard has compiled an updating list of each services’ policies as they articulate them.

SoundCloud bans all AI-generated content on its platform that impersonates human artists or otherwise clones their voices, and it requires that AI-generated or manipulated content complies with the same platform requirements as any other music uploaded to SoundCloud. The platform itself has said it does not use any music content to train generative AI models and would not do so any time in the future without prior consent.

In an open letter in November 2026, SoundCloud CEO Eliah Seton wrote that the company will also be adding protections against users who use AI to spam the platform with slop, and it will beef up its defenses against streaming manipulation, a common byproduct of AI. Those who violate its policies will be blocked from the platform.

SoundCloud, however, has no issue with AI music on its platform in general. Unlike other listening platforms, SoundCloud has leaned into partnering with compliant, licensed AI music companies, like ElevenLabs and Moises, to provide a direct pipeline for AI users to share their work online.

Spotify is attempting to tackle the negative uses of AI music, while leaving the door open for users to enjoy the songs if they want to. Last year, the service announced policies against spam, impersonation and streaming fraud — all of which are not issues specific to AI, necessarily, but that can be exacerbated by the technology. In April 2026, Spotify provided a way for creators using AI to disclose exactly how they did it in the credits, including whether AI was used in just one guitar lick or in the entire production.

The service also announced verification badges for human creators and is rolling out a service called Artist Profile Protection which allows participants to essentially lock their artist pages from getting random uploads added to them, a persistent issue on Spotify and other streaming services.

The company’s boldest step yet came in August when the Swedish streamer announced the creation of a new label that will be applied to AI-generated artists and begin rolling out in mid-September. Music from artists bearing the label, called “AI Persona,” will not be included in personalized recommendations on the platform.

Apple Music is working towards visible labels for AI-generated music on the platform. The system, dubbed Made With AI, was announced in August and is set to roll out by the end of 2026. The platform noted that any song that is tagged as being “materially generated using AI” by content providers, like record labels and distributors, will receive these labels.

The move builds on Apple Music’s March announcement, requiring content providers to disclose and tag where AI is being used in the creative process, whether that’s in an upload’s artwork, recording, composition or music video. The system relies on disclosure, rather than an automated AI detection and flagging system, even though Apple Music head Oliver Schusser has told Billboard the company has a detector the team uses internally to monitor the rise of AI content.

YouTube requires that anyone uploading generative AI content, including music, discloses when AI has been used. Then, the platform adds a label to that content for viewer transparency. Beyond that, YouTube has not formed other policies around AI music specifically. (Google, YouTube’s parent company, has also been pursuing an AI music-making model of its own, Lyria 3, after acquiring ProducerAI earlier this year.)

Amazon Music has systems in place to prevent impersonation, deception and spam, all of which are commonly found in AI music uploads, but beyond that the company does not have any policies related to AI music. In a statement, Amazon said, “The broader music community is defining what labeling standards should look like, and we’re part of those conversations. As those discussions continue, we’ll continue to explore ways to help customers make informed choices about what they’re listening to.”

In June, Tidal, which had previously stayed silent on the issue of AI-generated music, came out with some of the strictest rules against the content type in the industry. This included the announcement that Tidal will not monetize AI-generated content and is making distributors responsible for identifying AI-generated music before uploading it. It will also try to identify AI content itself and will administer labels for AI music, which began in mid-July, so that users know the origins of what music they are hearing. This labeling system will start with fully AI generated music before being expanded to identify and label “substantially AI-generated” music as technology improves and “AI-detection methods become more reliable.”

“The responsibility to identify and tag AI-generated content should not rest with Tidal alone,” the company said in a press release. “We expect — and will begin to enforce — that content distributors identify AI-generated content before it reaches our platform.”

In January, Qobuz announced that it is “taking a human-first stand on AI-generated music,” and vowed that its editorial curation — which includes playlists, articles and reviews created by human experts — would remain “100% human,” and that it would work to ensure its “Discover” page “filter[s] out AI-generated tracks.” The service also said it would identify fraudulent streams and exclude them from the royalty pool and that it was “in the process of developing detection and monitoring systems to identify AI-generated content and fraudulent streaming patterns.”

Additionally, Qobuz now has contractual clauses prohibiting delivery of 100% AI-generated music, and it will integrate “dedicated DDEX fields for artists and labels to report AI-generated content.”

Deezer has been the most outspoken of the streaming services about AI music thus far. In January 2025, the French service began reporting the number of AI songs it was detecting on its service. Back then, it was reported that 10% of daily uploads were fully AI-generated, according to their in-house detector. Now, as of July, it was reported as more than 50% at “peak level in June 2026.”

The company has announced a number of policies designed to combat the negative uses of AI music. It uses an AI detector to label fully AI tracks and excludes that music from algorithmic or editorial recommendation. Plus, as of July, Deezer started removing any AI tracks that it suspects have been used in streaming fraud scams or that haven’t been streamed for six months or more. (Deezer has said previously that up to 85% of the streams on fully AI tracks were fraudulent).

In January, Bandcamp announced a total ban on AI-generated songs on its platform, whether that AI use is in all or a “substantial part” of the creative process. This was a first for online music platforms, but it came a few months after iHeartRadio announced a similar ban on “play[ing] AI music that features synthetic vocalists pretending to be human,” on its radio stations nationwide. Bandcamp also noted that it does not allow the use of any AI tools “to impersonate other artists or styles.”

I’m A Professional Cook &amp; My Secret To Perfectly Smooth Gravy Is This $4 Pantry Staple

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gravy is one of the most problematic dishes on the Thanksgiving table. Every year, home cooks struggle and sweat over gravy that’s too thin, too greasy, or—worst of all—lumpy. All while your relatives watch and the turkey gets cold.

Making the gravy the day before is a surefire way to avoid the last-minute panic. But on holidays when prepping ahead just isn’t in the cards, one simple hack can solve your gravy woes: a $4 pantry staple that will thicken up any gravy in mere minutes—without any lumps.

The secret to perfectly smooth gravy is Wondra flour. Known to chefs and home cooks alike for its bright blue cylindrical can, Wondra is wheat flour that has been pre-gelatinized, dried, and finely milled so it dissolves more easily and thickens sauces faster than regular flour. This means you can whisk it directly into any gravy (no matter the temperature!) without worry about clumping—and it will thicken that gravy almost immediately.

If you’re making gravy from scratch with no other thickener besides Wondra, I recommend starting with 1 tablespoon for every cup of stock and/or drippings. You can do this by heating the drippings or stock in a pot on the stove, then just whisking in the Wondra. It should thicken within a few minutes. If your drippings don’t have a ton of fat, I recommend whisking in a little butter too, to help with the flavor.

If you’re using Wondra to troubleshoot a gravy that’s too thin, I recommend whisking in a few teaspoons at a time. The Wondra should do its thickening magic within a minute or two after it goes in, and then you can decide whether you need more.

While Wondra flour is great for thickening gravies and sauces, it’s far from a one-trick pony. I’ve used it to coat white fish (such as tilapia) before pan-searing to give it a beautifully thin, crispy crust—and help prevent the fish from sticking to the pan. It’s also a common ingredient in batters for fried foods, since its lower protein content helps form a lighter, crispier crust—and it’s also a magical way to get a flakier pastry dough. So even if you only make gravy once a year, that blue canister won’t go to waste.

As decision model use skyrockets, is customization the next frontier?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In October, more than one in four requests on Vercel’s AI model router went to decision models, not LLMs — an enormous share of work for a kind of computerized intelligence that was invented only a month earlier.

Decision models are LLMs that don’t produce output tokens; instead, they’re tuned to use their general intelligence to quickly and cheaply decide between preset options, while registering its level of confidence in the choice. They’re useful in production environments, where fast decisions can be more important than the reasoning provided by a slower and more expensive frontier model.

TypeSafe became a $7.5 billion company after releasing its Jev family of models, which continues to dominate usage (at least where we can see metrics from services like OpenRouter, which handled more than 1.5 billion queries to TypeSafe’s models last week).

However, it’s easy to make a decision model, and dozens were released in the wake of Jev. One Amazon engineer made one in a weekend, and soon every hyperscaler had its own option. On Tuesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said that his company is rolling out its decision model, Microsoft-Decision-1.1, for wide use on Azure immediately.

The action is happening on both ends of the ecosystem: Sqwish, a U.K.-based AI startup behind a decision model called kettei fat, announced a $3 million pre-seed round today. Co-founders Ushnish Sengupta and Federica Freddi, who met as Cambridge grad students, had been developing small LLMs when Jev arrived on the scene, and they realized their architectures could be repurposed as a powerful decision model.

Data appears to be the key to a successful model in this category — TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida told TechCrunch that the hardest part of building Jev was finely calibrating its judgement using statistically-adjusted synthetic data. Sqiwsh, too, has been working with synthetic data, but its unique offering is built-in post-training tools that use a customer’s own data to improve the model’s judgement.

“What makes us not a commodity, I think, is the fact that we are offering our customers the same power…they can keep uploading their production data, and just the same way that we trained our models, their models can also keep getting better at the tasks that they care about,” CEO Ushnish Sengupta told TechCrunch.

Attempting to rank decision models is difficult; Sqwish’s “kettei fat” model has performed well on some benchmarks, but most builders of the technology say that the real proof is in the pudding: it’s generally simple for developers to sub in one model for another and see how it does in a specific context.

“The benchmark is more of an invitation to try the model out, and then the continual tuning is the reason why why they should stick to us,” Sengupta says.

Microsoft’s Decision 1.1 model is also the result of the company carefully balancing across cost, latency, intelligence and calibration. Achint Srivastava, the engineering VP who led the project, agreed that too much focus on benchmarks is a mistake.

“What we see is that ‘benchmaxed’ models fall flat under real enterprise usage very quickly,” Srivastava wrote in an email. “Our testing underscores just how challenging it is to deliver accuracy, reliability, speed and cost efficiency in a single model. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 and Jev are among the few that perform strongly across all four dimensions, even when evaluated on ‘held out’ or unseen benchmarks reflective of real use cases.”

For startups like Sqwish, competition with hyperscalers and frontier labs is an opportunity. Freddi predicts that future systems will see so-called System One intelligence, like decision models, working in concert with System Two intelligence, like frontier LLMs.

“Even in that future, System One is maybe a $10 billion, $50 billion [opportunity], and OpenAI is doing this IPO with you know $1 trillion, $2 trillion market value in mind,” she told TechCrunch. “This is all we care about; whereas for them, this is just a side project.”

Data center slowdown would raise risks for utility investments

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An estimated 191 GW of planned data center capacity faces new risk after the midterm elections, with exposure concentrated in Texas, Ohio and Georgia, according to a report from Orennia.

Utilities could be left holding the bill for billions in infrastructure spending committed in anticipation of artificial intelligence data centers that are delayed, downsized or canceled, industry observers and experts told Utility Dive.

Opposition to data center construction has crossed traditional party lines, while Democratic gains in the midterms are adding pressure on earlier-stage investments across key markets. Together, these dynamics raise the stakes for project approvals and utility cost recovery, they say.

“From a financing perspective, prolonged uncertainty will increase the cost of capital, reduce available leverage and make investors more hesitant to commit significant development dollars before power is secured,” said Andrejka Bernatova, managing partner of investment firm Dynamix Capital Partners, which invests in energy infrastructure.

Even in regions that theoretically have enough capacity to accommodate new large loads, projects may struggle to secure a credible path to power on the timelines they need.

“Interconnection, permitting, and financing all matter, but they are part of the same execution question of whether the team can get reliable power to the site and actually deliver,” Bernatova said. “A customer commitment or a large announced capacity number does not answer that by itself.”

Ultimately, Bernatova expects investors to favor markets that offer certainty around power availability and timelines, potentially eroding the competitive advantage of markets like Texas, which until recently attracted substantial data center investment.

Over the summer, Gov. Greg Abbott, R, announced a pause on new data center interconnections pending an audit of the queue. In the midterms, he lost his bid for reelection to Democrat Gina Hinojosa, who has pledged to maintain pressure on the industry after taking office next year.

Nearly all of the 46 municipal ballot measures tracked by Bloomberg that addressed data center development resulted in restrictions, moratoriums or bans.

That unfavorability extends to a substantial share of the development pipeline. An estimated 191 GW of planned data center capacity faces new risk, with exposure concentrated in Texas, Ohio and Georgia, where incoming Democratic governors campaigned on pausing or restricting construction, according to a report from energy analytics firm Orennia that was shared with Utility Dive.

Texas accounts for nearly half of the pre-construction in development in the U.S., so a statewide approval freeze would have an outsized impact on the national buildout, but the “impact of delayed or unrealized data center build-out on power prices varies widely across technologies and markets,” Orennia said.

If 20% of projected data center demand in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas region fails to materialize, solar and wind generators could see their average wholesale revenue fall 38% and 37%, respectively, according to the report. Implied heat rates, a measure of the economics of gas-fired generation relative to fuel costs, could decline 39%. In the same scenario, two-hour battery storage arbitrage spreads could fall below new-build break-even requirements.

In PJM, delaying half of the planned data center buildout by two years could reduce four-hour battery storage arbitrage spreads by 16% and lower solar and wind capture prices and implied heat rates by about 12% through 2030, the modeling suggests.

The election results are unlikely to produce sweeping federal AI legislation but could lead to congressional hearings and greater transparency requirements, according to D.C.-based research and advisory firm Capstone. Its analysts expect much of the policy debate to take place at state utility commissions and local siting boards.

Despite a proliferation of large-load tariffs over the past year, safeguards may still leave customers exposed in the event of a slowdown, some say.

“Existing tariffs and regulatory frameworks likely won’t fully protect customers because they were designed for a different century,” Louisa Eberle, principal at the Regulatory Assistance Project, warned in a written statement.

Eberle also called for reforms to utility business practices “to turn this data center surge into updated infrastructure, cleaner energy and a better deal for average customers.”

The push for lower electricity bills is prompting policymakers to look more closely at how utilities use existing grid infrastructure.

Richard Caperton, vice president of policy at SPAN, a maker of smart electrical panels, anticipates greater emphasis on data center projects that can be served without major new grid investments.

“I’m really thrilled to see governors like Abigail Spanberger [in Virginia] campaign on affordability and then embrace utilization as the solution,” he said.

In April, Gov. Spanberger signed legislation requiring Dominion Energy and Appalachian Power to report how effectively they use existing grid infrastructure. The State Corporation Commission can use those metrics to establish improvement targets and evaluate utility spending.

“Winning candidates who emphasized lowering electricity prices are already looking for ways to deliver on their promises quickly,” Caperton said. “I expect that will lead to more policies and programs that boost grid utilization being ratified and implemented in early 2027. How those policies affect data center development and utility investments will vary by state.”

Stephen Rose, CEO of infrastructure software provider Render Networks, said he expects to see a “pause and necessary shakeout” in data center development.

“Fully permitted projects with real capital will keep moving,” Rose said. “The speculative end takes the hit: developers land-banking without firm power, and the contractors who staffed up for projects that won't break ground.”

Rose cited AEP Ohio's large-load tariff, approved in July 2025, which requires new data centers above 25 MW to pay for at least 85% of requested capacity for up to 12 years, with exit fees and financial guarantees. The utility’s data-center pipeline subsequently fell by more than half from more than 30 GW to about 13.

Rose expressed concern, however, that a broad slowdown wouldn’t affect hyperscalers as much as it would the businesses and people that build data centers. He recalled delays in Louisiana's Broadband Equity, Access and Deployment program in 2025, where service providers bore the costs of equipment and crews while waiting for approvals.

“When timelines stalled, the hit didn't land on big tech balance sheets. It landed on local drilling, civil and fiber contractors,” Rose said. “A data center slowdown hits the same people first, and Texas risks the same fallout.”

Permitting hurdles could make data center developers more selective about where they build and under what terms.

Andy Roehr, managing director in the tax advisory firm Baker Tilly’s energy and infrastructure practice, sees developers as more likely to redirect capital than abandon projects – though that could lead to a winnowing of queues in some jurisdictions.

“A developer is typically evaluating three, four or five sites at any given moment, so when permitting, regulation or local opposition makes one location difficult to read, the project is far more likely to be delayed or redirected than canceled,” Roehr said.

Roehr said states and municipalities competing for projects that can no longer move forward elsewhere could find themselves in a “bidding war.”

"The most interesting thing I am seeing is that these moratoria are creating a developer’s market since they have the assets, tax revenues and jobs that communities want or need,” he continued.

Some developers may shift to “less optimal sites where projects can get done at an effective price point and time-to-market, but the regulatory and legislative environment is less hostile,” he said.

Changes in expected demand could also lead to repricing across the supply chain, from gas turbines and processor racks to construction costs.

Another uncertainty is the possibility of federal permitting reform, which could make it easier to build certain kinds of energy projects.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which advanced in the Senate last week, faces a difficult path to passage following the midterm elections, analysts say.

Democrats are split over the proposal, with some expressing concerns over changes to environmental protections in the bill and urging party leaders to wait until next year when the Democrats will control both the House and Senate to pursue their priorities.

Roehr said in his view, the BAAJA takes an “all of the above” approach and would be as meaningful to solar as to nuclear or natural gas. But what the industry needs most, he said, is stability.

“Developers, investors and host communities need certainty to keep committing capital and local resources to these projects,” Roehr said. “These are 20-, 40-, even 60-year investments, and if the environment keeps whipsawing then jobs don’t appear, tax payments don’t appear and capital moves on — there will be no winners.”

Physicists at the Large Hadron Collider have detected a rare, 1-in-10-billion particle interaction that opens a view into the 'zeptoverse,' the smallest distance scale ever measured.

Using the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the world's largest particle accelerator, scientists have gotten the first glimpse of the "zeptoverse" — the universe as it behaves within less than a hundred-trillionth of a wavelength of visible light, the smallest scale that researchers have explored to date.

The new findings may one day help to answer long-standing cosmic mysteries, such as why there is far more matter than antimatter seen in the universe, and what makes up the dark matter that constitutes about 85% of all matter in the universe, the researchers announced Nov. 3 at a seminar at CERN (the European Organization for Nuclear Research), the organization that operates the LHC. The study, available as a preprint, has not yet been peer reviewed.

At the LHC, located near Geneva at the border of France and Switzerland, researchers were investigating elusive particles called beauty quarks. These subatomic particles are cousins of the standard "up" and "down" quarks that make up the cores of all atoms in the universe.

When beauty quarks are created — in this case, when the LHC smashes atoms together at incredibly high speeds — they live for just one and a half trillionths of a second before they decay. There are a number of ways beauty quarks can break down; researchers hope that analyzing rare forms of these decays might reveal that they might not unfold as expected.

Such outcomes could then shed light on new kinds of physics found only at very tiny scales of zeptometers, or trillionths of a nanometer. (For comparison, a wavelength of visible light ranges from about 380 to 750 nanometers.)

An artist's visualization of a beauty particle decaying into two muons.

By exploring this zeptoverse, scientists hope to discover new particles that have escaped detection until now.

"We're confident there must be new particles to discover, and these could help explain mysteries like dark matter and the existence of matter in the universe," study co-author Harry Cliff, a particle physicist at the University of Cambridge, told Live Science.

"Why haven't we seen these particles yet? One answer, and perhaps the most likely answer, is these new particles only become manifest at very, very short-distance scales. So by probing into the zepto scale, we give ourselves the best chance possible of finding these new particles that could explain these mysteries."

We're now recording in a single year what took 10 years to record in the past.

Now, after hunting for more than four decades, physicists have found the first evidence of the rarest kind of beauty-quark decay seen yet. "Only one in 10 billion beauty particles decay this way," Cliff said.

In this form of decay, technically known as the B0 to mu+ mu− decay, a beauty quark collides with an oppositely charged beauty antiquark. They annihilate each other, generating a pair of smaller particles ‪—‬ a muon and an antimuon. Previous research suggested that this requires a complicated set of interactions, making this kind of decay extraordinarily rare.

An experiment at the LHC called LHCb was specifically designed to study beauty quarks. In 2024, a major upgrade to LHCb made it possible to distinguish different types of particles. Using machine learning techniques, the researchers suppressed huge amounts of background noise, which helped them detect the first evidence of the B0 to mu+ mu− decay.

"We're now recording in a single year what took 10 years to record in the past," Cliff said. "And so we have this massive sample of beauty decays, and that is ultimately what allowed us to see this decay for the first time."

The new results suggest that the way this rare decay happens matches the predictions of the Standard Model of particle physics, the current best description of how the basic building blocks of the universe behave. As such, the researchers have not yet discovered signs of new particles or new physics beyond the Standard Model.

"For me, I think the most exciting thing about this result is the fact that we are probing these unimaginably short distance scales," Cliff said. "Seeing the evidence for this decay begins a new area of research where we can start to make precision measurements that will give us the best chance of finding new physics in the future."

The researchers would now like to upgrade LHCb to fully exploit the improvements to the LHC's luminosity that are currently planned for the 2030s.

"However, there is some doubt over that now because the U.K. recently withdrew funding from the project," Cliff said. "So we are really trying to figure out if we'll be able to continue this type of research."

In an ideal world, "we'll collect many hundreds of these decays in the future, make precise measurements and probe deep into the zeptoverse," Cliff said.

And not that this has to be said, but a designer collaboration isn't automatically a better running shoe. The fit, cushioning, and performance still matter, especially if you're planning to do more than walk around in them. Running clothes have gotten a lot more stylish in recent years, so why shouldn't the shoes follow?

Exceeding Expectations Every Step of the Way

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices Starck Real Estate

Most important thing you learned this past year: The value of recognizing and drawing out the potential in others, sometimes before they even see it in themselves.

Who has most influenced your success? My father, Andy, who led the company for nearly 40 years. I consider it a privilege and a blessing to have been able to work and learn alongside him.

As a third-generation member of your family’s real estate firm, how do you honor the firm’s legacy while bringing your own perspective and ideas to the business?

We consider ourselves a family business made up of family businesses, so inherent in that is a sense of responsibility to the people and the families we serve. And so it’s a stewardship of what has been entrusted, not only to me, but to everyone in our company. The more we grow and find success, the more we’re able to invest in our agents and give in meaningful ways to charitable organizations and the communities we serve.

What is the most valuable lesson you’ve learned from the previous generations of your family who worked in the business?

The firm was founded by my grandparents on the values of service to others over ourselves and doing business with complete truth and integrity, which is part of the reputation we’ve worked hard to build over the last 66-plus years. While we firmly believe that good things happen when you treat others the way you would want to be treated, we take it above and beyond the Golden Rule to the Platinum Rule, which is treating others the way they want to be treated. Taking that one step further, our focus is on providing double platinum experiences—or exceeding expectations in order to treat others in a way they can’t even conceive.

What factor has the largest influence on the real estate industry today?

Distractions, particularly the technology in our pockets, which brings to mind the following Robin Sharma quote: “The enemy of mastery is not mediocrity. It is distractions. The addiction to distractions ruins many potentially awesome lives.” We’re privileged to work in this noble profession, which will always be a relationship-based business. Technology can be a wonderful tool when it’s used properly, but we believe that focusing on relationships and service is the best way to build a sustainable business that’s successful in any market.

How does your company make its agents’ jobs easier?

We operate on the belief that we only succeed as a company when our agents succeed, so we’re passionate about providing value and support so that their businesses and families can thrive. That means saving them time or making their jobs easier by taking things off their plate so that they can focus on doing the things only they can do. Another core thing we talk a lot about is the go-giver mindset, which is rooted in the idea that when you give and give and then give some more, good things happen.

As we approach the end of 2026 and head into a new year, what are you most looking forward to?

Our business is constantly changing, so it’s all about embracing that and looking forward to how we’re going to grow and get better while at the same time serving our agents better. It’s all about controlling what we can control. I’m optimistic about the future.
