Three Iran-backed suspects plotted attacks including RAF Fairford incident, US says

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The US removed all its bombers from the UK's RAF Fairford base last month after a major incident in September that police described as "suspicious"

The US Department of Justice (DOJ) has announced charges against three Iran-backed operatives that it alleges were involved in a string of plots in the US and Europe.

The DOJ said Iranian agents most recently "orchestrated a terrorist plot at an air force base in the United Kingdom", saying they were involved in the major incident in September near RAF Fairford in the UK, where US armed forces were stationed.

The three men have been accused by the DOJ of being involved in 18 attacks and attempted attacks, including in London, Amsterdam and Paris. Some of the incidents were said to be directed by the men over FaceTime.

The DOJ said they acted in the name of the group Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya (HAYI).

Iranian Mohsen Mohajeri, Iraqi national Mohammad Ali al-Hassani and dual Iranian-Iraqi citizen Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi were charged in an indictment unsealed on Tuesday, the DOJ said.

The DOJ said that most recently, al-Hassani "orchestrated a terrorist plot" at RAF Fairford - where on 27 September a major incident was declared after suspicious vans were reported near the base.

Seven men have been arrested in the UK over the incident at RAF Fairford.

Iran has strongly denied any involvement.

Al-Hassani and Mohajeri, who were allegedly both in Iran at the time of the incident, the indictment claims.

Meanwhile, al-Saadi was coordinating with the pair through third parties from a prison in New York, where he is awaiting trial on previous charges after being arrested in August, it adds.

Mohammad Ali al-Hassani (left) and Mohsen Mohajeri (right) allegedly planned attacks on London from Iraq

Mohajeri and al-Hassani are not in custody, two sources told the BBC's US partner CBS News.

"Of the 18 terrorist attacks in the spring of 2026 for which HAYI claimed responsibility in Europe, seven took place in the UK," the DOJ statement said. It added that the attacks involved "arsons, explosives, and in one instance, a near-fatal stabbing in London of two Jewish men, including a dual US-British citizen".

Two Jewish men were stabbed in Golders Green, north London, in April. A suspect is due to stand trial next year.

The indictment also details an arson attack on a Jewish charity in London on 17 April, which allegedly saw al-Hassani "directing" an individual over FaceTime.

On 17 April , a building previously used by Jewish Futures, an educational charity, was set alight in Hendon, north-west London.

The following day, the DOJ said al-Hassani participated in another video call with an individual carrying out an attack on a synagogue in London.

On 18 April, Kenton United Synagogue in Harrow was targeted in an arson attack. A 17-year-old boy pleaded guilty to the incident earlier this year.

Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi is currently in prison in New York, awaiting trial for previous charges

The indictment also claims the three were involved in attacks on the Bank of New York Mellon building in Amsterdam on 15 March and on the Bank of America building in Paris on 28 March.

It added that the trio tried to carry out attacks on "Jewish institutions in the US", including attempting to target a synagogue in Manhattan, New York in March or April.

"This eight-count indictment shows the determination of Iran to commit terrorist attacks in Europe and the US," FBI Director Kash Patel said.

He added that one of the three "allegedly sent coded messages to assist in future acts of violence" from detention.

Patel said the accused were involved in nearly 20 attempted attacks in support of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) and the Iran-backed Iraqi militia Kataib Hezbollah.

Armed police officers were posted outside the RAF Fairford base following the incident in September

A major incident was declared on 27 September near the UK's RAF Fairford base, in the Cotswolds in Gloucestershire, after three suspicious vans were seen near the airbase.

No explosive materials were found in the vans, but a quantity of petrol was seized.

The DOJ said that in August, al-Hassani "allegedly directed and paid associates" in the UK to "conduct preparatory steps and arrange logistics for the RAF Fairford terrorist plot".

On 22 August, al-Hassani paid one individual to "perform pre-operational surveillance of RAF Fairford's perimeter", the department said.

In September, al-Hassani paid several people to help him prepare for the plot, the DOJ also alleged.

According to its statement, this included "procuring vans and barrels of fuel, researching the purchase of chemical accelerants, including large quantities of potassium chloride, and purchasing metallic objects that could be used as explosive shrapnel".

The suspects then gave the vans an appearance of being used by a fuel delivery business and a swimming pool supply business, the DOJ stated.

"On 26 September, al-Hassani allegedly tasked individuals to drive three vans, some of which were later determined to contain barrels of fuel, to the RAF Fairford base."

The DOJ also said al-Hassani was "in frequent communication" with the van drivers and helped to direct them where to drive.

"At least two of the three vans, while en route to the perimeter of RAF Fairford, encountered a security roadblock and were not allowed to proceed on their intended route," it added.

It was in the very early hours of 27 September, that the vans were spotted by a local farmer who was heading home from a party, and called the police.

The Iranian embassy in London has said it "categorically rejects and strongly condemns" what it called "unfounded and malicious speculations" linking Iran to the incident.

Two British nationals appeared in court last month in connection with the alleged Iran-linked plot.

The US withdrew all its bombers from RAF Fairford after the incident, with President Donald Trump citing "some threats" to the base as the reason.

RAF Fairford has been used by the US Air Force (USAF) for decades.

In recent months, the US has flown B-1 and B-52 bombers from there to the Middle East for "defensive" strikes on Iran.

In July, Iran said "any base used for aggression against Iranian territory constitutes a legitimate target for our forces".

In his breakdown, Vance cited polling that identified four key items disillusioned voters wanted to see from Republicans: ending the war in Iran, focusing on the cost of living, putting America first and changing the U.S.-Israel relationship.

"Our voters showed up. And the above four reasons are why many of them voted for Democrats," Vance wrote.

Alex Stamos says AI has pushed cybersecurity into a dark age

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Alex Stamos, Cognition’s security chief, says the cost of defence is not falling as fast as the cost of attacks. He wants Google and Amazon to fork the Linux kernel and rewrite it in Rust.

Alex Stamos speaking at Fortune Brainstorm Tech in Half Moon Bay, California, in 2021.

Every company needs to attack its own systems with AI hacking tools within about 90 days, or hackers will get in first. That is the warning from Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition. He told Nilay Patel on The Verge’s Decoder podcast that open-weight AI models now give low-skilled attackers much of the skill of elite hackers.

Stamos also lectures at Stanford University’s Cyber Policy Center. He said open-weight models can now automate most of the kill chain. That is the series of steps an attacker takes, from finding a flaw to stealing data or money. Attack tools now find bugs and write exploits in real time, he said.

“We are entering a dark age. Things are going to get more bleak,” Stamos told Decoder.

He pointed to ARTEX, a Chinese open-source attack tool with an English translation on GitHub. Attackers pair it with a version of the open-weight GLM-5.3 model that has its safety limits removed. That setup can break into a large number of organisations, he said.

The attacks began with three South Korean banks, according to Stamos. CrowdStrike linked those breaches to AI tools in October. Attackers hit about 200 more companies in the week before the interview, he said. He has been helping some of them.

Attackers no longer need frontier models, whose makers keep logs that police can reach, he added. Stamos said he runs a cut-down GLM-5.3 on his own Mac Studio.

Some attacks still rely on people. North Korea’s hackers took Binance mostly through social engineering, Stamos said. Binance has said a fake job interview led to its $6.3bn hack. Break-ins at AI companies look like the work of what he called China’s “cottage access industry”. These small contractors sell stolen access to larger Chinese firms.

“The cost per token for attackers is going towards zero, and the cost of defense for defenders is staying quite high,” Stamos said.

A colleague at a large security company told him that one investigation with a frontier cyber model costs $900. Security teams will have to hand their first levels of monitoring to AI, he said. Cheap classifiers would come first and pass alerts up to larger models.

At Cognition, the company’s own security agent can switch off staff accounts, he said. It stops at a fixed number until a human approves more. He also discounts the technical views of anyone who talks about AI attack “swarms” by about 80%. The attacks he sees use one agent, maybe two.

Every company will need to attack itself within 90 days, Stamos said. It can use ARTEX, a similar AI red-teaming product, or a hired firm.

“If you do not, you will get breached,” Stamos said.

Such tests could cause downtime, he said, but they would not steal data or shut down blood bank refrigerators, as happened in France. A cyberattack earlier this month pushed French blood bank freezers to extreme temperatures.

Patching alone cannot keep up, Stamos argued, because each new frontier cyber model brings another wave of Linux kernel fixes. He called on Google and Amazon to fork the Linux kernel for server hardware and rewrite it in Rust, a memory-safe language, at a cost he put at $100m to $1bn. The PHP flaws in October were a near miss, he said, because they proved hard to exploit in the wild.

Stamos wants AI makers to carry liability when their models escape and attack people directly. But he warned that OpenAI and Anthropic already refuse some requests from defenders. The labs treat legitimate companies in Europe and Asia “much more poorly” than American ones, he said. He would be fine with slowing down frontier AI so cheaper defensive models could catch up.

He also said chatbots should not have personalities. On Instagram, he said, Meta should verify real photos straight from the camera rather than try to detect AI-made ones.

Stamos, who joined Cognition in September, said he plans to build a “dark age index” that tracks patch volumes. Microsoft’s monthly Patch Tuesday has grown from about 150 fixes to more than 900, according to Stamos.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/carelon-agrees-pay-10m-settle-ghost-network-coverage-denial-allegations-new-york" hreflang="en">Carelon agrees to pay $10M to settle ghost network, coverage denial allegations in New York</a>

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Carelon agrees to pay $10M to settle ghost network, coverage denial allegations in New York" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Carelon will pay more than $10 million across two settlements in New York over mental health networks and coverage denials.

The company, a division of Elevance Health, will pay $2.35 million in penalties, fees and costs as well as up to $8 million in restitution for members who paid out-of-pocket for out-of-network mental healthcare, to reimburse those whose claims were improperly denied or to overhaul its networks.

New York Attorney General Letitia James said in an announcement that the settlements resolve two investigations from her office that determined the company misled members by directing them to seek care from providers that were out-of-network—through so-called "ghost networks"—and improperly denied coverage for treatment for substance abuse disorder.

James noted that Carelon administers behavioral health benefits for multiple plans, including Anthem Blue Cross, EmblemHealth, Independent Health and the Empire Plan, which covers state and local government workers.

Across these insurers, Carelon manages behavioral health benefits for more than 4 million people in the state, James said.

The alleged behavior also goes against a 2015 agreement with the AG's office, where the company agreed that it would maintain accurate provider networks and "use approved criteria" when denying substance abuse treatments.

"When someone is ready to seek help for a mental health condition or addiction, every day matters," said James. “Carelon left New Yorkers calling provider after provider who could not see them and made it harder for people to get the treatment to which they were entitled."

"These settlements will help people get the care they need and put money back in New Yorkers’ pockets," she said.

Elevance Health said in a statement to Fierce Healthcare that it is committed to ensuring patients have access to the most up-to-date provider information.

"Carelon Behavioral Health is committed to helping people find and access high-quality mental health and substance use disorder care. Accurate, up-to-date provider information is an important part of that effort," the company said. "We continue to invest in tools and processes that support accurate provider information and work closely with providers and other partners to help connect consumers with the care they need."

Carelon was hit with a lawsuit in April over the alleged ghost networks and their impact on the Empire Plan in particular. In the case, plaintiff's attorneys conducted secret shopper calls to analyze Carelon's network, calling 300 providers. Just 17% of those contacted took insurance and were open to new patients.

These "ghost networks" have also drawn concern in other markets, such as for maternal care services in Medicaid and in Medicare Advantage. A study released last month found that commercial plans also routinely have inaccurate provider directories.

Measles is surging in the U.S. — and outbreaks can cost millions

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Measles outbreaks aren't just a public health threat. They can also be a financial burden on the U.S. — and the cost could only deepen as the once-eliminated disease spreads more widely.

The U.S. is expected to lose its official measles elimination status following a review by the Pan American Health Organization this week, after cases ratcheted to recent highs this year.

More than 5,100 measles cases have been reported nationwide in 2026 as of early November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. Eight deaths associated with the disease have been confirmed by states so far in 2026: six in Pennsylvania, one in New York and one in Wisconsin.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The tool also estimates that 2025 measles outbreaks in the U.S. — which involved 2,289 confirmed cases nationwide – carried an economic cost of more than $37 million.

The costs extend beyond treating people who become sick, accounting for the resources health departments spend investigating and containing outbreaks, as well as wages lost when people have to miss work. The tool uses more recent, state-specific cost and financing data than an initial study by the researchers, which relied on historical outbreak costs.

The estimates from the tool are not a definitive accounting of what the outbreaks actually cost. But it illustrates the hefty bill tied to the spread of a disease that can be prevented through vaccination, and the potential economic consequences of falling childhood immunization rates. CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases.

"We're starting to see a whole bunch of people who decided not to get vaccinated as kids or have never gotten exposed to measles because they benefited from herd immunity, who now make it into adulthood and risk potentially getting quite seriously ill and requiring very significant medical expenses, or dying at the hospital," Bryan Patenaude, a health economist at the International Vaccine Access Center at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, who helped develop the tool, said in an interview.

Measles outbreaks can trigger a costly public health response, even when many people who are exposed never become sick.

When a case is identified, public health officials must investigate the infection, identify and notify people who may have been exposed, coordinate with hospitals and schools, and sometimes organize vaccination campaigns to prevent further spread. Those efforts can divert staff and resources from other public health priorities.

Costs could differ heavily depending on the state.

Johns Hopkins' data includes national averages alongside state-specific inputs, including inpatient hospital costs and wages from government data or trusted health policy sources. In New York, for example, the average inpatient hospital stay is roughly $4,200 a day, while the minimum wage is $16 per hour.

The tool estimates that the 48 cases reported by New York in 2025 carried an estimated $1.1 million in costs, including nearly $749,000 in public health responses, $333,000 in productivity losses and roughly $24,500 in direct medical expenses.

Meanwhile, the 803 cases in Texas, the epicenter of the largest U.S. measles outbreak in 2025, carried estimated costs of nearly $13 million, according to the tool. That includes $9 million in public health costs, $4 million in productivity losses and around $288,000 in direct medical expenses.

The tool builds on a study published by Patenaude and other researchers in August, which examined the costs of measles outbreaks in the U.S. The researchers reviewed published studies and government reports to compile historical estimates of medical expenses and public health response costs across 18 states.

The paper estimated that outbreaks carried a historical average cost of nearly $59,000 per case, just from the public health response. The researchers also estimated how costs scale with outbreak size, separating fixed up-front response expenses from the additional costs associated with each new case.

"A lot of this is incurred up front, and that's because of the human labor that's involved in contact tracing and all of the different actors that sort of need to be mobilized to deal with the fact that there's an outbreak," Patenaude told CNBC. "It's a largely labor-driven cost."

That model estimated roughly $298,000 in fixed costs and about $16,300 for each additional case. But it relied on historical data, including a particularly large 2019 outbreak in New York City, where contact-tracing labor costs were high. The researchers also excluded productivity losses from that model because most of the historical studies they reviewed did not consistently account for them.

The economic burden of measles extends beyond the costs of treating infected patients and organizing a public health response.

People who are exposed to measles may have to quarantine, even if they never develop symptoms. Parents can also be forced to miss work to care for sick or exposed children, while workers who contract the disease may lose wages during their recovery.

A 2025 study by Joey Mattingly, a pharmacist and health economist at the University of Utah, used published epidemiological and cost data to model the potential economic burden of a hypothetical 1,500-case outbreak. His analysis included direct medical expenses, public health spending and lost productivity, and examined how the estimates changed under different assumptions, including hospitalization rates.

The study estimated that the hypothetical outbreak could cost about $90 million, including roughly $26 million in productivity losses.

Those indirect costs can add up quickly because measles is highly contagious and a single infected person can expose dozens of others. Those contacts may need to quarantine or miss work while health officials determine whether they have been infected, creating costs even when they never develop the disease.

Mattingly estimated that each person who is quarantined could amount to roughly $300 in lost productivity alone.

If a single measles case exposes 40 to 50 people who then need to quarantine, that could translate into an estimated $12,000 to $15,000 in lost productivity, even if none of those contacts become sick.

That ripple effect is one reason Mattingly argues that the economic costs of outbreaks can quickly outweigh the investment required to vaccinate people against measles.

"Even if you don't get the infection, the contact costs you," he said. "And so it's just showing that there's this multiplication or exponential growth on the economic side that happens."

The financial burden can also fall unevenly across communities. While the costs of responding to an outbreak may be shared among local, state and federal agencies, Mattingly said the impact is often felt directly by local health departments.

"It's not like your health department is sitting around waiting for measles outbreaks," he said. "They're working, doing all these other things, and imagine having to just drop what you're doing because there's been a huge outbreak in the community."

Patenaude said the findings could help state and local health departments better anticipate the resources needed to contain outbreaks — particularly as measles cases continue to rise.

"It's not super expensive to treat and deal with a single case of measles compared to a single case of cancer," he said. "But if you have these outbreaks popping up and you're having to constantly divert resources that could be spent elsewhere by state and local health departments on measles, then you're going to wind up spending a lot of money very quickly."
