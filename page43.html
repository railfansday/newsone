‘The Incomer’ Attracted Domhnall Gleeson and Moby and Got Its Creator on the BAFTA Breakthrough List. Now, He’s Working on a Plan B Film.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scottish writer-director Louis Paxton takes THR through his feature debut, how much it reflects real-life experiences and what’s next for him.

Scottish writer-director Louis Paxton’s debut feature film, the dark comedy-drama The Incomer, has star power – in the form of Domhnall Gleeson (Star Wars sequel trilogy), Gayle Rankin (House of the Dragon), John Hannah (The Mummy franchise) and others. It also has an oddball, quirky vibe. And it has the backing of Focus Features, Universal Pictures International and Moby.

What else can you ask for!? Well, on Wednesday, the up-and-coming creative was unveiled as being among the names featured on the 2026 U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough list, supported by Netflix. Related Stories Movies 'Ish' Has Resonated Widely Amid "Authoritarian Turn," Says Its BAFTA Breakthrough Writer-Director News BAFTA Breakthrough U.K.: Ella Bruccoleri, Sophie Stone, Izuka Hoyle Among 21 Rising Creatives

The Incomer is set on a remote Scottish island and follows siblings who have lived in peaceful isolation for decades, hunting seabirds, chatting to mythic creatures, and defending their isle from dreaded Incomers. Their world is upended with the arrival of Daniel (Gleeson), an awkward council worker, tasked with moving them to the mainland.

Paxton talked to THR about the star-studded offbeat movie, how Moby got involved, his hopes for the BAFTA Breakthrough experience and what else is next for him.

“I’m thrilled to be part of BAFTA Breakthrough, and I’m really excited to meet the cohort, particularly all the other creatives from all of these different mediums, including games,” the filmmaker tells THR. “It will also be great to have the chance just to sit with people who are more experienced than me and have been working for a while, just ask them about their career and how it works.”

The Incomer is infused with a feel and stories he knows. “My mother’s family is from the Northern Isles of Scotland. They’re from Orkney,” Paxton tells THR. “I grew up in Edinburgh, but my mother moved back there about 10 years ago. We grew up going to these islands, which are unique. They are a very beautiful, quite remote part of Scotland, but have a very specific identity and culture.”

For example? “I went to other islands where people genuinely survived by eating birds,” he shares. “And at some point, these people had to leave, and I was just fascinated by this. What a big decision that would be if that was where your family had lived their entire lives and your heritage was that. Even if it was going to make your life better, how hard would it be to leave that behind?”

Was his youth as surreal as some of the scenes in the film? “I would say about me and my sister that there are parts of the sibling relationship that made it into the film,” Paxton explains. “When we were kids, we really knew how to push each other’s buttons and annoy each other, and so things could escalate from zero to 100 really quickly. And we definitely had some terrible fights when we were young.”

The creative credits casting director Kharmel Cochrane with bringing together a dream ensemble. “We wanted to work with as many Scottish actors as we could, and if they weren’t Scottish, that they were very good at doing a Scottish accent,” he recalls. “And thankfully Domhnall does an exceptional Scottish accent.”

His key to attracting all this talent was the script. “I spent a long time working on the script,” Paxton highlights. “And it was really reassuring in this age of IP and big projects and really humbling that a complete nobody, who’d only made short films, could write a script and that actors would want to be part of it just on the strength of the script.”

So, how did Moby’s production company Little Walnut Productions end up backing The Incomer? “They had been funding films and making films that were largely activist films,” Paxton says. “And then they’d widened their remit to include films that embodied the values that they had as people. They read The Incomer and just really liked it.”

Did Daniel being vegan have to do with that? “It’s funny because people have asked if the veganism in the film came after. Did I add that after Moby came on board?” Paxton says with a laugh. “But it wasn’t. It was already a big part of the film, and I think maybe he appreciated that. But Moby and [creative partner] Lindsay [Hicks] liked the film generally. And they were the best partners to work with – just excellent and really supportive.”

Can fans of The Incomer expect more from Paxton ahead? “It’s all about film for me, film and TV,” he tells THR. “I’ve always wanted to be a filmmaker. I’ve signed with CAA in the U.S., and I’ve been having really exciting conversations about my next projects. I’m writing and directing two projects which are U.K.-set.”

And he can share this much: “One’s a horror comedy, and the other is more of a straight comedy. But I’m also attached to direct a film called The Waffle House Index, which is a Plan B project,” meaning from Brad Pitt’s production company, in which French company Mediawan owns a majority stake.“We’ve got Inde Navarrette as the lead, and Apple are the studio we’re making it with, and we’re really excited about that,” Paxton says. “That’ll be shooting next year. I think that’s most likely to be my next project. I didn’t write it; Andrew Nunnelly wrote the script. It was on the Black List and is a terrific script – really original, really different. It feels totally in line with the kind of projects or films that people are really responding to at the moment. I’m so excited to be a part of that.”

Google offers weak response to accusations it would hold Search hostage if schools restrict AI tools

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google LLC just faced a barrage of criticism after the company warned that if schools restricted their students from using its artificial intelligence tools, the same students wouldn’t be able to use Search.

The fallout came after the company announced a slew of new products for classrooms. Teachers and schools were told that the new AI tools would come with features that will allow for limitations or complete blocks of the features, but if teachers or schools should take that route, students would not be able to use Google Search. Some media described it as rather cunning behavior from the tech giant.

“After listening to schools, teachers, leading researchers, and policymakers, we are now updating our approach to controls for Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search,” Google said in a blog post. “Access to these products will be entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on.”

A New York Times article followed the announcement and explained that where schools have banned the use of AI or if they limit AI in the classroom, the students would not have access to Search. With Google’s suit of products being a mainstay in classrooms all over the U.S., the backlash was imminent.

“Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools,” New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani wrote on X. “No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children. Our public schools exist to serve students, not to grow the profit margins of Big Tech.”

Sen. Bernie Sanders from Vermont was equally chastising, writing on X that, “Google is threatening schools,” and using what amounts to, “extortion.” Lina Khan, former chair of the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, was similarly unimpressed, writing that Google was trying to, “coerce schools into adopting AI”.

Google was quick to respond. In a post on X, the company said its new policy is about giving schools more control over how students use AI. Gemini chatbot, Gemini Notebook, the company explained, will be turned off by default, but “schools are in control.”

“We are also updating Search for schools to make it better for learning,” Google added. “Specifically, AI Mode and AI Overviews will offer step-by-step guided learning through problems, so that students don’t get quick answers to schoolwork.” The company explained that it was aware of the dangers of students embracing the “AI shortcut” and effectively not engaging their brains.

In what seemed like a sleight of hand or exercise in obfuscation, Google seemed to ignore the basis of the criticism regarding Search not being available when AI functions were turned off. Given the company has found itself under the gun many times in recent years for its sometimes-underhand business tactics, and in light of studies showing overuse of technology can have a harmful impact on children, the response seemed tepid at best.

Novartis: 3 Key Takeaways for Long-Term Investors

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Operating margins are on track to hit 40% by 2029 as cost discipline accelerates alongside new drug launches.

On its third-quarter 2026 earnings call, held on Oct. 27, 2026, Novartis (NVS -1.40%) raised its full-year 2026 core operating income guidance after delivering 4% net sales growth. This performance matters for long-term investors because the pharmaceutical giant is demonstrating that expanding sales from recently launched drugs can successfully replace revenue from older medicines losing patent protection. Three key insights from management explain how the company is maintaining its growth trajectory.

Novartis raised its full-year 2026 core operating income guidance, which tracks non-GAAP (adjusted) operating profit before one-time items, to in-line to low-single-digit growth, up from a previously projected decline. This operational shift shows that rising sales of priority medicines are offsetting patent expirations for key legacy treatments.

Management upgraded peak sales projections for key pipeline drugs, including Rhapsido for skin conditions such as chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), where hives appear without clear triggers. Promising trial results for remibrutinib in relapsing multiple sclerosis (RMS), a central nervous system disorder, further reinforce the company's ability to replace aging therapies.

Selling, general, and administrative (SG&A) expenses, which cover marketing and operational overhead, fell 7% during the third quarter. Lower third-party spending and efficiency gains from technology are driving progress toward a target of 40% core operating margin by 2029.

Sales momentum across newly launched drugs is outpacing legacy revenue losses, and management is openly highlighting this replacement capability. The company benefits from commercial scale across international markets and a deep pipeline, while management tracks patent litigation outcomes and pricing discipline.

Capital allocation remains focused on steady dividend growth and targeted share repurchases, supported by free cash flow generation. The long-term thesis hinges on whether late-stage clinical readouts maintain this momentum over the coming years.

Viettel Group and Vietnam's Government Cipher Committee Unveil Security Chip Embedding Vietnamese Cryptography in Silicon

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

HANOI, Vietnam, Nov. 3, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Vietnam's Government Cipher Committee and Viettel Group have unveiled VGC v1.0, a security chip integrating a domestically developed cryptographic algorithm directly into silicon, alongside the country's first domestically designed secure semiconductor platform.

The project addresses concerns over potential data exposure associated with reliance on imported chips. By embedding cryptographic functions directly into hardware, Vietnam's Government Cipher Committee and Viettel Group aim to strengthen Vietnam's control over the design and verification of technologies used to protect sensitive information.

VGC v1.0 contains 300 million transistors in a 10 Ã 10 mm package and is fabricated using a 28-nanometre CMOS process. In performance tests, it performed encryption and decryption approximately 15 times faster than a software-only solution carrying out the same tasks. The first batch passed technical tests and received certification for socio-economic applications in the first quarter of 2026. Technical safeguards were also applied to protect the design before fabrication, reducing risks of information leakage and reverse engineering.

The Government Cipher Committee's M2 unit established security requirements, developed the cryptographic algorithm and defined evaluation criteria, while Viettel Semiconductor Center designed and implemented the chip. Based on the open RISC-V instruction set architecture, the underlying platform enables Vietnamese engineers to modify processor designs and reuse established verification processes for future chip generations.

VGC v1.0 has already seen initial deployment in secure USB storage devices for Party, government and defence agencies. Planned applications include supporting Vietnam's national digital signature certification system, which has more than two million digital certificates in circulation. Other potential uses span financial data protection, connected cameras and sensors, and critical infrastructure systems in electricity, water, oil and gas, and telecommunications. Products intended to protect state secrets would require further testing and certification under applicable standards.

"Researching, designing and developing secure semiconductor chips is a formidable challenge, requiring years of accumulated expertise, technological capabilities and highly skilled talent. Yet we believe that starting later does not mean accepting dependence on others. With the right strategy, focused resources and sustained commitment, we can steadily close the gap and move towards mastering technologies of strategic importance to our country," said Lieutenant General Tao Duc Thang, Chairman and CEO of Viettel Group.

At the launch, the Vietnam's Government Cipher Committee and Viettel Group signed an agreement covering plans for volume production, multicore security chips, specialist workforce development and testing on real devices. Viettel is also building a semiconductor fabrication plant in Hoa Lac as part of broader efforts to develop domestic capabilities across chip design, manufacturing and packaging.

Beyond the initial chip, the initiative establishes a foundation for future generations of security hardware, supporting Vietnam's long-term efforts to reduce technological dependence and strengthen control over critical digital technologies.

Democrat Roy Cooper wins a North Carolina Senate seat

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Former North Carolina Gov. Roy Cooper, the Democratic Senate nominee, speaks at a campaign event on Sunday in Concord, N.C. Allison Joyce/Getty Images hide caption

Midterms coverage: Live results | What to know | Stories around the countryStay connected: NPR Politics Podcast | NPR Politics newsletter | NPR app

Democrat Roy Cooper has won North Carolina's open U.S. Senate seat, according to a race call from the Associated Press, providing the party with a key Senate flip.

The former governor becomes the first Democrat elected to the Senate from North Carolina since 2008.

Cooper defeated Michael Whatley, a former Republican National Committee chair who President Trump picked to replace retiring GOP Sen. Thom Tillis.

Cooper and national Democratic Party groups significantly outspent Whatley and Republicans. A few weeks before the election, national GOP organizations pulled TV ads from North Carolina and redirected their money to other battlegrounds like Kansas.

Cooper, who served as governor from 2017 to 2024, focused his campaign on affordability issues. Each campaign stop he made across the state was promoted as the "Make Stuff Cost Less" tour.

Meanwhile, Whatley's campaign centered around crime and immigration issues. He criticized Cooper over a COVID-era legal settlement that released inmates from state prisons to address overcrowding during the pandemic. But polls indicated that the economy — not crime — was the top concern for North Carolina voters this year.

The race was Whatley's first run for elected office, and Cooper's campaign painted him as a lobbyist for Big Oil who would become a rubber stamp for Trump in the Senate.

Patrick Sebastian, a Republican strategist who did not work on the Whatley campaign, said his party's candidate should have made the economy a bigger focus.

"Really it's the economy is Issue A and then Issue B is probably government corruption, that people are following the most," Sebastian said. "So when we're not talking about those issues, it's going to be hard to connect with people that go to Walmart for their groceries and can only get 80% of what they used to buy because the prices have gone up."

The North Carolina Senate race was initially expected to be one of the most competitive — and most expensive — in the country, but Whatley never led in polling, and Cooper's lead widened over the course of the race.

Even as pro-Whatley TV ads largely disappeared in October, Trump and Vice President Vance came to North Carolina for multiple rallies in the final weeks of the campaign. Sebastian says those visits likely helped improve base turnout for GOP voters, but weren't enough to save Whatley's campaign.

Tillis announced in 2025 he wouldn't seek reelection, after Trump threatened him with a primary challenger for failing to support the president's tax cut and spending bill. Rather than campaign for Whatley, he's been promoting his new book criticizing fellow Republicans.

Should You Buy Alphabet Stock After Anthropic Raised Its Google Commitment to $121 Billion?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Claude maker just raised its minimum spending commitment to Google. Can it pay?

Anthropic, the artificial intelligence (AI) company behind Claude, updated its initial public offering (IPO) filing on Monday, Nov. 2, adding its third-quarter results. On Sept. 30, it had $95.6 billion in cash and cash equivalents and $36.3 billion in short-term investments -- around $132 billion combined, near its total at the end of June.

Against this, Anthropic has signed purchase commitments and leases worth about $895.4 billion, almost all for compute and infrastructure. The first filing, on Oct. 26, left that figure blank.

And one of the largest parts goes to Alphabet (GOOGL +0.37%)(GOOG +0.08%). Anthropic now says it committed to spend at least about $121.3 billion with Google between April 2026 and July 2033, no matter how much it uses. Last week's filing had that number at about $114.5 billion.

With Alphabet shares near $283 as I write, is this durable revenue for Google Cloud, or a bet on a customer that lost money from operations last quarter? I think it's mainly the former over the next couple of years.

Google Cloud's revenue backlog (contracted revenue not yet recognized) hit $560.8 billion on Sept. 30. Three months earlier, it stood at $513.9 billion. And a year before, Alphabet's total backlog, mainly Google Cloud, was just $157.7 billion, under an older count that left out contracts for a year or less. But growth has slowed lately, with around $47 billion added in the third quarter versus almost $52 billion in the second.

Anthropic's $121.3 billion minimum equals around 22% of that cloud backlog. Alphabet doesn't say how much of the number comes from Anthropic, and some of the minimum has probably been spent since April. But it's a big share tied to one customer.

Spread across a bit more than seven years, the minimum averages around $16.5 billion a year. Google Cloud's third-quarter revenue climbed 103% year over year to $30.7 billion, a rate of around $123 billion a year. This puts Anthropic's average minimum at about 13% of the cloud unit's current sales -- important, but not the whole business.

Anthropic also estimates it could spend another $125 billion to $150 billion on Google's custom AI chips, though some of that might go through third-party providers rather than Google.

For the next year, Anthropic looks able to pay. Around $69.8 billion of its commitments are due in the 12 months after Sept. 30. Its cash and investments cover it almost twice, not counting $14.8 billion of credit it hasn't tapped or any proceeds from its IPO.

The business is helping. Third-quarter revenue climbed around 47% from the second quarter to about $17.0 billion, more than 12 times its revenue for the year-ago quarter. Multiply that by four, and it's close to what comes due in the next 12 months.

Operating cash flow for the first nine months of 2026 was a positive $6.6 billion, versus negative $2.0 billion for the same period of 2025.

But growth cooled from the second quarter's 144% sequential jump. Anthropic had a third-quarter operating loss of $567 million, which included around $2.0 billion of non-cash charitable contributions. A year before, it lost $3.1 billion.

Still, compute and infrastructure costs rose back to 58% of revenue in the third quarter, against 48% in the second, but they were 148% of revenue a year ago.

Later on, the bills get far bigger. Around $447.5 billion of the commitments are expected before the end of 2030 -- about $105 billion a year, well over Anthropic's current revenue pace.

"We must grow our customer base and expand revenue rapidly in order to generate the cash flows necessary to satisfy these commitments," Anthropic wrote in the filing.

If Anthropic spends less than its minimum, it must pay Google the difference. Of course, that payment only helps Google if Anthropic has the cash. And Google has agreed to provide up to $20 billion of financing, in convertible notes or stock, subject to conditions, so part of what Anthropic pays Google might come from Google itself.

Alphabet doesn't need Anthropic to work out perfectly for its stock to make sense. Shares trade at around 19 times expected 2027 earnings, about 31% under their 52-week high. And Google Cloud earned $11.1 billion of operating income in the third quarter, around three times the $3.6 billion it made a year before.

Does the amended filing change my take on Alphabet? Not much. But I'd treat the next few years of the Google commitment as solid revenue and the later years as a bet on Anthropic's growth.

Overall, I think Alphabet stock is still worth a look at this price.

AI cloud startup Applied Compute raises $350M after reaching key revenue milestone

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Applied Compute Inc., a fast-growing provider of artificial intelligence infrastructure, has raised $350 million in funding at a $3.25 billion valuation.

The startup announced the deal on Monday. It stated that prominent angel investor Elad Gil led the round with participation from Nvidia Corp., Shopify Ventures, HubSpot Ventures and over a dozen others.

Applied Compute operates a cloud platform that developers use to train large language models and perform inference. Additionally, it has a team of machine learning researchers that can lend customers a hand with their projects. The group takes on tasks such as creating quality evaluations for newly developed models.

The software stack that AI researchers use to train LLMs usually comprises two main components. One creates multiple copies of the model, assigns a different task to each one and turns the results into training data. The other component uses that training data to refine the LLM’s weights. The two components usually run synchronously, which means that each one has to wait for the other to complete its work before launching.

Applied Compute’s platform uses a more efficient training approach called asynchronous RL. It enables a training stack’s data generation and weight adjustment components to run at the same time, which speeds up LLM development. Additionally, the platform supports a second optimization method called self-distillation. It removes the need for teacher models, specialized neural networks that researchers use to supervise LLM training runs.

A built-in AI assistant automates repetitive aspects of customers’ work. It can, for example, summarize what new skills an LLM learned during a training run. Uncovering that information usually requires researchers to review lengthy reasoning logs.

Applied Computing’s platform also lends itself to running inference workloads. An autoscaling engine automatically adjusts the amount of hardware allocated to a model based on user traffic. A so-called online RL feature uses the prompts that the LLM receives to periodically fine-tune its weights, which keeps it aligned with customer requirements.

Applied Computing offers its training and inference features alongside a model router called Shift-1. It enables coding agents to send each user request to the LLM that can fulfill it in the most cost-efficient manner. Furthermore, it can split complex tasks among multiple LLMs.

Applied Computing disclosed in its funding announcement that its annualized recurring revenue has topped $100 million. The company, which launched less than 18 months ago, counts Microsoft Corp., DoorDash Inc. and other major tech firms as customers. It will use the newly raised capital to grow its market presence and buy more infrastructure.

“Training, inference, and routing are the first pieces of a much larger stack,” Applied Compute founders Rhythm Garg, Yash Patil and Linden Li wrote in a blog post. “We’re building the AI infrastructure every company will need to fully own their intelligence, from the models themselves to the systems that run, monitor, and secure them.”
