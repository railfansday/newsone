ABA President Barbara Howard says association will continue to prioritize the defense of democracy

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

It’s been a busy few months for Barbara Howard.

As the ABA’s president, Howard has worked to protect voting rights and urged her fellow members to do the same. She vowed to fight assaults on civil rights, which include critical threats facing unaccompanied children and other immigrants. She also spent three weeks in Europe, where she talked with lawyers in London and Copenhagen about the association’s efforts to defend the rule of law.

Howard, principal of Barbara J. Howard Co. in Cincinnati, is serving a one-year term that ends in August. She has a long history of leadership in the association, which includes being a member of the House of Delegates since 1986. She is also a past chair of the House.

In a conversation with the ABA Journal on Monday, Howard talked more about her first tasks as ABA president and what she plans to focus on in the months ahead. Our interview has been edited for length and clarity.

It’s been a few months since you became the ABA’s president, and about a week since the midterm elections. You’ve said that you want ABA members to help protect the right to vote. Why is that important?

I’ve put out two statements, one in September and one in October, and in both of them, I pointed to the role that we as citizens play in our democracy. As many before us have said, with democracy, it’s not one and done. It needs to be actively pursued and defended every day. It’s not the kind of thing where you can just sit back on your laurels and say, “Well, we’ve got our democracy.” And we all realized that. I think that’s what we saw as a country.

Voting is one of the most important responsibilities of an American citizen. It’s how we give our citizens the opportunity to hold our lawmakers and our officials accountable, and it’s how we have our voices heard. So for me, the focus was very much on getting out messages and encouraging lawyers around the country to talk with their fellow citizens, to engage with them, to assure them that our voting systems are very secure and that they should exercise the right to vote.

How have you been involved in those efforts?

Well, I spent Election Day as a poll worker. This was my fifth year. I worked the same polling place as I did last year. So that means on Monday night spending an hour, two hours setting up all the equipment, getting everything ready. Then it was about a 15-hour day, from roughly 5:15 in the morning until 8:15 at night. And we had probably about a 25% increase in the number of people who voted from the year before.

You’ve also expressed a need for the ABA to continue its fight against attacks on civil rights. What has that work looked like so far?

So far this year I’ve really been focused on the elections because we’ve had to be. As we now move past the elections, we need to be sure all the members of Congress are able to take their seats in January.

Another issue right now is immigration. We have seen some very negative developments with respect to the representation of especially unaccompanied minor children, but immigrants in general. ProBAR has had its funds pulled, and the entity with whom it contracts, Acacia, has had its grant terminated. There has been some money generated to allow ProBAR to set up a hotline for unaccompanied minor children to call in to get direction in terms of how they can get legal representation, which is required by Congress. That’s called the DORY hotline. Shelters have refused to give the phone number to minors who have asked for it. That’s a matter that is going to need some immediate attention.

There is also the overall issue with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services saying that rather than award a new grant to Acacia or someone else, it is going to internalize its representation so that the government is both representing the minor children and making the decisions on who stays and goes. That is an apparent, if not actual, conflict of interest. There’s a letter going out to Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. urging him to reassess that whole process. We will continue to monitor that very, very closely.

Turning to the ABA itself, many of its leaders have focused on how to make the association more efficient and serve its members better. What changes do you hope to see moving forward?

The Board of Governors has taken on what we’re calling “the renovation project.” It has become apparent as I’ve sat through several budget cycles and in talking to incoming section, division and forum chairs and staff directors, as well as others in the ABA, that we are too complex. We’ve gotten so complex that it’s made it really hard for new members, especially younger lawyers and potential new members, to navigate the ABA. When they get frustrated, they just give up. Because we’re seeing the nonrenewal rate of first-year lawyers is so much greater than for the rest of the association population, it’s crying out for reform.

We have five teams that are making their phone calls to entities, whether it’s a section, division, forum or a standing or special committee or commission. We are looking at all aspects of the association to see where we can make some changes that hopefully will simplify the structure, eliminate both silos and duplication of effort, and make it easier for people to find their way to their home or homes. We will be having an open forum at the midyear meeting for people to express their thoughts and ideas. We will really start working right after midyear on some proposals for revising our structure.

The first goal of the strategic plan is that the ABA is a home for all lawyers. So this is to help make that happen. The third goal is that we have a strong financial foundation to take us forward into our next 150 years as an association. So this is also to help in that effort.

I can imagine that is a big priority. Are there any others you’d like to mention?

The other one is the middle goal of the strategic plan, which is that we are the voice of the legal profession and defender of the rule of law. I have spoken a lot about the rule of law, both across the country and overseas. Lawyers overseas are very, very focused on what’s happening in the United States. We carry a great weight with our colleagues, and it’s important to them that they know what we’re doing to make sure that our democracy thrives in the face of some real attacks.

I have no preconceived idea that we are on an easy street now that the elections are passed. I think there will be continued efforts to dismantle parts of our democracy, and we will continue to defend it. That will certainly be a priority.

What have you enjoyed about the job and found most fun?

I’ve really enjoyed talking with people and helping create solutions. That is what we as lawyers do best—we solve problems. So helping advance the mission of the ABA and solve problems for either lawyers or entities within the ABA has been very rewarding. And I’ve certainly enjoyed traveling and getting to know both ABA members, and again, our colleagues around the world who are very much interested in the work of the ABA.

You’ve been an ABA member for a long time, and you know a lot of people well. But what is one thing other members might not know about you?

I am a third-generation lawyer. My maternal grandfather was also mayor of the city of Toledo, where I grew up, for 10 years. As a child, it was kind of fun walking down the street with him. People would greet him as Mr. Mayor. And then my paternal grandfather was not only successful in his practice, but he was also appointed as a probate court judge for a while.

Volcanic eruptions leave a lingering chill in the ocean

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Major volcanic eruptions may briefly strengthen ocean cold spells, but their cooling effects vary widely across different regions.

Pinatubo’s eruption was over long before the cooling was. The 1991 eruption cooled the lower atmosphere worldwide by as much as 0.9°F (0.5°C), with effects lasting about two years.

At sea, that chill had an uneven reach. A spell of unusually cold water could grow stronger in one region without doing so in another.

A new study follows those cold spells through six big eruptions. Each eruption preceded an increase in their strength overall, a short-lived reversal of the weakening seen across 175 years.

Marine cold spells are runs of days when the sea surface is unusually cold. An icy sea isn’t necessarily having one; it may just be as cold as it usually is.

The study used the coldest 10% of temperatures for a given place and time of year as its cutoff. The water had to stay that cold for five days or longer, rather than just dip for a day or two.

Jan Kempert, at South Westphalia University of Applied Sciences, worked with Raven Quilestino-Olario, at the University of Cologne’s Institute of Geology and Mineralogy, on the research. Both institutions are in Germany.

“We noticed that while the cooling effects of major volcanic eruptions have been widely studied, relatively little attention has been given to how these volcanic winters affect extreme cold events in the ocean,” Quilestino-Olario said.

Their source was COBE-SST3, a record from the Japan Meteorological Agency. It gives daily estimates of ocean surface temperatures from 1850 to 2024, based mostly on readings taken at sea.

For each cold spell they found, the pair added up how far below normal the water was, day by day. That total became the spell’s score.

The score thus counted the length of the spell as well as the cold. A longer stretch or a deeper chill would push it up.

In 79% of the ocean map squares, however, the scores went down over those 175 years.

Cold spells grew less frequent in about the same share of squares, extending a decline that satellites had already recorded for 1982 to 2020.

The eruption list began with Krakatau in 1883 and Santa Maria in 1902. It continued with Novarupta in 1912, Agung in 1963, El Chichón in 1982, and finally Pinatubo in 1991.

The stratosphere, high above the ground, is where sulfur-rich particles from large eruptions can linger. They cut down the sunlight reaching the surface, sometimes leaving months or years of cooling.

The comparison put cold spells from the 30 years before an eruption on one side and a short period after it on the other.

The later run always started the next year and lasted five years for Krakatau, two for Pinatubo, and one for each of the other four.

There was more than a volcano to reckon with in some of those years. El Chichón, in particular, had some inconvenient company.

The eruption came in 1982. Across 1982 and 1983, the Pacific also experienced one of only six “super” El Niño events since 1850.

Both events could leave a mark on the temperature record, so the researchers had to allow for the overlap.

Kempert and Quilestino-Olario removed the influence of El Niño and La Niña, along with the long-term weakening of cold spells.

After those adjustments, marine cold spells were stronger than expected following all six eruptions.

“Our study shows that although cold extremes in the ocean have generally become less frequent and less intense over the past 175 years, major volcanic eruptions may temporarily interrupt this long-term pattern,” Kempert said.

They also tried choosing the years without looking at eruption dates at all. In 10,000 random draws, each containing six years, they checked for an increase in cold-spell strength.

Just about 2.4% of the draws produced an increase as large as the one after the eruptions. In other words, most random choices of years didn’t produce anything of that size.

The world average was one thing, though. The maps were another.

From 1884 to 1888, about 80% of the ocean squares had stronger cold spells than expected. That was the period following Krakatau; for Pinatubo, the share came to 70%.

For Santa Maria and Agung it was roughly half. El Chichón and Novarupta came in below half, a reminder that an increase in the world average needn’t appear in most places.

Five eruptions had their biggest response in the middle latitudes, the bands between 30 and 60 degrees north or south of the equator. Novarupta broke that pattern.

The only high-latitude eruption in the six, Novarupta also had the weakest effect on the global average. The authors were cautious about that result.

None of these eruptions occurred in an otherwise unchanging ocean. Ocean circulation was still shifting water around, the seasons were changing, and natural swings in climate were still going on.

According to the authors, volcanic cooling and those ongoing changes together could account for the patchy map. Changes in circulation can even bring warmer winters to parts of the far north after an eruption.

Some parts of the ocean have very few nineteenth-century observations. The record is less reliable in places with few historical readings, and in places where sea ice was present.

For Pinatubo, the team ran the analysis again with a satellite-era record from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

The two records agreed in 79% of the map squares about whether cold spells were stronger or weaker than expected.

Another choice affected the answer: the years used to stand for normal temperatures. Comparing each eruption with its previous 30 years gave stronger cold spells after all six.

When the researchers used the same modern reference period for every eruption, fewer eruptions showed an increase.

The count was five with 1961 to 1990 as the comparison, and four with 1981 to 2010.

How much extra cold can be pinned on the volcanoes themselves remains uncertain. The records describe the aftermath, but can’t separate out every cause.

Additional tests removed a North Atlantic climate pattern as well as El Niño.

Several other natural cycles remained, partly because their consistent monthly records don’t reach as far back as 1850.

There’s a further difficulty with El Niño: an eruption may affect it. Remove El Niño from the calculation and some of the volcanic effect may go with it.

The authors call for further checks using other temperature records, measurements beneath the surface, and climate models.

Their own next project will look at marine heatwaves, periods of unusually warm ocean water.

“We hope to use the same long-term ocean temperature dataset to explore how marine heatwaves have changed over the past 175 years, this time placing greater emphasis on the uncertainties associated with historical observations,” Quilestino-Olario said.

The heatwave work could help establish whether eruptions change unusually hot conditions at sea as well as cold ones.

The full study was published in the journal Ocean-Land-Atmosphere Research.

Is AIIB's complaint mechanism failing affected communities?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The AIIB has a grievance redress mechanism for people affected by projects it finances. But those seeking to file a complaint face significant hurdles. Critics say reforms and greater transparency are needed.

For members of the Indigenous Sasak community on the Indonesian island of Lombok, the conflict over their land is not over.

For them, a tourism and infrastructure project co-financed by the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) is a source of frustration, as they believe it has led to the expropriation of their land and destruction of their livelihoods.

In late September, they officially filed a complaint with the bank. It remains unclear whether this complaint will lead to an independent investigation.

The AIIB, which finances major infrastructure projects across Asia, has a mechanism in place to deal with such complaints.

Individuals who suffer adverse effects from the bank-financed projects may, under certain conditions, file a complaint with the institution.

However, access to this process — known as the Project Complaints Mechanism (PPM) — remains controversial.

According to a report by The Diplomat, a magazine that covers political and economic developments in Asia, none of the 45 complaints listed on the AIIB's website had been deemed admissible as of mid-September.

The bank acknowledged that "no cases have passed the PPM's eligibility assessment," but it stressed that it is "only part of the story."

The AIIB emphasized that not every issue needs to be resolved through this mechanism.

The PPM is "only one part of AIIB's grievance redress ecosystem," the Beijing-based bank said in response to a query from DW.

Those affected should first attempt to resolve concerns directly with the project team at the local level, it said.

According to the bank, "twelve complaints were in fact resolved at the project level — as a direct result of AIIB's follow-up and intervention — and one was resolved through domestic courts."

But critics see this as a major weakness.

"The mechanism must be easily accessible," said Arntraud Hartmann, a development economist at the Johns Hopkins University School of Advanced International Studies (SAIS Europe) in Bologna, Italy.

She argued that people affected by the projects, for instance, are required to document potential violations of environmental and social standards from the start. "How is the affected party supposed to know which standards might have been violated?" she questioned.

The AIIB disputes this claim. "In terms of accessibility, we have made the submission form simpler and translated it into 12 languages," it said, adding that complaints can be submitted online, by phone, or by email.

Urgewald, a German environmental NGO, cites another problem with AIIB-financed projects.

For many of them, information on environmental impacts and resettlements is either missing or published late, it noted.

For 2024, the NGO found that 11 of the 16 projects it examined lacked complete environmental and social impact assessments. In many cases, information on the potential number of displaced residents was also missing, it added.

"The information published on the bank's website is often insufficient and inconsistent. Particularly important documents, such as environmental impact assessments or resettlement plans, vary widely or are missing entirely," said Nora Sausmikat, a China expert at Urgewald.

The lack of information makes it difficult for those affected to respond in a timely manner and report violations early on, she underscored, adding that the situation is particularly difficult for people without formal land titles.

Christoph Steinert, a political scientist at the University of Konstanz, said the mere existence of such grievance redressal mechanisms "is not enough."

Particularly vulnerable groups often know little about their rights, he said, adding that information should be available in local languages and targeted outreach directed at affected communities.

Criticism of the AIIB's procedures is not new.

A 2023 report by international aid organizations, including Recourse, Accountability Counsel, and Inclusive Development International, revealed that the AIIB's grievance mechanism had failed to accept a single complaint since its 2019 inception.

The study highlighted that 101 of the 219 AIIB-financed projects, or 46% of of the total projects at the time, were ineligible for review due to co-financing exemptions.

The AIIB typically provides funding without leading projects, a standard practice in development finance. When complaints arise, it directs complainants to the lead lender. As a result, nine of the 45 complaints fell outside its jurisdiction.

For Sausmikat, this is part of the problem. "The AIIB, however, decided against revising this requirement and established a mechanism that is very difficult to access," she said.

As of January 1, 2026, following two years of external consultations, the AIIB reformed its complaints mechanism. Under certain conditions, complaints can now be filed even while legal proceedings are ongoing.

But Sausmikat stressed that the changes are insufficient. Of the eight key recommendations made by an independent expert, only three were adopted, she underlined. "The revision did not remove the actual obstacles."

The AIIB stated that the reforms are intended to make the mechanism "more accessible and responsive."

The new rules have been in effect for only ten months, and their implementation is being closely monitored, the bank said.

Steinert said fear of reprisals poses another fundamental challenge to such procedures.

Documented cases from Latin America, for instance, show that complainants have faced administrative or legal problems, and in some cases even violence.

Such risks could deter others from filing complaints, Steinert said.

However, his research does not specifically address AIIB projects.

The bank said that it "does not tolerate retaliation against anyone who raises concerns."

"Requestors may ask for confidentiality, and the revised PPM policy strengthens protection against reprisals," it added.

Sausmikat said Germany — as a co-founder and the fourth-largest shareholder of the AIIB — could also play a role, working with other member states to push for changes.

Hartmann also stressed that the bank's shareholders bear responsibility and urged the AIIB's Board of Directors to amend the rules governing the grievance mechanism. "We need a policy based on good-practice standards."

A Planet 11 Times Bigger Than Jupiter Is Challenging What We Thought About Planet Formation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

New observations of the giant exoplanet Beta Pictoris b are challenging assumptions about how and where massive planets form.

New observations of Beta Pictoris b, a massive planet orbiting a young star, are challenging a method astronomers hoped could reveal where giant planets form. More precise measurements of the planet’s atmosphere have overturned an earlier result, raising doubts about whether a particular chemical signature can reliably trace a planet’s birthplace.

Located in a 23-million-year-old planetary system, Beta Pictoris b is approximately 11 times Jupiter’s mass. It orbits about 10 astronomical units (approximately 930 million miles) from its star, completing one revolution every 23 years. Earlier atmospheric measurements suggested the planet might have formed much farther away before migrating inward, but the latest evidence points to a different possibility.

Astronomers have proposed using the relative abundance of two forms of carbon monoxide (CO) to determine where giant planets formed within their original disks of gas and dust. These molecules contain different carbon isotopes, 12C and 13C, which have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Their slightly different masses can influence how they behave during chemical processes.

The method relies on the carbon monoxide snowline, the distance from a young star beyond which temperatures become cold enough for CO gas to freeze onto dust grains. Planets forming beyond this boundary may accumulate carbon monoxide ice, potentially leaving a different isotopic signature than planets that develop in warmer regions where CO remains gaseous.

Earlier observations of Beta Pictoris b using the original GRAVITY instrument produced a relatively low ratio of 12CO to 13CO. That result appeared consistent with formation beyond the snowline, followed by inward migration to the planet’s present orbit. However, the researchers cautioned that instrumental limitations could have affected the measurement.

A team led by Antonia von Stauffenberg, a doctoral student at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy (MPIA) in Heidelberg, Germany, has now revisited the planet using GRAVITY+. The upgraded instrument operates at the Very Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI), managed by the European Southern Observatory (ESO) in Chile.

The new observations reveal a substantially higher 12CO/13CO ratio than previously reported. While 12CO produces a readily detectable signal, its less abundant counterpart, 13CO, is considerably harder to measure accurately. GRAVITY+ provides improved adaptive optics and greater observational precision, allowing researchers to better distinguish the two molecules.

“The GRAVITY+ interferometric instrument is highly stable […], making it uniquely capable of high-fidelity characterization of directly imaged exoplanets,” says co-author and MPIA scientist Jonas Sauter.

The revised ratio agrees with independent measurements reported by González Picos et al. (2026) using a different instrument. This agreement supports the new result and indicates that systematic uncertainties affected the earlier GRAVITY measurements.

The researchers also detected tentative variations in the planet’s observed brightness. These fluctuations appear to follow a period of approximately 8.7 hours, close to the planet’s rotation period.

If confirmed, the variations could arise from atmospheric clouds or chemical differences moving in and out of view as the planet rotates. However, the evidence remains statistically weak, and more sensitive observations will be necessary to establish whether the changes are real.

“The ability to accurately constrain both isotopologues and potential rotational variability using ground-based observations of a bona fide planet such as Beta Pictoris b demonstrates the exceptional data quality achieved with the updated GRAVITY+ instrument,” says von Stauffenberg.

Under the proposed formation model, the revised carbon monoxide ratio would favor an origin inside the snowline, consistent with Beta Pictoris b’s current orbital distance. Yet the measurement also closely resembles ratios observed throughout the Solar System and the interstellar medium, the gas and dust occupying the space between stars.

Similar values have been measured in most of the roughly dozen young giant planets studied using this technique. This widespread consistency suggests that differences in carbon isotope abundances across planet-forming environments may be too small to distinguish reliably, limiting the method’s usefulness for reconstructing individual planetary histories.

“It is still difficult to utilize 13CO as a formation tracer of giant planets, due to the uncertainties that still persist in the models and measurements,” explains von Stauffenberg.

Current models may be missing important details of carbon monoxide ice chemistry in planet-forming disks. Until those processes are better understood, atmospheric carbon isotope ratios alone cannot establish whether a giant planet formed in a warm, gas-rich region or farther out among frozen materials. More precise observations and additional chemical indicators will be needed to reconstruct these planets’ origins, with GRAVITY+ offering a powerful tool for investigating both their formation and atmospheric behavior.

Lane splitting laws: how 10 U.S. and global rules differ for riders

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Lane splitting laws vary widely, from California's broad acceptance to Utah's narrow filtering rules, and this guide explains how the practice works and where riders can use it

A motorcyclist riding between two lanes of slow freeway traffic in California is using a maneuver the state's vehicle code now defines by name. In most other U.S. states, the same maneuver is prohibited. That gap is why lane splitting laws draw questions from riders, drivers and anyone planning a road trip across state lines.

Part of the confusion is vocabulary. Lane splitting, lane filtering and lane sharing sound alike, but they describe different things. Splitting usually means riding between two lanes of traffic moving in the same direction. Filtering usually means moving between stopped or slow vehicles, often at a red light. Sharing means two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane. Some states and countries draw these lines differently. A maneuver allowed under one name in one place may carry a different label somewhere else.

The U.S. picture is uneven. California has long accepted lane splitting without an explicit ban. Assembly Bill 51, signed in 2016, added a formal definition of the practice to the California Vehicle Code. It also directed the California Highway Patrol to develop educational safety guidelines. Those guidelines are not binding law, and state law sets no fixed speed limit for the practice. Utah's narrower filtering law took effect in 2019. It allows the practice only in stopped traffic, on roads with lower posted speed limits and at low riding speeds. Arizona, Montana and Colorado have their own filtering laws, each with conditions. Most other states prohibit the practice, either by statute or through rules that keep vehicles within a single lane.

Outside the U.S., the approach shifts again. The U.K.'s Highway Code addresses filtering in slow-moving traffic. France introduced a legal filtering experiment in 2021 in certain departments. In Australia, rules vary by state, and New South Wales allows filtering under set conditions.

The 10 slides that follow start with the basic definitions. They then move through the U.S. states with specific rules, the reasons most states ban the practice and the approaches taken abroad. The final slide explains the mechanics that matter for safety: the speed difference between a motorcycle and surrounding traffic, driver awareness and mirror checks.

Lane splitting is a motorcyclist riding between two lanes of traffic that are moving in the same direction. The rider travels along the line that divides those lanes, passing cars and trucks on both sides instead of waiting in a single lane behind them.

The maneuver depends on width. A motorcycle takes up far less of the road than a car, a pickup or a delivery van. That difference leaves a strip of usable space between vehicles when they sit side by side in neighboring lanes. A rider can guide the bike through that strip.

In practice, the rider lines the motorcycle up with the painted divider and moves forward through the gap between two rows of vehicles. The pinch points are usually where side mirrors stick out from cars and trucks. Larger vehicles such as buses and tractor-trailers narrow the gap further. When the space runs out or traffic picks up, the rider merges back into a regular lane.

The definition has firm edges. Both lanes must carry traffic going the same way. Crossing a center line to pass into oncoming traffic is ordinary overtaking, not lane splitting. Riding on a paved shoulder or in a bike lane is a separate act as well.

The practice tends to show up where roads have several lanes and traffic slows down. Multi-lane freeways at rush hour and wide city streets with long lines at signals are typical settings. On a two-lane rural road with one lane in each direction, there is no pair of same-direction lanes to split.

Riders use lane splitting mainly to keep moving when traffic stalls. A motorcycle stuck in stop-and-go traffic gains nothing from its small size. Riders on air-cooled bikes also deal with engine heat building up at low speed. In hot climates, riders sit exposed to the sun and to exhaust from the vehicles around them.

Whether the maneuver is allowed depends on the state or country where the rider is on the road. Some places accept it broadly, while others prohibit it outright.

Lane splitting, lane filtering and lane sharing are three different motorcycle maneuvers. Splitting is riding between two lanes of same-direction traffic, filtering is moving between stopped or slow traffic, and sharing is two motorcycles side by side in one lane.

The difference between splitting and filtering comes down to what the surrounding traffic is doing. In lane splitting, the cars on either side are moving. The rider travels in the gap between them, along the line that separates the two lanes.

Lane filtering typically happens when that traffic has stopped or slowed to a crawl. The classic example is a line of cars waiting at a red light. A rider who filters works forward between the stationary vehicles, often toward the front of the queue.

Lane sharing is a separate idea. It does not involve passing anyone. Two motorcycles occupy a single lane at the same time, riding next to each other rather than one behind the other. The question it raises is how much of a lane a motorcycle may use, not whether it can move between lanes.

These terms are not interchangeable in law. Each jurisdiction that addresses the practice chooses its own wording and its own definitions. Some U.S. states and some other countries define the terms differently. A word that covers a maneuver in one place may not cover the same maneuver somewhere else.

That distinction matters for anyone reading a statute or a rule book. A law that permits filtering under set conditions does not automatically permit splitting through moving traffic. Utah's statute is written as a filtering law, while California's vehicle code defines splitting.

The words also get blurred in everyday conversation. Riders and drivers often say "lane splitting" to mean any movement between lanes. When the question is what a rider may legally do in a specific place, the term written into that place's rules is the one that counts.

California is the only U.S. state where lane splitting has long been broadly accepted without an explicit prohibition. Assembly Bill 51, signed in 2016, gave the practice a formal definition in the California Vehicle Code.

For years, California's position rested on an absence rather than a grant of permission. The vehicle code did not forbid a motorcyclist from riding between lanes of traffic moving in the same direction. As a result, riders treated the maneuver as an accepted part of traveling on the state's roads.

That arrangement left a gap. A practice can be tolerated without being described anywhere in statute. Before 2016, nothing in the code said what lane splitting actually was.

Assembly Bill 51 closed that gap by putting a definition into the code. With the term defined, the practice gained a recognized place in state law instead of existing only by omission.

The bill did a second thing. It directed the California Highway Patrol to develop educational safety guidelines for lane splitting. Under the law, the agency's role is to inform riders and drivers about safer practice.

Those guidelines are not binding law. They are advice from the state's highway patrol. The legislature did not write them into the vehicle code as enforceable rules. A rider who departs from them is not violating a lane splitting statute for that reason alone.

The law also sets no fixed speed limit for splitting lanes. It does not cap how fast a motorcycle may travel between vehicles. Nor does it cap the speed gap between the motorcycle and the traffic around it. That leaves California without the kind of numeric thresholds that filtering laws in some other states use.

The lack of a lane splitting speed cap does not suspend other traffic rules. Posted speed limits still apply to a motorcyclist moving between lanes, just as they apply to every other vehicle on the road.

For drivers, the practical meaning follows from the same facts. A motorcycle passing between lanes in California is performing a maneuver that state law defines and does not prohibit. A driver who sees one approaching is sharing the road with a recognized practice, not watching a violation.

Utah allows motorcyclists to filter between lanes only when traffic is stopped. The rider must also be on a road with a speed limit of 45 mph or less and travel no faster than 15 mph. The state's lane filtering law took effect in 2019.

A rider has to meet all three conditions at once for the maneuver to fall within the law. Missing any one of them takes the rider outside the permission the statute grants.

The stopped-traffic requirement rules out filtering through moving cars. It describes a line of vehicles that is not moving, such as a queue waiting at a red light. Traffic that is only slow does not meet that condition. Once the queue starts moving again, the condition no longer holds. This separates Utah's rule from lane splitting in the broader sense, where a rider passes between lanes of moving vehicles.

The 45 mph threshold refers to the road's speed limit, not the speed traffic is actually traveling. A rider on a road with a limit of 45 mph or below can filter when traffic there comes to a halt. A road with a higher speed limit falls outside the law, even if the cars on it have stopped. In practice, that leaves out Utah's interstates, whose posted limits sit well above that threshold.

The 15 mph cap applies to the motorcycle itself. It sets the fastest a rider may travel while moving between the stopped vehicles. A rider going faster than that is no longer filtering within the law's terms.

Utah writes its speed figures directly into the statute. Riders there have specific numbers to follow. Each condition can be checked against a fixed number or a plain fact about the traffic. Taken together, the three conditions confine the practice to roads with lower posted limits and to traffic that has come to a standstill.

Arizona, Montana and Colorado each have laws that permit motorcycle lane filtering under conditions written into their own statutes. None of the three gives riders the broad acceptance of riding between lanes that California has long allowed.

The operative term in all three states is filtering, not splitting. Filtering generally describes a rider moving between vehicles that are stopped or barely moving, often while traffic waits at a red light. Lane splitting through traffic moving at normal speed is a broader maneuver. Each of these states wrote its law around the narrower practice.

The conditions in these laws generally address two things: how fast the surrounding traffic may be moving and how fast the rider may travel while passing between vehicles. Some also limit the kinds of roads where the maneuver applies.

Each legislature drafted its own rules. As a result, the conditions are not identical from one state to the next. A rider who knows the filtering rules in one of these states should not assume they apply after crossing into another.

A conditional filtering law sets out when the maneuver is allowed. It does not leave riders to judge for themselves. Outside those conditions, moving between lanes falls back under the ordinary traffic rules that apply to every vehicle.

Together with Utah, these three states form a small group that permits a limited version of the practice by statute. That sets them apart from most U.S. states. In those states, riding between lanes is prohibited outright or ruled out by laws requiring vehicles to stay within a single lane.

Riders can check the current statute text before filtering in Arizona, Montana or Colorado. Guidance from each state's motor vehicle or highway safety agency covers the same ground. For drivers in these states, the laws explain why a motorcycle may legally pass between stopped cars at an intersection.

Most U.S. states ban lane splitting because their traffic codes either prohibit riding between lanes outright or require every vehicle to stay within a single marked lane. A motorcyclist who rides the line between two rows of cars breaks one of those rules, even where no law uses the term lane splitting.

The first route is an explicit prohibition. Some state codes include a motorcycle-specific section that bars riders from operating between lanes of traffic or between adjacent rows of vehicles. That wording leaves little room for interpretation. It covers moving and stopped traffic alike. It rules out splitting at highway speed and also rules out filtering to the front of a line at a red light.

The second route is less direct. Most state traffic codes require a driver on a road divided into marked lanes to keep the vehicle entirely within one lane as nearly as practicable. A driver may leave that lane only after confirming the move can be made safely. A motorcycle counts as a vehicle under these codes. Its rider carries the same duty. Straddling a lane line to pass slower cars does not fit within it.

Many states pair the single-lane rule with a provision that entitles motorcycles to the full use of a lane. The same provision bars other drivers from crowding a motorcycle out of its lane. In effect, it treats each lane as space for one vehicle at a time. A rider squeezing past a car inside that car's lane runs against the same logic.

Some codes go a step further. They prohibit a motorcycle from overtaking and passing in the same lane occupied by the vehicle being passed. That rule covers in-lane passing, which sits between an outright ban on riding between lanes and a general lane-use requirement.

The charge a rider faces depends on which statute a state relies on. In states without an exception, moving between lanes can lead to a citation for an improper lane change, unsafe passing or a lane-use violation.

This structure also explains why allowing the practice in these states takes new legislation. A state that wants to permit filtering cannot just stop enforcing its existing rules. Lawmakers have to write an exception into the traffic code. That exception typically spells out where, when and at what speed riders may move between vehicles.

Motorcycle filtering is permitted in the U.K., and the Highway Code addresses it directly. The guidance covers riders moving through slow-moving traffic, not riding at speed between lanes of free-flowing vehicles.

The Highway Code is the official rulebook for road users in Great Britain, meaning England, Scotland and Wales. Northern Ireland publishes its own version. The code covers drivers, motorcyclists, cyclists and pedestrians in one document. The same text that speaks to riders about filtering is also the text drivers are expected to know.

British guidance uses the word "filtering" for this maneuver. It refers to passing between vehicles that are stopped or crawling, such as a queue of cars waiting at a red light or held up in congestion. Reading the code as permission to ride between lanes of traffic at highway speed would misread it.

The section written for motorcyclists places filtering inside its advice on maneuvering. Riders are told to know what is behind them and to their sides before they move. In traffic queues, the code tells them to watch for pedestrians crossing between vehicles. It also flags vehicles coming out of junctions or changing lanes. When filtering in slow-moving traffic, riders are told to take care and keep their speed low.

The code sets no number for that speed. It leaves the rider to judge what "low" means against the traffic around them.

Drivers get their own reference to the practice. The section on other road users warns that motorcyclists can be hard to see. It names filtering through traffic among the moments a rider is difficult to spot, along with overtaking and approaching from behind.

The code contains two kinds of instruction. Rules written with "must" or "must not" reflect legal requirements backed by legislation. Rules written with "should" or "should not" are advisory. The filtering advice uses the advisory wording. The code states that failing to follow advisory rules can still be used as evidence in court proceedings to help establish liability.

France introduced a legal lane filtering experiment in 2021 that applied in certain departments, not across the whole country. Riding between lanes of traffic is a widespread practice among motorcyclists in France. The experiment gave that practice a legal framework in the areas it covered.

Departments are administrative divisions of France. The 2021 experiment was limited to certain of them. As a result, a rider's legal position depended on where in France the riding took place.

A maneuver covered by the experiment in a participating department had no such coverage in a department outside the program. Location, not the maneuver itself, determined how the rules applied.

The word "experiment" matters here. It describes a trial arrangement tied to defined areas. It is not a single permanent rule written for the whole country. An experimental framework can be extended, changed or ended. The rules in force for a department at the time of riding are the ones that apply.

Across the rest of Europe, the picture is not uniform. Each country sets its own traffic law. Approaches to riding between lanes differ from one national code to the next. Some countries address the practice directly in their road rules. Others leave it to general provisions on lane use and overtaking.

That variation has a practical consequence for riders who cross borders. A maneuver accepted in one country may be treated differently a short drive away. The national traffic code of the country a rider is in determines what applies.

Motorcycle lane filtering rules in Australia depend on the state or territory where a rider is on the road. New South Wales permits filtering, but only under set conditions.

Each Australian state and territory sets and enforces its own road rules. A maneuver that is lawful in one state may not be lawful across the border. A rider's options in heavy traffic can change partway through a long interstate trip.

Permission to filter does not travel with the motorcycle or the license. A rider licensed in one state must follow the road rules of the state where they are riding at the time.

In New South Wales, filtering is a conditional maneuver rather than a general right to move between lanes at will. The conditions govern where, when and how a rider may move past slower or stopped vehicles. A rider who meets those conditions is filtering legally. A rider who moves between vehicles outside them is not covered by the permission.

That structure places NSW among the jurisdictions that allow filtering within written limits, rather than leaving the practice broadly open. The maneuver is permitted, but the state defines its boundaries.

Each state and territory publishes its current rules through its own road authority. Riders who plan to filter in Australia need to check the rules for the specific state or territory they are in before relying on them.

The main safety factor in lane splitting is the speed difference between the motorcycle and the traffic beside it. A small difference gives the rider and nearby drivers time to react. A large one cuts that time down.

That speed difference matters more than the rider's absolute speed. A motorcycle passing cars that are crawling has a very different margin than one passing cars moving close to the posted limit. When surrounding traffic speeds up, the gaps between vehicles tend to close. Drivers also start changing lanes more often. For riders, that shift is usually the point to merge back into a full lane.

Driver awareness is the second factor. Many drivers do not expect a vehicle to appear between lanes, especially in places where the practice is rare or banned. A motorcycle has a narrow profile. It can sit inside a car's blind spot or disappear behind a roof pillar or headrest. A rider between lanes has no confirmation of being seen until a driver's movement shows it.

Visibility affects that awareness. Bright riding gear and a consistent position between lanes make a motorcycle easier to pick out in traffic. A rider who weaves or changes position often is harder for drivers to track.

Mirror checks work in both directions. Drivers in slow or stopped traffic can glance at their side and rearview mirrors before drifting toward a lane line. A turn signal gives a rider coming from behind clear warning that a gap is about to close. Riders, for their part, watch the front wheels of nearby cars. A wheel turning toward the lane line often signals a lane change before the body of the car moves.

Mirrors are also a physical obstacle. Trucks, buses and wide pickups leave narrower gaps. Their mirrors can sit at a height that lines up with a motorcycle's handlebars. Saddlebags and wide touring motorcycles add width of their own. Passing between two large vehicles leaves very little room for error.

Stopped traffic brings separate hazards. A passenger may open a door into the gap, or a pedestrian may step out between cars. Checking a side mirror before opening a door reduces one of the risks specific to a traffic jam. Drivers can also hold a steady position in their lane rather than moving over to block a motorcycle.

None of these habits makes lane splitting legal where it is prohibited. Local law still decides whether the maneuver is allowed at all.

Hoskinson says he cannot run Cardano like a CEO. Who is accountable for its growth?

DReps approve spending, while recipients and administrators answer for funded work that ADA holders can trace through public reports.

Cardano founder Charles Hoskinson said around 28 minutes into his Nov. 7 AMA that he did not control Cardano's treasury or consensus and could not negotiate on behalf of all ADA holders. His argument that he cannot act as the ecosystem's CEO puts a practical question back before holders: who answers for the work meant to grow Cardano?

The answer depends on the decision. Delegated representatives, or DReps, approve treasury spending, the Constitutional Committee checks constitutionality, and funded recipients deliver under the terms of their proposals. Named administrators must monitor fund use and ensure deliverables are achieved. Cardano's treasury guidance explicitly separates approval from reporting on payouts and results.

Anyone can propose a treasury withdrawal. It needs DRep and Constitutional Committee approval, must comply with the Constitution and must remain within the applicable Net Change Limit, the cap on how much ADA can leave the treasury during a period.

Those roles answer different questions. DReps decide whether to support funding. The committee's mandate is limited to constitutionality; it must operate transparently and publish its decisions.

The Constitution's treasury standards require proposals to state their purpose, delivery period, costs and refund circumstances, and disclose whether the prospective recipient received treasury ADA in the previous 24 months.

Funding requests must also allocate ADA for periodic independent audits and oversight metrics, and designate administrators responsible for monitoring spending and ensuring delivery. While an administrator holds withdrawn funds before disbursement, they must remain in separate accounts the community can audit, with the Constitution's restrictions on delegation.

Two approval roles authorize funding. Recipients and named administrators carry distinct responsibilities for delivery and oversight.
