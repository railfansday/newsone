Audiences are divided over Netflix's new science-fiction movie.

War Machine, The Adam Project, and They Cloned Tyrone are among the original sci-fi movies that Netflix has previously made. While reviews for Alan Ritchson's War Machine were mixed, it became the streaming platform's tenth most-watched film of all time with 139.9 million views. The Adam Project fared even better, as it is in sixth place on the all-time chart thanks to 157.6 million views. Meanwhile, They Cloned Tyrone was acclaimed by critics, leading to a "Certified Fresh" 95% Tomatometer score, along with an 83% Popcornmeter score on Rotten Tomatoes.

Now, Netflix's new sci-fi movie, Allen, has debuted to a 50% Popcornmeter score on Rotten Tomatoes, with audiences split between positive and negative takes on the film. Since the average is based on fewer than 100 ratings as of this writing, it may fluctuate as more are added to the aggregation site.

Allen is about an alien of the same name, who returns to Earth after he mysteriously disappeared 20 years ago. During his initial visit, he was best friends with Petey, and now reunites with the grown-up version of the character (T.J. Power). What Petey has dreamed about for years takes a dark turn when he learns about the real reason for Allen's initial visit, though, and what it now means for him in the present.

On Rotten Tomatoes, many of the more positive audience reviews praise the filmmaking by Australian production company Ludo Studio, who makes all episodes of Bluey, and has now made their first live-action movie through Allen. Other positive takes highlight the story's humor, heart, delivering an original spin on the familiar premise of Steven Spielberg's E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial, and ultimately telling a hopeful and family-friendly story despite the dark plot twist.

As for some of the more negative audience reviews, they include criticism of the movie's overall execution not living up to the creative premise, Petey being too unlikable and erratic to root for despite being the protagonist, uneven pacing that leads to the story feeling disjointed, and being too similar to E.T. and other alien movies in the end rather than feeling by inspired by them. A Tomatometer score has not been calculated yet, but the seven critics' reviews on the site are also divided, as four are "Fresh" and three are "Rotten."

Despite the divisive reception, Allen became a Netflix streaming hit, which includes reaching sixth place on the streamer's U.S. chart and eighth place worldwide. In addition to the U.S., it has made it into the top 10 in Argentina, Brazil, Canada, the Dominican Republic, Egypt, Germany, Hungary, Jamaica, Kenya, Morocco, New Zealand, Nigeria, Switzerland, Thailand, Turkey, Ukraine, and the UK.

Allen was directed by Daley Pearson and Jeffrey Walker, based on a script written by Pearson and Sophie Miller. 10 Action Video Games That Are Bangers From Start to Finish

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When it comes to video games, some of the most popular have always been the likes of action/adventure titles. Video games provide a great escape for the player. Giving them the chance to be an action hero, swinging around New York City, stealing cars, shooting up bad guys, or becoming the samurai of their dreams makes them as popular as they've become.

Like any genre of any medium, though, there are some games that most certainly stand out among the others. This isn't to say that they're better than all the rest—there are too many games to make such an objective statement—but there are some certified bangers that come to mind when the discussion of action video games begins.

This wonderful series by Rocksteady Studios is known as one of the best superhero video game franchises of all time, and for very good reason. A lot of those reasons were either expanded upon from the first game, or established in the second title, Batman: Arkham City. This is known as one of the best superhero games out there, and has gone down in history for it.

Batman: Arkham City is well-known for the common notion that it makes you truly feel like Batman (Kevin Conroy). The gameplay mechanics are very well-executed, and the story is thrilling from start to finish. Not to mention, the return of Kevin Conroy as Batman and Mark Hamill as Joker is a huge win and makes this truly feel like some of the many definitive versions of the characters.

If there's one thing PlayStation is excellent at, it's having some amazing console-exclusive titles. One of the best of recent years is none other than Horizon Zero Dawn. Following the hunter known as Aloy (Ashly Burch), Horizon Zero Dawn takes players on a journey of trying to discover the truth about the hunter's origins, and, in turn, the world around her itself.

This project takes place in a post-apocalyptic setting, but not in the manner of another PlayStation exclusive, The Last of Us. The landscape of Horizon Zero Dawn is wide, vast, and filled with high-technology—machine creatures that are now the apex predators, to be exact. The combat—using Aloy's hunting skills and weapons—is wonderful, and such a blast to play. Not to mention, this new world is such a joy to explore and keeps the players actively wanting to discover more about it via the story and the open-world nature of the title.

When it comes to action, there are few that bring the amount that Hades does. That's because the entire point of the game is, well, action. While there is a story to Hades, the game, at its heart, is a rougelike dungeon crawler title that puts the player against hoards and rooms upon rooms of foes to face as the son of Hades (Logan Cunningham), Zagreus (Darren Korb), attempts to escape the Underworld.

Players will die but, every time they begin anew, they can spend their hard-earned resources to level up and go at it again with new powers. The method to the madness of Hades is failing, trying again, failing, and going until the player finally makes it through. It is an absolute thrill ride the entire way through.

While Sega is most known for the Sonic the Hedgehog franchise, they have another video game series that is widely beloved, Yakuza. The best game in said series is none other than Yazuka 0. The Yakuza series is a great time, and the way it brings together gritty crime drama with some hilarious comedy that keeps the players on their toes.

As stated, the best of the best, Yakuza 0, brings all of these things to the table and enhances them. It follows two stories simultaneously, and that really helps in keeping the players invested. The combat mechanics are phenomenal and the true, main highlight of the game. Yakuza 0 is truly the perfect example of how to make an incredible Yazkua game.

While the recent God of War games are easily some of the best PlayStation titles in recent years, if fans of action want something in the series to seriously deliver on that, they need to look no further than God of War II. This sequel truly delivers on action, spectacle, and fun gameplay. It has a blockbuster-level story that is engaging for the entire runtime.

Not to forget, the players get to take on the gods of Olympus. Kratos (Terrence "T.C." Carson) is out for blood, and he is going to stop at nothing to get it. This allows players to experience some serious spectacle as they fight their way through Olympus and take down the gods. It's a ride filled with intensity till the very end and worth playing every second.

Much like Batman: Arkham City, Insomniac Games' hit title, Marvel's Spider-Man, is well-regarded because of how much it makes the player truly feel like Spider-Man (Yuri Lowenthal). The swinging physics/mechanics are stellar, and easily some of the best in any Spider-Man game ever. Swinging mechanics are so much more important to a Spider-Man game than one would realize on paper. For quite some time, Spider-Man 2's video game was known to have the best.

Many consider it one of the best modern superhero video games of all time. The execution of Marvel's Spider-Man was done with grace and prowess. This reimagining of the Spider-Man world—where Peter Parker is older, experienced, and facing off with enemies he's never faced before—is so well-done, and it's a fan-favorite Spider-Man universe. Playing Marvel's Spider-Man truly does make the player feel like Spider-Man, and, alongside that, has an astounding story.

There are a few things in this life that will always be cool: cowboys, superheroes, spacemen, and . . . samurais. A modern game that might genuinely capture the samurai vibe the best is none other than 2020's Ghost of Tsushima. If there's one thing that can describe this game, it's that it is vastly cinematic. It feels like players are playing through a classic samurai film.

Playing the ever-gorgeous Ghost of Tsushima feels like playing through a movie in all the best ways. The camera, shot framing, battles, and more all make this story amazing to experience. The melee combat is borderline perfect. Sword duels and other action feel so natural and exhilarating to play—it's genuinely difficult to put this amazing game down.

2016's Doom is about as action-packed as it gets. It is a literal non-stop space adventure that has players firing bullets of all kinds into horrifying monsters. Doom rewards players for being aggressive and jumping straight into action, rather than strategy. There is always a battle or chase going down, and it never stops being abundantly exciting.

But players aren't just firing the same kind of gun the entire time—no, there is a gigantic arsenal of weapons players will have the choice to utilize throughout the entire game. Just as importantly, the score behind the action is one of the best action game scores of all time. Composed by the legendary Mick Gordon, everything that is great about Doom is enhanced by Gordon's excellent work.

Another sword-wielding adventure—reminiscent of Ghost of Tsushima—from recent years that is phenomenal is the ever-thrilling Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice. Taking place in the late-1500s, this 2019 adventure follows a shinobi named Sekiro (Daisuke Namikawa and Noshir Dalal), players get to step into the shoes of an incredible swordsman as he goes on a journey to rescue his captured lord, Kuro (Miyuki Sato and Amber Hood).

Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice is everything a typical FromSoftware game—Dark Souls, Bloodborne, and Elden Ring—is, in all the best ways. It truly challenges the player in ways other FromSoftware titles don't. It also has a setting unlike the others, too, being set in 1500s Japan. It is so distinct, vast, and exciting that it feels like a must-play for those who love some good sword combat . . . even if it has the classic tough FromSoftware difficuly.

There is absolutely no question that Grand Theft Auto VI is the biggest game of the decade—maybe even the last two decades. People have been waiting for this game for so long that the cultural impact has been increasing little by little every year, exploding in one great release this year. Ask anyone who's played it, and they'll most likely say, "It was worth the wait."

This game is truly something special. It has attention to detail unlike any other, gorgeous visuals, skilled performances, groundbreaking mechanics across the board, and a wonderful story at the core of it all. There are dozens of reasons to love this game, but one of the main highlights are the action sequences—of which there are plenty. Robberies, assassinations, gunfights, etc. bring Grand Theft Auto VI to the top of the "best of all time" list.

Mister Rogers' neighborhood is in mourning.

Beloved TV host Fred Rogers' youngest son, John Rogers, died at 65 years old, his family confirmed to TMZ. His cause of death was not shared.

John was born in 1961 to the puppeteer—who made television history as America's neighbor on Mister Rogers' Neighborhood from 1968 to 2001—and his wife Joanne Byrd Rogers following the arrival of son James Byrd Rogers in 1959.

John appeared with his brother on the children's show during their childhood in Pittsburgh, Pa., but went on to live a life outside of the public eye, which included a move to Florida in his 30s. Planes, Trains And Automobiles' Deleted Ending Was Rightfully Cut

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Planes, Trains and Automobiles was originally supposed to have a completely different ending, but thankfully it was removed before release. Few comedy movies have endings quite as emotionally satisfying as Planes, Trains and Automobiles. John Hughes' 1987 road-trip comedy spends most of its running time putting advertising executive Neal Page (Steve Martin) through an increasingly ridiculous series of travel disasters, largely thanks to his unwanted traveling companion, Del Griffith (John Candy). However, beneath the hilarious arguments, unfortunate misunderstandings and spectacular misfortunes is a surprisingly moving story about loneliness, friendship and the importance of appreciating other people.

Planes, Trains and Automobiles’ final moments bring that story together beautifully. After finally reaching Chicago, Neal initially leaves Del behind, only to realize that his traveling companion has nowhere to go. He returns to the train station, discovers the truth about Del's circumstances, and invites him home for Thanksgiving. It's a wonderfully understated conclusion that allows both characters to experience a genuine emotional breakthrough.

However, this wasn't always how Hughes intended to finish the movie. The original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay featured a considerably longer ending, including a more explicit explanation of Del's tragic circumstances and an extended Thanksgiving dinner with Neal's family. Although the additional material offered some interesting character details, Hughes ultimately made the right decision by cutting it.

In the original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay, Neal initially leaves Del behind after they finally reach Chicago. When he boards the train home, Del somehow turns up at his destination, prompting Neal to question why he is still following him. Del eventually explains that his wife, Marie, died eight years earlier and that he has been living out of his trunk, moving between motels and occasionally attaching himself to strangers for companionship (via Den of Geek).

The screenplay included a much longer explanation of Del's circumstances, detailing his wife's illness, their inability to have children, and his increasing difficulty coping with life alone. He also explains that Thanksgiving is particularly painful because it reminds him of the family he never had. Rather than Neal figuring out the truth, this version has Del explicitly explain his loneliness and why he attaches himself to other people.

The original ending then continued to Neal's family home, where Del joined the Thanksgiving celebrations. The screenplay included an extended dinner sequence, with Del expressing his gratitude for being welcomed into a family gathering. Audiences at test screenings reportedly did not respond well to the ending, finding Del’s monolog frustrating and unintentionally funny, prompting the change.

The change to the Planes, Trains and Automobiles ending gives Del considerably more dignity. Throughout Planes, Trains and Automobiles, his relentless friendliness is frequently presented as a source of frustration for Neal. He's loud, intrusive, and seemingly incapable of recognizing when somebody wants to be left alone. However, the final revelation reframes his behavior without suggesting that his personality is simply a disguise for his loneliness.

In the original screenplay, Del's explanation risks making his invitation to Thanksgiving feel like an act of charity. Stephen King's 6 Non-Horror Shows, Ranked Worst To Best

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Stephen King’s name is synonymous with horror, but he’s written in multiple genres over the decades. From sci-fi thrillers like The Dead Zone to fantasy series like The Dark Tower, King's books are incredibly varied. While it might seem like every TV adaptation of King’s novels involves horror (to be fair, most do), there have actually been some adaptations of his non-horror works over the years. However, as with most Stephen King adaptations, the quality varies across the board.

Some of these non-horror adaptations are mostly faithful to the source material, while others are so different from the original work, they have almost nothing in common. A few are well-written, incredibly compelling, and look fantastic. Then, there are the others that might be entertaining, but suffer from writing and pacing issues. A few Stephen King non-horror works still haven’t been adapted or used to their full potential, so while this handful of shows is all that’s available now, hopefully, more adaptations will be made in the future.

Golden Years is likely a show few remember, as it aired only seven episodes on CBS in 1991. For those wondering when they missed the Stephen King novel of the same name — there wasn’t one. This adaptation actually came after King had written down the idea for it in a notebook, where it sat for years. He eventually decided to write it as a “novel for television,” according to an interview in The New York Times, which resulted in this miniseries.

While Golden Years was designed as a lead-in to a regular series, CBS opted not to pick it up. As a result, the TV show ended on a cliffhanger (though the home video version changed a few minutes of the last episode to give it a more hopeful ending). The series followed Harlan Williams, an elderly janitor who is exposed to chemicals after an explosion in a secret lab.

As a result, he begins aging in reverse, which gets the attention of those in charge of the lab. Soon, Harlan and his wife have to go on the run, where they meet Terry Spann, who has decided to help protect them. Of course, Harlan’s sudden youthful tendencies aren’t the only powers to develop; as time goes on, other strange things begin to happen. Starring Keith Szarabajka, Frances Sternhagen, R.D. Call, and Felicity Huffman, Golden Years is a decent little sci-fi thriller that mostly suffers from pacing issues and underdeveloped plot elements (and a lack of conclusion).

The second-longest-running Stephen King adaptation, Haven, is based on King’s The Colorado Kid only in the loosest sense. While the novel is a crime mystery involving the mysterious death of a stranger and a pair of older journalists sharing his tale with a newer, much younger journalist, Haven basically borrows only a few names, the location, and minor elements from the novel. The show still involves a dead man nicknamed The Colorado Kid, but it’s more of a procedural with supernatural elements.

Some Stephen King TV shows have fallen under the radar, among them a five-season sci-fi thriller that all fans of the King of Horror should watch.

When FBI Agent Audrey Parker arrives in the small town of Haven on a routine case, she soon discovers more is going on beneath its surface. The people of the town are afflicted with what they call The Troubles. These supernatural afflictions range from having what one draws come true to being a human bullet magnet. She soon quits the FBI and joins the local police, helping those who are Troubled, while also trying to figure out the mystery of her past.

It’s kind of like The X-Files but with a lower budget and less charm, and eventually the lore gets amazingly convoluted. However, Emily Rose as Park is compelling, while Eric Balfour as Duke Crocker is a blast. Best of all, Edge (the wrestler who used to tag team with Christian in the early aughts) is in the show and does a surprisingly good job.

Under the Dome is another adaptation that varies considerably from its source material. However, in this case, Stephen King took to his website during its first season to reassure fans upset about the differences and to say he wholeheartedly approved of the changes thus far. He also pointed out that the novel covers eight days, while the TV show covers a far longer period, so some changes were necessary. Those changes worked, too, as the first season of the series was intriguing.

The series follows the town of Chester’s Mill, where, as one might guess, an indestructible, transparent dome appears one day, cutting the town off from the outside world. While the military and others outside try to figure out how to get rid of the dome, those inside are left to survive dwindling resources and the inevitability of people turning on one another as fear takes hold.

However, fans and critics both soured on the series when it returned for its second and third season. Even King himself eventually decided he didn't like Under the Dome's small-screen adaptation, having initially been a fan. Unfortunately, the idea of a town stuck under a dome, told over multiple seasons, was spreading the premise a bit too thin. Under the Dome was reasonably decent as standard sci-fi fare, but it also leaned into its ridiculous elements a bit too much. Add in characters making increasingly irrational decisions, and things went far off course.

The longest-running King adaptation was The Dead Zone, which ran for six seasons. A seventh season was expected, but unfortunately, the series was canceled, partially due to financial concerns. That left the series hanging on a cliffhanger that answered some questions but raised many more. While it doesn’t have a Rotten Tomatoes score, it does carry a 7.3 on IMDb.

Stephen King's underrated six-season show is now available to stream for free, promising a blend of genres to appeal to different viewers.

Like the 1979 novel and the 1983 movie, the TV series follows Johnny Smith, a teacher who falls into a coma after a car accident and wakes up five years later. When he wakes, he finds himself having visions of the future and the past when he touches people and things. Like the novel, Johnny also discovers through a vision that politician Greg Stillson will kick off the apocalypse in the future. Unlike the novel, the show is much more of a procedural as Johnny begins solving crimes with the help of his abilities.

Overall, the sci-fi drama was enjoyable, with pretty decent visual effects depicting Johnny’s visions, even if it does veer away from the novel due to its long run. However, along with lacking a proper conclusion, The Dead Zone sometimes relied on overly formulaic storylines andoccasional shallow writing.

King explored the age-old question of what a person would do if given the chance to go back in time and prevent a tragedy in his sci-fi novel 11.22.63, when teacher Jake Epping goes back in time to stop the assassination of John F. Kennedy by Lee Harvey Oswald. The miniseries adaptation does the same, though with a few changes to the original storyline. For instance, George MacKay plays the character Bill Turcotte in 11.22.63's TV iteration, someone who doesn't exist in the book.

As Epping undertakes his mission, time seemingly begins pushing back through accidents that crop up and get in the way of his goal. He also falls in love in the past and tries to prevent smaller events while he’s there. However, as with all sci-fi time travel, the smallest of changes can have fairly dire consequences in the future.

It: Welcome to Derry is easily one of the best takes on Stephen King’s works, but a handful of other TV adaptations of his books are arguably better.

This King adaptation is well-made, and the design is incredibly authentic to the era it’s set in (not surprising, given how much research King did to make the novel historically authentic). Some fans weren’t keen on the changes to the novel’s plot, but overall, it’s a thought-provoking, bingeworthy eight episodes. The pacing does tend to drag a bit in the middle, but the series' emotional depth and strong performances save it from cratering.

An adaptation of King’s as yet unbeatable Bill Hodges trilogy, which includes the books Mr. Mercedes, Finders Keepers, and End of Watch, Mr. Mercedes was one of the best TV shows based on King’s works. The series stays far more faithful to the novels than most adaptations, and focuses on Bill Hodges, a retired detective who is still haunted by one particular case where someone drove a stolen Mercedes into a group of people at a local job fair, killing 16.

David E. Kelley (The Practice, Big Little Lies) developed the series, so it was led by someone who knew what they were doing when it came to crime thrillers. The first season pits Bill Hodges against the young psychopath Brady Hartsfield, and soon the two are drawn into a serious game of cat-and-mouse. This season is instantly intriguing, full of high tension, rich in suspense, and complex characters. The performances are outstanding, particularly by Brendan Gleeson as Bill and Harry Treadaway as Brady.

This hard-boiled detective thriller can be bleak at times, and though it isn’t horror, the subject matter can still feel creepy. While the other two seasons were decent, the quality declined a bit, and some fans feel the series should have ended after the first season. Mr. Mercedes suffered from pacing issues as time went on, as well as inconsistent writing that did it no favors. Still, if one only wants to watch a single Stephen King non-horror adaptation, this is probably the best bet.

Focker-In-Law Review: A Few Good Laughs In A Film That Falls Flat

Ultimately, Meet the Parents was seen as a cult classic, and it was followed by two sequels, Meet the Fockers in 2004 and Little Fockers in 2010.

Unfortunately, neither Meet the Fockers nor Little Fockers found the success of their predecessor. It is against that backdrop that Stiller, somewhat surprisingly, decided to take another stab at it 16 years later with the brand-new fourth installment, Focker-in-Law.

Focker-in-Law sees the return of the core cast members, with Stiller's Greg and De Niro's Jack still taking center stage. However, the franchise has added some star power to the next generation, with the DCU's Skyler Gisondo playing Greg's son Henry and singer and actress Ariana Grande playing his fiancé, Olivia. Perhaps, with that beefed-up cast, Universal was hoping for a repeat of the original that side-stepped the two follow-up flops. Sadly, Focker-in-Law offers much of the same.

In truth, Focker-in-Law got several genuine laughs out of me. It's impossible to deny how naturally funny Stiller is, and his dynamic with De Niro clearly works, given the legacy of the franchise. Yet, for as much as Greg's antics occasionally find their original charm, they also raise questions about whether this concept is well and truly played out. In fact, in a moment that is played for laughs, the movie points out that Greg is now older than Jack was when they first met.

Rather than reading as funny, this revelation underscores the fact that Greg has evidently never matured, nor has he really grown as a person. If anything, he is all the worse in this movie. Whereas in Meet the Parents in particular, he is a well-meaning, nervous future son-in-law who accidentally creates mayhem wherever he goes, some of Greg's behavior in Focker-in-Law is outright mean, and borders on cruel.

Unfortunately, the addition of Grande's Olivia fails to help. Ahead of the movie's release, there were grumbles about Grande joining the cast; however, she had plenty of potential to shine in the role. Her appearance in projects like Wicked have proven that she can, indeed, act (and she actually started out acting), and many of her Saturday Night Live skits have been incredibly funny, suggesting that she was up to the task of a comedy.

Yet, in Focker-in-Law, Grande's charm and wit are largely absent. Hampered by a script that doesn't seem to know what it wants to do with this new character, most of Olivia's laugh lines fall flat, and while the Meet the Parents franchise has always stretched the limits of realism, Olivia's skills, talents, and apparent ability to succeed in every single area of her life border on the absurd. In fairness, this has less to do with Grande and more to do with Focker-in-Law's refusal to give her nuance – or, frankly, any realistic, humanizing flaws.

Gisondo, meanwhile, is a standout among the cast. Arguably, he out-acts his co-stars more often than not, and he consistently seems to be the only person on screen committed to conveying a mood other than silly or goofy. That may very well be because most characters were not granted the room to explore other emotions. Teri Polo's Pam and Blythe Danner's Dina are all but sidelined, with the latter in particular being almost non-existent.

Still, Focker-in-Law isn't a complete flop. Those looking for a few good laughs and a nostalgia-packed return of the franchise will no doubt enjoy the movie, and even those who are just hoping for a feel-good film for the holiday season will have plenty of fun. It's far from the most inventive movie of 2026, to be sure, but it shouldn't be counted out altogether.

Guy Pearce's Gory '90s Horror Western Movie Deserved Better At The Box Office

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the enduring American monster myths is the Wendigo or the forest spirit that possesses and compels men to become cannibals. Originally a myth of the Algonquian peoples, the Wendigo has become a monster of comic books, horror films, and more, like Stephen King's chilling novel "Pet Sematary."

Possibly the strangest Wendigo movie is the 1999 horror western comedy "Ravenous," inspired by real tales of 19th century cannibalism on the frontier like the Donner Party. During the Mexican–American War, Lieutenant John Boyd (Guy Pearce) plays dead during battle. Hidden among a cart of deceased soldiers, Boyd takes an enemy fort. A hero but also a coward, Boyd is then reassigned to a remote outpost in the Sierra Nevada mountains.

Curiously, during Boyd's seizure of the fort, he seemed to gain strength when the blood of the dead dripped down onto his tongue. In that moment, Boyd became an unknowing Wendigo, acquiring the strength of the men whose blood he drank — and he's not the only one on the mountain. A traveler named F.W. Colqhoun (Robert Carlyle) shows up at the fort, claiming to be the survivor of a band of wagon travelers killed and cannibalized by their guide, one Colonel Ives. But "Colqhoun" is Ives and, seizing control of the camp, plots to turn it into a cannibal colony. He recognizes Boyd as a seeming kindred spirit, and the movie becomes a test of whether Boyd will succumb to Ives' pressure and his own Wendigo hunger.

"Ravenous" was a box office bomb in its day, grossing $2 million against a $12 million budget, and it received largely underwhelmed or confounded reviews. But don't take that as a mark against the movie, which has a following to this day. (Any "Yellowjackets" fan should make watching "Ravenous" a priority.)

For "Ravenous" to work, Boyd's temptation needs to feel real. When you've got someone as charismatic as Robert Carlyle playing Ives, it sure does. That performance isn't the only way that Carlyle saved the movie.

"Ravenous" had a tumultuous production, and Guy Pearce has called "Ravenous" his worst experience shooting a movie. The film's original director Milcho Manchevski was fired mid-filming with 20th Century Fox moving to replace him with "Home Alone 3" director Raja Gosnell. The cast rejected that, and so Carlyle brought in director Antonia Bird, who he'd worked with on the 1997 crime film "Face." Bird is the credited director of "Ravenous," but was she able to wrangle the mess she'd inherited?

Even a "Ravenous" fan will admit the movie is, tonally, all over the place. After a scene of Ives revealing his true murderous self, the movie breaks out into a chase scene with comical banjo music. “Shaun the Sheep: The Beast of Mossy Bottom” initially began as a half-hour Valentine’s Day special, centered on The Farmer meeting The Vet (a new character in the “Shauniverse”) and their budding relationship. But in the shot of the ship leaving the dock, the unfinished side of the vessel began to show, which posed a challenge for Cox and Walker. In just three days, the creative team constructed the other half the ship to use for the shot.

The giant jack-o’-lantern was also another ambitious moment in the film. Created through 3D printing, the pumpkin was so large that it exceeded the capabilities of the printers at Aardman. The team ultimately had to outsource the job to another company to bring the massive prop to life.

The Extraordinary Music Worthy of an Extraordinary Life in ‘W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause’

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Rita Coburn‘s “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is an extraordinary documentary about an extraordinary man, a film that spans nearly 100 years in two hours, yet never feels rushed or superficial. In her storytelling, Coburn shares with her subject a talent for distilling complex ideas and expressing them with force and clarity; the result is a movie that fully honors the breadth and depth of Du Bois’ achievements as a groundbreaking Black writer, historian, and activist.

One of Coburn’s most potent tools in this regard is the music by composer Kathryn Bostic, who previously collaborated with the director on “Marian Anderson: The Whole World in Her Hands.” Bostic incorporates a wide array of styles and influences in her score, which references the various types of music Du Bois encountered and appreciated as a Black intellectual but isn’t beholden to them; as evocative of Bach and American folk music as it is of 19th-century spirituals and Asian motifs, it’s the sonic equivalent of Du Bois’ literary works. The scope of the project required Bostic to come at the material from multiple angles and perspectives. “Rita and I talked about what types of touchstones we needed to define the narrative musically,” Bostic told IndieWire. “There was the regional touchstone, the era-specific touchstone, the interior emotional aspects that we wanted to heighten with music. Then there’s always the discovery process as you begin to create.”

For Bostic, that discovery process is one of the most exciting aspects of film composing. While she had a number of reference points as she began work on the score, she soon found herself following intuition to take them in different directions than initially intended. “There’s always what I call the hands-on-the-clay moment,” Bostic said. “You have your list, and you have your frame of reference, but the process is fluid.”

According to Coburn, Bostic’s ability to bring a historical perspective to her scores made her an ideal collaborator for the film. “Kathryn pulled from the region of Great Barrington [where Du Bois was born] as well as New York in that time period, and then what was happening with the African-American community that Du Bois was entering,” Coburn said. “There wasn’t gospel then, so you were looking at a small group of Black people going to the churches there in New England. What were they listening to, and therefore what was he listening to?”

Bostic drew on the music of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, a Black a capella ensemble that inspired Du Bois, but then moved in a completely different direction to explore his time in Berlin, when he encountered and fell in love with classical music. She also incorporated Wolof music from Senegal and the Gambia in collaboration with Coburn’s daughter, who studied classical music and worked in the music department on “Rebel with a Cause.”

“She found a Senegalese professor online who worked with her so she could sing the Wolof,” Coburn said. “Kathryn and I agreed that we had to put the Wolof in the film because it connects Du Bois to his Ghanaian roots. But we had to find it, and we wanted it to be as authentic as possible.”

For Bostic, the challenge was taking an audience from 2026 and presenting the music in a way that would recreate the way in which Du Bois experienced it over a hundred years earlier. “There’s the historic arc of the times, as well as what he’s experiencing emotionally,” Bostic said. In the places where her score was informed by spirituals and the work of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, the composer tried to tie the emotional subtext to the context in which she knew Du Bois was consuming the music.

“When he went to Fisk University, his mother had recently died,” Bostic said. “So that music was a wellspring for his grieving, and I think that depth of sorrow and the catharsis that resulted from that sorrow inspired the sort of deep self-reflection that became such a deep part of who he was. That self-reflection led to a basic decency and humanity, and even now I think of him as being light years ahead of people in the way he embodied justice. I want to shine a light on that.”

Coburn felt a similar sense of purpose, especially since the more she dug into Du Bois’ life the more she realized how relevant his insights were to America in 2026. “He speaks to this current time,” Coburn said, adding that the importance of Du Bois’ work gave her own work a sense of urgency and responsibility. “I knew that it might be 20 or 30 years before we get another Du Bois documentary. I hope not, but I looked at his life and knew that I had to do the best job that I could. We did everything we could for Du Bois because we felt he had done everything he could for us.”

The greatness of “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is that it brings the viewer into Du Bois’ perspective as his world view continues to expand over a 95-year life, and the music is a major part of that subjectivity.

“Kathryn understands that music is a character in any documentary, and her music embodies the character of W.E.B. Du Bois,” Coburn said. “ In the music, we had to grow with him. We had to expand with him. We had to see that his love of Rembrandt and his understanding of classical music, of music that came from an Asian culture, that all of that was important to him. We were catching up with him, and he was driving us.”

The film was shot in Los Angeles in just 35 days. It then debuted at Sundance in January 1992, where it became a massive hit. Reservoir Dogs was released in theaters that October and grossed $22 million (an impressive haul for a film that cost about $1.5 million). It became an even bigger hit on home video.

Reservoir Dogs is about a crew of thieves pulling off a jewel heist organized by a crime boss named Joe Cabot (Lawrence Tierney). The six men in the crew don’t know one another, and each is given an alias (Mr. White, Mr. Pink, etc). The job, however, goes horribly wrong and results in a shootout with police, which also claims civilian lives. Reservoir Dogs set a high bar for all future Tarantino films, many of which had their own unique story structure and equally magnetic characters. Eventually, the two men are forced to not just reconcile their differences but admit their own failings while recognizing the humanity in one another. What could've been trite sentimentality in a comedy film is instead transformed into stirring humanism. 10 Greatest Film Noir Movies of the '80s, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Film noir has been the go-to for compelling entertainment since the Golden Age of Hollywood — back when this genre was first coined in the 1940s and '50s, when stylish black-and-white classics like Double Indemnity, Sunset Boulevard, and The Maltese Falcon captivated audiences with their blend of crime and drama, becoming timeless staples of the genre for decades. Today, it's still a current filmmaking style that has a huge impact on pop culture.

There's no shortage of compelling film noir classics throughout the decade. Many, as mentioned before, are from the early years of its inception. But one decade has stood out spectacularly for reinventing the genre and incorporating its thrilling potential into the creative mix of the time period. That was the glorious 1980s, a decade marked by cinematic excellence, where blockbusters ruled, and it was just an absolute blast to be a movie buff. The 10 below define the 1980s with their unique use of film noir mixed with their equally complex stories, proving that this was a time marked by this genre.

Best described as a rock-and-roll action roller-coaster ride of 1980s awesomeness, Walter Hill's cult classic Streets of Fire takes neo-noir filmmaking to new extremes. This kick-ass flick just screams the '80s, featuring a thrilling soundtrack and a unique, rebellious style that perfectly encapsulates the time period. Michael Paré, Diane Lane, and Willem Dafoe lead in this memorable tale of a dangerous ex-soldier on a mission to rescue his singer girlfriend kidnapped by a biker gang and taken somewhere in a dystopian city.

Though a box office flop and critically mixed upon initial reception, Streets of Fire has garnered a cult following that many other '80s films could only dream of. It's stylish, one-of-a-kind, and above all, very entertaining, featuring a perfect mix of action, sci-fi, and film noir to provide viewers with an unforgettable experience. This embodies the youthful spirit and angst of '80s culture and tries to be nothing else.

Hitting all the right notes to make one of the more fascinating and memorable neo-noir classics of the decade, Peter Wier's 1985 film Witness is a must-watch for audiences due to how motivating and emotional its story beats are. Acting icon Harrison Ford steals the show as always, this time in, to date, his only Oscar-nominated performance as a Philadelphia police detective who infiltrates an Amish community to watch over a young boy who witnessed an undercover cop's murder.

The film is perfectly shot, written, and notably acted, as well as tightly paced, providing decent thrills, a compelling mystery, and enough drama and heart to satisfy just about anyone. Witness was a sleeper hit at the box office, racking in an impressive number, and, of course, was a hit with critics, citing its excellent blend of film noir and drama genres, and for its mature storytelling.

A darkly erotic, one-of-a-kind nailbiter, Brian De Palma's cult classic Body Double became one of the most original entries in the film noir genre to come out of the '80s. Though there's a heavy emphasis on shocking and risqué storytelling, it's much in line with a classic Alfred Hitchcock film, following a voyeuristic young man who's pulled into a bizarre murder conspiracy after spying on a beautiful neighbor one night outside an apartment window.

This has the makings of a classic suspenseful set-up, much like Hitchcock's Rear Window, which further explores the themes of voyeuristic obsession as this film does. Except, here the suspense is much more tense, the shock and violence really crank things up a notch, and the unique '80s style really sets it apart and gives it a charm. Body Double offers an edge-of-your-seat experience with great thrills and mysterious presence, making it one of the more fascinating film noir classics that perfectly represent this decade.

Featuring the triumphant feature-film debut of Academy Award-winning filmmakers Joel and Ethan Coen, as well as cinematographer and future director Barry Sonnenfeld and Oscar-winning actress Francis McDormand, the 1984 neo-noir crime thriller Blood Simple is quite simply one of the most underappreciated but masterful cult favorites to enter the decade. McDormand shone, showcasing what she would later give to more prominent roles in a twisted story of romance and murder as a scorned saloon owner hires a private investigator to kill his wife and the bartender she's been cheating with.

This unique, stylish thriller not only showcased the talent of its up-and-coming stars but also became a slow-growing fan favorite among movie buffs over the years for its tense pacing and great concept. It perfectly fits into the genre, complete with a decent mix of crime and drama to grip viewers and disturb them with its creeping suspense. Not to mention, it's a Coen film, which, of course, features their signature filmmaking style, which is visually stellar and hard not to appreciate. Overall, it's a must-watch that would delight any film noir fan.

Based on the legendary Thomas Harris novel Red Dragon, 1986's Manhunter was the first compelling look into the exciting yet terrifying world of the Hannibal Lecter series before becoming more mainstream when Jonathan Demme's The Silence of the Lambs shocked the world. Directed by Michael Mann (yes, the man behind Heat), it delivers a tightly paced, slow-building thriller which sees an ace FBI profiler, Will Graham (William Petersen), as he asks the infamous Dr. Lecter (Brian Cox) to assist in his hunt to catch the elusive Tooth Fairy Killer (Tom Noonan).

Though it's a whole different beast compared to The Silence of the Lambs, Manhunter is a perfectly made and epically gripping classic in its own right. The performances are near flawless, especially the late Tom Noonan as the killer, who can easily give audiences the creeps even now. Not to mention, its mystery and suspense are well-paced and build to a satisfying payoff, which has steadily made this film such a rewatchable and adored cult classic that's gotten more appreciation over the years.

Packing a powerful mix of neo-noir storytelling and tense suspense, William Friedkin's 1985 classic To Live and Die in L.A. provides cinematic excitement, which was what the '80s were all about. William Petersen brings his services again to the genre in this action spectacular, which sees him as a vengeful U.S. Secret Service Agent who crosses the line to eliminate the counterfeit artist (Willem Dafoe) who killed his partner.

From its uniquely '80s style, the kick-ass Wang Chung soundtrack to the heavy, awesome fight sequences and tense chases, To Live and Die in L.A. is an absolute blast of entertainment, one that's not just one of the best of the film noir genre, but just one of the most exciting features of the 1980s, period. It instantly hooks you in, never letting go for even a moment, and fascinates you with its tale of revenge and justice.

Truly one of the most remarkable, bold, and sexiest noir thrillers to come out of the '80s was 1981's Body Heat, a film directed by Lawrence Kasdan and starring the late William Hurt and Kathleen Turner. A neat, twisted little spin on the genre, which in itself is an homage to 1944's Double Indemnity, it follows a naive lawyer manipulated into a murder plot by a seductive femme fatale who has a few tricks up her sleeves.

It's tensely paced, perfectly acted, competently shot, and doesn't lose your attention for a second. Body Heat is a highly rewatchable masterwork that pays tribute to the classic film noir genre, as well as standing in its own right, telling a dark and alluring crime tale that has to be experienced. Just a few minutes of this classic can instantly hook you in.

Michael Mann returns, this time with arguably one of his greatest, most captivating works, Thief, a 1981 neo-noir thriller starring the late James Caan. It follows Caan as a professional thief who is on the path to turning his life around, but his pursuit of this dream becomes far more difficult as he desperately tries to get one last big score and deals with his old associates who are unwilling to let him leave his life of crime.

A critical and commercial success, Thief was instrumental in keeping film noir hot going into the 1980s. Today, it's regarded as one of the genre's most recognizable cult favorites. From Caan's nuanced lead performance as Frank to Mann's remarkable filmmaking talents, to the overall thrilling '80s feel of the film, it's an absolute delight that keeps getting better with age.

The one and only David Lynch fascinated countless fans with his unusual charm and one-of-a-kind storytelling. The film that arguably showed the greatest potential of his talents outside of Twin Peaks was 1986's Blue Velvet, a favorite among many Lynch enthusiasts for its captivating mystery and bizarre tone. Kyle MacLachlan stars as a curious college boy who returns to his old town and is quickly dragged into the town's dark criminal underworld after finding a severed human ear in a field.

It's certainly strange, off, and borderline nonsensical at a few points, but its mystery plot is straight to the point and undeniably fascinating. That's the best way to describe Lynch's masterpiece. Blue Velvet incorporates all his best attributes, and his story is a complement as well as something new to the film noir genre, leading it to become one of the best films of the year.

But truly, at the end of it all, there was no other place for Ridley Scott's 1982 sci-fi and noir masterpiece Blade Runner than at number one. It's honestly hailed as one of the most significant movies in cinematic history, a near-perfect film with flawless visuals, competent performances, and thought-provoking themes. Harrison Ford shines once again as a former cop turned detective-for-hire who is tasked with finding and killing a group of rogue androids who have rebelled against their creator.

This is a film that's still widely talked about and appreciated today for its brilliance and complex storytelling. There's no denying Blade Runner's impact on film noir. It not only breathed new life into the genre but redefined it in every way. This continues to influence the current genre, and rightfully takes this final spot as the film that most defines the film noir genre in the 1980s.

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Mists are apparently rolling into Netflix.

A live-action Dungeons & Dragons series based on Ravenloft is in development at Netflix, giving the tabletop gaming franchise’s most famous horror setting its own shot at TV. The project, simply titled Ravenloft, comes from screenwriter John August, Alfonso Cuarón, and Hasbro Entertainment, and is described as the successor to Netflix's other, now-canceled D&D project, The Forgotten Realms.

While Dungeons & Dragons is often associated with taverns, dragons, treasure, and quippy adventuring parties, Ravenloft is the game’s gothic nightmare corner. It’s the place where D&D trades high-fantasy heroics for haunted castles, cursed lands, tragic monsters and the sense that there's always something lurking in the fog.

Ravenloft has been one of D&D’s most iconic settings for decades, dating back to the original 1983 adventure built around Count Strahd von Zarovich. Strahd remains the setting’s defining villain — he's a vampire lord, tragic monster, and tyrant trapped in the mist-shrouded land of Barovia.

The setting later grew into the Domains of Dread, a collection of horror realms ruled by Darklords who are often imprisoned in worlds shaped by their own sins and obsessions. The premise gives the TV show a lot of flexibility in the stories it tells, bouncing between gothic horrors, cursed folklore, and actual nightmares within the one setting.

That flexibility is a big part of why Ravenloft has endured. While Curse of Strahd remains the best-known modern entry point for many D&D players, newer books like Van Richten’s Guide to Ravenloft and Ravenloft: The Horrors Within have expanded the setting beyond Barovia, turning it into a toolkit for nearly every flavor of horror D&D can support.

According to a Deadline report, the Ravenloft project is also part of a broader push to expand D&D outside the core tabletop game. Netflix had also been developing The Forgotten Realms, though Deadline reports Ravenloft is now positioned as its successor. Meanwhile, a sequel to Dungeons & Dragons: Honor Among Thieves remains uncertain, even though co-writer and co-director Jonathan Goldstein recently said a script exists.

D&D’s reach has also been boosted by projects that don’t look like traditional tabletop products. People are still playing, modding and arguing about Baldur’s Gate 3 years after its 2023 launch, while Critical Role has helped turn actual play into a major entertainment lane of its own, with live shows, animated adaptations, and a huge audience beyond the table.

John Wayne Almost Played Clint Eastwood's Dirty Harry

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​August is a freelance film writer. She studied journalism and cinema at American University. She is a member of the journalism honor society, Kappa Tau Alpha. August has written for various networks and publications, including PBS, Gargoyle Magazine, TheThings, and Identity Theory Magazine. Before returning to entertainment writing, August worked in the Automotive section of U.S. News & World Report. In her free time, August enjoys watching movies in theaters, watching movies at home, listening to podcasts about movies, reading books about movies, watching video essays on movies, talking about movies, thinking about movies, and doing crosswords.

Hollywood film experienced a major shift in the 1970s, following the fall of American idealism and Golden Age of Hollywood. Traditionally optimistic genres such as westerns and musicals waned in popularity while gritty crime-dramas and conspiracy flicks took center stage. This left traditional Hollywood stars in a state of flux, perhaps having to transition into more ambiguous roles. Such changes were deemed necessary even for the likes of John Wayne, who nearly stepped into a major role that now feels tailor-made for Clint Eastwood.

John Wayne was on top of Hollywood for an incredible length of time. Shortly after his leading role in Stagecoach, Wayne gradually evolved into the face of the Western movie genre. He starred in a multitude of high-quality Westerns in the 1940s, and his career exploded further in the 1950s, with the actor making some of the best movies of his entire career, including The Searchers and The Quiet Man.

While still a period of success, the 1960s were a bit quieter for John Wayne, whose specific brand of Westerns gave way to the grittier films made popular by Spaghetti Westerns like Clint Eastwood and Sergio Leone's Dollars Trilogy. In the 1970s, Wayne adjusted to the changing demands of Hollywood, and that approach could very well have landed him in the role of a now-iconic crime thriller.

While Clint Eastwood famously held the titular role, he was not the first name in the running. Western legend John Wayne was reportedly considered for the role of Detective Callahan in Dirty Harry. There are conflicting reports concerning his reasons for turning down the role, or whether he was actually considered for the part at all. Despite some mixed information, it is undeniable that Dirty Harry would be a completely different film with Wayne in the lead.

Several Hollywood icons were reportedly in the running to lead Dirty Harry, including George C. Scott, Paul Newman, Frank Sinatra, and John Wayne (via Far Out Magazine). Sinatra was said to have signed on until a hand injury forced him to withdraw. Reports suggest that Wayne was next on the list, but turned down the role. Warner Bros. released a press release on December 17, 1970, announcing Eastwood as Harry Callahan in the coming drama (via AFI).

There are several reasons as to why Wayne would decline such an opportunity. Michael Munn's 2001 biography, John Wayne: The Man Behind The Myth, quotes Wayne as admitting that the decision came down to pride and dissatisfaction with the character. He was reluctant to accept what he considered “Sinatra’s rejections.” He was also reluctant to inhibit a crooked cop character, which went against his morals and Hollywood persona. However, as the book notes, he later came to realize that he had played rogue heroes before, in Westerns such as The Searchers.

"I turned it down for what seemed to me to be three very good reasons. The first is that they offered it to Frank Sinatra first, but he’d hurt his hand and couldn’t do it. I don’t like being offered Sinatra’s rejections. Put that one down to pride. The second reason is that I thought Harry was a rogue cop. Put that down to narrow-mindedness because when I saw the picture I realized that Harry was the kind of part I’d played often enough; a guy who lives within the law but breaks the rules when he really has to in order to save others.”

Outside of Wayne's own feelings about the role, both he and Sinatra were also considered too old to play Harry. Wayne was a good 15 years older than Eastwood. For this reason, some sources suggest that Wayne was never officially offered the titular lead.

Dirty Harry may not have achieved such astronomic success without Eastwood helming the cast. The actor possessed the intensity and edge that Wayne lacked.

While both Wayne and Eastwood came from a Western background, Eastwood was more versed in playing lawless anti-heroes. Wayne gained popularity in traditional Westerns of the 1940s and ‘50s, portraying generally altruistic figures. Meanwhile, Eastwood entered the genre during the Spaghetti Western period in which morally grey protagonists were in vogue. He played anti-heroic leads in hits such as 1966’s The Good, the Bad and the Ugly.

There is little question about how Wayne would have played Harry Callahan, as he later starred in two Dirty Harry imitations—McQ and Brannigan. In both instances, he seemed ill-suited to the rogue-cop archetype. Wayne read as a man entrenched in a traditional American idealism, clearly uncomfortable with the crime genre's random violence. In contrast to Clint Eastwood, he was ultimately stiff and unconvincing, contrary to the commanding presence he exudes in his Westerns.

Indian auteur Girish Kasaravalli looked back on his career in Kannada-language cinema at the 57th International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa, in a masterclass titled “The Moral Universe of Parallel Cinema: 50 Years of Girish Kasaravalli.”

The 75-year-old spoke about the moral contradictions of society and the relationship between cinematic form and social content. He also argued that even entertainment is a political act. The session was moderated by Variety‘s Asia editor Naman Ramachandran. Across films such as “Ghatashraddha” (1977), “Tabarana Kathe” (1986), “Thaayi Saheba” (1997), “Dweepa” (2002) and “Gulabi Talkies” (2008), Kasaravalli has kept returning to one question: how societies exclude individuals and communities, and how people who are themselves victims of oppressive structures can also become complicit in perpetuating them.

Asked whether the 1977 Kannada classic “Ghatashraddha,” which examines religious orthodoxy and social exclusion, still spoke to contemporary India, Kasaravalli said: “All my films talk about othering. I always talk about how society looks at some people from a different lens, and in that process they make them ‘other.'” He described this as “the biggest cruelty an individual or a group of people can impart on another person.”

Asked whether he considered himself a political filmmaker, Kasaravalli said: “Whoever makes something, whether he makes mainstream cinema or art cinema, he is making a political statement, either in support of the dominant ideology or opposing the dominant ideology. He cannot avoid it.” There is nothing called only entertainment, he added, and even entertainment is a political form.

On his characters, he said: “I don’t want any of my characters to become a kind of a mouthpiece to a certain ideology.” Drawing on Jean-Luc Godard, he compared understanding a character to buying a house: one must examine it from the outside and then from within before making a judgment. Similarly, a filmmaker must inhabit a character and then step back to assess it. For Kasaravalli, characters are too complex for a filmmaker to easily take sides.

On the evolution of his cinematic language, Kasaravalli described the visual construction of “Ghatashraddha,” as “ritualistic cinema.” “The style is not imposed on it; it comes from within the content itself,” he said. Since “Ghatashraddha” was about a ritual, he felt its cinematic form should reflect that structure.

Kasaravalli said he could not write a script until he had clarity about its images. “For me, the film is not merely making statements; it’s an attempt by the filmmaker to impart an experience.” He described writing detailed instructions for his crew, down to which hand a character uses to lift a glass, because even that changes the composition of a shot.

This attention to the relationship between character and environment shaped “Gulabi Talkies.” Kasaravalli told cinematographer S. Ramachandra that the coastal community had to be as important as the protagonist. He wanted the images to feel like “actuality coverage,” not a predetermined visual construction. Rejecting conventional scenic shots of the sea and rocks, he told his cinematographer: “Film should not have any pretty image.” They chose 16mm, allowing the grain to contribute to the film’s sense of actuality.

The discussion of visual aesthetics brought him to Cuban filmmaker Julio García Espinosa’s idea of “imperfect cinema,” which, for Kasaravalli, challenged the assumption that Western aesthetics should define cinematic perfection. “I don’t want to take your aesthetic sensibility as my sensibility,” he said, quoting Espinosa. Kasaravalli said each country and each region has its own aesthetic concerns. “We should make films according to that condition; we should never imitate the West. Why should I be perfect? I should be loyal to my own milieu.”

Asked whether parallel cinema belonged to a particular period, Kasaravalli disagreed, pointing to notable films being made across the country today. The larger problem, he said, was reaching audiences. Of streaming platforms, he said: “We all thought it is going to be a solution for all of us. But OTT [streaming] is exactly doing what the mainstream distribution network was doing earlier.”

In the audience Q&A, he addressed open endings and whether a filmmaker should provide answers. “A filmmaker who says that he knows answers for all the questions in life is telling a lie,” he said. “He is also as confused as you are. He only throws up options so that you can start thinking. There is nothing called closure… life is always open.”

As the session concluded, IFFI festival director Ashutosh Gowariker described Kasaravalli’s work as “textbooks,” saying he had returned to “Ghatashraddha” while developing a new film. Why The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping Is The Darkest Movie In The Series

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The following post contains spoilers for "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping."

It's rare for a franchise to get better after 4 or 5 movies, especially when they are prequels that explore questions no one needed answered. It didn't work for "Star Wars," it didn't work for "Harry Potter," and it shouldn't work for "Hunger Games." At best, these prequels just introduced characters that don't matter in the large scheme of things because everything they do or accomplish gets erased in the original story, at worst they try so hard to answer questions no one cares about that it ends up tarnishing the original work.

And yet, when it comes to "Hunger Games," not only has the franchise improved with time, but the two spin-off prequels are arguably better than even the original series. The latest film, "Sunrise on the Reaping" deepens what came before, while introducing new characters that are as engaging as those we know and add to the larger worldbuilding of Panem. Or, as /Film's review puts it, "Beyond the shock value, this story remains human, emotional, and gripping from beginning to end."

Even if this movie doesn't have the compelling romance that was central to "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes," it delivers a harrowing tale of rebellion. What's more, this is somehow the darkest movie in the "Hunger Games" series, and it's not even close. Not just in terms of deaths — though there are some truly brutal deaths in the movie (which are somehow tamer than the book!) — but also in the overall tone the movie explores. This is the "Hunger Games" equivalent of "Andor" or "Rogue One," a bleak tragedy about one man who dared stand up against a tyrannical government and paid for it with everything he had.

The "Hunger Games" movies are full of death and suffering; it's part of the game. But "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes it to another level. Though it takes a long while before the nearly 3-hour runtime gets to the actual games, there's a feeling of hopelessness from the first moment. Early on, a kid gets shot for trying to escape the Reaping. Later, on the day of the opening ceremony, the youngest tribute from District 12, Loella, gets brutally killed in a chariot accident.

But it's not just the fact that there are plenty of deaths, or that they're horrible to watch. Sure, seeing poor Ampert get completely mauled by a swarm of cute forest animals and Maysilee Donner getting gored to death by birds is harrowing. The Capitol doesn't just kill children; it tortures them and pulls them apart and makes their parents watch.

Indeed, more than the deaths themselves, it's the meanness around them that makes this movie stand out. When Loella dies, it isn't a murder done by another tribute trying to get an advantage. Instead, it's an accident caused by Capitol audiences throwing flares at the parade circuit knowing full well it will cause the chariot horses to go berserk, because they want to see the tributes suffer. Compared to every other "Hunger Games" movie, the tributes are not only livestock sent to slaughter, or pawns in a political game. Here, they are subjects of absolute torture, playthings to be slowly toyed with and brutalized before getting discarded for the pleasure of others.

We especially see this in the presence of Ampert, whom we learn is only in the games as a punishment for his father Beetee's rebellious activities. Beetee is forced to mentor his own son and then watch him die.

Throughout "Sunrise on the Reaping," there is a sense of hopelessness and inevitability. We know that, eventually, the Hunger Games end and the Capitol falls — but the characters in the movie don't know that. At every turn, Haymitch and his allies see their every effort get squashed and mocked. Their attempts at leveling the playfield just a little bit are met with overwhelming force. Even before the games begin, Haymitch knows he'll never get to go back home because President Snow has personally seen to it that Haymitch dies for his insubordination during the opening ceremony.

Then we get to the third act, where the movie just becomes a cruel feel-bad experience, as Plutarch Heavensbee convinces Snow to let Haymitch survive to prolong his suffering. Haymitch is forced to watch Maysilee die brutally at the hands of waterbird mutts while he is virtually unscathed. Then, even after he wins the games, Haymitch discovers that the televised games were heavily edited to make him look selfish and cowardly, a traitor to every one of his allies, who won only because he was lucky. As if that wasn't enough, Haymitch has to see his family and his girlfriend die horrible deaths after he goes back home from the death games, in what's without a doubt the most nihilistic ending in the franchise.

It makes sense, however. "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes place at a pivotal moment in the "Hunger Games" timeline, at the height of President Snow's power. We are far removed from the games being almost canceled due to lack of interest like in the previous movie, but also far removed from the simmering rebellion of the original movies. Here, Snow gets away with everything, and he relishes in the cruelty of his games.
