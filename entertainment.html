Patrick Stewart Made Anthony Mackie ‘Freak Out’ on Avengers: Doomsday Set

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Patrick Stewart died on Oct. 28, meaning that his performance as Professor Charles Xavier in Avengers: Doomsday will be the last of his long career. Stewart first played the telepathic mutant in Bryan Singer’s X-Men in 2000, leading the superhero team in the fight against Ian McKellen’s Magneto. The two actors had met decades before at the Royal Shakespeare Company, but they became close friends after working together on the X-Men films. The actors reunited for Avengers: Doomsday, and Anthony Mackie said he and his The Falcon and the Winter Soldier co-star Sebastian Stan were so excited about it that they embarrassed themselves.

“The day before [Stewart] passed, I was talking to Sebastian for some reason – I talk to Sebastian Stan a lot – and we were laughing about when we were on set,” Mackie told Polygon during a video interview. “Sir Patrick and Sir Ian come on set, and they hug, and they just stand there and talk in full costume. We start to freak out, like, 'Oh shit! Yo, this is amazing, right?' They look at us. They give us this quizzical look like, ‘What's wrong with you two?’ and they walk off.”

Despite the awkward first impression, Mackie said Stewart was great to work with.

“He was so kind, and he was just so easy to be around,” Mackie said. “He was very personable. At that level, at that stage in his career, I just didn't know what to expect. I was very happy and very honored that we had the opportunity to work with him.”

Mackie has been part of the Marvel Cinematic Universe since 2014, playing Sam Wilson as partner to Chris Evans’ Captain America. Wilson then took on the mantle himself after Avengers: Endgame, officially donning the Captain America suit in The Falcon and the Winter Soldier. McKellen played one of Marvel’s most iconic villains, but Mackie discovered on set that he doesn’t keep up with the franchise.

“I go to Ian McKellen, he goes, ‘Hello, son.’ I’m like, ‘Ian McKellen, sir. You’re amazing. It’s amazing to work with you,’” Mackie told IMBb. “He goes, ‘So what are you doing in the film?’ and I’m like, ‘I’m Captain America.’ And he goes, ‘Wonderful... and you deserve that.’”

Amazon has greenlit a new period romance piece for TV.

The Gilded Age meets Bridgerton in a new period romance drama from Amazon, who've revealed new details about the upcoming show. TV period dramas have become popular across various streaming platforms in recent years. From the romantic overtones of Bridgerton to the dramatic tension in The Gilded Age, period dramas have plenty to say across various settings. Now, Amazon has confirmed that it will be developing a new period romance drama titled The American Heiress for MGM+. The new series stars Emmy-winning actor Julianna Margulies, pulled from the pages of Daisy Goodwin's bestselling novel of the same name. The eight-episode series sees Magulies take on the role of Isabella Cash. The story focuses on her daughter, Cora Cash, a young princess brought to England against her will despite being in love with American artist Teddy. However, upon visiting the 12th Duke of Wareham's castle, things begin to change.

The plot synopsis says that Cora is the main protagonist, focusing on her interactions with her new, English environment as she wrestles with what she truly wants in life. In the meantime, Isabella finds a challenge in the Double Duchess, a woman who has connections to many different dukes. The story will see the pair adjusting to their new life, all while dealing with the expected challenges women in the 1890s faced.

The American Heiress is a seductive mix of captivating characters, old world class politics, and romantic ambition. Having Julianna Margulies lead this amazing cast with her unique strength, intelligence and charm is ideal. We’re thrilled to work with Daisy, Stacey, and the MGM+ team on this deeply compelling story of love warped by power, which we look forward to bringing to life for audiences around the world. Following the success of projects such as American Classic and The Westies, The American Heiress marks an exciting milestone in the continued growth of our partnership with MGM+.

Actually, Robert Pattinson Would Make a Perfect James Bond

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

My colleagues and editors are sick of hearing me say it, but I believe it with my whole chest: The actor playing James Bond should never be more famous than James Bond. There’s something about Bond—his cultural footprint, and the dangerous yet seductive life he leads in his stories—that makes him too big to be a footnote in a great actor’s resume. James Bond creates leading men; James Bond is not for leading men to create.

So you have to know why it’s not easy for me to say this either: Robert Pattinson would be fantastic, and exactly the new James Bond we’re looking for.

You’ve probably heard by now that Amazon, which acquired full creative control over all things Bond from longtime owners Eon Productions and the Broccoli family, has been searching for the next 007 in a movie to be directed by Denis Villeneuve.

What was once an arena of speculation has exploded into a circus, with different names being bandied about at once. Namely: Callum Turner, Josh O’Connor, and Jack Lowden. (There is obviously a specific type of white man they’re going for here.) Just when it seemed as if the new Bond would finally be unveiled, there came a rush of two really big names suddenly in the running. Among them is Pattinson, reported by The Hollywood Reporter on November 6, along with Nicholas Hoult. Pattinson secretly auditioned for the role during a break in filming for The Batman: Part II, which halted filming in October when Matt Reeves attended to a personal matter.

No shade to Hoult or any of the other actors, but Pattinson is now the most interesting candidate despite a whiff of overexposure this year. Between The Drama in the spring, The Odyssey in the summer, Primetime in the fall, and soon Dune: Part Three in the dead of winter, 2026 has been shaping up into a banner year for Pattinson. Not since Twilight has the man been so prolific on our screens.

But even before the year began, Pattinson had long ago outgrown his image of a tween heartthrob. He’s a prodigious talent capable of daring choices and executing on them in a way you can’t ever expect. (Who else would say "cock" like this?) His popular line from The Odyssey—“Somebody get these beggars out of here!”—didn’t become a meme on its own. Pattinson made “Beggars” sound like a slur, and in the same breath, his nervous blinking and quivering smile cemented Antinous as an unconfident weasel whose puffed-out chest was, in fact, hot air. Unlike previous Batmans, who often played the Caped Crusader like a knight in matte black armor, Pattinson’s interpretation was/is that of an antisocial weirdo everyone stares at like a freak. Which he is.

Like Batman, Bond has also been a role that people misunderstand, sometimes even by the men who play him. (It is not my biases talking here, I really do think Daniel Craig is the only actor to ever nail the nuances and pathology of the character.) Bond is stylish and can say the right thing at the right time, be it to enemies or lovers. But he’s not a dashing or noble hero. He’s a brute in Brunello Cucinelli. A thug with a $9,000 Omega. He literally kills for a living—and had to kill to get that right—and his attachment to his job is downright destabilizing.

Recall in Skyfall, after Judi Dench’s M orders Moneypenny to shoot, Bond copes with endless drinking, womanizing, and gambling. That might sound like a fun weekend in Vegas, but in the context of Bond, his fallout from discovering his expendability led him to a really, really dark path. It is not normal to drink shots with a scorpion on your hand, nor is it healthy to be up in the morning reaching for booze from behind the bar. Bond might live a life many of us men dream of, but it comes at a severe cost. I don’t know what kind of Bond movie Villeneuve has in mind, but if the creative team is lining up like this, we’re in for something special.

You can’t exactly guesstimate what kind of 007 Pattinson might be based on his previous roles. There is truly little common ground between Batman and Bond. But one thing is for sure: if Pattinson gets the part, we are in for a goddamn show.

Rihanna Is Committed to This It Bag From the 2000s

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Fashion’s nostalgia craze isn’t going anywhere. These days, It girls from Bella Hadid to Anya Taylor-Joy, Hailey Bieber, Blake Lively, and Kim Kardashian are pulling from the archives to deliver striking ensembles that look to fashion eras of the past. It’s a little trick our favorite funky dresser, Rihanna, knows plenty about.

The Fenty Beauty founder has a special appreciation for the ’90s and early aughts. She’s shown us this through her wardrobe for years—what with her tracksuit jackets, sporty jumpsuits, and most importantly, her handbags.

Rihanna has stepped out carrying everything from the timeless Lady Dior of the 1990s to the Anaconda bag from Tom Ford’s Spring 1996 collection for Gucci. But she is especially drawn to a good ol’ Louis Vuitton monogram.

In the past, we’ve seen the “Umbrella” singer carry the colorful LV x Takashi Murakami Monogram Speedy 30, the Speedy Nano in blue denim with a white monogram, and the Speedy Soft 30 Boho in the classic brown-and-tan pattern. Yet the most fabulous piece in her LV collection may be her Speedy P9 25, which she carried out this week, and which debuted in the Menswear Spring 2024 show—Pharrell Williams’s first collection as men’s creative director. In this style, the Speedy’s iconic monogram and colors remain traditional, but the leather is crocodile. (The design is one of the most luxurious from Williams’s Speedy P9 series.)

Now, some backstory. Louis Vuitton introduced its monogram canvas in 1896 and expanded it into travel and everyday bags—including the Keepall and its smaller sister, the Speedy (originally called the “Express”)—in 1930. While the Speedy debuted in linen with leather handles, it was reintroduced in 1959 in the monogram coated canvas we know today. Some years later, in 1965, the one and only Audrey Hepburn requested a smaller, sleeker version, which is how the Speedy 25 was born.

The house’s creative directors have since given new life to the style, while fashion muses, like Rihanna, continue to prove its icon status.

Avengers Endgame: Encore Officially Gets Disney Plus Streaming Date

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

With Avengers: Doomsday roughly a month away, Disney just delivered some good news for MCU fans who want to be fully caught up before Doctor Doom blows through the Marvel pantheon. Avengers Endgame: Encore is coming to Disney Plus very soon.

For those not in the know, Encore is a recut version of Endgame that adds scenes tying into Doomsday. The film was released in theaters on Sept. 25, grossing $142.9 million and making Avengers: Endgame the highest-grossing film of all time at $2.942 billion. Avatar and Endgame have been trading the box office crown for some time now: Endgame’s original 2019 release made it the highest-grossing film over Avatar, before a 2021 Avatar re-release let Cameron's sci-fi epic temporarily retake the No. 1 spot. Marvel fans who missed Encore in theaters can see the film on Disney Plus on Nov. 13.

As for what’s changed in Encore, the film has four extra scenes that add four minutes to theruntime. For those who don’t mind spoilers, here’s an outline of what’s different. Encore adds an extended ending, two scenes that play mid-credits, and another sequence at the end of the credits.

In the extended ending, Steve Rogers and Peggy Carter are visited by a version of Loki who promises to help them — this is likely why Captain America will appear in Doomsday, despite seemingly livinga happy life with Peggy in the past. As for the mid-credits scenes, one shows Bruce Banner self-isolating in a military complex due to an “incident” (presumably referring to the events of Spider-Man: Brand New Day). As Banner experiments with a way to suppress the gamma radiation in his body, Doctor Doom teleports him out of his cell.

In the second mid-credits scene, a bunch of vaguely confusing multiverse stuff happens that’s built on the TV series Loki. Specifically, Time Variance Authority employees Ouroboros, Casey, and Mobius M. Mobius see that the alternate universes Earth-10005 and Earth-1127 are about to collide. More overlaps are detected, revealing that the multiverse is collapsing. In the final post-credits scene, the sound of metal being hammered during the credits is revealed to be Doctor Doom forging his mask.

These scenes were added to segue more smoothly into Avengers: Doomsday, which didn’t have the same degree of buildup as previous Avengers films. While several films leading up to Avengers: Infinity War and Endgame hyped Thanos as an antagonist and teased the Infinity Stones, Doomsday came together far more hastily.

In 2022, Disney announced that the next Avengers movie would be subtitled The Kang Dynasty. However, this concept was scrapped after Jonathan Majors, who played Kang, was found guilty of assault and harassment charges. Marvel fully pivoted in 2024, revealing that the next Avengers film would instead be centered around Doctor Doom.

On Thursday Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner will travel to Moscow and Dublin to hold talks with the Russian and Ukrainian presidents

Trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi between Russia, Ukraine and the US aimed at ending the war in Ukraine have concluded after a second day.

Although the American side said they saw "some openings", there was no hint of any breakthroughs to end the nearly five year-long war sparked by Russia's invasion.

Davyd Arakhamia from Ukraine's negotiating team said that this round of talks - the first since February - "was one of the hardest."

"I believe the Russians came with very strict directives, so it was extremely difficult to soften the discussion," he said. But he added that he felt "good momentum" and urged for a second round of talks to be organised as soon as possible.

Both Moscow and Kyiv confirmed that a large-scale prisoner of war exchange was in the works and could be carried out soon.

After the talks it was announced that on Thursday US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner - President Donald Trump's son-in-law - would travel to Moscow to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin, and then to Dublin to speak to Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky.

This latest round of talks had been framed as centred around the "security architecture" of Europe.

For Kyiv, this may include reassurances that allies will come to its aid if Ukraine comes under attack again, despite the country not being a Nato member.

For Moscow, it is likely to include a limit to the influence of Nato in eastern Europe - a central concern for the Kremlin since the alliance's eastward expansion.

Earlier this week Kushner said the US hoped to "create a new framework" of security between Ukraine, Russia and Europe that could "secure lasting peace", while Kremlin negotiator Vladimir Medinsky said the world needed a "new" and "fair" security system.

Ukraine and Europe have long been concerned about the current US administration's commitment to European security and its softening towards the Kremlin.

Representatives of Britain, Nato and various EU countries were also in Abu Dhabi and met with US and Ukrainian negotiators, but the Kremlin - with whom the EU does not officially communicate - said its delegation had not had any contact with them.

Moscow has been dismissive of European involvement, although Europeans have long argued that they are central to any post-war dynamics on their continent.

Many - particularly in eastern Europe - feel their security is linked to that of Ukraine. Agreements cannot be implemented "if no consensus can be reached among Nato members," a German government spokesperson said on Wednesday.

Kushner said the US was keen to bring the Europeans on board and said that getting them "added to the format" would be on the agenda for his talks with Putin.

There is also disagreement on whether discussions on a future security framework should be preceded by a ceasefire, as the Europeans and Kyiv would want, or whether the fighting only stops after an agreement has been reached, as the Kremlin has insisted.

Any agreement on a peacetime security framework, however, is contingent on the war ending, Kushner noted. But that cannot occur "until there's an agreement on territory and on all other bilateral issues," he added.

Since talks began last year, a core obstacle to any settlement has been Ukraine's eastern region of Donetsk, which is mostly occupied by Russia. Moscow has demanded that Ukraine relinquish the parts of Donetsk it still holds, while Kyiv wants to freeze the contact line to maintain control over those areas.

This matter was the "hardest", Kushner acknowledged, while Zelensky's chief of staff and negotiator Kyrylo Budanov refused to be drawn on whether Russia was still demanding a Ukrainian withdrawal from the region.

"These deals are very hard and complex, and this one is very emotional, very complex," Kushner said. "The reality is there is no trust right now between the sides and that is to be expected after a conflict like this."

Reacher’s moments of vicious gore and jaw-dropping violence ground the show in something resembling recognizable reality, meaning the title character’s adventures always have tangible stakes, even if he is seemingly indestructible. The actor recalled that in that moment, he came to the realization that “you will do anything to avoid getting killed.”

At another point in the interview, O’Brien asked the Los Angeles native to detail his move to New York. Hoffman relocated across the country in 1958 at 21, hoping to pursue a career in acting.

As O’Brien put it, Hoffman worked “a lot of absurd odd jobs” at this point in his life, including a role at Macy’s, where he was tasked with selling toys—like foosball and table hockey—to adults.

As an aspiring actor, Hoffman put his own spin on the job, and decided to embody a character of his own making; a retired, injured French hockey player.

“Gene Hackman was right there at adult games, also,” Hoffman said, noting that the fellow actor was his coworker at the time. Decades later, the pair would go on to work together again, starring in the 2003 film, “Runaway Jury.”

Hackman and Hoffman weren’t just coincidental coworkers. They lived together, too.

“You go home to your roommates, and they are Gene Hackman and Robert Duvall. The three of you, I’m picturing a bunk bed with three levels. And you guys are snoring in unison like ‘The Three Stooges.’ That’s what I’m picturing,” joked O’Brien.

Hoffman said that the three men, who eventually each became very famous actors, “didn’t live together quite like that.”

Although he seems to look back on the experience with a sense of humor now, his memoir tells a different story. In an excerpt from the book, Hoffman wrote that he “was so frightened of New York.”

“I didn’t want to go outside. I’d been excited to get there, but once I arrived it scared the hell out of me,” he said. “All these thousands of people who knew what they were doing, whereas I was completely in the dark. It was intimidating.”

At one point, Hoffman lived with Hackman and his first wife, Faye Maltese, in “a little apartment on the East Side.”

“I slept on the kitchen floor next to their refrigerator, which shook and made a lot of noise, like it was having a heart attack once or twice a night,” Hoffman recalled.

Hoffman had originally only planned to stay with Hackman and his wife for a few days, but the arrangement lasted three weeks before “the Hackmans ran out of patience and told me it was time to move on.”

Hackman advised Hoffman to, “go up Riverside Drive and knock on the doors and ask to speak to the superintendent,” to find a new place to live. The actor recalled starting at 60th Street and Central Park West, and knocking on “every door of every building all the way to the Hudson River and back again” until he eventually found a place on 109th street.

He rented a top-floor apartment in a building in Spanish Harlem for $80 a month, and knew he’d “need roommates to afford it,” which is where Duvall came in.

“We had a lot of rowdy nights in that apartment: pizza, unemployed actors, candles in chianti bottles,” Hackman wrote. “I would play the piano. Duvall would sing country music songs. If someone had pointed at Bob and Gene and me at one of our parties and said, ‘Those three are going to be movie stars,” everyone in the apartment would have laughed.”

Even after Hoffman got his first big break, it was a while before he’d find his footing financially. During the podcast interview, he said he was paid $3,000 for his role in "The Graduate," which was released in 1967. But after the film’s premiere, he still needed more funds.

“You didn’t have any money, and so you went back to the unemployment office and someone at Life magazine took a photo of you,” O’Brien said. “The most famous face in America at the time, waiting in the unemployment line to get a check.”

Hoffman confirmed that the story was true, adding that the check was for about $50.

“$55, I think was the most you could get,” he continued, quipping that at that time, people “could live on” about “$200 a month.”

‘Here There Are Blueberries’ Broadway Review: Evil As Banal As A Bowl Of Fruit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Here There Are Blueberries, the exceptional Pulitzer finalist docu-drama that opened Off Broadway two years ago to great admiration, finally makes its Broadway debut tonight at the Barrymore Theatre. The play, about a mysterious real-life cache of one-of-a-kind Auschwitz photos submitted to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in 2007, remains as intriguing as ever – and with source material as grotesquely mesmerizing as these candid snapshots, how could it not?

In fact, the sheer emotional heft of that photo album and everything it suggests dominates Blueberries to an extent that the play itself can seem rather thin at times, not entirely unworthy of the material at hand but not on equal footing either. Revelations about the banality of evil carry a force no matter how familiar we are with the concept, but familiar we are, and Blueberries can feel somewhat naive at times in its presumption that no one in the audience has ever heard of Hannah Arendt.

But in the grand scheme of things, and in light of the historical tale being told here, those quibbles with dramaturgy shouldn’t diminish the real achievement that is Here There Are Blueberries. The play is vital and more often than not riveting. Watch on Deadline

Performed by an excellent cast – any production that features the great Kathleen Chalfant is an opportunity not to be missed – Here There Are Blueberries is, at its core, a detective tale. In 2007, a young Holocaust museum archivist named Rebecca Erbelding (Delia Cunningham) receives a letter at her D.C. office from a man who wants to donate a cache of WWII snapshots.

The offer isn’t unusual – the museum serves as a repository for such keepsakes – but the nature of his collection most certainly is. The photo collection long in the man’s possession is more than a rarity, it’s absolutely unique: The images are of Nazi officers – including some of the highest ranking and most notorious, from Mengele to Höss – in their living quarters just over the wall from the camp’s atrocities. Some are of an almost surreal vacation lodge a few miles away, where officers, SS guards and female office workers alike would be treated to days of rest and relaxation for jobs well done. One officer is given five days there for his murder of two Jews attempting to escape the camp.

Such images had never been observed in photographs before, and their discovery is nothing less than historic, generating headlines across the globe, with much of the public fascination falling on the young German female camp office workers enjoying their vacation lodge days and food-and-drink treats. “Here,” says the caption (in German) of one such photo, “there are blueberries.”

The play is structured as an investigation, lead by the young archivist, as the museum staff seeks to uncover the circumstances under which these snapshots were taken, who took them, who is pictured, and, perhaps most intriguingly, how on earth did they survive when all over such evidence was destroyed by the Nazis themselves as the regime fell?

One such question – how did the man who possesses the photos discover them? – is easily answered: A retired U.S. Army Lieutenant Colonel (Michael Cerveris) found the album in the abandoned apartment he briefly inhabited while stationed in Frankfort at the end of the war. Why he kept them in his possession, and did so secretly, is unknowable – he died shortly after making the museum donation.

Other questions posed by the photos are of a more philosophical nature. Did those seemingly carefree young women at the lodge have any idea that the office work they were doing contributed to the genocide next door? And if so, how could they?

Blueberries, for all its demands on facing harsh truths, plays a bit coy in feigning shock at the answers, as if Judgment At Nuremberg never existed, an excusable but still noticeable manipulation. A bit more difficult to overlook are other red herrings seemingly designed to give the play momentum, such as will the museum board accept the donation of the Nazi photos when its mission is to chronicle the victims, not the victimizers? It’s a stick man argument.

Blueberries is on sturdier dramatic ground when new characters – the relatives of the Nazis and camp workers in the photographs – begin arriving mid-show to offer their perspectives. The sister of an office worker sticks with the usual denials, but others are revelatory, not least the grandson of Rudolph Höss, who relates an unnerving tale of his family’s complicity and his own struggles with the violence he fears is an inheritance.

The grandson’s tale is riveting, as is Brandon Flynn’s performance, a stand-out in a uniformly excellent cast that, in addition to those mention above, includes Conrad Ricamora, Scott Barrow, Michelle Beck and Montego Glover, all playing a variety of roles. Derek McLane’s stark set design, dominated by the many tall-legged stand-up desks used by the museum archivists to study the photos, is stellar, as are the crucial projections (designed by David Bengali) that allows us to see what the archivists are seeing. The photos projected onto the walls would be transfixing even without the superb lighting design (David Lander) and sound design (Bobby McElver) that further an entirely earned mood of dread and even despair, never more so than during a late scene that finally gives all those unseen victims their moment in this story, and in a manner so wrenching it won’t be spoiled here except to say that another photo album enters the story, and its story of survival is every bit as astonishing as the first.

In the end, Here There Are Blueberries has sailed past its own here-and-there shortcomings to land on a final thought, artfully told and with clarity of conviction: Just imagine how many untold boxes of forgotten family memorabilia and grim relics are waiting to be discovered, with secrets as efficiently hidden as those sealed away in the human heart?

I’m a 70-Year-Old Woman. So What's Ahead?

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

I turned 70 in October. My best friend, who is two months older than me, kept telling me it is okay and that once I decide how I’m going to mark the day, my life will get back on track. I knew she was right, but as the weeks approached, I started to think about what 70 meant for me. None of the other decades had hit me in this way.

Is this moment a reminder? An opportunity to think about how to make meaning and how I want to live my life from now? Do I want change?

I wondered why I was reflecting on this, anyway. I figured that by thinking about it intentionally (that new overused word that I love) it must mean that there is some sort of change happening. An awareness of another passing.

I'm taking emotional stock of where I am.

I have a full life with my husband of 48 years, and we are both healthy. Big tick.

I have three children who are all partnered, settled, and have worthwhile creative lives. I have five beautiful grandchildren, from 15 to almost 3, and another due next year. They light up my life, and I love spending time with them. They are growing up quickly. Even the 3-year-old already has a full calendar.

I have friends and family in Australia and in the U.S.

So the question is, why would I want to change anything?

Then I realised that I was on the precipice of a new phase of letting go. My playbook, guided by the work of Edna Furman, to let go of my children at each stage holds, and I know at 70 I have yet a new bridge to cross. A vital one.

But what is this bridge? My children don't need me on a day-to-day basis; they are all separate, self-sufficient, and inhabit their own orbits.

Seventy is a recalibration. There is a sort of tussle with my own and my husband’s mortality. Not in a morbid way, but in a real way that I hadn’t thought about before.

It's a recognition that while we are okay now, a time will come when we go to the doctor and… I am constantly aware that many other people, including those much younger than us, must manage this reality daily.

I go back 12 years to when I single-handedly took my twin grandchildren to the park and chased them both as they went in different directions. I am very active, but could I do this now? I have trouble safely keeping up with a 3-year-old running down the street.

I need to reconsider how to find my new meaning. I turn to role models, those women who are the same stage as me. I find comfort once again in the words of my best friend, who has been a major part of my life for 58 years. She is also in the process of letting go of her children; we have talked about it incessantly for the last 10 years. While she has three daughters that she is very close to, and is available when really needed, she made the intentional decision not to be “the sleepover Nanna." Her artwork nourishes her, as does her time with her family. She refuses to rush, and she has found peace. Most of the time.

I think about how this can apply to me. I know I’d like to read more because it feeds my soul. I have a particular volunteer programme that I am going to embark on.

And I want to continue my work on letting go, mothering, and maybe some literature and film. I’m not sure yet.

The reality is my disquiet will lead me somewhere. It always does.

As always, I go to psychological theory to help me.

The words of Edna Furman come rushing back into my mind. They have guided me to let go, that others are "there to be left." I am faced with yet another new adjustment. The post I wrote last April needs an update.

Then I go to respected psychoanalyst Erik Erikson, whose developmental life stages offer guidance for psychosocial growth. Traveling one light-day took the space probe 49 years. it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft is on track to set another historic record as its mission approaches the half-century mark. On Nov. 18 at 5:16 a.m. ET, the probe will reach a distance of one light-day from Earth, a journey of nearly five decades that places it farther from our planet than any human-made object in history.

It’s quite an accomplishment for a spacecraft built in the mid-1970s One light-day is the distance that light travels in a single day at the speed of light, which is approximately 186,282 miles per second. That translates to 25.9 billion kilometers or about 16.1 billion miles in a single day. At an average human walking speed of 3 mph, it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

Fortunately, Voyager 1 has been traveling a bit faster than that. It currently zips along the cosmos at a steady 37,763 mph, a speed it achieved in 1980 after a gravity-assisted Saturn flyby. It has been flying at that speed ever since.

The milestone comes 14 years after its previous one, when Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the point where the sun’s heliosphere ends, and entered interstellar space. It’s also 28 years after passing Pioneer 10 to become the human-made object that has traveled the farthest into space.

The historic milestone isn’t without its drama. Voyager 1 has been in space for nearly half a century, and in that time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done everything it can to extend the life of Voyager 1. That includes troubleshooting issues, performing maintenance and shutting down various systems to save power and extend the spacecraft’s life-span.

Several of those efforts took place in 2026. The team running Voyager 1 spent weeks shutting down three devices and powering on three low-power alternatives to reduce overall power usage. This initiative, which was internally called The Big Bang, was also performed on Voyager 2. The transition was completed in early September, giving Voyager 1 more time with its dwindling power supply while also keeping it warm enough to continue operating in the frigid conditions of interstellar space.

It was a tricky maneuver that required the team to simultaneously turn multiple instruments on and off. The difficulty of the maneuver was amplified by the fact that it took almost a full day for any signal to reach Voyager and another full day for Voyager to send a signal back, with hours of downtime while the spacecraft performed the operations.

All of this took place several months after Voyager 1 experienced a sudden drop in power, which necessitated shutting off one of its three remaining instruments still in operation. Voyager 1 now has only two instruments still in operation.

Making it a whole light-day away from Earth is likely going to be Voyager 1’s final milestone while it still has enough power to communicate with Earth. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They are essentially nuclear-powered batteries that generate energy by converting the steady heat from decaying plutonium-238.

When Voyager 1 first launched, its radioisotope thermoelectric generators produced about 470 watts of power. But plutonium-238 doesn’t last forever, and after 50 years in space, the RTGs now produce about 215 watts of power, with an average loss of about 4 watts per year. Most of that power is used to keep Voyager 1 up and running, which is why NASA has shut down eight of the 10 instruments that Voyager had when it launched.

At its current rate of usage and decay, Voyager 1 is expected to shut down its final science instrument in the 2030s and will eventually lose communication with Earth in 2036 due to insufficient power to send back a radio signal.

The same fate is expected for Voyager 2, Voyager 1’s twin, which has also spent half a century in space. While it is labeled the sequel, Voyager 2 actually launched 16 days before Voyager 1, and the two have spent nearly identical amounts of time traversing the cosmos. Thanks to its slower speed and trajectory, it likely won’t reach the one light-day distance until after its instruments shut down.

Thanks to the vastness of space, Voyager 1 will lose all of its power before it hits its next milestone, which is the beginning of the Oort Cloud, a vast, icy shell of comets at the edge of our solar system. It’ll take about 300 years for Voyager 1 to make it there.

Thanks to the near-perfect vacuum of outer space, Voyager 1 will continue to fly at its current speed even after it shuts down entirely. So it’ll still hit that milestone; it just won’t be able to phone home to tell us about it.

Remembering Sebastián Lelio, an Auteur Who Centered Women’s Stories With Style and Sensitivity

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Chilean filmmaker, who passed away on Friday aged 52, leaves behind a diverse but cohesive body of work including 'Gloria,' 'The Wonder' and the Oscar-winning 'A Fantastic Woman.'

When it was announced, in 2017, that the Chilean writer-director Sébastian Lelio was to helm a U.S. remake of his 2013 breakout film “Gloria,” admirers of the original — and indeed of Lelio himself — had cause for concern. English-language do-overs of world cinema successes frequently go awry; furthermore, we had the cautionary tale of Dutchman George Sluizer, who directed a hopeless 1993 remake of his own impeccable 1988 chiller “The Vanishing,” to remind us that even having the original filmmaker in charge was no guarantee against a travesty. The news came at a crucial moment in Lelio’s career. The first of his two films to premiere in 2017, the vibrant, heartfelt transgender character study “A Fantastic Woman,” was riding a wave of festival and critical success that began with a Best Screenplay win at the Berlinale, and culminated with an Oscar for Best International Feature (or Best Foreign Language Film, as the award was still called then) — the first ever win for a Chilean film in the category, sealing a resurgence of the country’s cinema also being led by such contemporaries as Sebastián Silva and Lelio’s own producer Pablo Larraín.

The second, “Disobedience,” was Lelio’s English-language debut — another story of queer identity, but an altogether different tonal and stylistic proposition. A sober portrayal of an illicit romance between two women from North London’s strict Orthodox Jewish community, it was sensitively observed and beautifully acted by leads Rachel Weisz and Rachel McAdams, with a restraint that was occasionally countered by moments of frank, charged sensuality. The contrast between “A Fantastic Woman” and “Disobedience” confirmed Lelio as a particularly empathetic and versatile teller of women’s stories — and “Gloria,” a warm and unsentimentally joyous comedy of a fiftysomething divorcée’s reawakening, was also good one. But why go back, when his career was barreling forward?

As it turned out, Lelio knew better than his doubters. The remake, which eventually premiered in 2019 as “Gloria Bell,” hewed close to the template of the original, largely inheriting its pleasures and poignancies. But if that makes it sound redundant, it proved a canny and fascinating exercise in performer-as-auteur influence: It was clear that Lelio, self-evidently a champion of women on screen, was interested to see how a wholly different actress would change the tone and temperature of his original story.

A lot, we learned. Paulina García was a funny and resilient force of nature in the 2013 original; in “Gloria Bell,” Julianne Moore brought a yearning, lonely fragility to the material that gave even the film’s uplifting trajectory an undertow of melancholy. The remake got less than its due — Moore, at the very least, deserved some awards attention that year — but it sits beside “Gloria” as an illuminating companion piece rather than a competing interpretation.

I lead with the two Glorias because their respective successes improbably prove a truth about Lelio, a restlessly curious and compassionate filmmaker who passed away, far too soon, on Friday, aged just 52: Even when ostensibly making the same film twice, he never made the same film twice.

His 2005 debut “The Sacred Family” was confident and a bit scrappy, its handheld jaggedness far removed from his later polish, its droll comic tale of a bourgeois man falling for his son’s girlfriend also distinct from the searching feminist portraiture that would define his later career. But it was lively and promising, made in a semi-improvised fashion that he carried forward into his next, more complex film, the troubled youth study “Navidad,” which made its way into the 2009 Cannes Directors’ Fortnight program. But his third film, 2011’s “The Year of the Tiger,” was a considerably more sophisticated effort: an exquisitely understated exploration of faith, guilt and trauma in the wake of Chile’s devastating 2010 earthquake.

“The Year of the Tiger,” which competed at Locarno, was too soulful and richly considered a work on its own terms to be seen as a bid for arthouse-major status — but that’s how it worked out anyway, even if the film didn’t get quite the global exposure it deserved. But by the time “Gloria” came along two years later, the word on Lelio was out and expectations were high: The film was selected for the main competition at Berlin and was an immediate, ebulliently received crowdpleaser. If Larraín had established himself as the dark prince of new Chilean cinema with spiky, perverse, politically bristling works like “Post Mortem” and “No,” Lelio was poised to become its great humanist: his touch light but not soft, his perspective probing but not abrasive.

“A Fantastic Woman,” four years later, delivered on that promise. Propelled by trans star Daniela Vega’s extraordinary, emotionally unguarded performance, its story of a grieving trans woman spurned by the prejudiced family of her dead lover arrived at a vital tipping point for the visibility of transgender narratives and artists in popular culture, and delicately essayed an arc of emphatic, even triumphant self-definition amid a climate of isolation and rejection — all without feeling glibly or opportunistically issue-driven. It was to Lelio’s credit that he was fairly self-effacing in his promotion of the film, letting Vega — with whom he had closely developed the protagonist, aligned with her experience — be its chief ambassador and spokesperson.

“A Fantastic Woman” earned Lelio the most acclaim and kudos of his career — though his finest film as a director, to my mind, was comparatively shortchanged. A period drama set in 19th-century Ireland and adapted from a novel by “Room” author Emma Donoghue, “The Wonder” didn’t outwardly look or feel much like a Lelio joint — with its austerely repressed milieu shot, by Ari Wegner, with such a damp, chilly verdigris finish you could practically sense moss growing on the screen as you watched. Yet its story of feminine resolve and dueling moral and spiritual convictions meshed well with the director’s interests, as did Florence Pugh’s tenacious, stout-hearted performance; the film’s earthy folktale trappings stood in exciting contrast to its stark formalist construction.

If “The Wonder” was an ambitious and grandly realized reach, I was less sold on the huge stylistic swing of Lelio’s most recent film, last year’s political musical “The Wave,” which returned him to his homeland. Inspired by a national spate of #MeToo-related feminist student protests that shut down universities across Chile in 2018, it was rhetorically heavy-handed but composed and choreographed with dynamic aplomb, positively thrilling to the spectacle of massed bodies in motion: I wrote in my review that it made one eager to see Lelio tackle more musical fare on screen, never thinking that the film would be his last.

In one of “The Wave’s” more playful flourishes, the fourth wall is broken to acknowledge Lelio’s own presence as the director of the movie — and to ask, pointedly, what a male director was doing on this expressly feminist project anyway. By now, it's no secret that Apple TV has a knack for releasing great science fiction; you can pretty much choose one at random, and it will still be a great choice. Saying one of them is the best feels even a little unfair to the others, so we have to raise the bar and look beyond the screen and individual taste, focusing instead on what kind of accomplishment it represents. All that considered, it's almost impossible not to choose Foundation, the adaptation of Isaac Asimov's seminal sci-fi saga. Before its release, these books were considered straight-up unadaptable, so, in many ways, the greatest achievement of Apple TV's Foundation isn't just being an incredible work of science fiction, but that it exists at all, really.

Between original series and adaptations, science fiction has had a boom in productions ever since the dawn of the streaming age. The one work every fan of the genre had pretty much accepted would never get such treatment was Foundation. Even after its announcement back in 2018, everyone only believed the series was actually real when it premiered via Apple TV in 2021. That's because no other work in sci-fi has a scope as huge as Foundation — it was just too much to believe it could ever be made into a TV series.

Set in a future where humankind has conquered the cosmos, the original book comprises many smaller tales depicting humanity's efforts to overcome a thousand years of dark ages. The single common thread is Hari Seldon (Jared Harris), founder of psychohistory, a field of study that uses probability and psychology to predict future events on a galactic scale. Seldon devised a plan for future human leaders to follow, thus reducing the dark ages from tens of thousands of years to a single millennium.

It's a fascinating story and an easy read, filled with equally interesting characters. However, most never interact due to their respective stories being set decades and even centuries apart. This is a major roadblock for any kind of visual adaptation, because focusing on just one of them would mean neglecting centuries of human history in that universe. It just couldn't be done, and those centuries of human history are the whole point of Foundation. Something had to drastically change for these novels to fit the TV framework.

When translating a work of literature into visual media, it's always necessary to make some changes, including, sometimes, some severe ones. Modern television usually demands strong protagonists with whom the audience can sympathize, so, when David S. Goyer and Josh Friedman started working on adapting Foundation, this was one of the very first things to tackle — that and a way to unify and streamline the original narrative, so it could fit the episodic and seasonal framework.

Instead of multiple separate storylines spread out across the timeline, the series brings them together with time jumps and some classic sci-fi narrative devices. Thus, Apple's Foundation takes the main characters in the early Asimov books and turns them into actual protagonists, each with their own complexities and development arcs. Instead of two or three small chapters, then, characters like Gaal Dornick (Lou Llobell), Salvor Hardin (Leah Harvey), and even Seldon himself have central and much larger roles.

Despite its scope, though, the books are quite simplistic in worldbuilding early on, as the Foundation itself is still struggling to consolidate. Sci-fi television demands something greater, though, so the Apple series has grander and more cinematic settings than the books. Every planet has a complex culture and background, and even things like Seldon's vault, where his recording reveals the next steps of his plan every few decades, become central plot devices tied to the protagonists, keeping the story straight and fluid.

There was no way to make Foundation work as a series if it was an exact match with its book counterpart, and a significant part of why it's so good is how it allows itself to go beyond Asimov's original writings. The best example is the Genetic Dynasty, an entirely new idea created for the series. In the books, the Galactic Empire's decay is what causes the dark ages, but it's the series that actually goes into why that is, since the original focuses mostly on the other side, the Foundation.

For that, the series introduces one of the most brilliant concepts in recent sci-fi: the Genetic Dynasty. It perfectly sums up the notion that the Empire is so stagnant, to the point where every emperor is a clone of the same man, Cleon I. Brothers Dawn (Cassian Bilton), Day (Lee Pace), and Dusk (Terrence Mann) are essentially different versions of the same person, each struggling to establish their own identity while living under the shadow of their predecessor. Another great example is Demerzel (Laura Birn), whose role is considerably expanded from the books, made in the Apple adaptation into a central figure in the Empire's political machinations, with a twist coming straight from the author's wider Robot universe.

These additions give Apple's Foundation an identity of its own without losing sight of the ideas that made the original novels so influential. Ultimately, that's what makes it such a great adaptation, focusing more on understanding and expanding on the potential of the original story rather than simply adapting it. Bringing Foundation to television was always going to require some creative liberties, but the series shows just how much can be achieved when an adaptation understands what makes its source material special.
