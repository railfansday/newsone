52 Years Later, One Of Sci-Fi's Most Legendary Space Operas Finally Gets Its Streaming Finale

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of pop culture's longest-running space operas celebrates the end of an era. The space opera genre is most famously represented by Star Wars, the movie-turned-franchise that overhauled sci-fi cinema with its revolutionary use of VFX and SFX before creating a massive multimedia empire that only continues to grow half a century later. Dune became a phenomenon of its own earlier than Star Wars, though it only met its full potential thirty-seven years after its first movie adaptation and fifty-six years after the first novel's publication. On television, Star Trek pioneered optimistic cosmic exploration and Battlestar Galactica brought survival-driven drama to extraterrestrial fleet warfare.

Tucked beneath those household names, dozens of underrated space operas still await the mainstream recognition they deserve. The Jim Henson Company's Farscape is a unique title in the genre due to its high-quality puppetry, and Babylon 5 popularized serialized five-year television storytelling long before the streaming era. In animation, Titan A.E., Crest of the Stars, and Outlaw Star delivered heartfelt starfaring adventures that often get overshadowed by bigger franchises. There are also countless literary gems like Vernor Vinge’s A Fire Upon the Deep, Lois McMaster Bujold’s Vorkosigan Saga, and Arkady Martine’s A Memory Called Empire.

Right alongside Star Wars and Star Trek, a fan-favorite space opera reaches a new milestone half a century after its first installment.

Fifty-two years after the original 1974 series, one of anime's most influential space operas has delivered its complete streaming finale with all twenty-six episodes of Star Blazers: Space Battleship Yamato 3199. The series' final chapter weaves together the 1980 theatrical feature Be Forever Yamato and the third classic television season, Space Battleship Yamato III, into a cohesive new timeline. Set in the year 2207, Star Blazers: Space Battleship Yamato 3199 follows Susumu Kodai and the scattered veterans of the Yamato as they reunite in secret after a colossal structure called the Grand Reverse breaches Earth's defenses and allows the enigmatic Dezarium army to occupy the capital.

Star Blazers: Space Battleship Yamato 3199 presents a striking blend of classic mechanical designs and mature military drama that respects decades of canon and keeps veteran fans guessing at every turn, plus sweeping orchestral music that makes the space opera feel even more majestic. The seventh and final chapter adds episodes 23 through 26 to the catalog after its October 2026 theatrical debut in Japan. With the entire twenty-six-episode arc all available at once, newcomers and longtime admirers alike can experience the complete saga without interruption after more than fourteen years of anticipation.

Star Blazers: Space Battleship Yamato 3199 brings classic space opera back to its roots with every classic space opera trope that originally inspired modern sci-fi icons like Star Wars and Battlestar Galactica. Throughout its seven chapters, 3199 delivers daring espionage behind enemy lines and thundering fleet battles led by captain Susumu Kodai, tactical ace Yuki Mori, veteran tactician Shiro Sanada, and the rapidly growing Sasha alongside the menacing Dezarium commander Alphon. Standout installments like the tense opening invasion in Chapter 1, the underground resistance intrigue of Chapter 3, and the breathtaking battles in Chapters 6 and 7 showcase breathtaking choreography and Akira Miyagawa's lush orchestral score.

If you love Star Trek, Captain Kirk’s ongoing series needs to be on your radar, especially if you’re a lore fanatic.

Now that all twenty-six episodes of 3199 have wrapped up the series, the future of the Space Battleship Yamato franchise looks remarkably bright as it enters its second half-century. Since launching the modern remake continuity with Yamato 2199 in 2012, studios like Xebec, Satelight, and Studio Mother have reimagined the classic 1970s and early-1980s saga to immense critical and commercial success. Given the warm reception to 3199 and chief director Harutoshi Fukui's clear passion for the material, a new installment could present a modern reimagining of the 1983 theatrical epic Final Yamato and its legendary Planet Aquarius crisis alongside the upcoming movie Cosmoship Yamato √2199.

‘The Odyssey’ Cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema Breaks Down Filming Climactic Thunderstorm Scene in Imax

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Christopher Nolan shot “The Odyssey” to be experienced on the biggest screen possible so audiences could feel the full impact and be fully immersed in that experience — so everything was shot practically.

One of the most challenging sequences to pull off was the film’s final storm scene as Matt Damon’s Odysseus faces the wrath of Poseidon and the full force of Zeus. It was to be climactic and intense, and Odysseus loses all his men as a result of the final insult to the gods. “We had been talking about going out there to sea, sit on real boats and shoot on Imax,” cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema explains.

A large amount of the film was shot on the open ocean, where Nolan, his cast and crew spent four months capturing how hard the journey would have been as they endured crashing waves. And while the climactic moment ended up being shot in Los Angeles, Nolan didn’t spare any expense in pulling off the sequence, relying instead on old-fashioned filmmaking.Speaking with Variety for Inside the Frame, Van Hoytema explains the scene provided a challenge: “How to replicate something and make it feel visceral and real? How can we do that and avoid a huge amount of CG?”Two decommissioned jet engines were used as wind machines. Van Hoytema’s chief lighting technician and gaffer Adam Chambers built light boxes that would emulate thunder and lightning. The boat was then put on a gimbal, all while being shot on an Imax camera. “It was like shooting a real storm, just under safety supervision,” Van Hoytema says.He adds, “We did a lot of different setups in that whole scene. It is designed to feel extremely chaotic; it’s designed to sort of not understand really what’s going on.”The lighting design was something he also had to think about. “It was interesting because I was thinking, if you’re out at sea at night 2,000 years ago, without electricity and you end up in a thunderstorm, what would that feel like? I was thinking, if you can see everything around you, that’s going to look fake.”

A CGI storm didn’t feel right because the entire crew had been meticulous about keeping everything as grounded and as experiential as possible. Van Hoytema says the team built overhead lighting rigs that were approximately 60×30 feet, hanging on cranes above the ship. “It meant that you would see flashes that progressively move from behind them to the front of them, as it opens up the sky. “What you see is short bursts and there’s just darkness or a little bit of a glow. It’s like trying to see this relentless endless sea, this weather, this saltwater and they’re just so close to death. It’s extremely claustrophobic at the same time.”

When it came to framing Odysseus, Van Hoytema was wired in to keep him in place on the gimbal. His Imax camera on his shoulder was also wired in.

“I was not trying to be in any way precious about framing whatsoever. I was just trying through with a jet engine blowing, gallons of water in my face. I was literally just trying to identify if it was even Matt Damon in the shot. It was extremely visceral, and it’s almost hard to grasp. It was not a situation in which you could just start a take or not. Every take was an event.”

Van Hoytema says the camera starts to roll and the jet engines start. “They rise, and at some point, they sound like two fighter jets are next to your ear. And it gets really wild because they start moving the gimbal as well, and you’re holding on for your life. The camera gets turned; it’s the original Imax camera, not the blimp and the Imax camera is very loud. However, in that situation, you don’t even know if it’s turned on. You do a take, and you have a crazy powerful adrenaline rush.”

In contrast, Van Hoytema recalled a moment when the crew was out to sea, in open waters and a pod of bottlenose dolphins appeared. It was completely unplanned.

Van Hoytema recalls, “It’s interesting because it was also pretty violent that day. It was very harsh weather and I remember a lot of people on the crew got seasick. As we started filming, dolphins started to jump out of the water. What is interesting is that it symbolizes that no matter what the weather does, we take the weather as it comes and accept it as the reality you work in that day.”

The years 2000 to 2009 are some of the most significant years and helped birth some of the most popular franchises in cinematic history as a whole.

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man series, the X-Men franchise, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight series and so much more all got their big breaks in this era. Without these abundantly important 10 years, superhero movies would not even be close to where they are today. Their significance wouldn't even be close to what they are, and events like Avengers: Endgame most likely would not have ever happened or had the success they did without these foundational projects that are, to simply state, masterpieces.

Spider-Man is the most profitable superhero on the planet—he has been for quite some time now—and that meant that no one was surprised that, when the technology was finally there, he'd get a live-action movie. Come 2002, fans were finally treated to Sam Raimi's Spider-Man, which seriously rocked the world. Sure, X-Men came out just a few years earlier, but Spider-Man really helped drive home and fully prove that comic book characters could work on the big screen and the Hollywood landscape.

This 2002 flick was an all-time smash hit and proved that superhero movies could be genuinely amazing financial investments for big studios, with the project grossing an astounding about $825 million worldwide, with a phenomenal $114.8 million opening weekend. It was the first film to ever cross $100 million in its opening weekend and if that's not proof of how great this movie is, then what could be? It turned the franchise from what the franchise was in Blade to a high-speed, thrilling, gothic, and all-around fire flick.

Snipes brings his everything to the role—as per usual—and keeps Blade feeling like a character that the audience genuinely wants to keep watching.

Paul Rudd And Seann William Scott's R-Rated Comedy Is Worth Revisiting 18 Years Later

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Studios abandoned big comedies years ago. But in the 2000s and early 2010s, we really had it good. This was the age of the Judd Apatow comedy, in which the director not only delivered such modern classics as "The 40-Year-Old Virgin" and "Knocked Up," but influenced almost every comedy released during the era. Alongside the main Apatow offerings you had those which he produced such as "Anchorman: The Legend of Ron Burgundy," or those made by his collaborators, such as "Superbad." Then, there were those films that featured Apatow associates but were made without his direct involvement. The brilliant "I Love You, Man" is one example which, despite being criminally overlooked, remains one of Paul Rudd's best films.

As does 2008's "Role Models," which saw Rudd team up with Seann William Scott for a film that, like pretty much every comedy movie released at the time, was defined by the improvisational, R-rated humor that had made Apatow's films so successful. This particular movie, however, paired Apatow allies Rudd and Christopher Mintz-Plasse with former members of sketch comedy troupe The State in director David Wain and writer/actor Ken Marino.

As someone who watched "Role Models" when it came out, I simply cannot believe this movie is 18 years old. Despite being less well known than the aforementioned "Knocked Up" and its ilk, "Role Models" became instantly beloved by me and my friends upon its release. Almost two decades later, it's strange to think that an entire generation has grown up without films such as this shaping their sense of humor. If you happen to be part of that group, now's the time to acquaint yourself with this  oft-overlooked comedy. If, like me, you remember "Role Models" fondly, you'll be happy to hear it holds up.

Like so many of the best comedies of the 2000s, "Role Models" succeeds because despite being an R-rated comedy with frequent gross-out gags and unapologetically immature humor, it's also a surprisingly tender story with its heart in the right place.

Paul Rudd plays Danny who, alongside Seann William Scott's Anson Wheeler, works as a promoter for the energy drink "Minotaur." Danny feels deeply unfulfilled and has become deeply cynical as a result, spending most of his time correcting people on their speech. This causes his girlfriend, Beth (Elizabeth Banks), to break up with him, after which Danny loses it during a presentation at a high school. His freak out leads to both he and Anson being assigned to community service at Sturdy Wings, a big brother-style program where the pair become mentors to two "littles."

Anson is paired with Ronnie (Bobb'e J. Thompson) a foul-mouthed kid with no friends, while Danny is given Augie Farks (Christopher Mintz-Plasse), a teenager obsessed with his medieval LARP group. At first, Danny and Anson are merely going through the motions. But over time they develop a bond with their "littles" — one which just might melt Danny's cold, cynical heart.

The secret star of "Role Models" is Joe Lo Truglio, who plays Kuzzik, a particularly dedicated LARP-er who, if you watch the bloopers, made it almost impossible for Rudd to get through any of his scenes without breaking. But there are a ton of standout performances here, from A.D. Miles as overly earnest Sturdy Wings counsellor Martin Gary to Ken Jeong as the self-serious LARP king Argotron and Jane Lynch as a former addict turned Sturdy Wings leader.

"Role Models" hit theaters in November 2008 and grossed a solid $92.6 million on a $28 million budget. It also garnered positive reviews, earning a 77% "certified fresh" rating on Rotten Tomatoes. Dana Stevens of Slate found "Role Models" to be "strewn with enough serendipities to make it a worthy variation on the dudes-grow-up genre," while the San Francisco Chronicle's Peter Hartlaub noted how, while Seann William Scott and Paul Rudd were "from two different moviemaking cliques" they ultimately "get along fine in a Bing Crosby/David Bowie kind of way."

Meanwhile, Rafer Guzman of Newsday noted how Rudd, who co-wrote the script with David Wain, Timothy Dowling, and Ken Marino, had "clearly learned a few things working on Judd Apatow comedies." By that point, Rudd had appeared in Apatow's "The 40-Year-Old Virgin" and "Knocked Up," as well as several Apatow-produced projects including "Anchorman" and "Forgetting Sarah Marshall." As such, he was well-established as one of the most prominent figures of the 2000s R-rated comedy boom.

But The State boys certainly brought their own flair to the film and the pairing of theirs and Rudd's sensibility along with Scott's lovable knucklehead charm made for a hilarious and surprisingly heartwarming experience that deserves to be remembered as fondly as some of the more well-known comedies of the era. Robert Pattinson's Batman Movie Franchise Is Officially Finished

No, I didn’t forget about The Dark Knight Rises

With Robert Pattinson confirmed to be the next James Bond, one of the biggest takeaways is that Matt Reeves’ The Batman series will likely end with The Batman: Part II. Pattinson is signed on for three films as Bond, and while that’s one less film than the four-film deal that Amazon MGM Studios was seeking for the other Bond contenders, it’s pretty hard to imagine that Pattinson will juggle the role of 007 with an ongoing commitment to DC — much less Battinson’s integration into the wider DCU, which some DC fans had hoped for. To be clear, nothing has been confirmed about the status of a third The Batman movie, but the latest news combined with the extreme delays facing The Batman Part II feel like a pretty clear indication that the Batmobile has hit a dead end and will need yet another reboot.

However, the loss is even bigger than you might realize, as this will be the third time in a row that Batman fans will be robbed of a good Batman trilogy.

Tim Burton’s 1989 film Batman, of course, revolutionized the character of Batman and comic book movies as a whole. Before then, the general public’s awareness of Batman was mostly associated with the campy, comedic Adam West Batman, but Burton changed that and presented a dark, heavily stylized version starring Michael Keaton. He then followed it up with Batman Returns, which many still consider to be one of the best Batman films ever.

Warner Bros., however, felt it was too dark and got rid of Burton for the third film, replacing him with Joel Schumacher. Keaton followed Burton out the door, not wanting to continue as Batman without him. Batman Forever, starring Val Kilmer, was the result, and while that film has its defenders, it felt like a different franchise and a different Batman altogether. The same can be said for Batman and Robin.

Then in 2005 came Batman Begins, directed by Christopher Nolan and starring Christian Bale. Taking a far more grounded approach to the character, Batman Begins turned Batman’s origin into an epic film saga, where it had only been relegated to brief flashbacks in previous films. Nolan topped himself with The Dark Knight by putting Batman in the center of a gritty crime drama with Heath Ledger’s Oscar-winning turn as the Joker. For a second time, a Batman film revolutionized comic book films. Nearly 20 years later, many still cite The Dark Knight as the greatest comic book movie ever made.

Unfortunately, Nolan’s third Batman film wasn’t nearly as good as the first two. Perhaps because of Ledger’s untimely death before the release of The Dark Knight, Nolan at first seemed disinterested in a third film, and it is generally understood that he had to rework the story he had in mind to remove the Joker from its narrative. Regardless of the reasons why, The Dark Knight Rises is a deeply uneven, disorganized film that most view as the low point of Nolan’s Batman, if not an outright bad film (I tend to think it is quite bad).

After Nolan’s trilogy concluded, Ben Affleck became the next Batman, in both Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice as well as the various versions of Justice League. Regardless of what you think of these films — I personally find them insufferable — they were deeply divisive. Despite an Affleck-led Batman film being rumored for a time, we never got him in any standalone films, much less three of them.

In 2022, The Batman offered new hope. While some were skeptical of Pattinson as Batman, he proved to be a really solid take on the Dark Knight, which prioritized Batman’s skills as a detective, something missing from previous Batman films. He was complemented by Paul Dano’s frightening take as the Riddler and Colin Farrell’s scene-stealing Penguin.

Reeves also presented the best-ever take on Gotham City. While Burton’s Gotham was bold and interesting, neither of his films made Gotham feel like a real city. Nolan’s Gotham, with its unconcealed usage of Chicago and New York City, had little personality at all. But Reeves’ Gotham felt like a broken city that desperately needed a Batman to come to its rescue, with production combining parts of Chicago and Scotland to give the city a more naturalistic, gothic vibe. The film’s nearly three-hour runtime only assisted in the viewer’s immersion into Reeves’ Gotham.

But three hours in Reeves’ Gotham still wasn’t enough. The Batman Part II, which seems to be telling a story with Two-Face as a villain, was delayed for years due to a personal issue going on with Matt Reeves. Filming finally began in June, but in early October, Reeves had to step away, most likely for the same family issue. Steven Soderbergh then stepped in to finish the filming Reeves began, which is currently ongoing. While I applaud Reeves for prioritizing his family over a film, this means that it will be six years between The Batman and its sequel and it's hard to imagine that Skydance, the new owners of DC Studios, will want to keep Batman on the shelf for that long again, nor do I think they’ll want to schedule around Pattinson’s Bond films.

And, while it never seemed like a real possibility, I was still hoping that Pattinson would get folded into what James Gunn and Peter Safran had been building for the DCU if only because I don’t think it's all that easy to keep finding great guys to be Batman, and while he wasn’t quite as good as Bale — or even Keaton — Pattinson truly is/was a great Batman. It’s just too bad that his take on the character will be cut short before we get a proper trilogy.

Spider-Man: Brand New Day, , the fourth entry into the MCU Tom Holland era of Spidey movies at Sony, is at 90% fresh on Rotten Tomatoes, nearly as critically acclaimed there as perceived large-scale Oscar contenders The Odyssey and Project Hail Mary which are also in the 90s ( and even some of the more arthouse-style contenders). Both of those, along with the upcoming unseen Dune: Part Three, are in Gold Derby’s projection of the current top 10 movies with best chances to land a Best Picture nomination. Pundits are basically ignoring the year’s biggest film, so we have to ask why.

Of the previous eight live-action Spider-Man films Sony has released since 2002, the only Oscar nominations they have received have been in the Visual Effects and Sound categories, the most recent nom for Visual Effects for 2001’s No Way Home. 2004’s Spider-Man 2 is the only one to actually win an Oscar, that one also for Visual Effects. On the animated front, 2018’s Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse won for Best Animated Feature, and its 2023 sequel was also nominated.

Not one of these 10 films has cracked the Best Picture race. Rothman feels Brand New Day can, and deserves to be the breakthrough.

“I think to be honest, it’s because there are things like Gold Derby, you know, whatever that is, that just tend to think only about the art film world, and look, I’m an art film guy. But I’m a traditionalist, and I was never for expanding the number of Best Pictures from five to 10. Okay, I liked it the old way, but I believe that the very reason it was extended from five to 10 was so that the Academy would have room to recognize really quality commercial filmmaking,” he said pointing (correctly) to Christopher Nolan’s Best Picture-snubbed superhero movie The Dark Knight as the key reason.

“I think wise heads know that quality commercial filmmaking is extremely difficult, and to make a movie like this, never mind the fact that it’s the highest-grossing movie in history of America, forget that. The studio plans a “robust” screening strategy starting just before Thanksgiving, including multiple FYC Q&As with filmmakers and department heads. When you see the movie those assumptions are undone. It’s a very moving, quite personal film. Destin (Daniel Cretton) is a really personal filmmaker. This is the guy who made Short Term 12. Hollywood CEO Paydays May Get Harder to Track as Federal Regulator Proposes Disclosure Revamp

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Fewer executives will be tracked, and less data will be shared if the SEC's proposed disclosure changes move forward.

It’s no secret that Hollywood CEO pay is often a hot-button story.

Chief execs in media, entertainment, and technology are annually among the most highly compensated people on Earth, with mid-eight-figure pay packages a common occurrence and nine-figure paydays increasingly common.

But those days might be ending soon. Not the paydays, just the disclosures, should new rules from the Securities and Exchange Commission go into effect.

The SEC on Friday proposed a revamp of its executive compensation disclosure rules, simplifying things while also changing what those disclosures will look like. The result will be that pay is disclosed for fewer executives, with only the CEO and CFO likely to be disclosed should the rule change pass (no more COO, general counsel or other C-suite pay), while the way the pay packages are disclosed shifts. In addition, the threshold for perquisites is being raised from $10,000 to $150,000, meaning that some perks like club memberships will no longe require disclosure, and companies will get more discretion to determine if something is a perk at all, such as CEO security costs.

At the heart of the changes are simpler disclosures, with three tables: “Earned Compensation Table, Granted Compensation Table, and Status of Equity Awards Table” with a particular emphasis on salary, bonus and equity grants.

“As applied to executive compensation disclosure, a panelist at last year’s roundtable aptly analogized current Item 402 to an ice cream parlor that carries rum raisin and maple walnut; flavors desired by only a single customer,” SEC chairman Paul Adkins said in a statement about the proposed changes. “The proposed amendments would, if adopted, revise Item 402 to focus on an objective standard regarding needs of the reasonable investor, rather than on the specialized tastes of a specific investor.”

Executives, investor relations and corporate communications teams are surely cheering the move, which will result in less gargantuan numbers than previous disclosures. Some of those changes may be warranted: After all, equity may not end up vesting if certain metrics are not achieved, and a high strike price can invalidate whatever value is in some shares. Those are things that CEOs have complained about for years: The news coverage has a headline number, but their actual take-home could be lower (or much higher!) depending on the performance of the shares.

The new disclosure structure will eliminate the paper gain spikes associated with unvested equity, and likely to be closer to what an executive really takes home, though if the old model sometimes overstated thing, the new disclosure may understate things, if the stocks consistently rise.

There will also be changes to the employee-to-CEO pay ratio, which focuses it on U.S. full-time employees and could make for more favorable comparisons.

And the changes to perquisite reporting mean that things like security, parking, country club memberships and even some private jet use could no longer be disclosed, all of which would be music to CEO ears. Even if those disclosures sometimes help tell a bigger story.

Burn drove that home in the Dolby mix of the film, pushing the sound design. The 8 Best TV Dramas From Before the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We are currently undeniably living in the era of streaming, but for the first few decades of its existence as a medium, network television reigned supreme. Since network shows are broadcast on a strict weekly timetable, they're usually built on very particular structures potentially containing built-in breaks before cutting to commercials, cliffhangers, and very consistent pacing. As television has evolved, so too have the characteristics of network series changed.

However, there are eight particular broadcast television dramas which are easily the greatest in history prior to the advent of streaming. Whether we're talking about classics like Twin Peaks or modern masterpieces like Game of Thrones, these are the kinds of generation-defining dramas that anyone who loves small-screen content should watch at least once in their lives. There is no understanding network television without experiencing these masterpieces.

The 1990s gave birth to a second Golden Age of television, and were an absolutely groundbreaking time for auteur-driven television. The medium shifted away from traditional network-controlled episodic formulas, and instead began embracing more serialized storytelling in line with the vision of creatives like David Lynch. Indeed, Lynch and Mark Frost's Twin Peaks is one of the most foundational auteur-driven shows of all time.

It's one of those network TV shows that are visually stunning, impeccably written, masterfully directed, and pretty much impeccable in every way that matters. Though the show saw a dip in quality right in the middle of season two, this has never stopped fans from greatly enjoying the show's intentionally uncanny tone, campy supernatural elements, and brilliant mix of melodramatic soap opera tropes and Lynch's signature style of surrealism.

Never in history has the small screen seen a prequel series even remotely as incredible as Vince Gilligan and Peter Gould's Better Call Saul. Making a prequel to a network drama widely considered the single greatest TV show of all time was never going to be an easy task, and as such, fans were very much skeptical when this project was announced. In the end, though, Better Call Saul ended up being one of the best TV shows since 2010, a near-perfect masterpiece through and through.

Better Call Saul is both an exceptional prequel adding a lot of depth and texture to the Breaking Bad world, and an incredible crime drama and legal thriller in its own right. With some masterful character writing, an excellent balance between poignant tragedy and genuinely laugh-out-loud-funny comedy, and the faultless work of an ensemble led by Bob Odenkirk at the top of his game, it's no wonder why it's the most acclaimed TV prequel in history.

Genre stories were always of vital importance to network television, and science fiction in particular was a key driver of technical innovation and a great platform for social commentary throughout the broadcast era of TV. Over the years, never has the small screen seen a sci-fi show more foundational or widely influential than Rod Serling's legendary anthology series The Twilight Zone, a gem that's essential viewing for anyone who loves sci-fi.

Serling wanted to make a show that would allow him to tackle controversial yet essential social and political issues, but since network executives refused to let him write direct socially-conscious shows, he decided to circumvent that issue. So, he made a sci-fi series that disguised its treatment of taboo topics—like racism and McCarthyism—as sci-fi, fantasy, and horror. Like any anthology series, Twilight Zone had its fair share of ups and downs throughout its run, but it's packed with some of the greatest episodes in the history of anthology TV series.

HBO was launched all the way back in 1972, but it wouldn't be until the '90s and 2000s that they would become the kings of broadcast television and the pioneers of what is now called "prestige TV." Case in point: Band of Brothers, a war drama miniseries executive-produced by Tom Hanks and Steven Spielberg that has been the highest-rated miniseries in history on IMDb for quite some time. It's easy to see why: miniseries or otherwise, history has never seen a war drama quite like this on TV.

It's one of the best HBO shows shorter than 20 episodes, a hard-hitting and faithful adaptation of the non-fiction book of the same name by historian Stephen E. Ambrose. Treating World War II with the same kind of sensitivity and raw realism as Saving Private Ryan, Band of Brothers is uncompromisingly realistic and respectful toward its characters, with a potent shift in narrative perspective every episode that gives every minute of its run a consistently surprising, thought-provoking feel.

Though its penultimate season was divisive and its final few episodes were nothing short of universally panned, Game of Thrones proves that a network drama doesn't need to be absolutely perfect in order to be counted among the best in history. Indeed, it would be silly to pretend that this HBO adaptation of George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire book series, which was a gargantuan pop culture phenomenon over the course of its eight seasons, is anything less than legendary.

Where can one even begin singing the praises of such a praise-worthy masterpiece? For one, the production values are off-the-charts, including some of the greatest visuals and music in the history of fantasy television. Add to that some exceptional writing, an unrelentingly bleak and violent tone, and one of the best TV cast ensembles of the 2010s, and you get a genre-defining masterpiece whose quality far transcends any mistakes that its final season may have made.

As if any more proof were needed that there's just no matching HBO's quality and legacy when it comes to the greatest dramas in the history of network television, there's also The Wire. Creator David Simon had spent 12 years covering crime and city systems as a police reporter for The Baltimore Sun, while co-writer Ed Burns had worked inside the Baltimore Police Department as a homicide detective. Inspired by their experiences and what they'd seen, the duo co-created The Wire.

It's a scathing study and deconstruction of five particular institutional layers of Baltimore: the illegal drug trade, the port system, the government, education, and the media. But far from feeling niche or exploitative, The Wire instead delivers the most complete portrait of American urban decay ever put to screen. Full of richly layered characters and potent thematic work, it's one of the many pinnacles of 21st-century crime drama television.

Network television would simply have never become what it is today had it not been for The Sopranos. In television's history, there's a "before The Sopranos" and an "after The Sopranos," as David Chase's crime drama completely re-defined what the medium was able to do. Never before had television seen such darkly antiheroic characters, such cinematic quality, and such cleanly serialized storytelling. Thus, prestige television was born, and the small screen was never the same again.

But though it was a pioneer of this new version of network television, The Sopranos doesn't feel dated in the slightest all these many years later; on the contrary, it's still generally considered the best HBO show to date. Bolstered by the phenomenal writing, James Gandolfini's powerhouse lead performance, and some of the richest psychological depth that a broadcast drama has ever seen, it's a must-see for anyone who loves television.

Over a decade after its conclusion, most people still widely refer to Vince Gilligan's Breaking Bad as the single greatest TV drama in history, and it will take something very special to ever dethrone it. Gilligan came up with the concept after fellow writer Tom Schnauz joked that they should solve their lack of work by buying up an RV and setting up a mobile meth lab. The idea stuck in his mind for years, and would eventually transformed into the most complex, cinematic, and irresistibly riveting drama that television has ever produced.

Breaking Bad is one of those drama shows that have aged like fine wine, a masterpiece whose every element works in flawless concert with the others to deliver the most enthralling experience possible. The production values are fantastic, the ensemble is full of actors delivering career-best work, and the character writing is the dramatic fuel that pushes the narrative momentum forward throughout. From top to bottom and beginning to end, Breaking Bad is fully worthy of being officially named the greatest TV drama of all time.

As someone who’s used Letterboxd religiously for quite a few years now, the best way to explain it to people who've not heard of Letterboxd (they do exist, especially if you venture outside the online world!) is to say it’s like Goodreads, but for movies instead of books. And most people have heard of Goodreads, even if they don’t themselves have accounts there.

If you're not in the know, then it basically lets you keep track of what you're reading, what you’ve read, and what you'd still like to read. You can rate titles, review them if you want, or even “DNF” them (marking something as “did not finish”). The site is a bit of a popularity contest, sure, but it’s interesting to see what appeals to people, with the following fantasy books all being quite loved on Goodreads, and more than 100,000 ratings each, not to mention generally high average ratings across the board, too.

His Dark Materials was a trilogy that’s since grown into a series, though it’s the first three books that are most essential. The Subtle Knife is the second, and probably the strongest chapter of that trilogy, since it skillfully adds another central character to the proceedings while effectively continuing on from the surprising way the first book (Northern Lights) ended, in relation to the characters who were established in that initial novel.

The third book, The Amber Spyglass. is kind of chaotic, but still interesting, and the way the overall trilogy works in some science fiction elements alongside the fantasy as things go along is also pretty great. All have well over 100,000 ratings on Goodreads, and the only one that might cause some disappointment is The Amber Spyglass, and even then, probably only the final chunk of The Amber Spyglass, so it’s kind of a case of two-and-a-half out of three ain't bad.

Things sure do happen in The Master and Margarita, and it’s hard to call the book boring in any way, but then it’s harder to try to summarize all those things that do happen and articulate how those things converge into something potentially definable as a plot. It’s the kind of fantasy that’s heavy on surrealism and extremely light on explanations for why anything is happening, outside of saying “Oh, this madness is happening because it’s making some kind of satirical point about x.”

And all that can be lost on you, if you're not knowledgeable about the history of the Soviet Union and maybe some other things (The Master and Margarita was written a few decades before it was ultimately published, and so knowing about the Soviet Union in the 1930s is more helpful than knowing its history closer to the middle of the century). Crazy things happen, you will feel like you're hallucinating at multiple points, and maybe it gets labeled as “fantasy” because it’s hard to know what other genre to put this one into.

It is The Hobbit, or The Hobbit, or There and Back Again if you want to use its full title and all, so of course it’s worth mentioning for present purposes. J.R.R. Tolkien sure did write another legendary fantasy book that has an even stronger reputation, and that one will be addressed shortly, but The Hobbit, or There and Back Again is like a prologue or introduction to the whole of Middle-earth, but even if it weren't, it’d still be an extremely compelling work of fantasy in its own right.

It has more than 4.6 million ratings on Goodreads, and then it has a very strong overall score of 4.3/5, so all that really just further cements how readable and essential this is.

There’s a Hobbit here, and then a quest, plus some gold, and a dragon, so, like, lots of expected fantasy things, but the execution is what counts, and then there’s also the influential nature of The Hobbit, or There and Back Again to take into account. It has more than 4.6 million ratings on Goodreads, and then it has a very strong overall score of 4.3/5, so all that really just further cements how readable and essential this is as a fantasy novel.

As is the case for most series, there are fewer ratings given out the further The Dark Tower goes along… but it is curious that the seventh book has a few more ratings overall than the sixth book. The idea of reading the seventh book without the sixth is wild, since the sixth book is almost entirely set-up for all the bombastic things that happen in book #7, but either some people forgot to rate the sixth book, or they treated their rating for The Dark Tower VII as a rating for the entire saga.

If they're in the latter camp, that’s almost understandable, to be honest. The Dark Tower VII is such a grand finale to a weird and compelling series, and it represents The Dark Tower at its best, strangest, and most ambitious. It is one of Stephen King’s best books, without a doubt, and even if you’ve heard parts of it are, um, kind of divisive (to say the least), you still shouldn’t let that put you off finishing your own journey to the Dark Tower.

Like His Dark Materials, Mistborn was a trilogy that later became a whole series, whether you want to judge the Mistborn series as its own thing (thanks to a sequel trilogy and some other stuff), or whether you want to look at the whole of Brandon Sanderson’s Cosmere saga as one massive series. Of that original trilogy, The Final Empire is the strongest entry, with its real sense of unpredictability and the way it works as something of a heist narrative with a fantasy setting.

If you do like The Final Empire, then the chances of you liking books #2 and #3 are strong, too. And for now, Sanderson can only really be appreciated through books, but movie and/or TV adaptations of some of his works are pretty much inevitable (watch this space and all). Anyway, as for Goodreads, 1.1 million ratings, an average score of 4.48/5… yeah, honestly, fair enough.

More than a million people have rated The Odyssey on Goodreads, and it’s fitting that something so old and influential has been widely read. Maybe a fair few of those ratings came about because people were made to read it in high school, sure, but there’s a lot here worth studying and analyzing, to put it mildly. Whether to even call it a book, though, in the traditional sense is something you might be able to debate.

Well, it exists as a book now, even if it was a story that was originally formed and told (rather than written down) orally. You can keep the tradition alive, to some extent, by listening to a recording of The Odyssey rather than reading it, because it’s one of those rare instances where listening to a book rather than reading it is technically an experience more in line with how you're “supposed” to take in the text.

The Stand is another Stephen King book, sure, but this is a pretty different one from The Dark Tower VII, even if both The Stand and The Dark Tower (as a series) feature Randall Flagg, admittedly. The Dark Tower was a bit of every genre, including dark fantasy, whereas The Stand… okay, The Stand also belongs to a good many genres, just not really the Western one, nor a few others.

It’s still very much dark fantasy, though, leaning more into that than science fiction, even if the majority of books about the end of the world (and what comes after) do so with a sci-fi spin rather than a fantasy one. The Stand is also huge, especially if you read the uncut version published in 1990, yet it’s incredibly rewarding and makes those hundreds upon hundreds of pages fly by real fast.

Any of the three parts on their own would be worthy of putting in the top spot here, and then if you add all those parts together, and read The Lord of the Rings as one epic, then yeah, obviously it’s even more worthy of topping a ranking like this one.

All the parts have had more than 1,000,000 ratings each, and each of those three parts has a high average rating overall. McVay was asked during his press conference whether he has talked to the 34-year-old Woods, who was a receiver for the Rams for five years, about potentially playing again.

“I have, yes,” McVay answered with a laugh.

McVay acknowledged that there are Collective Bargaining Agreement rules against a coach becoming a player, but he said Woods is still in great shape and it would be fun to give it a try.

“I’ve looked at him, and the way he looks out there, I’ve definitely been tempted,” McVay said. “I think that’d be a violation of the CBA. I don’t know what he’d have to do, but he looks damn good and still is smooth out there. Very tempting.”

Realistically, even with his intimate knowledge of the Rams’ offense, he probably wouldn’t be able to contribute as much on the field as whatever receiver the Rams could add off waivers or another team’s practice squad.

If Woods and the Rams actually wanted him to become an active player, it wouldn’t be as simple as signing him to the active roster, as the league has rules preventing that. Richard Avedon, Fashion Guru, Does Old Age

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

A moving, incisive photography exhibition in Toronto and Montreal explores the way of all flesh.

Left: Richard Avedon, Gloria Swanson, actor, New York, September 4, 1980, gelatin silver print. Center: Richard Avedon, Jacob Israel Avedon, father of Richard Avedon, Sarasota, Florida, October 7, 1969, gelatin silver print. Right: Richard Avedon, The Duke and Duchess of Windsor, Waldorf Astoria, suite 28A, New York, April 16, 1957, gelatin silver print.

Old faces — some seem to crease and crumble as we watch — were a specialty for Richard Avedon (1923–2004), the photographer famous for his fashion work for Vogue, Harper’s Bazaar, Revlon, and Elle, magazines not known for plugging geezers. I didn’t know this, so I learned something new in my first minutes in Richard Avedon Immortal: Portraits of Aging, 1951–2004, finishing its tour next month, when it goes to The Image Center in Toronto and the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts. Avedon was a pioneer in presenting cool, stark, tough beauty — he gave us a nude Nastassja Kinski with a boa constrictor licking her earlobe, Brooke Shields in jeans, and lots of movie stars and potentates at their peaks. But old age? Shades of decadence are one thing, but decrepitude?

The Image Center is Canada’s premiere photography museum and part of Toronto Metropolitan University. The exhibition there is similar to Montreal’s edition and displays 100 Avedon portraits. Avedon died more than 20 years ago, and the show spans 50 years, so the subjects are from another era. That said, old age is timeless as well as time-limited. On view are superstars such as Gloria Swanson, Ronald Reagan, the Duke and Duchess of Windsor, and the Dalai Lama. Then there are the period pieces like Dorothy Parker, Oscar Levant, Ezra Pound, and Coco Chanel.

Cristian Mungiu’s Palme d’Or winner “Fjord” will open the 26th European Film Festival Malaysia (EUFF) on Nov. 26 at TGV Pavilion Kuala Lumpur.

This year, for the first time, some of the festival’s films will also go to home audiences through Malaysian media and entertainment group Astro.

The festival runs through Dec. 13 under the theme “Atlas.” Its 20 films represent 20 countries. Eight of them premiered at Cannes, and three come from last year’s Venice competition. “Audiences today are more discerning, with interests that go beyond familiar genres, languages and cultures,” said Henry Tan, group CEO of Astro. He added that the Astro First release would “bring these films beyond the cinema and into homes across Malaysia, reaching audiences who may not otherwise have the opportunity to experience them.”

In “Fjord,” Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve lead the cast as members of a devout Romanian family. After the family moves to Norway, the way they bring up their children comes under scrutiny. It is Mungiu’s second Palme d’Or. The Venice titles include Paolo Sorrentino’s “La Grazia,” which won Toni Servillo the Volpi Cup, and Ildikó Enyedi’s “Silent Friend.” That film stars Tony Leung Chiu-wai and Léa Seydoux and won the Fipresci Prize. The National Film Development Corporation Malaysia (FINAS), TGV Cinemas and Astro are official partners.

EUFF started around the turn of the millennium. In its early years, the embassies of EU member states in Malaysia organized it. Trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi between Russia, Ukraine and the US aimed at ending the war in Ukraine have concluded after a second day.

Although the American side said they saw "some openings", there was no hint of any breakthroughs to end the nearly five year-long war sparked by Russia's invasion.

Davyd Arakhamia from Ukraine's negotiating team said that this round of talks - the first since February - "was one of the hardest."

"I believe the Russians came with very strict directives, so it was extremely difficult to soften the discussion," he said. But he added that he felt "good momentum" and urged for a second round of talks to be organised as soon as possible.

Both Moscow and Kyiv confirmed that a large-scale prisoner of war exchange was in the works and could be carried out soon.

After the talks it was announced that on Thursday US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner - President Donald Trump's son-in-law - would travel to Moscow to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin, and then to Dublin to speak to Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky.

This latest round of talks had been framed as centred around the "security architecture" of Europe.

For Kyiv, this may include reassurances that allies will come to its aid if Ukraine comes under attack again, despite the country not being a Nato member.

For Moscow, it is likely to include a limit to the influence of Nato in eastern Europe - a central concern for the Kremlin since the alliance's eastward expansion.

Earlier this week Kushner said the US hoped to "create a new framework" of security between Ukraine, Russia and Europe that could "secure lasting peace", while Kremlin negotiator Vladimir Medinsky said the world needed a "new" and "fair" security system.

Ukraine and Europe have long been concerned about the current US administration's commitment to European security and its softening towards the Kremlin.

Representatives of Britain, Nato and various EU countries were also in Abu Dhabi and met with US and Ukrainian negotiators, but the Kremlin - with whom the EU does not officially communicate - said its delegation had not had any contact with them.

Moscow has been dismissive of European involvement, although Europeans have long argued that they are central to any post-war dynamics on their continent.

Kushner said the US was keen to bring the Europeans on board and said that getting them "added to the format" would be on the agenda for his talks with Putin.

There is also disagreement on whether discussions on a future security framework should be preceded by a ceasefire, as the Europeans and Kyiv would want, or whether the fighting only stops after an agreement has been reached, as the Kremlin has insisted.

Any agreement on a peacetime security framework, however, is contingent on the war ending, Kushner noted. But that cannot occur "until there's an agreement on territory and on all other bilateral issues," he added.

Since talks began last year, a core obstacle to any settlement has been Ukraine's eastern region of Donetsk, which is mostly occupied by Russia. Moscow has demanded that Ukraine relinquish the parts of Donetsk it still holds, while Kyiv wants to freeze the contact line to maintain control over those areas.

This matter was the "hardest", Kushner acknowledged, while Zelensky's chief of staff and negotiator Kyrylo Budanov refused to be drawn on whether Russia was still demanding a Ukrainian withdrawal from the region.

"These deals are very hard and complex, and this one is very emotional, very complex," Kushner said. "The reality is there is no trust right now between the sides and that is to be expected after a conflict like this."
