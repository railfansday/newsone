Elizabeth Debicki Thanks the People Who Opened Doors for Her in Her ELLE Women in Hollywood Speech

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Elizabeth Debicki knows that no career is built alone. On Monday night, the actress accepted her honor at ELLE’s 2026 Women in Hollywood celebration, taking a moment to recognize the people who helped make her career possible.

After being introduced by Alexander Skarsgård, Debicki thanked her fellow honorees before reflecting on the support she has received throughout her journey.

“I really love to work. It is my great love,” she said. “And standing here tonight, I feel a really deep sense of gratitude for all the people—the small army of people—who have fought for me to be able to do my work.”

Debicki recalled how casting director Ronna Kress helped her secure an audition for Baz Luhrmann’s The Great Gatsby, which became her breakout film role. At the time, she was just 20 years old, had recently graduated from drama school in Australia, and had never done a screen test.

“Now, Baz, who is my fairy godfather, he fought very, very hard for me after I auditioned for him,” she explained. “But the only reason I was able to get that audition was because Ronna Kress watched the shoddy rookie tape I made back home and somehow managed to convince Baz to sit still long enough to watch it (which, if you know, you know is an actual miracle).”

She continued, “Ronna fought very, very hard for the studio to fly me out here to L.A.—I had never been here before—and to read for the part. And truly, that changed everything. That was really, really my door. And Ronna and Baz, they held that open for me.”

Debicki also praised the other women being recognized that evening: Julianne Moore, Penélope Cruz, Florence Pugh, Inde Navarette, Rosalind Eleazar, and Carrie Coon.

“In your work, your audiences are so privileged to witness your talent and your intelligence,” she said. “And this generosity in the way that you work, it makes us feel sane, and it makes us feel seen. So thank you, so, so much.”

Carrie Coon Speaks Out About Women’s Representation at ELLE’s Women in Hollywood Event

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Carrie Coon had a question for her fellow Women in Hollywood honorees: “Can we start a studio?”

On Monday night, the actress took the stage at ELLE’s 2026 Women in Hollywood celebration in Los Angeles, where she called for women in the entertainment industry to come together and create more opportunities for themselves.

Coon was introduced by her Gilded Age co-star Louisa Jacobson, who celebrated her recent career achievements.

“Carrie is a crusader, always fighting tirelessly for artistic integrity in a landscape that seems to value high gloss and profit over honest, nuanced storytelling,” Jacobson said. “And if you know Carrie, you know that she is completely allergic to superficiality.”

In her acceptance speech, Coon reflected on the importance of women’s stories, drawing inspiration from feminist literature and End Times Fascism, a book by Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor.

“Patriarchy, capitalism...these are hierarchical systems of domination that rely on fear,” she said. “And in this paradigm, women and girls, like our natural resources, are a disposable commodity—and as such, according to philosopher Kate Manne—our stories are interchangeable and of equal power. But of course, they’re not. That’s not true. And we’re not a monolith.”

Coon went on to discuss the performances of her fellow honorees, arguing that their work represented something extraordinary. “In these women, I see the fight for the living world. I see moral clarity. I see imagination. I see courage,” she said. “And these are, in fact, the antidotes to fascism. That’s how important this storytelling is.”

Toward the end of her speech, Coon questioned how women in Hollywood could create lasting change beyond events celebrating their individual achievements.

“How do we forge our collective safety and solidarity in an ethic of communalism?” she asked. “Okay, so this is the part of the speech where I don’t actually have the answer to that question, but I make the appeal that nights like these shouldn’t be one-off nights.”

She concluded with the aforementioned proposition for her peers: “And so I’m going to start with a really stupid question, but I’m not afraid to ask a stupid question: Can we start a studio?”

9 Classics Where the Audiobook Version Is Better

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Audiobooks were technically born as far back as 1877, with Thomas Edison's invention of the phonograph. However, it wasn't until 1932 that the American Foundation for the Blind officially named them "Talking Books," introducing them as a medium for visually impaired people to enjoy literature as an alternative to Braille. Throughout the entire 20th century, and even well into the 21st, many classics have been turned into audiobooks so phenomenal that they're arguably even better than the original book.

Audiobooks and physical books are, of course, vastly different media that fulfill vastly different purposes, so comparing them can feel a bit like comparing apples to oranges. Still, sometimes, an audiobook is so good that it becomes entirely reasonable to say it provides a better experience than the original. These are audiobooks that anyone who enjoys classic literature should consider essential listening material. "Classic" is defined for the purposes of this list as any book that was originally published before the 21st century.

Folkloric vampires predate Bram Stoker's Dracula by hundreds of years. Inspired by such stories, as well as by Irish folklore and historical figures like Vlad the Impaler, Stoker wrote the 1897 classic that is nowadays widely remembered as the most foundational pillar of modern vampiric fiction. It's undoubtedly one of the best horror books of all time, which made the announcement of an original Audible audiobook-slash-audio-drama all the more worthy of skepticism.

In the end, however, Bram Stoker's Dracula ended up being one of the greatest horror audiobooks that readers have been treated to in years. With a stacked cast that includes the likes of Ella Purnell, Jonathan Bailey, and Aimee Lou Wood, the audiobook takes the classic epistolary novel and makes it feel even more immersive, cinematic, and well-paced. It's worth noting that it's an abridged audio drama rather than a full reading of the original novel, which is not ideal, but those interested in such a concept should love it.

Originally serialized between 1875 and 1877, Russian author Leo Tolstoy's Anna Karenina is widely regarded as one of the most foundational pieces of world literature in history, a must-read masterpiece whose daunting 1,000-page length shouldn't deter anyone from considering it essential reading. Since it was one of her favorite books of all time, Maggie Gyllenhaal agreed to narrate an Audible original audiobook that really does justice to one of the best epic books of all time.

Tolstoy's novel was such a monumental artistic achievement that the author was notoriously drained and exhausted by the whole experience by the time he was done, and reading such a vast book can itself also be a massive undertaking. With the raw immediacy and many emotional variations of Gyllenhaal's narration, listening to Anna Karenina can be a far more engaging and far less tedious experience than many may initially think. At over 30 hours long, it's definitely a time commitment, but oh so worth every minute.

Upon publication back in 1997, J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone (published in the US as Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone) was an immediate commercial and critical hit, kicking off one of the biggest multimedia franchises in history. The book's first audiobook version came out in 1999, and the performers varied by region. Whereas Jim Dale narrated the book in the US and Canada, it was Stephen Fry's voice that was heard in the British and international releases. If there is any one Harry Potter book that's better heard than read, it's Fry's performance of Philosopher's Stone.

As a whole, this is one of the most essential fantasy books to read before kicking the bucket, but there's nothing quite like hearing it through Fry's rich, velvety baritone voice. On the page, Philosopher's Stone is beautifully imaginative and cozy, but at times feels like an overly simple debut novel in terms of Rowling's prose. Fry elevates every word and makes the entire story feel like a wonderful fairy tale being told by the fire on a cold weekend night outdoors.

One of Stephen King's first novels was 1983's Pet Sematary, published before he had fully established and cemented himself as the true King of Horror. Fans had been begging for an audiobook for years, and once the project finally materialized, it was Michael C. Hall of Dexter fame who publisher Simon & Schuster picked as the right voice to narrate the story. The result is one of the best Stephen King audiobooks to date, and considering just how many great ones there are, that's saying a lot.

Pet Sematary was already absolutely legendary well before 2018, widely regarded by many—including King himself—as the author's most genuinely terrifying work. However, once the horror aspects really pick up steam after a relatively slow-burning first few pages, Hall makes it hit even harder. Getting an actor well-known for projects as death-related as Six Feet Under and Dexter to narrate a horror novel all about death and grief was not only brilliant meta-casting, but also hugely beneficial for the story as a whole.

Multiple books are almost always brought up when the endless debate of what "The Great American Novel" is gets brought up, but John Steinbeck's East of Eden is the most noteworthy. This family saga was likely key to Steinbeck being awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962, and it more than lives up to its reputation as one of the most perfect American books ever written. The first audiobook version of the classic ever recorded was narrated by respected character actor Richard Poe.

East of Eden was already widely recognized as one of the most perfectly written masterpieces of all time, and it's definitely a must-read, but there's something about the way Poe complements the narrative that makes his version arguably even more unforgettable. Steinbeck wrote East of Eden in such a sweeping, almost Biblical voice that it almost feels like the narration of a legend, and Poe's resonant yet deeply human voice allows that narration style to feel all the more intimate and personal.

The book that earned Harper Lee a Pulitzer Prize was 1960's To Kill a Mockingbird, one of the most essential and widely discussed American classics of the 20th century. Though certain elements of its narrative are undeniably dated by modern standards, its heart will forever be in the right place, with its critiques of racial prejudice and systemic injustice being as timely and hard-hitting today as they were back in the '60s. In 2014, legendary actress Sissy Spacek was tasked with narrating the audiobook version of the story.

The reasons why this is one of those great books that are even better as audiobooks are aplenty, and even though the Pulitzer-winning original remains absolutely foundational, Spacek brings a level of nuance and authenticity to the book that feels like a much-welcome sense of modernity. The actress' beautifully melodic Texan drawl makes the small-town Alabama setting feel all the more evocative and atmospheric, and the way she flawlessly captures Scout's personality as both a child and an adult is incredible.

Stephen King's 22nd book was 1986's It, still remembered by many as the author's best and most iconic work to date. At over 1,100 pages in length, it's an absolute brick of a horror novel, but its epic scope still works marvelously all these many years later. To narrate the 2016 audiobook, Simon & Schuster decided to go for an actor who already had a pre-existing tie to the Stephen King universe: Steven Weber, who played Jack Torrance in the 1997 three-part miniseries version of The Shining.

It was already one of those horror books where every chapter is perfect—something quite admirable for such a thick novel—but Weber took that literary doorstop and somehow turned it into an even bigger, more immersive 45-hour juggernaut of an audiobook. The actor gives each of the Losers a distinct voice both in their child and adult versions, and he goes all in on Pennywise without ever teetering too far into distracting camp. Perfectly paced and intense to the core, It is a must-listen-to audiobook through and through.

American poet, essayist, and civil rights activist Maya Angelou was a towering figure in modern American literature, completely reshaping how Black American identity was expressed in the written word. The first of her seven published memoirs, 1969's I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, broke new ground and became one of the first major autobiographies by an African American woman to achieve global bestseller status. When an audiobook version was released in 2011, it was Angelou herself who narrated it.

Taking one of the best memoirs of all time and turning it into an audiobook worthy of its legacy was always going to be a tough task, so who better to achieve it than the author herself? By having Angelou narrate her own story with her unmistakably theatrical contralto voice, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings feels even more honest and intimate than it already does on the page, balancing poignancy and humor to absolute perfection.

Well before he wrote what many still consider the greatest and most influential fantasy epic of the 20th century, J. R. R. Tolkien published the fantasy children's classic The Hobbit, almost inarguably the most perfect fantasy novel of the 1930s. There are multiple audiobook versions of The Hobbit available, but one reigns supreme as perhaps the audiobook that most elevates the experience of the original text: the 2020 version narrated by Andy Serkis.

It's easily one of the best audiobooks of all time, with Serkis not only delivering a much-expected powerhouse performance as Gollum, but also giving each of the other characters a distinct voice that makes them even more memorable than they are on paper. The Hobbit is also filled with songs and poems, and Serkis brings them to life in a way that reading the physical book simply can't match. In Serkis' voice, The Hobbit really does feel like the wonderful myth that it was always meant to be.

Brian Flores: Vikings plan to rotate players in order to deal with altitude in Mexico City

📅 2026-11-17  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The 49ers are spending the week in Colorado in order to acclimate to the altitude at which their Week 11 game in Mexico City will be played. The Vikings are not.

But the Vikings have a plan for ensuring that their players are ready for the conditions.

“It’s a tough place to play,” Vikings defensive coordinator Brian Flores told reporters on Tuesday regarding the game in Mexico. “I think it’s certainly taxing, you know, I think you’re — I think as a staff, we’re already talking about, ‘Hey, we’re gonna have to rotate these guys and get, you know, everybody’s gonna have to be ready to play.’ And so, yeah, I mean, it’s certainly something that we’re going to have to deal with.

“But there’s always, you know, elements that we’re dealing with, whether it’s cold or rain or things like that. Obviously, we’re indoors, so we don’t deal with much of that. But that’s the environment we’re gonna be in. I think we prepare them to play in that environment, but I’m not gonna be silly and say, ‘Hey, everybody, hey, you’ve got to go in there for 12 straight plays.’ I mean, we’re not doing that.

“So we’ll have rotations, we’ll have a plan in place. Inevitably, we’re gonna have to fight through some of that. It’s not gonna be your average game where, you know, altitude, you just have — you literally have less oxygen. So we’ve got to do a great job as a staff of making sure — watching guys, and making sure we got guys in and out, you know, as much as we can.”

His broader message to the defense is clear: “We’re gonna need everybody.”

Regardless of the plan to rotate players, the question becomes whether the 49ers will be able to rely more heavily on their best players because they’re properly acclimated to the thinner air. The Vikings are choosing a different strategy, and it’s going to create an obvious apples to apples comparison between the approaches that the two teams have taken — especially if the 49ers players seem to be fresh and the Vikings players seem to be gassed.

Billy Bob Thornton isn't a stranger to the thriller genre. His breakthrough came in 1992, when he co-wrote and starred in the critically acclaimed crime thriller One False Move. Since then, he has received three Academy Award nominations in his career. And now one of his best movies - A Simple Plan - is streaming for free.

He wrote and directed the 1996 thriller Sling Blade and also starred in the lead role, Karl Childers, which earned him his first Oscar nomination as an actor. His script won the Academy Award in the Best Adapted Screenplay category that year.

While Sling Blade may have been a career-defining project for Thornton, two years later, he starred in an acclaimed but lesser-known crime thriller that led to his third Oscar nomination.

Directed by Sam Raimi, A Simple Plan features Thornton as Jacob, the odd older brother of Hank Mitchel, who discovered 4.4 million dollars and a plane with their friend Lou. The movie follows the three as they decide to keep the money.

Adapted from Scott B. Smith's novel of the same name, A Simple Plan's premise is very straightforward, but the escalation comes as a surprise. The movie is brilliantly paced, with the first half loaded with psychological tension and suspense and the second half filled with action and consequences.

Thornton's character, Jacob, is layered and tragic. He first appeared to be somewhat goofy and greedy, fast to side with Lou who proposed on taking the 4.4 million dollars. His brother Hank appeared to be the more law-abiding person, who was against stealing. However, as the film progresses, viewers will soon find the roles reversed.

A Simple Plan received high praise from critics, including Roger Ebert, when it came out in 1998. In addition to Thornton's Best Supporting Actor nomination, Smith's script also received a nomination for Best Adapted Screenplay. It's hard to imagine another crime thriller matching the brilliance of Sling Blade, but A Simple Plan comes very close.

Billy Bob Thornton's performance is nuanced and deliberate. He makes Jacob seem like the least likable and least intelligent brother at first, but there are moments where the characters manage to surprise viewers. Jacob has depth and a conscience, which makes his tragic ending heartbreaking.

What's even more uncanny is how much the Landman actor seems to reveal about Jacob and Hank's father through his delivery of dialogue. He peels back the layers of an easily overlooked, normal-day guy and reveals the person underneath, who is good-natured, kind, observant, and almost too disillusioned and witty for his own good. Even though Jacob isn't the lead character, he manages to steal all the spotlight.

A Simple Plan officially joined Tubi's free streaming platform in November, in addition to Pluto TV, Paramount+, and YouTube subscriptions. Despite the 1998 neo-noir crime thriller being a masterpiece, it's often overlooked in both Sam Raimi and Billy Bob Thornton's filmographies.

Several factors have contributed to its lack of hype. Known for directing horror and action movies such as Evil Dead and the original Spider-Man trilogy, Raimi has a kinetic visual style. The film's slow-burning, atmospheric style takes a detour from the director's usual high-energy, flashy aesthetics.

It also came out two years after Fargo, which established a cultural touchstone by blending dark comedy with a snowbound Midwestern crime aesthetic. A Simple Plan is often considered a darker and bleaker cousin of the Coen brothers' film. While A Simple Plan was critically acclaimed, the movie wasn't a huge box office hit. It struggled in theaters and grossed only $16 million.

Robert Pattinson's casting as James Bond has left Google feeling shaken, not stirred.

Following the announcement that Pattinson would be our next 007, Google introduced a new Bond-themed Easter egg. If you search up Pattinson's name, the search results shake a little, and a small twist of lemon falls from the top of the screen to rest daintily on Pattinson's head.

The Easter egg is a nod to James Bond's classic cocktail order: a vodka martini, shaken, not stirred. Google has essentially turned Pattinson into the cocktail he'll be ordering onscreen.

Pattinson's casting as Bond came after weeks of speculation and reported screen tests for potential Bond candidates, including Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, Josh O'Connor, and Nicholas Hoult.

Pattinson will star in the as-yet unnamed 26th Bond film, the first since Daniel Craig took his last bow in 2021's No Time To Die. Dune helmer Denis Villeneuve is set to direct. The pair have also worked together on the upcoming Dune: Part Three. Villeneuve and Pattinson's Bond film does not yet have a release date.

Bond won't be the first franchise Pattinson has led to be commemorated with a Google Easter egg. In 2022, tied to The Batman's release, if you searched terms such as "Bruce Wayne" or "Gotham City," a yellow Bat-Signal would appear alongside the results. Clicking on it turned the page into a moody Gotham night, complete with thunder, the Bat-Signal, and Batman swinging around the screen.

Plus, if you search up "Twilight" now, you'll be treated to a small line of scrolling text that reads "hoa hoa hoa season," flanked by falling leaf emojis. The Easter egg references Twilight fans' loving nickname for fall, drawn from the so-called "hoa hoa hoa" vocals of Blue Foundation's "Eyes on Fire" from the Twilight soundtrack. Netflix's New Psychological Thriller Series With Near Perfect RT Audience Score Defines A Major Era

Netflix's new psychological thriller series that has become a global streaming sensation comes to cement a 2026 trend and define a major era for the streamer. Netflix has always had success when it comes to thrillers, which is no surprise, as the first Netflix Original series to make waves, both in terms of viewership and awards, was Kevin Spacey's House of Cards.

The political thriller series has since ended, but it went on a successful 6-season run, being a long-running hit for the streamer. Over the years, Netflix's hall of thriller series has been upgraded with a wide array of shows, including political, spy, legal, medical, and many other thriller subgenres, with all of those shows fitting Netflix's patented binge release model.

Penn Badgley's You, at five seasons, Money Heist, also at five seasons, The Diplomat, recently renewed for season 5, and others have gotten to go on long runs at the streamer. ‘The Odyssey’ Cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema Breaks Down Filming Climactic Thunderstorm Scene in Imax

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Christopher Nolan shot “The Odyssey” to be experienced on the biggest screen possible so audiences could feel the full impact and be fully immersed in that experience — so everything was shot practically.

One of the most challenging sequences to pull off was the film’s final storm scene as Matt Damon’s Odysseus faces the wrath of Poseidon and the full force of Zeus. It was to be climactic and intense, and Odysseus loses all his men as a result of the final insult to the gods. “We had been talking about going out there to sea, sit on real boats and shoot on Imax,” cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema explains.

A large amount of the film was shot on the open ocean, where Nolan, his cast and crew spent four months capturing how hard the journey would have been as they endured crashing waves. And while the climactic moment ended up being shot in Los Angeles, Nolan didn’t spare any expense in pulling off the sequence, relying instead on old-fashioned filmmaking.Speaking with Variety for Inside the Frame, Van Hoytema explains the scene provided a challenge: “How to replicate something and make it feel visceral and real? How can we do that and avoid a huge amount of CG?”Two decommissioned jet engines were used as wind machines. Van Hoytema’s chief lighting technician and gaffer Adam Chambers built light boxes that would emulate thunder and lightning. The boat was then put on a gimbal, all while being shot on an Imax camera. “It was like shooting a real storm, just under safety supervision,” Van Hoytema says.He adds, “We did a lot of different setups in that whole scene. It is designed to feel extremely chaotic; it’s designed to sort of not understand really what’s going on.”The lighting design was something he also had to think about. “It was interesting because I was thinking, if you’re out at sea at night 2,000 years ago, without electricity and you end up in a thunderstorm, what would that feel like? I was thinking, if you can see everything around you, that’s going to look fake.”

A CGI storm didn’t feel right because the entire crew had been meticulous about keeping everything as grounded and as experiential as possible. Van Hoytema says the team built overhead lighting rigs that were approximately 60×30 feet, hanging on cranes above the ship. “It meant that you would see flashes that progressively move from behind them to the front of them, as it opens up the sky. “What you see is short bursts and there’s just darkness or a little bit of a glow. It’s like trying to see this relentless endless sea, this weather, this saltwater and they’re just so close to death. It’s extremely claustrophobic at the same time.”

When it came to framing Odysseus, Van Hoytema was wired in to keep him in place on the gimbal. His Imax camera on his shoulder was also wired in.

“I was not trying to be in any way precious about framing whatsoever. I was just trying through with a jet engine blowing, gallons of water in my face. I was literally just trying to identify if it was even Matt Damon in the shot. It was extremely visceral, and it’s almost hard to grasp. It was not a situation in which you could just start a take or not. Every take was an event.”

Van Hoytema says the camera starts to roll and the jet engines start. “They rise, and at some point, they sound like two fighter jets are next to your ear. And it gets really wild because they start moving the gimbal as well, and you’re holding on for your life. The camera gets turned; it’s the original Imax camera, not the blimp and the Imax camera is very loud. However, in that situation, you don’t even know if it’s turned on. You do a take, and you have a crazy powerful adrenaline rush.”

In contrast, Van Hoytema recalled a moment when the crew was out to sea, in open waters and a pod of bottlenose dolphins appeared. It was completely unplanned.

Van Hoytema recalls, “It’s interesting because it was also pretty violent that day. It was very harsh weather and I remember a lot of people on the crew got seasick. As we started filming, dolphins started to jump out of the water. What is interesting is that it symbolizes that no matter what the weather does, we take the weather as it comes and accept it as the reality you work in that day.”

The 2000s were huge years for superhero cinema—arguably the biggest for the landscape of all time. It was in this decade that superhero movies, as audiences know them today, were defined, and the standards were set. The years 2000 to 2009 are some of the most significant years and helped birth some of the most popular franchises in cinematic history as a whole.

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man series, the X-Men franchise, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight series and so much more all got their big breaks in this era. Without these abundantly important 10 years, superhero movies would not even be close to where they are today. Their significance wouldn't even be close to what they are, and events like Avengers: Endgame most likely would not have ever happened or had the success they did without these foundational projects that are, to simply state, masterpieces.

Spider-Man is the most profitable superhero on the planet—he has been for quite some time now—and that meant that no one was surprised that, when the technology was finally there, he'd get a live-action movie. Come 2002, fans were finally treated to Sam Raimi's Spider-Man, which seriously rocked the world. Sure, X-Men came out just a few years earlier, but Spider-Man really helped drive home and fully prove that comic book characters could work on the big screen and the Hollywood landscape.

This 2002 flick was an all-time smash hit and proved that superhero movies could be genuinely amazing financial investments for big studios, with the project grossing an astounding about $825 million worldwide, with a phenomenal $114.8 million opening weekend. It was the first film to ever cross $100 million in its opening weekend and if that's not proof of how great this movie is, then what could be? The performances across the board from especially the likes of Willem Dafoe are wonderful, the directing from Raimi shows how unashamed he was in the source material, and the CGI work was revolutionary for the times.

The first Blade movie was a big moment for comic book movies, being one of the first ones to ever be rated-R and be a success. So, when it came time for the sequel, Blade II, the stakes were high. Thankfully, legendary director Guillermo del Toro and leading man Wesley Snipes, delivered a project that was a great success. It turned the franchise from what the franchise was in Blade to a high-speed, thrilling, gothic, and all-around fire flick.

Guillermo del Toro continues to be a masterclass of a director here with his emphasis on practical effects, making the more horror-based elements of the project sell better than they ever could in CGI. Snipes brings his everything to the role—as per usual—and keeps Blade feeling like a character that the audience genuinely wants to keep watching.

The first X-Men movie was incredibly important. It established these amazing characters and began to send a great message about the effects of discrimination and bigotry. Mutants became the talk of the town, and Hugh Jackman stepped into the boots of Wolverine for the very first time. However, it's highly agreed upon that the sequel, X2: X-Men United, is the superior film for a plethora of reasons.

This is, without a doubt, the Star Wars: Episode V — The Empire Strikes Back for the X-Men franchise. The stakes are raised significantly, and the themes and messaging are more prominent than ever before. It has one of the best opening scenes in a superhero movie—Nightcrawler (Alan Cumming) infiltrating the White House to attack the President (Cotter Smith), brings more classic X-Men characters to the forefront, has a great villain in William Stryker (Brian Cox), and so much more.

2008 was arguably the biggest year in superhero cinema for quite some time because of two major movies: The Dark Knight and none other than the beginning of the MCU itself, Iron Man. Brought to life by the amazing Jon Favreau in the director's chair and Robert Downey Jr. as the Armored Avenger, this 2008 project birthed one of the most popular and profitable cinematic franchises ever put to screen—one that still runs in massive success to this very day.

This served not only as a major comeback for Downey Jr.'s career, but was a huge step in CGI for filmmaking, as well. Iron Man looks perfect in pretty much every shot, and is still looked back on to have better-looking CGI than some modern Marvel films. The biggest standout, however, is the performance from Downey Jr. He solidifies himself at Tony Stark and continued to play him for over 10 years after the release of Iron Man, now even returning to the MCU again in 2026—albeit as a new character, Doctor Doom. This movie is the majority reason the superhero movie landscape has become what it is today.

Alongside the main Apatow offerings you had those which he produced such as "Anchorman: The Legend of Ron Burgundy," or those made by his collaborators, such as "Superbad." Then, there were those films that featured Apatow associates but were made without his direct involvement. The brilliant "I Love You, Man" is one example which, despite being criminally overlooked, remains one of Paul Rudd's best films.

As does 2008's "Role Models," which saw Rudd team up with Seann William Scott for a film that, like pretty much every comedy movie released at the time, was defined by the improvisational, R-rated humor that had made Apatow's films so successful. This particular movie, however, paired Apatow allies Rudd and Christopher Mintz-Plasse with former members of sketch comedy troupe The State in director David Wain and writer/actor Ken Marino.

As someone who watched "Role Models" when it came out, I simply cannot believe this movie is 18 years old. Despite being less well known than the aforementioned "Knocked Up" and its ilk, "Role Models" became instantly beloved by me and my friends upon its release. Almost two decades later, it's strange to think that an entire generation has grown up without films such as this shaping their sense of humor. If you happen to be part of that group, now's the time to acquaint yourself with this  oft-overlooked comedy. If, like me, you remember "Role Models" fondly, you'll be happy to hear it holds up.

Like so many of the best comedies of the 2000s, "Role Models" succeeds because despite being an R-rated comedy with frequent gross-out gags and unapologetically immature humor, it's also a surprisingly tender story with its heart in the right place.

Paul Rudd plays Danny who, alongside Seann William Scott's Anson Wheeler, works as a promoter for the energy drink "Minotaur." Danny feels deeply unfulfilled and has become deeply cynical as a result, spending most of his time correcting people on their speech. This causes his girlfriend, Beth (Elizabeth Banks), to break up with him, after which Danny loses it during a presentation at a high school. His freak out leads to both he and Anson being assigned to community service at Sturdy Wings, a big brother-style program where the pair become mentors to two "littles."

Anson is paired with Ronnie (Bobb'e J. Thompson) a foul-mouthed kid with no friends, while Danny is given Augie Farks (Christopher Mintz-Plasse), a teenager obsessed with his medieval LARP group. At first, Danny and Anson are merely going through the motions. But over time they develop a bond with their "littles" — one which just might melt Danny's cold, cynical heart.

The secret star of "Role Models" is Joe Lo Truglio, who plays Kuzzik, a particularly dedicated LARP-er who, if you watch the bloopers, made it almost impossible for Rudd to get through any of his scenes without breaking. But there are a ton of standout performances here, from A.D. Miles as overly earnest Sturdy Wings counsellor Martin Gary to Ken Jeong as the self-serious LARP king Argotron and Jane Lynch as a former addict turned Sturdy Wings leader.

"Role Models" hit theaters in November 2008 and grossed a solid $92.6 million on a $28 million budget. It also garnered positive reviews, earning a 77% "certified fresh" rating on Rotten Tomatoes. Dana Stevens of Slate found "Role Models" to be "strewn with enough serendipities to make it a worthy variation on the dudes-grow-up genre," while the San Francisco Chronicle's Peter Hartlaub noted how, while Seann William Scott and Paul Rudd were "from two different moviemaking cliques" they ultimately "get along fine in a Bing Crosby/David Bowie kind of way."

Meanwhile, Rafer Guzman of Newsday noted how Rudd, who co-wrote the script with David Wain, Timothy Dowling, and Ken Marino, had "clearly learned a few things working on Judd Apatow comedies." As such, he was well-established as one of the most prominent figures of the 2000s R-rated comedy boom.

No, I didn’t forget about The Dark Knight Rises

With Robert Pattinson confirmed to be the next James Bond, one of the biggest takeaways is that Matt Reeves’ The Batman series will likely end with The Batman: Part II. Pattinson is signed on for three films as Bond, and while that’s one less film than the four-film deal that Amazon MGM Studios was seeking for the other Bond contenders, it’s pretty hard to imagine that Pattinson will juggle the role of 007 with an ongoing commitment to DC — much less Battinson’s integration into the wider DCU, which some DC fans had hoped for. However, the loss is even bigger than you might realize, as this will be the third time in a row that Batman fans will be robbed of a good Batman trilogy.

Tim Burton’s 1989 film Batman, of course, revolutionized the character of Batman and comic book movies as a whole. Before then, the general public’s awareness of Batman was mostly associated with the campy, comedic Adam West Batman, but Burton changed that and presented a dark, heavily stylized version starring Michael Keaton. He then followed it up with Batman Returns, which many still consider to be one of the best Batman films ever.

Warner Bros., however, felt it was too dark and got rid of Burton for the third film, replacing him with Joel Schumacher. Keaton followed Burton out the door, not wanting to continue as Batman without him. Batman Forever, starring Val Kilmer, was the result, and while that film has its defenders, it felt like a different franchise and a different Batman altogether. The same can be said for Batman and Robin.

Then in 2005 came Batman Begins, directed by Christopher Nolan and starring Christian Bale. Taking a far more grounded approach to the character, Batman Begins turned Batman’s origin into an epic film saga, where it had only been relegated to brief flashbacks in previous films. Nolan topped himself with The Dark Knight by putting Batman in the center of a gritty crime drama with Heath Ledger’s Oscar-winning turn as the Joker. For a second time, a Batman film revolutionized comic book films. Nearly 20 years later, many still cite The Dark Knight as the greatest comic book movie ever made.

Unfortunately, Nolan’s third Batman film wasn’t nearly as good as the first two. Perhaps because of Ledger’s untimely death before the release of The Dark Knight, Nolan at first seemed disinterested in a third film, and it is generally understood that he had to rework the story he had in mind to remove the Joker from its narrative. Regardless of the reasons why, The Dark Knight Rises is a deeply uneven, disorganized film that most view as the low point of Nolan’s Batman, if not an outright bad film (I tend to think it is quite bad).

After Nolan’s trilogy concluded, Ben Affleck became the next Batman, in both Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice as well as the various versions of Justice League. Regardless of what you think of these films — I personally find them insufferable — they were deeply divisive. Despite an Affleck-led Batman film being rumored for a time, we never got him in any standalone films, much less three of them.

In 2022, The Batman offered new hope. While some were skeptical of Pattinson as Batman, he proved to be a really solid take on the Dark Knight, which prioritized Batman’s skills as a detective, something missing from previous Batman films. He was complemented by Paul Dano’s frightening take as the Riddler and Colin Farrell’s scene-stealing Penguin.

Reeves also presented the best-ever take on Gotham City. While Burton’s Gotham was bold and interesting, neither of his films made Gotham feel like a real city. Nolan’s Gotham, with its unconcealed usage of Chicago and New York City, had little personality at all. But Reeves’ Gotham felt like a broken city that desperately needed a Batman to come to its rescue, with production combining parts of Chicago and Scotland to give the city a more naturalistic, gothic vibe. The film’s nearly three-hour runtime only assisted in the viewer’s immersion into Reeves’ Gotham.

But three hours in Reeves’ Gotham still wasn’t enough. The Batman Part II, which seems to be telling a story with Two-Face as a villain, was delayed for years due to a personal issue going on with Matt Reeves. Filming finally began in June, but in early October, Reeves had to step away, most likely for the same family issue. Steven Soderbergh then stepped in to finish the filming Reeves began, which is currently ongoing. While I applaud Reeves for prioritizing his family over a film, this means that it will be six years between The Batman and its sequel and it's hard to imagine that Skydance, the new owners of DC Studios, will want to keep Batman on the shelf for that long again, nor do I think they’ll want to schedule around Pattinson’s Bond films.

And, while it never seemed like a real possibility, I was still hoping that Pattinson would get folded into what James Gunn and Peter Safran had been building for the DCU if only because I don’t think it's all that easy to keep finding great guys to be Batman, and while he wasn’t quite as good as Bale — or even Keaton — Pattinson truly is/was a great Batman. It’s just too bad that his take on the character will be cut short before we get a proper trilogy.

Hollywood CEO Paydays May Get Harder to Track as Federal Regulator Proposes Disclosure Revamp

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Fewer executives will be tracked, and less data will be shared if the SEC's proposed disclosure changes move forward.

It’s no secret that Hollywood CEO pay is often a hot-button story.

Chief execs in media, entertainment, and technology are annually among the most highly compensated people on Earth, with mid-eight-figure pay packages a common occurrence and nine-figure paydays increasingly common.

But those days might be ending soon. Not the paydays, just the disclosures, should new rules from the Securities and Exchange Commission go into effect.

The SEC on Friday proposed a revamp of its executive compensation disclosure rules, simplifying things while also changing what those disclosures will look like. The result will be that pay is disclosed for fewer executives, with only the CEO and CFO likely to be disclosed should the rule change pass (no more COO, general counsel or other C-suite pay), while the way the pay packages are disclosed shifts. In addition, the threshold for perquisites is being raised from $10,000 to $150,000, meaning that some perks like club memberships will no longe require disclosure, and companies will get more discretion to determine if something is a perk at all, such as CEO security costs.

At the heart of the changes are simpler disclosures, with three tables: “Earned Compensation Table, Granted Compensation Table, and Status of Equity Awards Table” with a particular emphasis on salary, bonus and equity grants.

“As applied to executive compensation disclosure, a panelist at last year’s roundtable aptly analogized current Item 402 to an ice cream parlor that carries rum raisin and maple walnut; flavors desired by only a single customer,” SEC chairman Paul Adkins said in a statement about the proposed changes. “The proposed amendments would, if adopted, revise Item 402 to focus on an objective standard regarding needs of the reasonable investor, rather than on the specialized tastes of a specific investor.”

Executives, investor relations and corporate communications teams are surely cheering the move, which will result in less gargantuan numbers than previous disclosures. Some of those changes may be warranted: After all, equity may not end up vesting if certain metrics are not achieved, and a high strike price can invalidate whatever value is in some shares. Those are things that CEOs have complained about for years: The news coverage has a headline number, but their actual take-home could be lower (or much higher!) depending on the performance of the shares.

The new disclosure structure will eliminate the paper gain spikes associated with unvested equity, and likely to be closer to what an executive really takes home, though if the old model sometimes overstated thing, the new disclosure may understate things, if the stocks consistently rise.

There will also be changes to the employee-to-CEO pay ratio, which focuses it on U.S. full-time employees and could make for more favorable comparisons.

And the changes to perquisite reporting mean that things like security, parking, country club memberships and even some private jet use could no longer be disclosed, all of which would be music to CEO ears. Even if those disclosures sometimes help tell a bigger story.

Burn tells Variety he experienced a mild version of hyperacusis in his late teens. “People who suffer from it chronically go through a devastating experience of not being able to possibly recover or go outside. I had this situation where I witnessed an explosion, and I went 100% deaf for about 20 minutes. When my hearing came back, I had hyperacusis and extreme sensitivity to everyday sounds.”

When Burn received the script, his first response was, “I know what this is.” His next step was researching what this world sounded like. To understand that soundscape, Burn had microphones placed inside his eardrums and went about his daily life. “I was recording things from my ears’ point of view, and playing it back later to understand what things become fairly loud that your brain would normally dial out because they’re not threatening, such as putting a pan in the dishwasher or even a shower tap — everyday sounds that can break through.”

The goal was to teach audiences how Niki interacts with his environment and what the dangers are. Burn worked to create various sound levels, including one from Niki’s point of view that allowed audiences to understand his world and another where Niki has ear defenders on, which help restrict sound. One example Burn gives is when Niki sits at a traffic light and a motorbike comes alongside him. “That’s really about teaching the audience that there are these different states in which he can be listening to things,” Burn adds. “The aim was to have the audience experience it alongside him, which is obviously with hyperacusis. It’s not like deafness or anything. It’s a situation where everyday sounds can become dangerously loud for you.”

Burn drove that home in the Dolby mix of the film, pushing the sound design. He and Roher sat in the mix, fine-tuning every decibel. “The different points of view of Niki’s hearing were a fundamental part of whether or not the film worked. I believe we cracked that. The Dolby Atmos mix was a further development of that because it gave us the ability to predominantly play sound at a much greater volume all around us.”

The 8 Best TV Dramas From Before the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We are currently undeniably living in the era of streaming, but for the first few decades of its existence as a medium, network television reigned supreme. Since network shows are broadcast on a strict weekly timetable, they're usually built on very particular structures potentially containing built-in breaks before cutting to commercials, cliffhangers, and very consistent pacing. As television has evolved, so too have the characteristics of network series changed.

However, there are eight particular broadcast television dramas which are easily the greatest in history prior to the advent of streaming. Whether we're talking about classics like Twin Peaks or modern masterpieces like Game of Thrones, these are the kinds of generation-defining dramas that anyone who loves small-screen content should watch at least once in their lives. There is no understanding network television without experiencing these masterpieces.

The 1990s gave birth to a second Golden Age of television, and were an absolutely groundbreaking time for auteur-driven television. The medium shifted away from traditional network-controlled episodic formulas, and instead began embracing more serialized storytelling in line with the vision of creatives like David Lynch. Indeed, Lynch and Mark Frost's Twin Peaks is one of the most foundational auteur-driven shows of all time.

It's one of those network TV shows that are visually stunning, impeccably written, masterfully directed, and pretty much impeccable in every way that matters. Though the show saw a dip in quality right in the middle of season two, this has never stopped fans from greatly enjoying the show's intentionally uncanny tone, campy supernatural elements, and brilliant mix of melodramatic soap opera tropes and Lynch's signature style of surrealism.

Never in history has the small screen seen a prequel series even remotely as incredible as Vince Gilligan and Peter Gould's Better Call Saul. Making a prequel to a network drama widely considered the single greatest TV show of all time was never going to be an easy task, and as such, fans were very much skeptical when this project was announced. In the end, though, Better Call Saul ended up being one of the best TV shows since 2010, a near-perfect masterpiece through and through.

Better Call Saul is both an exceptional prequel adding a lot of depth and texture to the Breaking Bad world, and an incredible crime drama and legal thriller in its own right. With some masterful character writing, an excellent balance between poignant tragedy and genuinely laugh-out-loud-funny comedy, and the faultless work of an ensemble led by Bob Odenkirk at the top of his game, it's no wonder why it's the most acclaimed TV prequel in history.

Genre stories were always of vital importance to network television, and science fiction in particular was a key driver of technical innovation and a great platform for social commentary throughout the broadcast era of TV. Over the years, never has the small screen seen a sci-fi show more foundational or widely influential than Rod Serling's legendary anthology series The Twilight Zone, a gem that's essential viewing for anyone who loves sci-fi.

Serling wanted to make a show that would allow him to tackle controversial yet essential social and political issues, but since network executives refused to let him write direct socially-conscious shows, he decided to circumvent that issue. So, he made a sci-fi series that disguised its treatment of taboo topics—like racism and McCarthyism—as sci-fi, fantasy, and horror. Like any anthology series, Twilight Zone had its fair share of ups and downs throughout its run, but it's packed with some of the greatest episodes in the history of anthology TV series.

HBO was launched all the way back in 1972, but it wouldn't be until the '90s and 2000s that they would become the kings of broadcast television and the pioneers of what is now called "prestige TV." Case in point: Band of Brothers, a war drama miniseries executive-produced by Tom Hanks and Steven Spielberg that has been the highest-rated miniseries in history on IMDb for quite some time. It's easy to see why: miniseries or otherwise, history has never seen a war drama quite like this on TV.

It's one of the best HBO shows shorter than 20 episodes, a hard-hitting and faithful adaptation of the non-fiction book of the same name by historian Stephen E. Ambrose. Treating World War II with the same kind of sensitivity and raw realism as Saving Private Ryan, Band of Brothers is uncompromisingly realistic and respectful toward its characters, with a potent shift in narrative perspective every episode that gives every minute of its run a consistently surprising, thought-provoking feel.

Though its penultimate season was divisive and its final few episodes were nothing short of universally panned, Game of Thrones proves that a network drama doesn't need to be absolutely perfect in order to be counted among the best in history. Indeed, it would be silly to pretend that this HBO adaptation of George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire book series, which was a gargantuan pop culture phenomenon over the course of its eight seasons, is anything less than legendary.

Where can one even begin singing the praises of such a praise-worthy masterpiece? For one, the production values are off-the-charts, including some of the greatest visuals and music in the history of fantasy television. Add to that some exceptional writing, an unrelentingly bleak and violent tone, and one of the best TV cast ensembles of the 2010s, and you get a genre-defining masterpiece whose quality far transcends any mistakes that its final season may have made.

As if any more proof were needed that there's just no matching HBO's quality and legacy when it comes to the greatest dramas in the history of network television, there's also The Wire. Creator David Simon had spent 12 years covering crime and city systems as a police reporter for The Baltimore Sun, while co-writer Ed Burns had worked inside the Baltimore Police Department as a homicide detective. Inspired by their experiences and what they'd seen, the duo co-created The Wire.

It's a scathing study and deconstruction of five particular institutional layers of Baltimore: the illegal drug trade, the port system, the government, education, and the media. But far from feeling niche or exploitative, The Wire instead delivers the most complete portrait of American urban decay ever put to screen. Full of richly layered characters and potent thematic work, it's one of the many pinnacles of 21st-century crime drama television.

Network television would simply have never become what it is today had it not been for The Sopranos. In television's history, there's a "before The Sopranos" and an "after The Sopranos," as David Chase's crime drama completely re-defined what the medium was able to do. Never before had television seen such darkly antiheroic characters, such cinematic quality, and such cleanly serialized storytelling. Thus, prestige television was born, and the small screen was never the same again.

But though it was a pioneer of this new version of network television, The Sopranos doesn't feel dated in the slightest all these many years later; on the contrary, it's still generally considered the best HBO show to date. Bolstered by the phenomenal writing, James Gandolfini's powerhouse lead performance, and some of the richest psychological depth that a broadcast drama has ever seen, it's a must-see for anyone who loves television.

Over a decade after its conclusion, most people still widely refer to Vince Gilligan's Breaking Bad as the single greatest TV drama in history, and it will take something very special to ever dethrone it. Gilligan came up with the concept after fellow writer Tom Schnauz joked that they should solve their lack of work by buying up an RV and setting up a mobile meth lab. The idea stuck in his mind for years, and would eventually transformed into the most complex, cinematic, and irresistibly riveting drama that television has ever produced.

Breaking Bad is one of those drama shows that have aged like fine wine, a masterpiece whose every element works in flawless concert with the others to deliver the most enthralling experience possible. The production values are fantastic, the ensemble is full of actors delivering career-best work, and the character writing is the dramatic fuel that pushes the narrative momentum forward throughout. From top to bottom and beginning to end, Breaking Bad is fully worthy of being officially named the greatest TV drama of all time.

Ukraine and Europe have long been concerned about the current US administration's commitment to European security and its softening towards the Kremlin.

Representatives of Britain, Nato and various EU countries were also in Abu Dhabi and met with US and Ukrainian negotiators, but the Kremlin - with whom the EU does not officially communicate - said its delegation had not had any contact with them.

Moscow has been dismissive of European involvement, although Europeans have long argued that they are central to any post-war dynamics on their continent.

Many - particularly in eastern Europe - feel their security is linked to that of Ukraine. Agreements cannot be implemented "if no consensus can be reached among Nato members," a German government spokesperson said on Wednesday.

Kushner said the US was keen to bring the Europeans on board and said that getting them "added to the format" would be on the agenda for his talks with Putin.

There is also disagreement on whether discussions on a future security framework should be preceded by a ceasefire, as the Europeans and Kyiv would want, or whether the fighting only stops after an agreement has been reached, as the Kremlin has insisted.

Any agreement on a peacetime security framework, however, is contingent on the war ending, Kushner noted. But that cannot occur "until there's an agreement on territory and on all other bilateral issues," he added.

Since talks began last year, a core obstacle to any settlement has been Ukraine's eastern region of Donetsk, which is mostly occupied by Russia. Moscow has demanded that Ukraine relinquish the parts of Donetsk it still holds, while Kyiv wants to freeze the contact line to maintain control over those areas.

This matter was the "hardest", Kushner acknowledged, while Zelensky's chief of staff and negotiator Kyrylo Budanov refused to be drawn on whether Russia was still demanding a Ukrainian withdrawal from the region.

"These deals are very hard and complex, and this one is very emotional, very complex," Kushner said. "The reality is there is no trust right now between the sides and that is to be expected after a conflict like this."

Kidnapper, 74, escapes from house arrest in Argentina by abseiling from balcony

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Leonardo Bertulazzi was first arrested in Buenos Aires in 2002

A 74-year-old Italian man who was found guilty of a kidnapping carried out by a far-left militant group in the 1970s has escaped while under house arrest in Argentina and is on the run.

Leonardo Bertulazzi used a harness to abseil down from a fifth-floor balcony in the capital, Buenos Aires.

His escape was not immediately registered because he had tampered with his electronic ankle monitor so that it continued to transmit body temperature measurements even after he had prised it open and absconded.

Bertulazzi already spent decades as a fugitive after he was sentenced in absentia in Italy for the 1977 kidnapping of Italian naval engineer Pietro Costa.

He was awaiting extradition to Italy and was being monitored by the Argentine ministry of security through an ankle tag which registers the wearer's body temperature and movements.

Officials said that they had become suspicious when they noticed that the tag had remained stationary.

When they entered the fifth-floor apartment in the centre of Buenos Aires, Bertulazzi was gone, but the ankle monitor remained.

Wary of Bertulazzi's past with the Marxist guerilla group the Red Brigades and his bomb-making skills, police called in explosives experts in case it had been rigged to detonate, according to Argentine newspaper ClarÃ­n.

However, the experts found that while Bertulazzi had tampered with the device, he had done so to prevent the alarm from sounding once he removed it.

Italian media say that his extradition to Italy had been imminent after his last appeal was rejected.

Bertulazzi was first arrested after using a false passport to cross into Argentina from neighbouring Chile in 2002.

However, he was granted refugee status in 2004 and lived freely in the South American country until his re-arrest in 2024, when the current right-wing president, Javier Milei, took office.

Milei's government said at the time that "Bertulazzi is responsible for crimes that undermined democratic values and the lives of many victims".

He was a member of the Red Brigades, which kidnapped and killed a number of state officials in the 1970s and '80s.

Bertulazzi was part of the Red Brigades' Genoa section that kidnapped Pietro Costa on the street and then held him for 81 days before receiving a big ransom.

The money it made from the kidnapping was then used to buy a flat in Rome, which was used in 1978 for the Red Brigades' most notorious crime, the kidnap and murder of Italian former Prime Minister, Aldo Moro.

Moro was abducted when his car was ambushed on the way to the opening of parliament. He was held in the flat for 54 days before he was shot and his body abandoned in the boot of a Renault car in the centre of Rome.
