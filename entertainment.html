HBO Max’s New 6-Part Rom-Com Is a Masterclass in Breaking the Genre’s Rules

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

Romantic comedies tend to follow a familiar formula, where two people meet, fall in love, face a few obstacles, and eventually find their way back to each other. The best ones might even convince you that love can survive just about anything. But what happens when the relationship falls apart before the story really gets going? That's the question at the heart of Break Clause, a new British romantic comedy that finds plenty of laughs, awkward encounters, and genuine heartbreak in the aftermath of a breakup. Now streaming on HBO Max, the six-part series makes a compelling case for why sometimes the hardest part of ending a relationship isn't letting go. It's figuring out who gets to keep the apartment.

Created by Jess Bray and directed by Alice Snedden, Break Clause stars Samuel Bottomley as Ben and Lara Ricote as Lil, a young couple whose plans for a fresh start in London fall apart almost immediately. After four years together at university in Leeds, Lil lands a job in the capital and convinces Ben to make the move with her, but Ben has been keeping a secret. After a drunken indiscretion on their final night in Leeds, he eventually confesses to cheating on Lil, leaving the couple newly single just as they've signed a rental agreement.

Unfortunately, neither can afford to just pack up and leave. With six months of the lease to run, Ben and Lil have no choice but to become roommates, creating an uncomfortable situation where both must live separate lives while sharing a living space, kitchen, and bathroom facilities. And that's where Break Clause excels: instead of treating a break-up as the end of a romantic story, it shows the complications that follow.

One of the story's strongest points is that it clearly shows the ridiculousness of Ben and Lil's situation while still acknowledging the feelings beneath it. Because of this, the show can turn the storyline by focusing on two people who have already broken up instead of uniting them, as most romantic comedies usually do. They are no longer close, but they still find it hard to stop acting like a couple, at least for a bit.

Four love stories, four completely different ideas of what it means to be somebody's person. This is the one that matches how you actually fall, and what it would ask of you.

You do not fall for strangers. You fall for the person you have already been talking to for years, which is the slowest and by some distance the most durable way anybody has found to do this.

You want a partner, not an audience. Somebody who wants their own life as badly as you want yours, and who would never ask you to be smaller to make the relationship easier.

The intensity was never the problem. The secrecy is. You are drawn to a love that lives entirely in private, and to the particular ache of wanting somebody you are not supposed to want.

You do not do this cautiously. When it happens for you it is immediate and total and it rearranges everything, and you would not accept a smaller version of it.

Notably, the idea of getting used to failure also appears inthe breakup. Generally, when people go to a different city, they think that they are starting a new chapter of life. However, Ben and Lil face unemployment and financial issues and conclude that their relationship was not as strong as they thought. The rental contract complicates the situation by creating ethical problems for everyone involved.

The story's financial aspect adds another layer to the main conflict. Ben, who is trying to become a writer and struggles to find work, starts looking for food in garbage bins to save money and even considers participating in clinical research. Lil, meanwhile, has already secured a position at an architectural firm, creating an imbalance that becomes another source of tension between them. Ben’s negative attitude, tied to their financial situation, can sometimes spark arguments and problems in their relationship.

The central relationship wouldn't work without the two actors playing it. Bottomley and Ricote give Ben and Lil enough chemistry to make their shared history believable, even when they're at their least compatible. Ben is withdrawn, insecure, and often too preoccupied with his own shortcomings to recognize what's happening around him. Lil is much more outgoing, approaching life with an enthusiasm that occasionally gets her into trouble. Their personalities make for an entertaining contrast, particularly when they're forced to deal with the same uncomfortable situations.

One unique performer in the cast is Ricote. She plays Lil in a way that captures the character's positive, contagious energy instead of portraying her as a peculiar person with a variety of characteristics. Although her positivity is lovable, it also creates room for mistakes. For instance, when Lil tries to attend a street dance but then realizes that nobody else is interested, it makes the character so unique. While Bottomley often explores Ben's awkwardness and embarrassment, he also shows his attempts to reconcile with life in a ridiculous yet realistic way. Both make their characters more memorable and show why it matters whether they find a solution.

There's plenty of material here beyond whether Ben and Lil will reconcile, from disastrous dinner parties to uncomfortable reunions with university friends, Break Clause finds comedy in everyday situations that become considerably more complicated when your ex is still living across the hall. Its humor can be wonderfully strange and deadpan, but Bray also understands when to give a scene space rather than turn every uncomfortable moment into a punchline.

Ben and Lil are not at home just waiting for their lease to end; they are trying to come to grips with what “growing up” means when the future they planned for no longer exists. The world around them has changed, and their friends are moving on with their careers and their lives, which makes them think about the future and their relationships with those who were once dear to them.

This six-part comedy turns ridiculous movie pitches into some of the year’s funniest television.

In the end, the series resorts to clichéd romantic comedy tropes it seems to want to avoid at the beginning, but that doesn't have to bea flaw. The mere potential for reconciliation gives the narrative an emotional arc, even as Ben and Lil navigate new relationships and increasingly muddled emotions. The issue is not only whether they still love one another but also whether they should even want the kind of relationship that they had.

Why The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping Is The Darkest Movie In The Series

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The following post contains spoilers for "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping."

It's rare for a franchise to get better after 4 or 5 movies, especially when they are prequels that explore questions no one needed answered. It didn't work for "Star Wars," it didn't work for "Harry Potter," and it shouldn't work for "Hunger Games." At best, these prequels just introduced characters that don't matter in the large scheme of things because everything they do or accomplish gets erased in the original story, at worst they try so hard to answer questions no one cares about that it ends up tarnishing the original work.

And yet, when it comes to "Hunger Games," not only has the franchise improved with time, but the two spin-off prequels are arguably better than even the original series. The latest film, "Sunrise on the Reaping" deepens what came before, while introducing new characters that are as engaging as those we know and add to the larger worldbuilding of Panem. Or, as /Film's review puts it, "Beyond the shock value, this story remains human, emotional, and gripping from beginning to end."

Even if this movie doesn't have the compelling romance that was central to "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes," it delivers a harrowing tale of rebellion. What's more, this is somehow the darkest movie in the "Hunger Games" series, and it's not even close. Not just in terms of deaths — though there are some truly brutal deaths in the movie (which are somehow tamer than the book!) — but also in the overall tone the movie explores. This is the "Hunger Games" equivalent of "Andor" or "Rogue One," a bleak tragedy about one man who dared stand up against a tyrannical government and paid for it with everything he had.

The "Hunger Games" movies are full of death and suffering; it's part of the game. But "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes it to another level. Though it takes a long while before the nearly 3-hour runtime gets to the actual games, there's a feeling of hopelessness from the first moment. Early on, a kid gets shot for trying to escape the Reaping. Later, on the day of the opening ceremony, the youngest tribute from District 12, Loella, gets brutally killed in a chariot accident.

But it's not just the fact that there are plenty of deaths, or that they're horrible to watch. Sure, seeing poor Ampert get completely mauled by a swarm of cute forest animals and Maysilee Donner getting gored to death by birds is harrowing. The Capitol doesn't just kill children; it tortures them and pulls them apart and makes their parents watch.

Indeed, more than the deaths themselves, it's the meanness around them that makes this movie stand out. When Loella dies, it isn't a murder done by another tribute trying to get an advantage. Instead, it's an accident caused by Capitol audiences throwing flares at the parade circuit knowing full well it will cause the chariot horses to go berserk, because they want to see the tributes suffer. Compared to every other "Hunger Games" movie, the tributes are not only livestock sent to slaughter, or pawns in a political game. Here, they are subjects of absolute torture, playthings to be slowly toyed with and brutalized before getting discarded for the pleasure of others.

We especially see this in the presence of Ampert, whom we learn is only in the games as a punishment for his father Beetee's rebellious activities. Beetee is forced to mentor his own son and then watch him die.

Throughout "Sunrise on the Reaping," there is a sense of hopelessness and inevitability. We know that, eventually, the Hunger Games end and the Capitol falls — but the characters in the movie don't know that. At every turn, Haymitch and his allies see their every effort get squashed and mocked. Their attempts at leveling the playfield just a little bit are met with overwhelming force. Even before the games begin, Haymitch knows he'll never get to go back home because President Snow has personally seen to it that Haymitch dies for his insubordination during the opening ceremony.

Then we get to the third act, where the movie just becomes a cruel feel-bad experience, as Plutarch Heavensbee convinces Snow to let Haymitch survive to prolong his suffering. Haymitch is forced to watch Maysilee die brutally at the hands of waterbird mutts while he is virtually unscathed. Then, even after he wins the games, Haymitch discovers that the televised games were heavily edited to make him look selfish and cowardly, a traitor to every one of his allies, who won only because he was lucky. As if that wasn't enough, Haymitch has to see his family and his girlfriend die horrible deaths after he goes back home from the death games, in what's without a doubt the most nihilistic ending in the franchise.

It makes sense, however. "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes place at a pivotal moment in the "Hunger Games" timeline, at the height of President Snow's power. We are far removed from the games being almost canceled due to lack of interest like in the previous movie, but also far removed from the simmering rebellion of the original movies. Here, Snow gets away with everything, and he relishes in the cruelty of his games.

People who practice animal law depend on scholars and advocates in other fields to provide the information they need to do their important work, but does this advice really reduce or end exploitation? In their fascinating and important new book Empirical Animal Law: Testing the Assumptions of a Movement, Justin Marceau, David A. Dana, and Janice Nadler offer answers to this and other important questions by insisting that lawyers working in this field must empirically test their assumptions and learn from their mistakes.

This book should be read not only by lawyers and legal scholars but also by researchers like me. It's heartening and extremely important to see actual data about the effectiveness of research in the field of cognitive ethology, the study of animal minds, and whether or not what we learn makes a difference in the lives of the animals themselves. I remain optimistic that their detailed analyses will be a positive move for increasing animal well-being across diverse species.

Here's what Marceau had to say about this first comprehensive, data-driven account of how animal law operates in practice in which he and his co-authors call for a new empirically informed movement. This book is a gold mine of information and I couldn't agree more with what they write.

Marc Bekoff: Why did you and your colleagues write Empirical Animal Law?

Justin Marceau: It is hardly controversial to recognize that the law does a poor job of protecting the lives and interests of animals. The law allows activists who attempted to rescue beagles from cruelty to be treated as criminals, while shielding directly or through practice the very institutions that harm animals. Factory farming has increased across the U.S. in recent decades. Animals continue to be bred for our fashion, consumption, entertainment, research, and sport. And almost all of this is perfectly legal.

Much of this harm remains invisible to the public despite decades of work by activists and lawyers. For some in the movement, this means that all hope is lost. There are people out there claiming that animal protection efforts have failed. Our book is a bit more optimistic, or at least focused on what is working and what is not working.

Specifically, we are legal scholars, including Janice Nadler, a renowned social scientist, and we wanted to study the strategies and assumptions of animal advocates. What kind of assumptions are we making about how best to make progress for animals, and what could be done better? In short, we wrote the book to test the assumptions of our movement about what is working and what does not work.

JM: It is an academic book so we expect it to be useful to researchers. But we also wrote the book in an accessible format so that it can be picked up and discussed by animal advocates and lawyers across the country. Our critiques and guidance are borne out of the more than a dozen original empirical studies we conducted for the book, and we hope that the data we share in the book will help inform the next generation of advocacy on behalf of animals.

MB: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

JM: The book has several different types of studies and analysis: (1) studies on the role of incremental reform in achieving more radical reform or animal rights; (2) factors that influence our opinions about the moral standing of animals; (3) the impacts for animals and humans of a carceral or tough-on-crime approach to animal law; (4) the role of protests in social change; (5) the theory of “winning by losing” in litigation or lawmaking; and (6) questions of who are the best messengers and what are the best messages for animal advocacy and outreach.

We think that each of these topics and studies brings new insights into questions that the animal protection field has either ignored or taken for granted. The major theme of the book, captured by the subtitle, is: Testing the Assumptions of a Movement.

One set of studies that we think are particularly important examines the relationship between an animal’s perceived moral standing and their Intelligence, emotional intelligence, or sentience.

Animal lawyers and advocates have long assumed that the law will "follow the science.” That is to say, the law will inevitably provide more protection as we learn more about the intelligence or capabilities of animals. "Just show them more science!" it is often proclaimed.

Our studies, drawing on thousands of participants, suggest that learning more about the inner lives of animals probably does matter, but it is complicated.

Intelligence, for example, does raise moral concern for animals, but people may disregard such findings for animals they already eat, though not always, as our results for fish show. Thus, in our studies, describing pigs as highly intelligent did not significantly raise their moral standing.

Information demonstrating that an animal can feel pain, on the other hand, increased the perceived moral standing for every species we tested, including pigs and cows. And that is a pretty remarkable finding. Informing people about scientific evidence of sentience can significantly increase the animal’s perceived moral standing.

Perhaps the most intriguing finding in this section is that learning about an animal's emotional life (that the animal is loving and cooperative, and mourns its dead) may be the most powerful factor of all. In our studies, learning about the emotional lives of animals increased moral concern even for cows, an animal most people regularly eat.

The idea that animal emotions or emotional intelligence can impact or challenge our entrenched habits of consumption or compassion for animals is fascinating to us. So far as we know, we are the first to empirically test this idea and we think it deserves considerably more study.

MB: How does your work differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

JM: To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive effort to take up the key assumptions of animal advocates and subject them to empirical scrutiny. We can’t wait to hear what folks think about what we have found!

MB: Are you hopeful that, as people learn which kinds of information about animals’ minds, emotions, and sentience actually shift attitudes, researchers will do more to make their work accessible to a wider audience?

JM: This is key. Our research suggests that learning about the inner lives of animals can make a difference in how people perceive animals. This means that animal protection efforts need to do more to understand the science about animal lives and to translate it into forms that are digestible to the public at large.

12 Best Acting Performances In Steven Spielberg Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Steven Spielberg is one of, if not the most iconic directors working today. The filmmaker’s first project dates all the way back to 1971 with the release of Duel on ABC (a direct to television film), and he has since gone on to helm countless other titles. In total, Spielberg has directed a whopping 36 projects, and is still active to this day, with the release of Disclosure Day coming earlier this year.

Many of the classic films from the last forty years can be attributed to Spielberg. The director helmed 1993s Jurassic Park, for example, and he also did the first four Indiana Jones films, along with Schindler’s List, Saving Private Ryan, Jaws, and many, many more. Impressively, Spielberg has switched genres frequently in his career, never staying in one area for too long (his two most recent projects, for example, are The Fablemans and Disclosure Day, and both are vastly different).

Spielberg isn’t just known for his pure directing talents, however. He’s also made a reputation for himself as one of the best “actor’s directors” working today; that is, having the ability to work with all kinds of unique performers and get the best out of them each and every single time.

There’s plenty of evidence to back up that claim, too. Just as some of the best movies of the last forty years have come from Spielberg, some of the best performances from actors have come in these projects as well. And while the actors naturally deserve great credit for these turns, Spielberg should also get his plaudits for being the mastermind behind these performances.

One of Spielberg’s earlier films was the iconic Jaws. Everyone knows the story of this one — a giant shark circles the waters of a coastal town, with a small group of townsfolk warning others of the dangers, but are up against a mayor who is unwilling to shut down the beaches.

Interestingly, Spielberg reportedly assembled a small group of A-list actors that he wanted to appear in the film. At the same time, the director was also hesitant to choose anyone too well known, as he didn’t want their fame to overshadow the movie. As such, the likes of Robert Redford, Paul Newman, and Steve McQueen, all names Spielberg had in mind, were not in Jaws.

One name that was in Jaws, however, is Robert Shaw. The English actor was well known at the time, both in the United Kingdom and the United States (although he was especially famous in the former, his homeland). Before Jaws, Shaw had appeared in the likes of From Russia With Love, The Taking of Pelham One Two Three, and A Man for All Seasons (for which he received an Academy Award nomination).

In Jaws, Shaw plays Quint, a grizzled and hardened former soldier who survived a massive shipwreck during World War II. He, very famously, has a strong hatred for sharks, but along with that, he also carries extensive knowledge of these animals as well. Shaw himself is arguably the standout performer of the film, especially because of his speech on the USS Indianapolis, in which he says that sharks have “lifeless eyes, black eyes, like a doll’s eyes.”

Another standout performer from Jaws is the film’s lead hero, Roy Schneider, who plays Chief Martin Brody. Like Shaw, Jaws ended up being the biggest role of Schneider’s career (and one he would reprise in future sequels, including Jaws II).

Ironically, Chief Brody has a deep-rooted fear of the water, which makes his quest for the shark even more intense. The audience can truly feel Brody’s anxiety and stress through Schneider’s performance, which, in turn, makes the movie feel more lived in and real. At the same time, Schneider does brilliantly to ensure his character isn’t defined by fear, making this one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg movie.

Just four years after Jaws, Steven Spielberg launched what ended up being another one of his most classic projects. Spielberg developed the character of Indiana Jones alongside George Lucas and Lawrence Kasdan, and then helmed Jones’ first movie, Raiders of the Lost Ark.

Harrison Ford’s first performance as Indiana Jones remains just as iconic as it was nearly fifty years ago. Ford exudes confidence and swagger, making Indiana Jones one of the very best heroes in movie history. After the first Star Wars movie in 1977, it would have been hard to predict that Ford would come up with another character just as iconic, if not more so than Han Solo, but he does just that in Raiders of the Lost Ark (thanks, in part, to Steven Spielberg’s stellar direction).

As far as intimate projects go, arguably no film in recent history has been as meaningful to its director as The Fablemans. The 2022 film serves as Spielberg’s very own origin story, showing his upbringing and how he became interested in filmmaking in the first place. His parents play big roles in the film, especially his mother, who’s played by Michelle Williams, in what is one of the finest performances of her entire career.

Williams has given many memorable turns over the years, but The Fablemans truly stands out. Williams is truly outstanding in this film, playing a character who is so conflicted (and someone that Spielberg himself clearly has complicated feelings about). It’s far from the easiest role to play, especially when factoring in that this is her director’s mother, but Williams deserves great credit.

Arguably the main attraction of Jurassic Park as a film are the dinosaurs. The special effects were exceptional in 1993 and remain so to this day, and along with Spielberg’s direction, help to make these prehistoric creatures feel truly alive. Yet, at the same time, the human characters are just as impressive in Jurassic Park, particularly Jeff Goldblum’s Dr. Ian Malcom.

Malcom is a mathematician who excels in chaos theory, and is highly skeptical about John Hammond’s Earth-shattering invention right from the get-go. Goldblum plays Malcom tremendously, making him a witty character that, at the same time, also demands to be taken seriously. Goldblum helps tie the entire movie together, in fact, making this another top-tier performance in a Steven Spielberg film.

Spielberg incredibly made not one, but two incredible films in 1993. One was the aforementioned Jurassic Park, while the other was a vastly different story — Schindler’s List. The film told the true story of a German industrialist named Oskar Schindler who put his life on the line to save thousands of Jewish people from the Holocaust.

Liam Neeson plays Schindler in what is likely the finest performance of his entire career. There’s so much emotion entrenched in Schindler’s List, and that doesn’t just come from the script or Spielberg’s direction (both of which are excellent). Neeson is extraordinary here, proving himself more than capable of delivering an iconic dramatic performance.

Another iconic 1990s film from Spielberg is Saving Private Ryan, which focuses on a mission to save Private James Francis Ryan (Matt Damon) after his three brothers were killed during the Battle of Normandy. Ryan is the core subject of the film, but Tom Hanks’ Captain John Miller is the one leading the mission.

Hanks is often labeled as one of the best actors to ever live, and Saving Private Ryan is often cited as evidence for this. It’s for good reason, too, as Hanks is this film’s standout. Despite Hanks’ immense fame, it’s easy to forget that he’s there at all, and instead, audiences are likely to simply see Captain Miller, which makes Saving Private Ryan feel all the more realistic.

Arguably the most divisive entry on this list, Tom Cruise received mixed responses to his performance as the leading protagonist in Spielberg’s War of the Worlds remake. The film itself received mixed reception, too, but Cruise does deserve a lot of credit for his performance here. Cruise plays a single dad incredibly well, and embodies what it feels like to be a parent during moments of danger.

One of the very best movies that Steven Spielberg ever made also features Tom Hanks - Catch Me if You Can. The film, which centers on Leonardo DiCaprio’s Frank Abagnale Jr., is actually a true story that you have to see to believe. DiCaprio’s Abagnale Jr. is eccentric, coming up with lies on the spot. As a result, he lives several different lives throughout the film, all with the FBI hot on his trail.

Hanks is, predictably, excellent as the FBI agent leading the charge. Yet, he’s outshone by DiCaprio, only in his early 20s at this time. And despite the fact that this was relatively early on in DiCaprio’s career, it remains one of his very best, and one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg film.

Whoopi Goldberg had her breakout role in Steven Spielberg’s 1985 adaption of The Color Purple. It’s no wonder that Goldberg became a star after, too, as she steals the film, vastly improving the story and making it more than worth the watch. A huge reason for this is the pain that Goldberg embodies throughout, making her character feel like a truly three-dimensional and real human being.

Patty’s voice sounds like a shy version of Cristin Milioti’s adult baby from “30 Rock.” She wears silly hats, can’t drive, and struggles to stick up for herself. She kinda, sorta believes she’s a demigod (after reading one of Neville’s books on Greek mythology as a kid), and she’s always been an outcast (for these reasons and more). Watching Patty come into her own — in ways both delightfully weird and universally endearing — is the most rewarding and compelling aspect of “Dig”; a strange, distinctive spark that could grow into a roaring fire if the series continues (and learns from its strongest asset).

But that’s a big “if.” The first season (consisting of 10 episodes) pivots around the four women finding a rare statue and making the questionable decision to keep it secret from Neville. At the time, they say it’s because their boss takes credit for everything, and they want something that’s just their own, but that contention doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses because Neville doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses. Neville is whoever he needs to be in any given episode — a mentor, an oppressor, a goofball, a stick in the mud, a nice old man, and a villainous monster — and while Laurie can pivot with the best of them, his inconsistent character development betrays a show that’s hesitant to commit to much of anything.

The love triangle between Gary, Dylan, and Clare fizzles out without any substantial conflict. Elsewhere, an inappropriate crush is introduced and tossed away without consequence. Great guest stars like Jason Mantzoukas (a local looter) and Max Greenfield (as a sketchy art dealer) feel like they were hired for arcs that ended up getting cut, while regulars (like Gary) can feel adrift in their ample, listless screentime. Premises primed for comic hijinks never get there, mysteries teed up for suspense are left slack, and relationships that should create a little friction when brought together instead find a way to slide off each other without anything sticking.

Poehler, at the center of it all, is a problem. While it’s theoretically interesting to see the former Leslie Knope take the opposite tact — playing another hard-working public servant (every artifact they uncover remains in Greece) only without the unbridled optimism — Elise’s glum demeanor limits her ability to crack jokes, stir passion, and generally light up a scene. Patty is the only outsized character in a bunch of overly composed normies, and while everyone gets a moment or two to strut their silly stuff, it’s not enough in a series where the humor is already spread so thin.

“Dig” has all the talent it needs to be whatever show it wants to be. The most obvious option would be a comedy, but I have faith in the cast and creatives to excel in other genres, too. They just need to pick one and, when they do, dig a whole lot deeper than this.

9 Classics Where the Audiobook Version Is Better

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Audiobooks were technically born as far back as 1877, with Thomas Edison's invention of the phonograph. However, it wasn't until 1932 that the American Foundation for the Blind officially named them "Talking Books," introducing them as a medium for visually impaired people to enjoy literature as an alternative to Braille. Throughout the entire 20th century, and even well into the 21st, many classics have been turned into audiobooks so phenomenal that they're arguably even better than the original book.

Audiobooks and physical books are, of course, vastly different media that fulfill vastly different purposes, so comparing them can feel a bit like comparing apples to oranges. Still, sometimes, an audiobook is so good that it becomes entirely reasonable to say it provides a better experience than the original. These are audiobooks that anyone who enjoys classic literature should consider essential listening material. "Classic" is defined for the purposes of this list as any book that was originally published before the 21st century.

Folkloric vampires predate Bram Stoker's Dracula by hundreds of years. Inspired by such stories, as well as by Irish folklore and historical figures like Vlad the Impaler, Stoker wrote the 1897 classic that is nowadays widely remembered as the most foundational pillar of modern vampiric fiction. It's undoubtedly one of the best horror books of all time, which made the announcement of an original Audible audiobook-slash-audio-drama all the more worthy of skepticism.

In the end, however, Bram Stoker's Dracula ended up being one of the greatest horror audiobooks that readers have been treated to in years. With a stacked cast that includes the likes of Ella Purnell, Jonathan Bailey, and Aimee Lou Wood, the audiobook takes the classic epistolary novel and makes it feel even more immersive, cinematic, and well-paced. It's worth noting that it's an abridged audio drama rather than a full reading of the original novel, which is not ideal, but those interested in such a concept should love it.

Originally serialized between 1875 and 1877, Russian author Leo Tolstoy's Anna Karenina is widely regarded as one of the most foundational pieces of world literature in history, a must-read masterpiece whose daunting 1,000-page length shouldn't deter anyone from considering it essential reading. Since it was one of her favorite books of all time, Maggie Gyllenhaal agreed to narrate an Audible original audiobook that really does justice to one of the best epic books of all time.

Tolstoy's novel was such a monumental artistic achievement that the author was notoriously drained and exhausted by the whole experience by the time he was done, and reading such a vast book can itself also be a massive undertaking. With the raw immediacy and many emotional variations of Gyllenhaal's narration, listening to Anna Karenina can be a far more engaging and far less tedious experience than many may initially think. At over 30 hours long, it's definitely a time commitment, but oh so worth every minute.

Upon publication back in 1997, J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone (published in the US as Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone) was an immediate commercial and critical hit, kicking off one of the biggest multimedia franchises in history. The book's first audiobook version came out in 1999, and the performers varied by region. Whereas Jim Dale narrated the book in the US and Canada, it was Stephen Fry's voice that was heard in the British and international releases. If there is any one Harry Potter book that's better heard than read, it's Fry's performance of Philosopher's Stone.

As a whole, this is one of the most essential fantasy books to read before kicking the bucket, but there's nothing quite like hearing it through Fry's rich, velvety baritone voice. On the page, Philosopher's Stone is beautifully imaginative and cozy, but at times feels like an overly simple debut novel in terms of Rowling's prose. Fry elevates every word and makes the entire story feel like a wonderful fairy tale being told by the fire on a cold weekend night outdoors.

One of Stephen King's first novels was 1983's Pet Sematary, published before he had fully established and cemented himself as the true King of Horror. Fans had been begging for an audiobook for years, and once the project finally materialized, it was Michael C. Hall of Dexter fame who publisher Simon & Schuster picked as the right voice to narrate the story. The result is one of the best Stephen King audiobooks to date, and considering just how many great ones there are, that's saying a lot.

Pet Sematary was already absolutely legendary well before 2018, widely regarded by many—including King himself—as the author's most genuinely terrifying work. However, once the horror aspects really pick up steam after a relatively slow-burning first few pages, Hall makes it hit even harder. Getting an actor well-known for projects as death-related as Six Feet Under and Dexter to narrate a horror novel all about death and grief was not only brilliant meta-casting, but also hugely beneficial for the story as a whole.

Multiple books are almost always brought up when the endless debate of what "The Great American Novel" is gets brought up, but John Steinbeck's East of Eden is the most noteworthy. ‘The Odyssey’ Cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema Breaks Down Filming Climactic Thunderstorm Scene in Imax

Christopher Nolan shot “The Odyssey” to be experienced on the biggest screen possible so audiences could feel the full impact and be fully immersed in that experience — so everything was shot practically.

One of the most challenging sequences to pull off was the film’s final storm scene as Matt Damon’s Odysseus faces the wrath of Poseidon and the full force of Zeus. It was to be climactic and intense, and Odysseus loses all his men as a result of the final insult to the gods. “We had been talking about going out there to sea, sit on real boats and shoot on Imax,” cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema explains.

A large amount of the film was shot on the open ocean, where Nolan, his cast and crew spent four months capturing how hard the journey would have been as they endured crashing waves. And while the climactic moment ended up being shot in Los Angeles, Nolan didn’t spare any expense in pulling off the sequence, relying instead on old-fashioned filmmaking.Speaking with Variety for Inside the Frame, Van Hoytema explains the scene provided a challenge: “How to replicate something and make it feel visceral and real? How can we do that and avoid a huge amount of CG?”Two decommissioned jet engines were used as wind machines. Van Hoytema’s chief lighting technician and gaffer Adam Chambers built light boxes that would emulate thunder and lightning. The boat was then put on a gimbal, all while being shot on an Imax camera. “It was like shooting a real storm, just under safety supervision,” Van Hoytema says.He adds, “We did a lot of different setups in that whole scene. It is designed to feel extremely chaotic; it’s designed to sort of not understand really what’s going on.”The lighting design was something he also had to think about. “It was interesting because I was thinking, if you’re out at sea at night 2,000 years ago, without electricity and you end up in a thunderstorm, what would that feel like? I was thinking, if you can see everything around you, that’s going to look fake.”

A CGI storm didn’t feel right because the entire crew had been meticulous about keeping everything as grounded and as experiential as possible. Van Hoytema says the team built overhead lighting rigs that were approximately 60×30 feet, hanging on cranes above the ship. “It meant that you would see flashes that progressively move from behind them to the front of them, as it opens up the sky. “What you see is short bursts and there’s just darkness or a little bit of a glow. It’s like trying to see this relentless endless sea, this weather, this saltwater and they’re just so close to death. It’s extremely claustrophobic at the same time.”

When it came to framing Odysseus, Van Hoytema was wired in to keep him in place on the gimbal. His Imax camera on his shoulder was also wired in.

“I was not trying to be in any way precious about framing whatsoever. I was just trying through with a jet engine blowing, gallons of water in my face. I was literally just trying to identify if it was even Matt Damon in the shot. It was extremely visceral, and it’s almost hard to grasp. It was not a situation in which you could just start a take or not. Every take was an event.”

Van Hoytema says the camera starts to roll and the jet engines start. “They rise, and at some point, they sound like two fighter jets are next to your ear. And it gets really wild because they start moving the gimbal as well, and you’re holding on for your life. The camera gets turned; it’s the original Imax camera, not the blimp and the Imax camera is very loud. However, in that situation, you don’t even know if it’s turned on. You do a take, and you have a crazy powerful adrenaline rush.”

In contrast, Van Hoytema recalled a moment when the crew was out to sea, in open waters and a pod of bottlenose dolphins appeared. It was completely unplanned.

Van Hoytema recalls, “It’s interesting because it was also pretty violent that day. It was very harsh weather and I remember a lot of people on the crew got seasick. As we started filming, dolphins started to jump out of the water. What is interesting is that it symbolizes that no matter what the weather does, we take the weather as it comes and accept it as the reality you work in that day.”

Since its inception, the twenty-five Bond films have grossed over $7 billion dollars world wide.

Robert Pattinson's Batman Movie Franchise Is Officially Finished

No, I didn’t forget about The Dark Knight Rises

With Robert Pattinson confirmed to be the next James Bond, one of the biggest takeaways is that Matt Reeves’ The Batman series will likely end with The Batman: Part II. Pattinson is signed on for three films as Bond, and while that’s one less film than the four-film deal that Amazon MGM Studios was seeking for the other Bond contenders, it’s pretty hard to imagine that Pattinson will juggle the role of 007 with an ongoing commitment to DC — much less Battinson’s integration into the wider DCU, which some DC fans had hoped for. To be clear, nothing has been confirmed about the status of a third The Batman movie, but the latest news combined with the extreme delays facing The Batman Part II feel like a pretty clear indication that the Batmobile has hit a dead end and will need yet another reboot.

However, the loss is even bigger than you might realize, as this will be the third time in a row that Batman fans will be robbed of a good Batman trilogy.

Tim Burton’s 1989 film Batman, of course, revolutionized the character of Batman and comic book movies as a whole. Before then, the general public’s awareness of Batman was mostly associated with the campy, comedic Adam West Batman, but Burton changed that and presented a dark, heavily stylized version starring Michael Keaton. He then followed it up with Batman Returns, which many still consider to be one of the best Batman films ever.

Warner Bros., however, felt it was too dark and got rid of Burton for the third film, replacing him with Joel Schumacher. Keaton followed Burton out the door, not wanting to continue as Batman without him. Batman Forever, starring Val Kilmer, was the result, and while that film has its defenders, it felt like a different franchise and a different Batman altogether. The same can be said for Batman and Robin.

Then in 2005 came Batman Begins, directed by Christopher Nolan and starring Christian Bale. Taking a far more grounded approach to the character, Batman Begins turned Batman’s origin into an epic film saga, where it had only been relegated to brief flashbacks in previous films. Nolan topped himself with The Dark Knight by putting Batman in the center of a gritty crime drama with Heath Ledger’s Oscar-winning turn as the Joker. For a second time, a Batman film revolutionized comic book films. Nearly 20 years later, many still cite The Dark Knight as the greatest comic book movie ever made.

Unfortunately, Nolan’s third Batman film wasn’t nearly as good as the first two. Perhaps because of Ledger’s untimely death before the release of The Dark Knight, Nolan at first seemed disinterested in a third film, and it is generally understood that he had to rework the story he had in mind to remove the Joker from its narrative. Regardless of the reasons why, The Dark Knight Rises is a deeply uneven, disorganized film that most view as the low point of Nolan’s Batman, if not an outright bad film (I tend to think it is quite bad).

After Nolan’s trilogy concluded, Ben Affleck became the next Batman, in both Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice as well as the various versions of Justice League. Regardless of what you think of these films — I personally find them insufferable — they were deeply divisive. Despite an Affleck-led Batman film being rumored for a time, we never got him in any standalone films, much less three of them.

In 2022, The Batman offered new hope. While some were skeptical of Pattinson as Batman, he proved to be a really solid take on the Dark Knight, which prioritized Batman’s skills as a detective, something missing from previous Batman films. He was complemented by Paul Dano’s frightening take as the Riddler and Colin Farrell’s scene-stealing Penguin.

Reeves also presented the best-ever take on Gotham City. While Burton’s Gotham was bold and interesting, neither of his films made Gotham feel like a real city. Nolan’s Gotham, with its unconcealed usage of Chicago and New York City, had little personality at all. But Reeves’ Gotham felt like a broken city that desperately needed a Batman to come to its rescue, with production combining parts of Chicago and Scotland to give the city a more naturalistic, gothic vibe. The film’s nearly three-hour runtime only assisted in the viewer’s immersion into Reeves’ Gotham.

But three hours in Reeves’ Gotham still wasn’t enough. The Batman Part II, which seems to be telling a story with Two-Face as a villain, was delayed for years due to a personal issue going on with Matt Reeves. Filming finally began in June, but in early October, Reeves had to step away, most likely for the same family issue. Steven Soderbergh then stepped in to finish the filming Reeves began, which is currently ongoing. While I applaud Reeves for prioritizing his family over a film, this means that it will be six years between The Batman and its sequel and it's hard to imagine that Skydance, the new owners of DC Studios, will want to keep Batman on the shelf for that long again, nor do I think they’ll want to schedule around Pattinson’s Bond films.

And, while it never seemed like a real possibility, I was still hoping that Pattinson would get folded into what James Gunn and Peter Safran had been building for the DCU if only because I don’t think it's all that easy to keep finding great guys to be Batman, and while he wasn’t quite as good as Bale — or even Keaton — Pattinson truly is/was a great Batman. It’s just too bad that his take on the character will be cut short before we get a proper trilogy.

Why Casting Robert Pattinson as James Bond Is a High-Risk, High-Reward Gamble for Denis Villeneuve

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Throughout his 64 years in movies, James Bond, the hard-living superspy, has been the star of the 007 franchise. But by granting Robert Pattinson a license to kill, the producers of the next Bond film are making a bet that one of the most famous men on the planet can slide into a role shaped by the likes of Sean Connery, Daniel Craig, Roger Moore, and Pierce Brosnan without bringing any Edward Cullen-sized baggage.

It’s quite a gamble. After all, Connery, Craig, Timothy Dalton, and poor, delusional George Lazenby were relative unknowns when they were tapped to play the secret agent, and Brosnan and Moore were TV-famous but unproven as film stars. That’s not the case with Pattinson, who has been one of the most recognizable people on the planet for two decades, and who is currently anchoring another massive franchise in “Batman.” So why did director Denis Villeneuve, producers David Heyman and Amy Pascal, and Amazon MGM Studios decide to hand over the keys to the Aston Martin to such a prominent and expensive candidate? Well, some of it has to do with Villeneuve’s comfort level. He’s worked with Pattinson on “Dune: Part Three” and knows what the mercurial star can bring to a role. Clearly, he wants to build a darker, brooding Bond. Then, there’s also the need to have insurance. Amazon is launching a new iteration of 007 into a world that looks very different than it did in 2019 when the last chapter, “No Time to Die,” was conceived and shot (it wouldn’t be released until 2021 thanks to COVID). We’ve lived through #MeToo, a pandemic, wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, the fraying of the Western World and, with it, the “special relationship” Bond has sworn to protect. From the start, Amazon was interested in picking a higher-profile actor to outfit in one of Bond’s signature tuxedos. Before the search began in earnest last May, executives were high on the idea of Jacob Elordi.

Plus, some of the other finalists, a group that included Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, Josh O’Connor, and Nicholas Hoult, lacked the kind of sex appeal and danger that the part required. Hoult apparently impressed executives with his audition, but came up short, as he did when he was in contention for Batman (losing out to Pattinson) and Superman (getting Lex Luthor as a consolation prize). He remains very well liked in the industry. With a salary estimated at roughly $2.5 million, Hoult would have been a cheaper option than Pattinson, who one industry source speculated would cost north of $10 million to hire.

But the higher price tag may be worth it. This isn’t just the first Bond to be released under Amazon’s ownership — it’s the first to be produced without the Broccoli family at the helm. The keepers of the 007 flame sold their creative control to Amazon in 2025, leaving the series without a guiding force. Both Heyman and Pascal are old hands, having overseen the casting of the Harry Potter and Spider-Man franchises, respectively, but those are still big shoes to fill.

What’s in it for Pattinson? Well, a chance to put his stamp on an iconic role, as well as an opportunity to reunite with Villeneuve, one of the hottest directors in Hollywood. His commercial bona fides have also, arguably, never been higher. Not only has he shown he can anchor a superhero film with “The Batman” and its upcoming sequel, but he’s also opened more idiosyncratic fare, such as the recent A24 hits “The Drama” and “Primetime.” Clearly, he can bring in the younger audiences driving the box office turnaround while retaining a kind of indie cool that could take Bond in a new and exciting direction. Will he be our perviest 007?

Plus, the recent shake-up at DC Studios, with James Gunn and Peter Safran replaced by former Marvel executive Jeremy Latcham, may spell trouble for Matt Reeves’ “The Batman” series. Although it was conceived as a trilogy, the sequel was delayed multiple times as Reeves dealt with personal matters, forcing him to leave production last month, with Steven Soderbergh stepping in to finish shooting. DC may not want to wait as long to make another installment, and there’s a sense that Latcham may want something brighter and more mainstream than Reeves’ moody, hard PG-13 take on The Dark Knight.

But Pattinson better get ready for a lot more scrutiny than the questions that greeted his casting as Bruce Wayne. “With Batman, there’s a mask to hide behind,” said one industry source. “With Bond, your face is front and center.”

Even Craig, widely considered to be the best Bond since Connery, faced fierce blowback when his casting was announced. He was too blonde, too unconventionally handsome. A website, CraigNotBond, sprang up where fans vented their displeasure.

But when “Casino Royale” debuted in 2006, and Craig showed that he was perfectly capable of kicking ass and ordering his martini shaken, not stirred, the haters were quickly silenced. Will Pattinson be able to pull off the same stunt? It’s a high-risk, high-reward gamble. Just the kind of bet James Bond lives to make.
