James Dean Biopic Casts ‘Half Man’ Actor Mitchell Robertson as Star’s Purported Gay Lover (Exclusive)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Brandon Flynn is set to portray the Hollywood icon in writer-director Guy Guido's feature 'One Hand Tied' that adapts Bill Bast's 2006 memoir.

A planned biopic focusing on James Dean‘s personal life has found its co-lead.

Mitchell Robertson, the Scottish actor known for a breakout performance on Netflix’s Half Man, has been cast in writer-director Guy Guido’s indie film One Hand Tied, The Hollywood Reporter has learned. Robertson will play the star’s close friend and UCLA roommate Bill Bast and joins Brandon Flynn, who was previously announced as playing Dean.

Formerly known as Willie and Jimmy Dean, One Hand Tied adapts Bast’s 2006 memoir, Surviving James Dean. In his book, Bast described meeting Dean at the UCLA theater program at the age of 19 and wrote that the two men became roommates, close friends and eventually lovers. Bast claimed they kept their fling private to avoid hurting Dean’s career while the rising star had relationships with female stars, with Bast remaining hopeful that he and Dean would soon live together again. Five years after meeting Bast, Dean died in a car accident in 1955 at the age of 24, with two of his signature films, Rebel Without a Cause and Giant, released posthumously. His only movie released before his death was 1955’s East of Eden, which is back in the zeitgeist following the recent release of Netflix’s limited series that also adapts John Steinbeck’s novel.

Guido’s script is set from the first meeting between Bill Bast and Dean to the actor’s death on Sept. 30, 1955. Guido spoke to Bast in 2011, four years before his death at age 84. The film will focus on Bast and Dean navigating their evolving relationship and Bast coming to terms with being gay during the 1950s.

Producers include Steven J. Wolfe, known for such projects as (500) Days of Summer, and Flavia Amon, who spent 21 years at Fox Searchlight. Wolfe and Guido are continuing to cast the remaining roles, including the prominent part of Bast’s mother, Beatrice. Guido previously directed the Hulu feature Madonna and the Breakfast Club.

Guido has heard a mixture of reactions from the public in the time since THR first broke the news about the feature. “James Dean has been romanticized and mythologized for more than 70 years, and I think people have become very attached to a particular image of him: the rebellious, brooding, heterosexual heartthrob with a cigarette dangling from his mouth,” he tells THR. “For some, the suggestion that his sexuality may have been more complex than that challenges a fantasy they’ve held onto for decades. But others are genuinely excited by the possibility of seeing a more layered, vulnerable and more dimensional James Dean — someone who wasn’t necessarily confined to the identity Hollywood created for him. And that’s what really interests me. I’m not looking to completely redefine him, but I am looking to explore the human being who existed beyond the mythology.”

Robertson played the younger version of Jamie Bell’s character in Half Man and can soon be seen in the BBC drama Counsels. “The moment I saw him in Half Man, I knew he was the one,” says Guido. “I was looking for vulnerability and an innate likability, but also strength. Bill lived in a time that was hostile to anyone even perceived as gay, yet he sustained a five-year roller-coaster relationship with James Dean. That takes resilience.”

For his part, Robertson notes, “Guy has crafted a beautifully tender script that steps past the image of the icon and brings to life the human behind it. Through Bill’s eyes, we see James Dean before his name was in lights, before the famous red jacket with the upturned collar. We see him flawed, ambitious and, ultimately, as a young man bursting with love for his friend.”

Wolfe says of Robertson’s audition, “His performance was so beautiful and authentic, it felt as if I were watching dailies from the actual production. I can’t imagine anyone more fitting and worthy of this role.”

Flynn’s previous credits include True Detective, 13 Reasons Why and The Parenting. He makes his Broadway debut this fall with Here There Are Blueberries.

Robertson is represented by Taylor Jefferson at The Artists Partnership and Maryam Hunwick at Hunwick Associates.

Dustin Hoffman Recalls Being Held at Gunpoint in Bed and Early Gritty NYC Days Living with Robert Duvall and Gene Hackman

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Dustin Hoffman is opening up about his younger years with the release of his memoir, “Look at Me.”

The actor, who broke into Hollywood with his role in “The Graduate,” appeared on the Nov. 9 episode of the “Conan O’Brien Needs a Friend” podcast and revealed that he had once been held at gunpoint by a man whose wife he was having an affair with during his “youth youth.”

“I was having an affair with a married woman,” Hoffman, 89, admitted. “She thought that once her husband went to asleep, that he stayed asleep.”

Explaining that he had been invited to the woman’s home when her husband had presumably gone to bed, when suddenly it had become clear that the man was well aware of what was going on.

“There we are, having a festival of sorts, and he walked in with a gun,” the “Rain Man” actor shared. “He pointed it at me and he said, I remember what he said, ‘Get out, get out.’”

“When you have a gun pointed at you,” he explained, “You realize how, any second, any second he’s going to pull that trigger. His finger is on the trigger and you think any second he’s going to. You realize, ‘That’s it, I’m gonna die. I’m gonna die, the next second, the next second, the next second.”

The actor recalled that in that moment, he came to the realization that “you will do anything to avoid getting killed.”

At another point in the interview, O’Brien asked the Los Angeles native to detail his move to New York. Hoffman relocated across the country in 1958 at 21, hoping to pursue a career in acting.

As O’Brien put it, Hoffman worked “a lot of absurd odd jobs” at this point in his life, including a role at Macy’s, where he was tasked with selling toys—like foosball and table hockey—to adults.

As an aspiring actor, Hoffman put his own spin on the job, and decided to embody a character of his own making; a retired, injured French hockey player.

“Gene Hackman was right there at adult games, also,” Hoffman said, noting that the fellow actor was his coworker at the time. Decades later, the pair would go on to work together again, starring in the 2003 film, “Runaway Jury.”

Hackman and Hoffman weren’t just coincidental coworkers. They lived together, too.

The upcoming adaptation of Brandon Sanderson's Stormlight Archive looks set to be the most exciting upcoming fantasy TV series currently in development. While original TV series like Stranger Things have made their mark on the fantasy landscape, most adaptations, with the exception of Dungeons & Dragons projects, have not fared so well. The Wheel of Time and The Witcher looked set to be groundbreaking achievements for the genre, but where they failed to live up to their potential, The Stormlight Archive may be the fantasy TV show franchise we need.

One main reason is due to Brandon Sanderson's unprecedented creative control over his projects. The author has been more involved than virtually any other, which has allowed him to oversee the creative process, including the casting, which he has been passionate about. The best fantasy TV show ensemble casts have excellent chemistry and acting ability, but bringing an already beloved character to life is a challenge for any actor, and while Sanderson has a very specific vision, these actors could do the characters justice.

Of all the actors who would be perfect for Brandon Sanderson's Stormlight Archive, Aramis Knight has repeatedly drawn the attention of fans, who have seen him as a potentially perfect choice for Kaladin Stormblessed. This internal struggle makes Kaladin one of fantasy's most interesting heroes, and as he also needs to inspire loyalty from the people he commands, this adds an extra layer of nuance. Shallan Davar is one of the central characters in The Stormlight Archive, and a fan favorite who has inspired considerable debate over her potential casting. Coming from a troubled family background, she uses humor as a shield, often projecting a confident and charming exterior as she struggles beneath the surface.

This description could easily apply to Stranger Things' Max, Sadie Sink's breakout role, which won her the Hollywood Critics Association TV Award for Best Supporting Actress in a Streaming Series, Drama. Sadie Sink's best movies and TV shows all showcase her ability to play a multifaceted character with emotional depth that shows in her face without the need for extensive dialogue, and Shallan feels like a continuation of these characters, but at a more mature level.

Practically unstoppable on the battlefield, the highprince of Alethkar was once a ruthless warrior whose fearsome reputation as "the Blackthorn" preceded him. The fantasy genre is full of archetypes like this, but Brandon Sanderson's Cosmere books stand out for the way they turn these tropes upside-down, and the character we meet is no longer a warlord. ‘Here There Are Blueberries’ Broadway Review: Evil As Banal As A Bowl Of Fruit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Here There Are Blueberries, the exceptional Pulitzer finalist docu-drama that opened Off Broadway two years ago to great admiration, finally makes its Broadway debut tonight at the Barrymore Theatre. The play, about a mysterious real-life cache of one-of-a-kind Auschwitz photos submitted to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in 2007, remains as intriguing as ever – and with source material as grotesquely mesmerizing as these candid snapshots, how could it not?

In fact, the sheer emotional heft of that photo album and everything it suggests dominates Blueberries to an extent that the play itself can seem rather thin at times, not entirely unworthy of the material at hand but not on equal footing either. Revelations about the banality of evil carry a force no matter how familiar we are with the concept, but familiar we are, and Blueberries can feel somewhat naive at times in its presumption that no one in the audience has ever heard of Hannah Arendt.

But in the grand scheme of things, and in light of the historical tale being told here, those quibbles with dramaturgy shouldn’t diminish the real achievement that is Here There Are Blueberries. The play is vital and more often than not riveting. Watch on Deadline

Performed by an excellent cast – any production that features the great Kathleen Chalfant is an opportunity not to be missed – Here There Are Blueberries is, at its core, a detective tale. In 2007, a young Holocaust museum archivist named Rebecca Erbelding (Delia Cunningham) receives a letter at her D.C. office from a man who wants to donate a cache of WWII snapshots.

The offer isn’t unusual – the museum serves as a repository for such keepsakes – but the nature of his collection most certainly is. The photo collection long in the man’s possession is more than a rarity, it’s absolutely unique: The images are of Nazi officers – including some of the highest ranking and most notorious, from Mengele to Höss – in their living quarters just over the wall from the camp’s atrocities. Some are of an almost surreal vacation lodge a few miles away, where officers, SS guards and female office workers alike would be treated to days of rest and relaxation for jobs well done. One officer is given five days there for his murder of two Jews attempting to escape the camp.

Such images had never been observed in photographs before, and their discovery is nothing less than historic, generating headlines across the globe, with much of the public fascination falling on the young German female camp office workers enjoying their vacation lodge days and food-and-drink treats. “Here,” says the caption (in German) of one such photo, “there are blueberries.”

The play is structured as an investigation, lead by the young archivist, as the museum staff seeks to uncover the circumstances under which these snapshots were taken, who took them, who is pictured, and, perhaps most intriguingly, how on earth did they survive when all over such evidence was destroyed by the Nazis themselves as the regime fell?

One such question – how did the man who possesses the photos discover them? – is easily answered: A retired U.S. Army Lieutenant Colonel (Michael Cerveris) found the album in the abandoned apartment he briefly inhabited while stationed in Frankfort at the end of the war. Why he kept them in his possession, and did so secretly, is unknowable – he died shortly after making the museum donation.

Other questions posed by the photos are of a more philosophical nature. Did those seemingly carefree young women at the lodge have any idea that the office work they were doing contributed to the genocide next door? And if so, how could they?

Blueberries, for all its demands on facing harsh truths, plays a bit coy in feigning shock at the answers, as if Judgment At Nuremberg never existed, an excusable but still noticeable manipulation. A bit more difficult to overlook are other red herrings seemingly designed to give the play momentum, such as will the museum board accept the donation of the Nazi photos when its mission is to chronicle the victims, not the victimizers? It’s a stick man argument.

Blueberries is on sturdier dramatic ground when new characters – the relatives of the Nazis and camp workers in the photographs – begin arriving mid-show to offer their perspectives. The sister of an office worker sticks with the usual denials, but others are revelatory, not least the grandson of Rudolph Höss, who relates an unnerving tale of his family’s complicity and his own struggles with the violence he fears is an inheritance.

The grandson’s tale is riveting, as is Brandon Flynn’s performance, a stand-out in a uniformly excellent cast that, in addition to those mention above, includes Conrad Ricamora, Scott Barrow, Michelle Beck and Montego Glover, all playing a variety of roles. Derek McLane’s stark set design, dominated by the many tall-legged stand-up desks used by the museum archivists to study the photos, is stellar, as are the crucial projections (designed by David Bengali) that allows us to see what the archivists are seeing. The photos projected onto the walls would be transfixing even without the superb lighting design (David Lander) and sound design (Bobby McElver) that further an entirely earned mood of dread and even despair, never more so than during a late scene that finally gives all those unseen victims their moment in this story, and in a manner so wrenching it won’t be spoiled here except to say that another photo album enters the story, and its story of survival is every bit as astonishing as the first.

In the end, Here There Are Blueberries has sailed past its own here-and-there shortcomings to land on a final thought, artfully told and with clarity of conviction: Just imagine how many untold boxes of forgotten family memorabilia and grim relics are waiting to be discovered, with secrets as efficiently hidden as those sealed away in the human heart?

Traveling one light-day took the space probe 49 years. it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft is on track to set another historic record as its mission approaches the half-century mark. On Nov. 18 at 5:16 a.m. ET, the probe will reach a distance of one light-day from Earth, a journey of nearly five decades that places it farther from our planet than any human-made object in history.

It’s quite an accomplishment for a spacecraft built in the mid-1970s One light-day is the distance that light travels in a single day at the speed of light, which is approximately 186,282 miles per second. That translates to 25.9 billion kilometers or about 16.1 billion miles in a single day. At an average human walking speed of 3 mph, it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

Fortunately, Voyager 1 has been traveling a bit faster than that. It currently zips along the cosmos at a steady 37,763 mph, a speed it achieved in 1980 after a gravity-assisted Saturn flyby. It has been flying at that speed ever since.

The milestone comes 14 years after its previous one, when Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the point where the sun’s heliosphere ends, and entered interstellar space. It’s also 28 years after passing Pioneer 10 to become the human-made object that has traveled the farthest into space.

The historic milestone isn’t without its drama. Voyager 1 has been in space for nearly half a century, and in that time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done everything it can to extend the life of Voyager 1. That includes troubleshooting issues, performing maintenance and shutting down various systems to save power and extend the spacecraft’s life-span.

Several of those efforts took place in 2026. The team running Voyager 1 spent weeks shutting down three devices and powering on three low-power alternatives to reduce overall power usage. This initiative, which was internally called The Big Bang, was also performed on Voyager 2. The transition was completed in early September, giving Voyager 1 more time with its dwindling power supply while also keeping it warm enough to continue operating in the frigid conditions of interstellar space.

It was a tricky maneuver that required the team to simultaneously turn multiple instruments on and off. The difficulty of the maneuver was amplified by the fact that it took almost a full day for any signal to reach Voyager and another full day for Voyager to send a signal back, with hours of downtime while the spacecraft performed the operations.

All of this took place several months after Voyager 1 experienced a sudden drop in power, which necessitated shutting off one of its three remaining instruments still in operation. Voyager 1 now has only two instruments still in operation.

Making it a whole light-day away from Earth is likely going to be Voyager 1’s final milestone while it still has enough power to communicate with Earth. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They are essentially nuclear-powered batteries that generate energy by converting the steady heat from decaying plutonium-238.

When Voyager 1 first launched, its radioisotope thermoelectric generators produced about 470 watts of power. But plutonium-238 doesn’t last forever, and after 50 years in space, the RTGs now produce about 215 watts of power, with an average loss of about 4 watts per year. Most of that power is used to keep Voyager 1 up and running, which is why NASA has shut down eight of the 10 instruments that Voyager had when it launched.

At its current rate of usage and decay, Voyager 1 is expected to shut down its final science instrument in the 2030s and will eventually lose communication with Earth in 2036 due to insufficient power to send back a radio signal.

The same fate is expected for Voyager 2, Voyager 1’s twin, which has also spent half a century in space. While it is labeled the sequel, Voyager 2 actually launched 16 days before Voyager 1, and the two have spent nearly identical amounts of time traversing the cosmos. Thanks to its slower speed and trajectory, it likely won’t reach the one light-day distance until after its instruments shut down.

Thanks to the vastness of space, Voyager 1 will lose all of its power before it hits its next milestone, which is the beginning of the Oort Cloud, a vast, icy shell of comets at the edge of our solar system. It’ll take about 300 years for Voyager 1 to make it there.

Thanks to the near-perfect vacuum of outer space, Voyager 1 will continue to fly at its current speed even after it shuts down entirely. So it’ll still hit that milestone; it just won’t be able to phone home to tell us about it.

The Chilean filmmaker, who passed away on Friday aged 52, leaves behind a diverse but cohesive body of work including 'Gloria,' 'The Wonder' and the Oscar-winning 'A Fantastic Woman.'

When it was announced, in 2017, that the Chilean writer-director Sébastian Lelio was to helm a U.S. remake of his 2013 breakout film “Gloria,” admirers of the original — and indeed of Lelio himself — had cause for concern. English-language do-overs of world cinema successes frequently go awry; furthermore, we had the cautionary tale of Dutchman George Sluizer, who directed a hopeless 1993 remake of his own impeccable 1988 chiller “The Vanishing,” to remind us that even having the original filmmaker in charge was no guarantee against a travesty. The news came at a crucial moment in Lelio’s career. The first of his two films to premiere in 2017, the vibrant, heartfelt transgender character study “A Fantastic Woman,” was riding a wave of festival and critical success that began with a Best Screenplay win at the Berlinale, and culminated with an Oscar for Best International Feature (or Best Foreign Language Film, as the award was still called then) — the first ever win for a Chilean film in the category, sealing a resurgence of the country’s cinema also being led by such contemporaries as Sebastián Silva and Lelio’s own producer Pablo Larraín.

The second, “Disobedience,” was Lelio’s English-language debut — another story of queer identity, but an altogether different tonal and stylistic proposition. A sober portrayal of an illicit romance between two women from North London’s strict Orthodox Jewish community, it was sensitively observed and beautifully acted by leads Rachel Weisz and Rachel McAdams, with a restraint that was occasionally countered by moments of frank, charged sensuality. The contrast between “A Fantastic Woman” and “Disobedience” confirmed Lelio as a particularly empathetic and versatile teller of women’s stories — and “Gloria,” a warm and unsentimentally joyous comedy of a fiftysomething divorcée’s reawakening, was also good one. But why go back, when his career was barreling forward?

As it turned out, Lelio knew better than his doubters. The remake, which eventually premiered in 2019 as “Gloria Bell,” hewed close to the template of the original, largely inheriting its pleasures and poignancies. But if that makes it sound redundant, it proved a canny and fascinating exercise in performer-as-auteur influence: It was clear that Lelio, self-evidently a champion of women on screen, was interested to see how a wholly different actress would change the tone and temperature of his original story.

A lot, we learned. Paulina García was a funny and resilient force of nature in the 2013 original; in “Gloria Bell,” Julianne Moore brought a yearning, lonely fragility to the material that gave even the film’s uplifting trajectory an undertow of melancholy. The remake got less than its due — Moore, at the very least, deserved some awards attention that year — but it sits beside “Gloria” as an illuminating companion piece rather than a competing interpretation.

I lead with the two Glorias because their respective successes improbably prove a truth about Lelio, a restlessly curious and compassionate filmmaker who passed away, far too soon, on Friday, aged just 52: Even when ostensibly making the same film twice, he never made the same film twice.

His 2005 debut “The Sacred Family” was confident and a bit scrappy, its handheld jaggedness far removed from his later polish, its droll comic tale of a bourgeois man falling for his son’s girlfriend also distinct from the searching feminist portraiture that would define his later career. But it was lively and promising, made in a semi-improvised fashion that he carried forward into his next, more complex film, the troubled youth study “Navidad,” which made its way into the 2009 Cannes Directors’ Fortnight program. But his third film, 2011’s “The Year of the Tiger,” was a considerably more sophisticated effort: an exquisitely understated exploration of faith, guilt and trauma in the wake of Chile’s devastating 2010 earthquake.

“The Year of the Tiger,” which competed at Locarno, was too soulful and richly considered a work on its own terms to be seen as a bid for arthouse-major status — but that’s how it worked out anyway, even if the film didn’t get quite the global exposure it deserved. But by the time “Gloria” came along two years later, the word on Lelio was out and expectations were high: The film was selected for the main competition at Berlin and was an immediate, ebulliently received crowdpleaser. If Larraín had established himself as the dark prince of new Chilean cinema with spiky, perverse, politically bristling works like “Post Mortem” and “No,” Lelio was poised to become its great humanist: his touch light but not soft, his perspective probing but not abrasive.

“A Fantastic Woman,” four years later, delivered on that promise. Propelled by trans star Daniela Vega’s extraordinary, emotionally unguarded performance, its story of a grieving trans woman spurned by the prejudiced family of her dead lover arrived at a vital tipping point for the visibility of transgender narratives and artists in popular culture, and delicately essayed an arc of emphatic, even triumphant self-definition amid a climate of isolation and rejection — all without feeling glibly or opportunistically issue-driven. It was to Lelio’s credit that he was fairly self-effacing in his promotion of the film, letting Vega — with whom he had closely developed the protagonist, aligned with her experience — be its chief ambassador and spokesperson.

“A Fantastic Woman” earned Lelio the most acclaim and kudos of his career — though his finest film as a director, to my mind, was comparatively shortchanged. A period drama set in 19th-century Ireland and adapted from a novel by “Room” author Emma Donoghue, “The Wonder” didn’t outwardly look or feel much like a Lelio joint — with its austerely repressed milieu shot, by Ari Wegner, with such a damp, chilly verdigris finish you could practically sense moss growing on the screen as you watched. Yet its story of feminine resolve and dueling moral and spiritual convictions meshed well with the director’s interests, as did Florence Pugh’s tenacious, stout-hearted performance; the film’s earthy folktale trappings stood in exciting contrast to its stark formalist construction.

If “The Wonder” was an ambitious and grandly realized reach, I was less sold on the huge stylistic swing of Lelio’s most recent film, last year’s political musical “The Wave,” which returned him to his homeland. Inspired by a national spate of #MeToo-related feminist student protests that shut down universities across Chile in 2018, it was rhetorically heavy-handed but composed and choreographed with dynamic aplomb, positively thrilling to the spectacle of massed bodies in motion: I wrote in my review that it made one eager to see Lelio tackle more musical fare on screen, never thinking that the film would be his last.

In one of “The Wave’s” more playful flourishes, the fourth wall is broken to acknowledge Lelio’s own presence as the director of the movie — and to ask, pointedly, what a male director was doing on this expressly feminist project anyway. It’s a wink-wink joke that more seriously encapsulated the generous, conscientious, community-minded approach that Lelio brought to his filmmaking: His auteur signature was subtly distinctive, yet he never made himself the star of his work.

The 5 Scariest Books of All Time, According to Stephen King

It’s interesting to find out what scares one person, and compare their fears to those of another person, perhaps naturally, but then when you're looking at someone who is usually the person who causes fear, then finding out what scares them (if anything) is even more intriguing. Enter Stephen King (no, wait, not like that!), who probably doesn’t need much of an introduction, not just at this stage, but really any stage in the last several decades. His earliest published novels came out in the 1970s, and so many of his initial classics belonged wholly or in part to the horror genre.

You'd be missing out on great books like 11/22/63, much of The Dark Tower, and most of Different Seasons if you only read King’s works of horror, but sure, horror is the genre he’s most associated with for honestly fair reasons. He’s also read and absorbed a lot of horror made by other people, as the non-fiction book Danse Macabre (1981) demonstrates, but more recently, he gave his top 5 scariest books to USA Today in an interview. It’s subjective, and it’s also not the same as Stephen King saying they're the five “best” scary books ever written, but it’s worth looking at what makes the arguable master of horror himself feel fear (no one is safe from being affected by a scary story, it seems).

In the past, Stephen King has also been asked about which stories of his he views most favorably, and he made some unexpected choices there, and also, he highlighted something recent at the time he was asked: 2021’s Billy Summers. Maybe he likes doing that, and it is interesting to not just go straight back to the classics and/or the heavy-hitters, so he could've been trying to do something like that again when one of his five picks was the (at the time of writing) very recent Trad Wife. It’s a 2026 novel about very contemporary things, as you might guess from the title, because the idea of a “tradwife” is a more online and modern-day thing, but it’s all about wanting women to be traditional wives, as things might've been in older days that some people choose to romanticize.

Trad Wife has divided people to some extent, if you go over the other reviews out there, but Stephen King is clearly a champion of the book.

That’s probably the gentlest and least provocative way to define it without rattling anyone. So, the book’s about a journalist who wants to document someone who appears to be a “tradwife,” and she does that while on the property she lives on with her family, but then things slowly get strange and more horrific, though potentially not in the ways you're expecting. Trad Wife has divided people to some extent, if you go over the other reviews out there (admittedly there aren’t a ton, seeing as it’s still a fairly new release), but Stephen King is clearly a champion of the book, being confident enough to label it the best horror novel of 2026.

More of an established heavy-hitter now, Stephen King is a big fan of The Silence of the Lambs, which was a sequel to Red Dragon. Now, Red Dragon got a movie adaptation in the form of Manhunter, but the film version of The Silence of the Lambs wasn’t really a sequel to that film, and then there was a prequel to The Silence of the Lambs (1991) that was called Red Dragon about a decade later, but that one wasn’t great. Oh, and you get some Red Dragon in the TV show, Hannibal, though chronologically, that one didn’t have enough seasons to get to The Silence of the Lambs material.

Anyway, point is, you can not read all that much, and you still probably know the deal with this series, to some extent, and you likely have some familiarity with Hannibal Lecter and what a great character he is. The Silence of the Lambs was obviously a great movie, but the source material is also remarkable, and it’s one of those great cases where a high-quality book seamlessly became a high-quality movie (something that cannot be said about every Stephen King novel that’s made its way to the big screen, but at least there'll always be Stand by Me, Misery, and The Shawshank Redemption, among others).

It’s fair that some of Stephen King’s supernatural horror books get a lot of attention, but just as skilled is what he often manages to do when he tackles psychological horror (and sure, when he has the two collide, like in The Shining, that can also be phenomenal). He singles out The Other as a psychological horror book that frightened him to a particularly great extent, and with it being published a few years before King’s first published novel, you can probably assume it had some kind of influence on his own work, or at least some of his subsequent books.

At the center of The Other is a pair of twins, with one of them being very troubled and the other being seemingly anything but, and the overall novel does increasingly unsettling things with that premise. It’s a slow-burn without ever running the risk of feeling too slow, and there is also something worth celebrating about a book that can prove frightening while sounding, on paper, a bit ordinary or even too straightforward. The Other just does a lot of stuff right, and going into the things it does right would run the risk of ruining things. Just trust King and the other people who are fans of this one (it was very popular back when released, but is perhaps a little more obscure nowadays than the other older books King’s highlighted as being the most frightening to him).

Oh, and now this one isn't obscure by any metric. The Exorcist was a big deal as a book, for a little while, and then it was a potentially even bigger deal as a movie, with that movie getting made not long after the book came out. A similar thing happened with The Godfather novel being the book equivalent of a blockbuster, and then the movie was made rather fast, and ultimately became even more culturally significant. With The Exorcist, though, the source material here is very much worth reading even if you’ve seen the movie, since there are ideas it explores – and sights it describes – that prove more unsettling when they're placed in your head, versus seeing/experiencing those things on a screen.

And that’s saying a lot, because the movie is still absolutely surprising and effectively horrifying with what it shows and explores. Guess it’s the case that you’ve got a winning work of horror either way, with The Exorcist (1971) being potentially the definitive demonic possession novel, and The Exorcist (1973) almost definitely being the definitive demonic possession movie. Also, there is a blending of more supernatural (or otherworldly) horror with psychologically troubling stuff in The Exorcist and, once more, the interplay just serves to make both those strands of horror hit even harder.

The Exorcist is singled out in polls on Goodreads as being one of the scariest novels of all time, among those voting, and Lord of the Flies is one you'll often see in such polls, too. And hey, it’s relevant here, because it was one of King’s five picks when he was asked about books that scared him. It’s interesting, because Lord of the Flies isn't outwardly a horror book, probably more definable as a survival story, or maybe like a small-scale dystopian novel without real sci-fi elements, but it’s horrifying without necessarily being full-on horror. And of course it is, because it’s about young boys getting stranded on an island and finding themselves clashing over various things, all in increasingly violent ways.

People tend to read Lord of the Flies when they're younger as well, which has an effect on it being more frightening, like if it marks the first time you're forced to confront some really troubling ideas. Stephen King himself has written some pretty great books that are comparable to Lord of the Flies, like The Mist (technically a novella, with it involving people trapped in a supermarket), and Under the Dome (in which a dome does indeed come down over a very small town, trapping people under it). Godzilla Minus Zero Director Reveals Why He Scrapped Original Sequel Plans

Godzilla Minus Zero director doesn't believe that a sequel would necessarily benefit the massive franchise.

Godzilla Minus Zero director Takashi Yamazaki has opened up about the challenges of following his Oscar-winning film. He explained why he ultimately abandoned the idea of making a sequel designed specifically to surpass the original. The director initially wanted to create a movie that could replicate or outdo the success of Godzilla Minus One. However, he eventually decided that approaching the project with those goals could compromise the quality of the content.

In an interview with IGN, Yamazaki discussed the pressure surrounding his return to the franchise following the overwhelmingly positive reception of his Academy Award-winning 2023 film. The director said that he thought about creating a more dramatic and explosive movie that would be bigger than its predecessor. However, he eventually scrapped that approach after realizing how bigger isn't always better, as well as how only focusing on commercial success and more awards could sacrifice the effectiveness of the storyline. He added that if he went with his original plans, he would have been doing it for the wrong reasons.

Instead of focusing on making the sequel outperform the first film, Yamazaki decided to concentrate on creating an entertaining experience with a meaningful story. According to the director, he dropped the idea that he needed this sequel to beat Godzilla Minus One. As a result, he was able to give himself and his team more creative freedom. He described the change in plans as an important moment for Godzilla Minus Zero, helping him move forward without worrying about living in the shadow of Godzilla Minus One.

In the beginning I thought, ‘Well, how do I outdo its predecessor and how do we make a movie that's going to win all these awards and it's going to be bigger in scale?' But I think it's very dangerous to create a movie with that type of mindset because you're going to risk diluting what makes the movie great in the first place. So those are the wrong reasons to make a movie and it ends up becoming an antagonist in your moviemaking process.

Yamazaki's original ideas are understandable considering the massive success of Godzilla Minus One. Released in 2023, the Japanese monster movie explored the dire state of the country after an awful war while introducing a terrifying new version of the iconic monster. The film was praised by both critics and casual audiences alike for its emotional plot, interesting characters, and amazing visual effects.

The movie made history for not just the Godzilla franchise but also its country at the 96th Academy Awards in 2024, becoming the first Japanese production to win Best Visual Effects. It was also the first installment in the property to receive an Oscar ever. Its achievement was incredibly impressive because its visual effects team was working with way less resources than what major Hollywood blockbusters have access to.

Apple TV's "Unadaptable" 3-Season Series Is Mandatory Viewing for Sci-Fi Fans

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Julio is a Senior Author for Collider. He studied History and International Relations at university, but found his calling in cultural journalism. When he isn't writing, Julio also teaches English at a nearby school. He has lived in São Paulo most of his life, where he covers CCXP and other big events. Having loved movies, music, and TV from an early age, he prides himself in knowing every minute detail about the things he loves. When he is older, he dreams of owning a movie theater in a small countryside town.

By now, it's no secret that Apple TV has a knack for releasing great science fiction; you can pretty much choose one at random, and it will still be a great choice. Saying one of them is the best feels even a little unfair to the others, so we have to raise the bar and look beyond the screen and individual taste, focusing instead on what kind of accomplishment it represents. All that considered, it's almost impossible not to choose Foundation, the adaptation of Isaac Asimov's seminal sci-fi saga. Before its release, these books were considered straight-up unadaptable, so, in many ways, the greatest achievement of Apple TV's Foundation isn't just being an incredible work of science fiction, but that it exists at all, really.

Between original series and adaptations, science fiction has had a boom in productions ever since the dawn of the streaming age. The one work every fan of the genre had pretty much accepted would never get such treatment was Foundation. Even after its announcement back in 2018, everyone only believed the series was actually real when it premiered via Apple TV in 2021. That's because no other work in sci-fi has a scope as huge as Foundation — it was just too much to believe it could ever be made into a TV series.

Set in a future where humankind has conquered the cosmos, the original book comprises many smaller tales depicting humanity's efforts to overcome a thousand years of dark ages. The single common thread is Hari Seldon (Jared Harris), founder of psychohistory, a field of study that uses probability and psychology to predict future events on a galactic scale. Seldon devised a plan for future human leaders to follow, thus reducing the dark ages from tens of thousands of years to a single millennium.

It's a fascinating story and an easy read, filled with equally interesting characters. However, most never interact due to their respective stories being set decades and even centuries apart. This is a major roadblock for any kind of visual adaptation, because focusing on just one of them would mean neglecting centuries of human history in that universe. It just couldn't be done, and those centuries of human history are the whole point of Foundation. Something had to drastically change for these novels to fit the TV framework.

When translating a work of literature into visual media, it's always necessary to make some changes, including, sometimes, some severe ones. Modern television usually demands strong protagonists with whom the audience can sympathize, so, when David S. Goyer and Josh Friedman started working on adapting Foundation, this was one of the very first things to tackle — that and a way to unify and streamline the original narrative, so it could fit the episodic and seasonal framework.

Instead of multiple separate storylines spread out across the timeline, the series brings them together with time jumps and some classic sci-fi narrative devices. Thus, Apple's Foundation takes the main characters in the early Asimov books and turns them into actual protagonists, each with their own complexities and development arcs. Instead of two or three small chapters, then, characters like Gaal Dornick (Lou Llobell), Salvor Hardin (Leah Harvey), and even Seldon himself have central and much larger roles.

Despite its scope, though, the books are quite simplistic in worldbuilding early on, as the Foundation itself is still struggling to consolidate. Sci-fi television demands something greater, though, so the Apple series has grander and more cinematic settings than the books. Every planet has a complex culture and background, and even things like Seldon's vault, where his recording reveals the next steps of his plan every few decades, become central plot devices tied to the protagonists, keeping the story straight and fluid.

There was no way to make Foundation work as a series if it was an exact match with its book counterpart, and a significant part of why it's so good is how it allows itself to go beyond Asimov's original writings. The best example is the Genetic Dynasty, an entirely new idea created for the series. In the books, the Galactic Empire's decay is what causes the dark ages, but it's the series that actually goes into why that is, since the original focuses mostly on the other side, the Foundation.

For that, the series introduces one of the most brilliant concepts in recent sci-fi: the Genetic Dynasty. It perfectly sums up the notion that the Empire is so stagnant, to the point where every emperor is a clone of the same man, Cleon I. Brothers Dawn (Cassian Bilton), Day (Lee Pace), and Dusk (Terrence Mann) are essentially different versions of the same person, each struggling to establish their own identity while living under the shadow of their predecessor. Another great example is Demerzel (Laura Birn), whose role is considerably expanded from the books, made in the Apple adaptation into a central figure in the Empire's political machinations, with a twist coming straight from the author's wider Robot universe.

These additions give Apple's Foundation an identity of its own without losing sight of the ideas that made the original novels so influential. Ultimately, that's what makes it such a great adaptation, focusing more on understanding and expanding on the potential of the original story rather than simply adapting it. Bringing Foundation to television was always going to require some creative liberties, but the series shows just how much can be achieved when an adaptation understands what makes its source material special.

Foundation is available to stream on Apple TV.

If things had gone just a little bit differently, The Mandalorian may not have happened. One of the many previously announced and later canceled Star Wars movies may have taken its place. If that had happened, Star Wars as a whole would have been significantly worse off. For possibly the first time, The Mandalorian has proven that it was actually for the best that Disney and Lucasfilm failed to finish a project they announced.

Years before The Mandalorian premiered in 2019, Disney and Lucasfilm were planning on making a standalone Star Wars movie based on Boba Fett. The now-canceled Boba Fett movie was announced in 2014 and was originally supposed to be a spinoff film akin to Rogue One. Over the years, the Boba Fett movie lost its director, got shuffled around behind the scenes while Solo: A Star Wars Story was being made, and ultimately got canceled in 2018.

The cancelation of the Boba Fett movie was disappointing at the time. Boba Fett has long been a fan-favorite character, and the movie was a chance to finally use him to his fullest potential decades after The Empire Strikes Back. However, it's now clear that canceling the Boba Fett movie was the best decision Disney and Lucasfilm could have made, as it made The Mandalorian possible. Put simply, The Mandalorian never would have been created if the Boba Fett film hadn't been axed.

Since Star Wars Episode VII: The Force Awakens hit theaters in December 2015, but the franchise has moved onto even better things on smaller screens.

Both of the possible outcomes of the Boba Fett movie being released would have led to Din Djarin not being created. If it was a hit, Star Wars would have doubled down on Fett, meaning there was no need for Djarin. As the failure of The Book of Boba Fett proved, there's really only room enough in Star Wars for one helmeted hero at a time. If it was a flop, Star Wars would have moved away from Mandalorians entirely to focus on a different part of the franchise. There's no way around it: if the Boba Fett movie happened, The Mandalorian wouldn't have.

The cancelation of the Boba Fett movie ended up being a massive blessing in disguise. That's because the creation of The Mandalorian was absolutely crucial for Star Wars, and not just because it's a great show. The Mandalorian came at exactly the time Star Wars needed it most, and it ended up rejuvenating the franchise and ushering in an era of live-action shows on Disney+.

The Rise of Skywalker premiered just a month after The Mandalorian started, and it marked a significant low point for the franchise. The sequel trilogy was already divisive and the subject of the so-called culture war, especially after The Last Jedi divided the fan base into those who loved and those who hated it. Solo: A Star Wars Story had also become the franchise's first box office bomb in 2018. The Rise of Skywalker then unified those fans, but it mostly unified them in a shared hatred of the trilogy's final chapter.

Star Wars was in a spiral at the end of 2019, and it may have only gotten worse if The Mandalorian hadn't been there to absorb some of the blow. For the first time since Disney purchased Star Wars, The Mandalorian managed to be nearly universally acclaimed by both camps of the culture wars. It was a resounding hit that drew in old and new fans of the franchise and of Baby Yoda. It also proved the viability of live-action Star Wars shows, which Disney would focus on for the next six years.

The Mandalorian certainly didn't solve all of Disney and Lucasfilm's problems, but it was more than enough to keep Star Wars healthy for nearly a decade. Instead of taking a hiatus to lick its wounds after two underwhelming movies back to back, Star Wars was huge from 2020 to 2025. The franchise produced nearly a dozen live-action and animated shows in that time. While the fanbase was largely still divided, The Mandalorian gave viewers something to come together on.

While it is possible that Boba Fett's solo movie could have been a rejuvenating force for Star Wars, there's almost no chance it would have had the same impact as The Mandalorian. The main reason for that is because Boba Fett is a legacy character from the original trilogy, while Din Djarin was a wholly new creation. As popular as Boba Fett was back in the 2010s, he also carried some baggage.

The Mandalorian was a hit precisely because it was so broadly accessible. You didn't need to watch a minute of Star Wars to understand the show. As long as you had a vague understanding of the Force, Yoda, and the Empire, you were all set. Viewers weren't even required to know what a Mandalorian is, as Din Djarin was part of a secretive and traditionalist sect. Anyone and everyone could tune in to see Baby Yoda and a space cowboy go on adventures together.

Boba Fett, on the other hand, would have been immediately polarizing. Whether the solo movie required extensive knowledge of the franchise or not, it would be perceived by the public as a very Star Wars-y story. That alone would make it very hard for the movie to find a wide audience, especially with the public perception of the franchise being as bad as it was in the wake of The Rise of Skywalker. Even if the Boba Fett movie was the best Star Wars movie ever made, Disney and Lucasfilm would have had a hard time getting people to see it.

The Mandalorian also had much more room for growth than Boba Fett did. Din was a new character that could go in any direction, he was part of a new, mysterious, and intriguing sect of Mandalorians that wasn't beholden to established lore, and he was in a standalone story. Boba Fett, on the other hand, had a set personality, a set backstory, and very little intrigue left. There's simply no way Boba Fett's movie would have been as important to Star Wars as The Mandalorian was.

There's still no word on The Mandalorian season 4 after three years and running, and The Mandalorian and Grogu lost tens of millions of dollars at the box office. Star Wars itself also seems to be leaving Din and Grogu behind to shift its attention back to the sequel era.

The struggles The Mandalorian has endured since 2023 obviously aren't great, but they also don't detract from the show's overall impact on Star Wars at large. As previously mentioned, Star Wars would be a fraction of the franchise it currently is without this show. Nearly a dozen Star Wars shows wouldn't have been made, the fanbase would be even more divided than it already is, and Disney and Lucasfilm would likely still be trying to pivot away from the sequel trilogy.

The Mandalorian is, by a wide margin, the single most important Star Wars show ever made. Nothing can take away the magic of those first two seasons or the impact they had on the franchise at large. That's why it was for the best that the Boba Fett solo movie never got made. Without it, The Mandalorian got its chance to change Star Wars forever and for the better.

10 Best HBO Shows With Less Than 20 Episodes, Ranked

Since the dawn of television’s golden era in the late ‘90s and early 2000s, HBO has been intrinsically affiliated with the very fabric of small-screen brilliance, with such series as The Sopranos, Game of Thrones, and Succession compelling viewers year after year and season after season. After Earth is decimated by war, two androids are tasked with raising humanity’s children on a foreign planet. They find their assignment growing increasingly complex and treacherous when the colony begins to fray due to religious differences.

Buoyed by the creative involvement of Ridley Scott as both executive producer and occasional director, the series delivers an arresting mixture of bold world-building, unsettling atmospheric minimalism, and thought-provoking storytelling. It aired just 18 episodes across its two-season tenure, and yet it has only grown in relevance since it was axed in 2022 courtesy of its attention to A.I.’s limited view of humanity and the at-time destructive role religion plays in society.

Unfortunately, HBO’s bold re-imaging of said series didn’t fare quite so well, lasting just two seasons and a total of 16 episodes before being canceled by the network in 2023.

Spearheaded by Matthew Rhys’ typically sublime lead performance, it follows the origin story of the famous criminal defense attorney, Perry Mason, focusing on his humble beginnings as a low-fee private eye scouring 1930s L.A. while struggling to overcome trauma tied to his service in WWI and a recent divorce. It is chiefly a scorching deconstruction of the American justice system, revolving around the trial of Nasir (Riz Ahmed), who stands accused of murdering a woman he was partying with after waking up to find her stabbed to death.

From the claustrophobic intensity of its first episode, The Night Of absorbs viewers as a relentless yet agonizingly patient descent into the darker side of the legal system, from the overwhelming power of the police department to the terrifying environment in Rikers Island. It delivers 10 outstanding episodes of war drama, and it remains one of the finest feats in the history of television.

25-Episode Netflix Adaptation Of A Classic Book Series Delivers Perfect Worldbuilding

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Worldbuilding is one of the most important ingredients in any TV show, but it’s easier with some genres than others. For fantasy and sci-fi, spectacular elements like magical kingdoms and distant planets make for great foundations to build lore on. Comedies and dramas grounded in the real world, however, have much less to work with. Making the familiar feel distinctive is no easy task.

That makes one Netflix adaptation particularly deserving of recognition. Across three seasons between 2017 and 2019, it created a setting with more personality than many fictional universes that can boast elaborate maps and centuries of invented history. Its secretive world feels vast and governed by peculiar rules that make it seem quite unlike our own reality despite being set there. Even more impressively, it achieved all this across just 25 episodes. That show is Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events.

Based on Daniel Handler’s 13 novels, published under the name Lemony Snicket, A Series of Unfortunate Events follows the three Baudelaire orphans as they bounce between adopted parents and foster families all while pursued by the murderous Count Olaf (Neil Patrick Harris). It has the sinister humor and theatrical atmosphere of a quirky fantasy, yet there’s no magic or supernatural elements to be found. Despite this, the world of A Series of Unfortunate Events feels distinct and different, and how the Netflix show turned everyday reality into somewhere so convincingly alien deserves celebration.

Netflix's A Series of Unfortunate Events may take place in the real world, but it doesn’t feel like it. It feels like a nightmare-like inversion of reality, both in terms of its aesthetics and in how the lives of the Baudelaire orphans play out following the death of their parents. Violet (Malina Weissman), Klaus (Louis Hynes), and baby Sunny (Presley Smith) are delivered from one incompetent caregiver to another, all while the authorities ignore the blatant schemes of Count Olaf and fail to keep them safe from him. They’re thrown into a system that definitely overlaps with the foster-care institutions of the real world, but operates with a disturbing incompetence that’s far removed from the genuine article.

As far as the aesthetics are concerned, A Series of Unfortunate Events creates its own world by making everything and everywhere look and feel slightly wrong despite being familiar. Its exaggerated sets make ordinary environments like a cheerily decorated living room seem capable of swallowing children whole. The unease this creates isn’t helped by the fact that the adults treat the obvious environmental dangers as perfectly reasonable.

The visual elements of A Series of Unfortunate Events’ worldbuilding are helped by the show’s deliberately scrambled relationship with history. There’s fashion, architecture, and technologies from various technologies, and this is once again presented as totally normal. Telegraphs and walkie-talkies, for example, are both treated like contemporary devices. The modern appears side-by-side with the antique in ways that technically could happen in the real world, but obviously aren’t the norm.

Patrick Warburton’s Lemony Snicket is the final masterstroke that ensures the reality presented by the Netflix show feels unique. He delivers his solemn narration like someone documenting an existing world, complete with personal regrets and information he cannot yet share. Every digression suggests another story happening beyond the children’s immediate predicament. Snicket’s musings make it clear that this world has a past just as rich as its present, making the absurdity of A Series of Unfortunate Events feel bizarrely plausible.

The convincing worldbuilding of A Series of Unfortunate events is definitely an achievement. However, the world of the Baudelaire orphans wasn’t created for the series. It came from Daniel Handler’s novels. This doesn’t make the creative vision of the show less noteworthy, however. Quite the opposite. Just as much as Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events stands out for its small-screen worldbuilding, it’s also an example of a near flawless literary adaptation. The Netflix show completely understands why the A Series of Unfortunate Events books are so beloved.

Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events succeeds as a show because it recognizes that the appeal of the books extends far beyond eccentric locations and elaborate misfortune (a mistake made by the divisive 2004 movie). The world of Handler’s books is a thematic exploration of children’s frustration with adults who possess authority without the intelligence or compassion to deserve it. It’s a setting whose central logic is that bureaucratic procedures are the governing power in all things, even when it blatantly puts the lives of children at risk. Any successful adaptation needs to bring this thematic core to screens, which the Netflix series definitely did.

The recurring failures of characters like Mr. Poe makes the world of both the A Series of Unfortunate Events Netflix show and books terrifying without requiring anything supernatural. The fact that those responsible for protecting Violet, Klaus, and Sunny repeatedly refuse to see danger that readers can is an essential part of why Handler’s novels are so engaging. The Netflix series recognized the importance of this, and that it was much more essential than the timeline of specific events or established visual details.

The show itself proves this, too, since the A Series of Unfortunate Events TV show makes multiple substantial changes to the timeline of the narrative. For example, it brings the Baudelaire’s connections to the shadowy V.F.D. organization forward so that their influence can be felt from the moment Violet, Klaus, and Sunny meet Count Olaf. Many other TV adaptations of books would have been lambasted for making such a huge alteration to the established story. A Series of Unfortunate Events gets away with it because the change helps to support a small-screen version of the narrative whose spiritual heart is a flawless recreation of everything that makes the source material so beloved.

Before ‘Band of Brothers,’ Tom Hanks Produced a Forgotten HBO Miniseries That Aged Perfectly

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the most impressive miniseries of all time is 2001's Band of Brothers. Executive produced by Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, the series is a compelling war drama that follows Easy Company, a part of the U.S. Army's 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment during World War II. Taking home 20 Emmy nominations and seven wins (including one for Outstanding Miniseries), Band of Brothers still holds up as being an authentic and powerful depiction of extraordinary historical events. But this wasn't the first time that Hanks had a hand in producing a show revolving around a true story. Just three years before, Hanks would also produce an HBO miniseries that is a must-watch today, especially for sci-fi fans who love the great beyond.

In 1865, science fiction writer Jules Verne penned a novel called From the Earth to the Moon. More than a century later, a team of Hollywood heavyweights would borrow the title for a 12-part television series depicting some of our country's most famous astronauts. The 1998 series was produced by Hanks, Ron Howard, Brian Grazer, and Michael Bostick — figures who created some of the most influential films of the 1990s. The miniseries tells the story of the Apollo program, which NASA ran during the 1960s and 1970s.

Although the episodes are dramatized, with stunning visuals and special effects by Ernest D. Farino, each episode explores one of the risky missions these astronauts undertook. Hanks introduces each episode, and then the viewer gets to dive into each mission, which is told from different perspectives. This allows every episode to showcase a variety of themes. A particularly brilliant installment is "The Original Wives Club" in Episode 11, which was directed by Oscar winner Sally Field. This episode shows how the wives of these astronauts lived through the Apollo program, leading to a behind-the-scenes look at the stress they endured while their husbands were put in danger, as well as the pressure they experienced in such public-facing positions.

From the beginning stages of the Space Race with the Soviet Union to a pseudo-documentary-style narrative in Episode 12 of the final lunar Mission (Apollo 17), every hour is as gripping as the last. While we're able to celebrate the triumphs of these astronauts as they travel where no man has before, From the Earth to the Moon never shies away from also depicting the tragedies that befell these men. We get to witness the horrific fire onboard Apollo 1 and the infamous Apollo 13 disaster, which ensures that the series is always honest in showing how truly dangerous these missions were. Although it's horrible to think of these traumas occurring to real people, the authentic storytelling makes the series even more harrowing and fascinating to watch.

The writers of From the Earth to the Moon were also intent on not just rehashing events that had already been depicted in movies prior to its release. For example, the 1983 film The Right Stuff portrayed the events of Project Mercury, so it's just briefly summarized in Episode 1. And then there's the 1995 movie Apollo 13, which was also directed by Howard and starred Hanks.

To avoid any repetition, and to ensure viewers still felt invested in the storytelling, From the Earth to the Moon tells the story of the mission from the eyes of the media covering the event. This type of creative storytelling allows the miniseries to feel fresh. While there were a few characters invented for the series, enough famous real-life figures, including Jim Lovell (Tim Daly), Buzz Aldrin (Bryan Cranston), Neil Armstrong (Tony Goldwyn), and Alan Shepard (Ted Levine), allow the show to fall into a familiar, yet captivating authenticity.

At the time of its release, From the Earth to the Moon was a critical darling, bringing home 17 Emmy nominations and three wins, including Outstanding Miniseries (just like Band of Brothers would do a few years later). It would also cement Hanks as someone who could tell historical stories in a respectful and intriguing way (paving the way for future projects such as The Pacific and Masters of the Air).

What makes From the Earth to the Moon so compelling nearly three decades later is its ability to capture both the wonder of space exploration and the enormous personal risks behind humanity's greatest achievements.

In the movie, Mothra has to convince Godzilla and Rodan to stop fighting and join forces to drive away King Ghidorah, an incredibly powerful alien monster who wiped out the alien race living on Venus and has since set his sights on Earth. Following Ghidorah defeat at the hands of Godzilla and Rodan, the villain returned for a rematch with the same two monsters in 1965's Invasion of Astro Monster.

In Destroy All Monsters, Ghidorah had an even more difficult path ahead of him when he had to fight Godzilla, Rodan, Mothra, plus an assortment of other kaiju. 19 years passed before Ghidorah was utilized again. The Godzilla franchise was rebooted in 1984 with a darker, less kid-friendly feel. Several of his allies and villains were gradually reintroduced into the franchise, with Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah giving Godzilla and Ghidorah their first one-on-one fight in 1991. Ghidorah's next two movie appearances were significant departures from the past. Nearly every movie appearance from Ghidorah - including his first and only Hollywood film - has defined Ghidorah roughly the same way. Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster established Ghidorah as an alien threat, an origin that every movie except Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack has kept. Being a film where Ghidorah was characterized as one of three benevolent guardians of the Earth, it remains an outlier in regards to the kaiju's portrayal.

Typically, his alien origins are important to the plot, such as when aliens controlled him in Invasion of Astro Monster and Destroy All Monsters. Legendary's King of the Monsters, for its part, didn't discuss it at length but heavily implied that his nature as an extraterrestrial being was the catalyst behind the destructive path he carved.

One particular movie went much deeper with his origin story. In Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah's telling of it, he doesn't begin the story as "King Ghidorah." Similar to the situation with his origins, depictions of King Ghidorah's powers are largely consistent as well. Each of Ghidorah's three heads are capable of firing blasts of yellow lightning known as "gravity beams." Godzilla: Final Wars' central villain, Keizer Ghidorah, possesses a stronger variation of the gravity beam called Destroyed Thunder, which actually allowed him to manipulate gravity.

Based on how his fights with Godzilla typically went, King Ghidorah is an incredibly powerful Godzilla villain, but not quite as strong as Destoroyah or SpaceGodzilla. But his relative, Keizer Ghidorah, has a strong case for being the most powerful Godzilla villain of all time. In any case, there's every reason to expect an intense matchup before watching Godzilla Minus Zero.

For Kim Kardashian, all's fair on the set of All's Fair.

That's why she has no problem eating prop food while filming scenes with costars Sarah Paulson, Niecy Nash-Betts, Naomi Watts, Glenn Close and Teyana Taylor for their Hulu legal drama.

"It's real food and I eat it the whole time," Kardashian said during the cast's Nov. 4 appearance on Good Morning America. "I'll be walking by after they cut and I'm like, 'Can I take a plate of that pie with me?'"

And her castmates have objections to her unusual snacking habit. As Nash-Betts quipped, "Do not let that tiny body fool you: She eats everything."

"I said, 'This is prop food! Why are you eating this?'" she continued with a laugh. "This is the greediest woman I've ever met in my life."

"We don't want to eat it all day," Paulson explained, with Watts adding, "It's a thing called continuity. If you eat it in the scene, you have to keep doing it."

Netflix's 42-Episode Sci-Fi Series Deserves Credit For Turning An Unknown Cast Into Stars

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When Stranger Things premiered in 2016, nobody expected it to completely take over the pop culture landscape the way it did, or turn its cast of then-unknowns into Hollywood A-listers. Created by the Duffer Brothers, the 1980s-set series about a group of misfit kids discovering supernatural threats and government conspiracies in their small town of Hawkins, Indiana, was a smash hit for Netflix from the start. Across nearly a decade, Stranger Things ran for a total of five seasons, broke streaming records, and became one of the biggest Netflix shows of all time.

Despite its enduring popularity throughout its run, Stranger Things became a victim of its own success. As viewership numbers swelled, so did the show's scope, lore, and, most detrimentally, its cast. Though some newer additions became breakout fan favorites, like the adorkable Robin Buckley (Maya Hawke) or the rebellious Eddie Munson (Joseph Quinn), by the time Stranger Things ended, the cast was so bloated that several OG characters got completely sidelined in the series finale.

This is a shame because these Stranger Things characters are why people fell in love with the series in the first place, whether it was the nerdy D&D group or the telekinetic Eleven (Millie Bobby Brown). When it first aired, Winona Ryder was the only famous name in the cast, but thanks to these incredible performances, the Stranger Things ensemble is now a veritable who's who of celebrity talent.

Although everyone in the Stranger Things cast was already a working actor when the show premiered, most of the young stars were just starting their careers. Nobody knew who Brown, Finn Wolfhard, Noah Schnapp, Gaten Matarazzo, or Caleb McLaughlin were yet. Even David Harbour, who had been working steadily for over two decades before Stranger Things, was unknown to the average viewer.

That all changed quickly. After the lightning-in-a-bottle magic the cast created in Stranger Things season 1, Hollywood quickly came knocking, and most cast members soon found themselves involved in major film or IP franchises during theshow's run. Brown scored the titular role in Netflix's Enola Holmes movies and appeared in the Godzilla franchise; Wolfhard had starring roles in the blockbuster franchises It and Ghostbusters; Harbour joined the MCU as the Red Guardian.
