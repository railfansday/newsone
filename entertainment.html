Prime Video's standout historical drama has announced the perfect Christmas treat for fans, with full details arriving weeks in advance. One of the streamer's most successful shows, the series has earned positive reactions from viewers worldwide and still holds a near-perfect 97% Popcornmeter score on Rotten Tomatoes. It has also performed strongly on the platform, reaching No. 1 in the US after Season 5 debuted in June 2025 and remaining in the streamer's top 10 through the end of the year.

Created, directed, and co-written by filmmaker Dallas Jenkins, The Chosen tells the poignant story of Jesus through the eyes of those who encountered him during his short life. They include his disciples, Jewish religious leaders, Roman government and military officials, and ordinary people. The Christian series' pilot aired on Facebook on December 24, 2017, before its first season premiered two years later on the faith-based streaming service VidAngel. Five seasons have been released so far, though Season 6 is set to premiere on November 15, 2026.

With The Chosen Season 6 just days away, the beloved franchise—which has spawned two spin-off series—returns in December with an all-new release, this time on network TV, per TV Insider. Titled The Chosen: The Three Kings, the drama is the final chapter in the franchise's Christmas trilogy, following 2017's The Shepherd and 2021's Christmas With The Chosen: The Messengers. The Christmas special premieres December 14, 2026, at 8/7c on Fox, then streams the next day on Hulu. It airs during The Chosen Season 6, after the first six episodes drop but before the two-episode theatrical finale about the Crucifixion debuts in March 2027.

As fans know, The Chosen features a large ensemble cast led by Jonathan Roumie as Jesus, alongside Shahar Isaac, Elizabeth Tabish, Paras Patel, Noah James, George H. Xanthis, and others. The Three Kings, by contrast, has a smaller cast, with Taye Diggs, Piter Marek, and Rahul Rai portraying the Three Wise Men from the Biblical story: Balthasar, Melchior, and Gaspar, respectively. Sara Anne and Raj Bond star as Young Mary and Joseph.

As the official description teases, The Chosen: The Three Kings follows the titular characters who, among many, witness an impossibly bright light over the tiny town of Bethlehem, signaling the birth of a new ruler. Fearing regime change or death, they journey to honor the newborn king. However, their shocking discovery upon arrival changes their lives and the world forever. Jenkins serves as executive producer, along with Ryan Swanson and Tyler Thompson.

The 8 Best TV Dramas From Before the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We are currently undeniably living in the era of streaming, but for the first few decades of its existence as a medium, network television reigned supreme. Since network shows are broadcast on a strict weekly timetable, they're usually built on very particular structures potentially containing built-in breaks before cutting to commercials, cliffhangers, and very consistent pacing. As television has evolved, so too have the characteristics of network series changed.

However, there are eight particular broadcast television dramas which are easily the greatest in history prior to the advent of streaming. Whether we're talking about classics like Twin Peaks or modern masterpieces like Game of Thrones, these are the kinds of generation-defining dramas that anyone who loves small-screen content should watch at least once in their lives. There is no understanding network television without experiencing these masterpieces.

The 1990s gave birth to a second Golden Age of television, and were an absolutely groundbreaking time for auteur-driven television. The medium shifted away from traditional network-controlled episodic formulas, and instead began embracing more serialized storytelling in line with the vision of creatives like David Lynch. Indeed, Lynch and Mark Frost's Twin Peaks is one of the most foundational auteur-driven shows of all time.

It's one of those network TV shows that are visually stunning, impeccably written, masterfully directed, and pretty much impeccable in every way that matters. Though the show saw a dip in quality right in the middle of season two, this has never stopped fans from greatly enjoying the show's intentionally uncanny tone, campy supernatural elements, and brilliant mix of melodramatic soap opera tropes and Lynch's signature style of surrealism.

Never in history has the small screen seen a prequel series even remotely as incredible as Vince Gilligan and Peter Gould's Better Call Saul. Making a prequel to a network drama widely considered the single greatest TV show of all time was never going to be an easy task, and as such, fans were very much skeptical when this project was announced. In the end, though, Better Call Saul ended up being one of the best TV shows since 2010, a near-perfect masterpiece through and through.

Better Call Saul is both an exceptional prequel adding a lot of depth and texture to the Breaking Bad world, and an incredible crime drama and legal thriller in its own right. With some masterful character writing, an excellent balance between poignant tragedy and genuinely laugh-out-loud-funny comedy, and the faultless work of an ensemble led by Bob Odenkirk at the top of his game, it's no wonder why it's the most acclaimed TV prequel in history.

Genre stories were always of vital importance to network television, and science fiction in particular was a key driver of technical innovation and a great platform for social commentary throughout the broadcast era of TV. Over the years, never has the small screen seen a sci-fi show more foundational or widely influential than Rod Serling's legendary anthology series The Twilight Zone, a gem that's essential viewing for anyone who loves sci-fi.

Serling wanted to make a show that would allow him to tackle controversial yet essential social and political issues, but since network executives refused to let him write direct socially-conscious shows, he decided to circumvent that issue. So, he made a sci-fi series that disguised its treatment of taboo topics—like racism and McCarthyism—as sci-fi, fantasy, and horror. Like any anthology series, Twilight Zone had its fair share of ups and downs throughout its run, but it's packed with some of the greatest episodes in the history of anthology TV series.

HBO was launched all the way back in 1972, but it wouldn't be until the '90s and 2000s that they would become the kings of broadcast television and the pioneers of what is now called "prestige TV." Case in point: Band of Brothers, a war drama miniseries executive-produced by Tom Hanks and Steven Spielberg that has been the highest-rated miniseries in history on IMDb for quite some time. It's easy to see why: miniseries or otherwise, history has never seen a war drama quite like this on TV.

It's one of the best HBO shows shorter than 20 episodes, a hard-hitting and faithful adaptation of the non-fiction book of the same name by historian Stephen E. Ambrose. Treating World War II with the same kind of sensitivity and raw realism as Saving Private Ryan, Band of Brothers is uncompromisingly realistic and respectful toward its characters, with a potent shift in narrative perspective every episode that gives every minute of its run a consistently surprising, thought-provoking feel.

Though its penultimate season was divisive and its final few episodes were nothing short of universally panned, Game of Thrones proves that a network drama doesn't need to be absolutely perfect in order to be counted among the best in history. Indeed, it would be silly to pretend that this HBO adaptation of George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire book series, which was a gargantuan pop culture phenomenon over the course of its eight seasons, is anything less than legendary.

Where can one even begin singing the praises of such a praise-worthy masterpiece? For one, the production values are off-the-charts, including some of the greatest visuals and music in the history of fantasy television. Add to that some exceptional writing, an unrelentingly bleak and violent tone, and one of the best TV cast ensembles of the 2010s, and you get a genre-defining masterpiece whose quality far transcends any mistakes that its final season may have made.

As if any more proof were needed that there's just no matching HBO's quality and legacy when it comes to the greatest dramas in the history of network television, there's also The Wire. Creator David Simon had spent 12 years covering crime and city systems as a police reporter for The Baltimore Sun, while co-writer Ed Burns had worked inside the Baltimore Police Department as a homicide detective. Inspired by their experiences and what they'd seen, the duo co-created The Wire.

It's a scathing study and deconstruction of five particular institutional layers of Baltimore: the illegal drug trade, the port system, the government, education, and the media. But far from feeling niche or exploitative, The Wire instead delivers the most complete portrait of American urban decay ever put to screen. Full of richly layered characters and potent thematic work, it's one of the many pinnacles of 21st-century crime drama television.

Network television would simply have never become what it is today had it not been for The Sopranos. In television's history, there's a "before The Sopranos" and an "after The Sopranos," as David Chase's crime drama completely re-defined what the medium was able to do. Never before had television seen such darkly antiheroic characters, such cinematic quality, and such cleanly serialized storytelling. Thus, prestige television was born, and the small screen was never the same again.

But though it was a pioneer of this new version of network television, The Sopranos doesn't feel dated in the slightest all these many years later; on the contrary, it's still generally considered the best HBO show to date. Bolstered by the phenomenal writing, James Gandolfini's powerhouse lead performance, and some of the richest psychological depth that a broadcast drama has ever seen, it's a must-see for anyone who loves television.

Over a decade after its conclusion, most people still widely refer to Vince Gilligan's Breaking Bad as the single greatest TV drama in history, and it will take something very special to ever dethrone it. Gilligan came up with the concept after fellow writer Tom Schnauz joked that they should solve their lack of work by buying up an RV and setting up a mobile meth lab. The idea stuck in his mind for years, and would eventually transformed into the most complex, cinematic, and irresistibly riveting drama that television has ever produced.

Breaking Bad is one of those drama shows that have aged like fine wine, a masterpiece whose every element works in flawless concert with the others to deliver the most enthralling experience possible. The production values are fantastic, the ensemble is full of actors delivering career-best work, and the character writing is the dramatic fuel that pushes the narrative momentum forward throughout. From top to bottom and beginning to end, Breaking Bad is fully worthy of being officially named the greatest TV drama of all time.

6 Upcoming Netflix Thriller Shows, Ranked by Hype

Netflix has never had trouble making thriller shows. The problem is that the streamer releases so many of them that it can be hard to keep up, since plenty of great ones vanish from the Top 10 within a week, replaced by the next viral hit. The real challenge is making one that people still talk about after the binge cycle is over, and that takes more than a twisty premise and a cliffhanger at the end of every episode.

Lately, Netflix has been aiming much higher. Some of the most acclaimed writers in TV are bringing their next big ideas to the streamer, and they’re pairing them with casts that would normally headline a prestige film. The result is an intentional, ambitious slate that could finally prove that the streamer’s thrillers are worth more than one lazy weekend on the couch. Here are six upcoming Netflix thriller shows, ranked by hype.

The Twin has a premise that sounds simple until it starts to spiral. Jamie Dornan plays identical twin brothers, Adam and Lee, in this six-episode crime noir set against the moody backdrop of the Scottish Highlands. Adam is stuck in a cold, loveless marriage with Nicola (Mackenzie Davis), and the return of his estranged brother throws their already fragile life into chaos. When tragedy strikes, a dark secret forces the twins’ lives to become dangerously tangled, and Lee and Nicola soon find themselves drawn to each other. However, they know their time together could run out at any moment. The show, based on the Norwegian series Twin, arrives on Netflix on January 1, 2027.

Twin stories are a thriller staple, but The Twin seems more interested in guilt and desire than in the typical identity-swap narrative. Dornan has already proven he’s great at playing men with something to hide thanks to his performance in The Fall, and playing two versions of the same face gives the actor plenty to work with. The love story at the center of it all only complicates things further and gives the audience one more reason to stay until the very end. Netflix is giving the series a New Year’s Day slot, which says a lot about how much faith the streamer has in it. If it holds, The Twin will be the perfect slow-burn mystery to kick off 2027.

The mere idea of the creator of Bodyguard reuniting with Richard Madden has viewers hooked on Trinity, and the show isn’t even out yet. The series, helmed by the brilliant Jed Mercurio, follows Gugu Mbatha-Raw as Commander Katherine Decker, a decorated U.S. Navy officer, who becomes romantically involved with the charismatic Secretary of Defense, Webb Preston (Madden), only to discover that he may be at the center of a dangerous conspiracy. As a nuclear crisis looms, Katherine has to figure out who she can trust before it’s too late. Marcia Gay Harden and Bruce Greenwood round out the cast, and the series premieres on December 1.

Now, Mercurio is a master of keeping the audience on the edge of their seats. His shows are known for tense, twisty conversations where a single line can change everything, and Bodyguard proved he knows exactly how to use Madden’s charm to keep viewers guessing. This time, the roles are flipped, since Madden is the powerful man under suspicion instead of the loyal protector. Putting a romance at the heart of a political conspiracy is also an interesting choice that gives the drama an emotional center the characters can keep coming back to. Trinity is all set to live up to the fans' expectations of Mercurio and become Netflix’s next big obsession with the holiday season.

The Boys from Brazil has one of the most chilling premises in thriller history, and Netflix is bringing it back with a cast that is almost too good to be true. The show is based on Ira Levin’s 1976 novel, which also inspired the 1978 film starring Gregory Peck and Laurence Olivier. The five-part limited series follows Nazi hunter Yakov Liebermann (Jeremy Strong). Liebermann stumbles onto evidence that a notorious Nazi doctor, long believed to be dead, is alive and orchestrating a mysterious plan to bring about a Fourth Reich. Gillian Anderson, Daniel Brühl, August Diehl, Shira Haas, and Lizzy Caplan co-star, and the series is being written by The Crown creator Peter Morgan.

All of that already sets The Boys from Brazil up to be a massive success. Anyone who knows the book knows how shocking the twist behind the plot is, and a limited series has far more room to explore it than the original film did. The story slowly builds as Liebermann realizes that dozens of ordinary, unrelated men in different countries are being murdered one by one. Figuring out what connects them is the heart of the mystery. Peter Morgan has spent years turning real history into gripping drama, and Strong is the perfect fit for Liebermann, a man whose obsession with justice consumes his entire life. Netflix hasn’t set a release date for the show yet, but The Boys from Brazil already looks like one of the streamer’s most ambitious and unsettling thrillers to date.

After years of terrifying audiences with Black Mirror, Charlie Brooker is using his sharp wit in a gritty detective drama. Blackmere follows the tormented Detective John Blackmere (Paddy Considine), who leaves the rain-soaked northern city of Bleakford for London to avenge his partner’s death and catch a ritualistic serial killer before they run out of victims. The cast of the show, which arrives on Netflix in early 2027, also features Georgina Campbell, Lena Headey and Rory Kinnear.

There is a catch, though. Netflix describes Blackmere as a deadly serious crime thriller, but early details make it clear that Brooker intends for the show to poke fun at every cliché in the genre. That makes the series one of the most unusual entries on this list, because it works as both a parody and a true mystery. Brooker is great at satirizing his subject matter, and gritty British detective shows give him plenty to work with. The showrunner has spent more than a decade proving that he understands what makes audiences squirm, and there is no doubt that Blackmere will be all over social media the moment it drops.

The Body sounds like a wild mix of coming-of-age drama and Catholic horror, with an extremely intriguing premise. The eight-episode series, created by Quinn Shephard, follows a group of students at a Catholic girls’ school whose strange visions soon plunge their small town into chaos. The whole thing starts as a ritual but quickly spirals into something much darker, as the line between faith and hysteria begins to blur. Kristina Bogic, Sara Boustany and Geena Meszaros lead the young cast, with Sofia Wylie also starring.

The series premieres on Netflix on November 11 and aims to put a spin on all the nightmares of being a teenager. Shepherd’s show takes obvious inspiration from similar films, including Carrie, Jennifer’s Body, and Heavenly Creatures, all of which talk about the dark side of growing up as a girl, where friendship and obsession can be hard to tell apart. The Body sets out to capture that same mix of camp and dread, and if it does that right, it can easily become a Netflix cult favorite.

Rabbit, Rabbit brings together some of TV's most exciting names for a compelling hostage thriller. The series follows Adam Driver as J-Will, an escaped convict who is cornered by police at a truck stop in Illinois and takes hostages to bargain for his freedom. Opposite him is Regina Hall as Poppy, a veteran FBI crisis negotiator trained in a technique known as “tactical empathy.” As the standoff between the two drags on, it turns into a twisted social experiment among the captives and an emotional battle of wits. Filming took place in New Jersey earlier this year, but the series does not have a release date yet.

The talent behind the camera is what makes Rabbit, Rabbit the most hyped show on this list. It’s written by Peter Craig, who co-wrote The Batman and Top Gun: Maverick, and every episode is directed by Philip Barantini, whose Adolescence became one of Netflix’s biggest phenomena. Barantini has a gift for trapping audiences in a single, suffocating moment, and a hostage standoff is the perfect playground for that style. It’s also Driver’s first major TV role since Girls, and watching him go head-to-head with Hall sounds like an acting showdown for the ages.

New York building collapse kills at least four people

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Watch: Aerial video shows survivor being pulled from collapsed building in New York

At least four people have died after a building in New York City collapsed into an open construction site on Wednesday afternoon.

Nineteen others have been injured, of whom four are in a critical condition, according to the BBC's US partner CBS News, which cited local officials.

The collapse sparked a massive search-and-rescue operation, with more than 250 first responders going to the scene of the fallen three-storey building in Brooklyn.

Aerial footage has shown firefighters pulling a survivor, covered in dust, out of the debris on a stretcher. She was later said to have been a pregnant woman.

Officials previously said they did not know how many others are missing. The BBC has contacted the New York City Fire Department (FDNY) for further comment.

An investigation is under way into what caused the sudden collapse.

Witnesses described a terrifying sound as the building came down at about 16:00 local time (21:00 GMT).

"I live literally right behind the building," one woman, Emmanuella Carilus, told the New York Times. "It sounded like a big boom and you just heard a bunch of screaming."

The newspaper published images showing a crowd of fire trucks and police vehicles at the same, and wrote that the noise of sirens had not stopped in the area.

"I've never seen anything like this," said another witness, named only as Dashik, who spoke to local television station PIX 11.

Officials are yet to identify those who died or were injured.

Officials say the rescue efforts could last for days

Locals have gathered at the scene to await news of those feared to have been caught in the collapse.

The rescue mission has been described as "extremely dangerous" by New York City Fire Department (FDNY) Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore.

She added that people from the collapsed building and also the construction site had been trapped.

"Members have been working methodically and haven't stopped since they arrived this afternoon," Bonsignore said.

The woman who was filmed being rescued from the rubble was identified by her husband.

Speaking to a reporter from CBS News New York, he described his relief after an anxious wait: "She is alive. She is still alive. I'm very happy. I'm very happy. I'm very happy. I thank all of you guys. She's pregnant, six months."

Rescuers have used search dogs, drones, axes and chainsaws to carry out their work. They say they anticipate the effort will last for several days.

The collapse exposed parts of the building's interior - including bedrooms and wardrobes

Some pictures of the collapsed building, on Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn, show an entire side of it gone, with bedrooms and closets exposed. Among the debris, smashed concrete and twisted metal, are household items and clothes.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani joined emergency responders at the scene, praising them for their rapid response.

They "arrived in under a minute and have spent hours working in dangerous and highly technical conditions to rescue New Yorkers," he later wrote on X.

The Brooklyn building was built in 1930, city records show, according to CBS, the BBC's US partner.

At the adjacent construction site, where plans have been approved for a new building, active work was going on when the incident occurred, according to FDNY Chief of Department John Esposito.

The activity was described by another official as "excavation work". It was not immediately clear whether the work had anything to do with the collapse of the neighbouring building.

8 Fantasy Books With Over 100,000 Goodreads Ratings That Are Worth the Hype

As someone who’s used Letterboxd religiously for quite a few years now, the best way to explain it to people who've not heard of Letterboxd (they do exist, especially if you venture outside the online world!) is to say it’s like Goodreads, but for movies instead of books. And most people have heard of Goodreads, even if they don’t themselves have accounts there.

If you're not in the know, then it basically lets you keep track of what you're reading, what you’ve read, and what you'd still like to read. You can rate titles, review them if you want, or even “DNF” them (marking something as “did not finish”). The site is a bit of a popularity contest, sure, but it’s interesting to see what appeals to people, with the following fantasy books all being quite loved on Goodreads, and more than 100,000 ratings each, not to mention generally high average ratings across the board, too.

His Dark Materials was a trilogy that’s since grown into a series, though it’s the first three books that are most essential. The Subtle Knife is the second, and probably the strongest chapter of that trilogy, since it skillfully adds another central character to the proceedings while effectively continuing on from the surprising way the first book (Northern Lights) ended, in relation to the characters who were established in that initial novel.

Trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi between Russia, Ukraine and the US aimed at ending the war in Ukraine have concluded after a second day.

Davyd Arakhamia from Ukraine's negotiating team said that this round of talks - the first since February - "was one of the hardest."

Both Moscow and Kyiv confirmed that a large-scale prisoner of war exchange was in the works and could be carried out soon.

After the talks it was announced that on Thursday US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner - President Donald Trump's son-in-law - would travel to Moscow to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin, and then to Dublin to speak to Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky.

This latest round of talks had been framed as centred around the "security architecture" of Europe.

For Kyiv, this may include reassurances that allies will come to its aid if Ukraine comes under attack again, despite the country not being a Nato member.

For Moscow, it is likely to include a limit to the influence of Nato in eastern Europe - a central concern for the Kremlin since the alliance's eastward expansion.

Earlier this week Kushner said the US hoped to "create a new framework" of security between Ukraine, Russia and Europe that could "secure lasting peace", while Kremlin negotiator Vladimir Medinsky said the world needed a "new" and "fair" security system.

Ukraine and Europe have long been concerned about the current US administration's commitment to European security and its softening towards the Kremlin.

Representatives of Britain, Nato and various EU countries were also in Abu Dhabi and met with US and Ukrainian negotiators, but the Kremlin - with whom the EU does not officially communicate - said its delegation had not had any contact with them.

Kushner said the US was keen to bring the Europeans on board and said that getting them "added to the format" would be on the agenda for his talks with Putin.

There is also disagreement on whether discussions on a future security framework should be preceded by a ceasefire, as the Europeans and Kyiv would want, or whether the fighting only stops after an agreement has been reached, as the Kremlin has insisted.

Any agreement on a peacetime security framework, however, is contingent on the war ending, Kushner noted. But that cannot occur "until there's an agreement on territory and on all other bilateral issues," he added.

Since talks began last year, a core obstacle to any settlement has been Ukraine's eastern region of Donetsk, which is mostly occupied by Russia. Moscow has demanded that Ukraine relinquish the parts of Donetsk it still holds, while Kyiv wants to freeze the contact line to maintain control over those areas.

This matter was the "hardest", Kushner acknowledged, while Zelensky's chief of staff and negotiator Kyrylo Budanov refused to be drawn on whether Russia was still demanding a Ukrainian withdrawal from the region.

Leonardo Bertulazzi was first arrested in Buenos Aires in 2002

A 74-year-old Italian man who was found guilty of a kidnapping carried out by a far-left militant group in the 1970s has escaped while under house arrest in Argentina and is on the run.

Leonardo Bertulazzi used a harness to abseil down from a fifth-floor balcony in the capital, Buenos Aires.

His escape was not immediately registered because he had tampered with his electronic ankle monitor so that it continued to transmit body temperature measurements even after he had prised it open and absconded.

Bertulazzi already spent decades as a fugitive after he was sentenced in absentia in Italy for the 1977 kidnapping of Italian naval engineer Pietro Costa.

He was awaiting extradition to Italy and was being monitored by the Argentine ministry of security through an ankle tag which registers the wearer's body temperature and movements.

Officials said that they had become suspicious when they noticed that the tag had remained stationary.

When they entered the fifth-floor apartment in the centre of Buenos Aires, Bertulazzi was gone, but the ankle monitor remained.

Wary of Bertulazzi's past with the Marxist guerilla group the Red Brigades and his bomb-making skills, police called in explosives experts in case it had been rigged to detonate, according to Argentine newspaper ClarÃ­n.

However, the experts found that while Bertulazzi had tampered with the device, he had done so to prevent the alarm from sounding once he removed it.

Italian media say that his extradition to Italy had been imminent after his last appeal was rejected.

Bertulazzi was first arrested after using a false passport to cross into Argentina from neighbouring Chile in 2002.

However, he was granted refugee status in 2004 and lived freely in the South American country until his re-arrest in 2024, when the current right-wing president, Javier Milei, took office.

Milei's government said at the time that "Bertulazzi is responsible for crimes that undermined democratic values and the lives of many victims".

He was a member of the Red Brigades, which kidnapped and killed a number of state officials in the 1970s and '80s.

Bertulazzi was part of the Red Brigades' Genoa section that kidnapped Pietro Costa on the street and then held him for 81 days before receiving a big ransom.

The money it made from the kidnapping was then used to buy a flat in Rome, which was used in 1978 for the Red Brigades' most notorious crime, the kidnap and murder of Italian former Prime Minister, Aldo Moro.

Moro was abducted when his car was ambushed on the way to the opening of parliament. He was held in the flat for 54 days before he was shot and his body abandoned in the boot of a Renault car in the centre of Rome.

Iconic Character Officially Confirmed For Buffy the Vampire Slayer's 2027 Return

As Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Angel head towards their 2027 return, it's been confirmed that several fan-favorite characters will return. However, a major unanswered question means that fans don't know exactly what version of a Buffyverse icon they'll be getting.

Beginning with the 1992 movie starring Kristy Swanson, Buffy the Vampire Slayer was rebooted into an iconic 1997 TV series starring Sarah Michelle Gellar as the titular hero. Now, the story will continue, though fans aren't yet clear on where the new narrative will pick up from.

One thing fans do know is that fan-favorite characters including Spike, Faith and Tara will return. But with some of them dead by the conclusion of the original TV series and others transformed over the course of the narrative, fans don't quite know what to expect...

In March 2027, Dynamite Entertainment will publish Buffy the Vampire Slayer #1, from Kelly Thompson, Stephen Byrne, Lee Loughridge and Jeff Eckleberry. The bumper-sized issue will begin a new narrative spread across the Buffy the Vampire Slayer series and a new Angel series, from Thompson and Giulia Giacomino.

Now, thanks to a press release from Dynamite and recent interviews by Thompson, fans know some details of which characters will return. Character designs shared by Dynamite (below) have confirmed several members of the Scooby Gang, including Spike, Dawn, Tara, Faith and Anya, with preview art confirming Angel's Fred and Thompson ruling out Oz for the time being.

Meanwhile, Dynamite promises the "comics debut of a beloved Buffy fan-favorite character" who is represented by a silhouette on the cover art shared so far. While these announcements are Manna from Heaven to longtime Buffy fans, major questions have been raised about the timeline and how it affects the cast.

Thanks to details like Tara being alive and Xander having both eyes, fans have been asking questions about how the comics fit into the TV series timeline. The question is a big deal for Spike, who only gains a soul late in season 6, significantly altering his outlook and motives.

The first issue's summary referencing Buffy's death has fans speculating that the comics will pick up after season 5 of the original show, though this would mean the majority of TV Angel wouldn't be in canon. The summary reads:

Eisner-winning writer KELLY THOMPSON (Absolute Wonder Woman, Black Widow) and superstar artist STEPHEN BYRNE (Wonder Twins) present a vision of BUFFY THE VAMPIRE SLAYER you've got to read to believe! Featuring the comics debut of a beloved Buffy fan-favorite character!

Into every generation, a slayer is born. And Buffy Summers may have broken the mold. Though Buffy's powers were varied and great, it was the love and devotion she inspired that was perhaps her greatest power. And when she gave her life to save the world, the people who loved her couldn't live with that. This is that story.

Speaking to AIPT, Thompson acknowledges that she's been deliberately cagey about the timeline, while implying the comic WILL respect the show timeline up to a certain point, saying:

I’m not pinning it down. I do think that anyone who’s paying attention to the sketches and stuff that have been out, Fred is in one sketch, and Dawn is there, too. So these things suggest it has to be at least a certain point. It’s a terrific non-ness.

Thompson also promises that Buffy the Vampire Slayer #1 will act as an "atom bomb" for the characters, suggesting that while the comics will likely begin at a certain point within the TV timeline, it won't necessarily feel the need to fit into the gap between that and future seasons, likely charting its own course in terms of ongoing continuity.

One area in which this is likely to be noteworthy is Tara's characterization. Thompson says:

I don’t want people to come and think they’re getting ‘The Tara Show.’ She’s a supporting character at best in this first arc. We’ve got to build to [more interesting characterization], especially with the big reveals that are in issue #1.

From what fans know so far, it's clear that the new Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Angel series are going to be an explosive return to the franchise, coming from a writer who knows it inside and out and isn't afraid to take big swings with this new era.

At the same time, the series' sense of independence from canon and where it fits into it is likely to lead to character regression in some places and evolution in others. Fans will find out exactly how these characters will change when Buffy the Vampire Slayer returns in 2027.

However, it was Reacher’s risky decision to go with TV-MA rating that allowed the show to spawn a spinoff, Neagley, and become a global streaming sensation.

Reacher season 1 started with Book 1, Killing Floor, before season 2 adapted Book 11, 2007’s Bad Luck and Trouble, season 3 brought Book 7, 2003’s Persuader, to life, and 2026's season 4 adapted 2009’s Book 13, Gone Tomorrow. However, while all of these novels were successful upon publication, Prime Video’s Reacher needed its TV-MA rating to stand out among the many other high-quality action thrillers on the small screen.

An R-rating was the biggest thing missing from 2012’s passable but unspectacular Jack Reacher movie adaptation and its 2016 sequel, even more so than star Tom Cruise not fitting Reacher’s physical description from the novels. What makes Prime Video’s Reacher and its spinoff Neagley so great is the fact that neither show shies away from the bloody brutality of the source novels, with Ritchson’s Reacher mercilessly snapping necks and killing villains throughout the series.

Before Reacher’s 2022 debut, few streaming action shows were as unapologetically harsh as the Prime Video hit, and this was a major reason that critics called Netflix’s Iron Fist, Prime Video’s Alex Rider, Citadel, and other high-profile releases forgettable. In contrast, since Reacher’s success, everything from Daredevil: Born Again to Lioness, to Cross, Black Doves, The Night Agent, and The Day of the Jackal has borrowed this once-rare approach.

The style of these action thriller shows varies wildly, with The Day of the Jackal being a tense psychological thriller while The Night Agent is more of an escapist adventure. However, one thing they all have in common, and one thing that is missing from the comparatively critically lambasted Taylor Sheridan series Marshals, is their shared R rating.

Reacher’s moments of vicious gore and jaw-dropping violence ground the show in something resembling recognizable reality, meaning the title character’s adventures always have tangible stakes, even if he is seemingly indestructible. It would have been very easy for Prime Video to cautiously tone down this approach with the 2026 spinoff Neagley, as Marshals did when moving the Yellowstone franchise to CBS.

However, the streaming service instead doubled down on its winning formula, and reaped the rewards quickly. Neagley is now almost as massive a hit with critics and audiences as c, and the era of the TV-MA action thriller is only getting stronger.

“I was having an affair with a married woman,” Hoffman, 89, admitted. “She thought that once her husband went to asleep, that he stayed asleep.”

Explaining that he had been invited to the woman’s home when her husband had presumably gone to bed, when suddenly it had become clear that the man was well aware of what was going on.

“There we are, having a festival of sorts, and he walked in with a gun,” the “Rain Man” actor shared. “He pointed it at me and he said, I remember what he said, ‘Get out, get out.’”

“When you have a gun pointed at you,” he explained, “You realize how, any second, any second he’s going to pull that trigger. His finger is on the trigger and you think any second he’s going to. You realize, ‘That’s it, I’m gonna die. I’m gonna die, the next second, the next second, the next second.”

The actor recalled that in that moment, he came to the realization that “you will do anything to avoid getting killed.”

At another point in the interview, O’Brien asked the Los Angeles native to detail his move to New York. Hoffman relocated across the country in 1958 at 21, hoping to pursue a career in acting.

As O’Brien put it, Hoffman worked “a lot of absurd odd jobs” at this point in his life, including a role at Macy’s, where he was tasked with selling toys—like foosball and table hockey—to adults.

As an aspiring actor, Hoffman put his own spin on the job, and decided to embody a character of his own making; a retired, injured French hockey player.

“Gene Hackman was right there at adult games, also,” Hoffman said, noting that the fellow actor was his coworker at the time. Decades later, the pair would go on to work together again, starring in the 2003 film, “Runaway Jury.”

Hackman and Hoffman weren’t just coincidental coworkers. They lived together, too.

“You go home to your roommates, and they are Gene Hackman and Robert Duvall. The three of you, I’m picturing a bunk bed with three levels. And you guys are snoring in unison like ‘The Three Stooges.’ That’s what I’m picturing,” joked O’Brien.

Hoffman said that the three men, who eventually each became very famous actors, “didn’t live together quite like that.”

Although he seems to look back on the experience with a sense of humor now, his memoir tells a different story. In an excerpt from the book, Hoffman wrote that he “was so frightened of New York.”

“I didn’t want to go outside. I’d been excited to get there, but once I arrived it scared the hell out of me,” he said. “All these thousands of people who knew what they were doing, whereas I was completely in the dark. It was intimidating.”

At one point, Hoffman lived with Hackman and his first wife, Faye Maltese, in “a little apartment on the East Side.”

“I slept on the kitchen floor next to their refrigerator, which shook and made a lot of noise, like it was having a heart attack once or twice a night,” Hoffman recalled.

Hoffman had originally only planned to stay with Hackman and his wife for a few days, but the arrangement lasted three weeks before “the Hackmans ran out of patience and told me it was time to move on.”

Hackman advised Hoffman to, “go up Riverside Drive and knock on the doors and ask to speak to the superintendent,” to find a new place to live. The actor recalled starting at 60th Street and Central Park West, and knocking on “every door of every building all the way to the Hudson River and back again” until he eventually found a place on 109th street.

He rented a top-floor apartment in a building in Spanish Harlem for $80 a month, and knew he’d “need roommates to afford it,” which is where Duvall came in.

“We had a lot of rowdy nights in that apartment: pizza, unemployed actors, candles in chianti bottles,” Hackman wrote. “I would play the piano. Duvall would sing country music songs. If someone had pointed at Bob and Gene and me at one of our parties and said, ‘Those three are going to be movie stars,” everyone in the apartment would have laughed.”

Even after Hoffman got his first big break, it was a while before he’d find his footing financially. During the podcast interview, he said he was paid $3,000 for his role in "The Graduate," which was released in 1967. But after the film’s premiere, he still needed more funds.

“You didn’t have any money, and so you went back to the unemployment office and someone at Life magazine took a photo of you,” O’Brien said. “The most famous face in America at the time, waiting in the unemployment line to get a check.”

Hoffman confirmed that the story was true, adding that the check was for about $50.

“$55, I think was the most you could get,” he continued, quipping that at that time, people “could live on” about “$200 a month.”

The upcoming adaptation of Brandon Sanderson's Stormlight Archive looks set to be the most exciting upcoming fantasy TV series currently in development. While original TV series like Stranger Things have made their mark on the fantasy landscape, most adaptations, with the exception of Dungeons & Dragons projects, have not fared so well. The Wheel of Time and The Witcher looked set to be groundbreaking achievements for the genre, but where they failed to live up to their potential, The Stormlight Archive may be the fantasy TV show franchise we need.

One main reason is due to Brandon Sanderson's unprecedented creative control over his projects. The author has been more involved than virtually any other, which has allowed him to oversee the creative process, including the casting, which he has been passionate about. The best fantasy TV show ensemble casts have excellent chemistry and acting ability, but bringing an already beloved character to life is a challenge for any actor, and while Sanderson has a very specific vision, these actors could do the characters justice.

Of all the actors who would be perfect for Brandon Sanderson's Stormlight Archive, Aramis Knight has repeatedly drawn the attention of fans, who have seen him as a potentially perfect choice for Kaladin Stormblessed. Kaladin's formidable martial arts and healing skills make him one of the series' most conflicted characters, suffering from depression and survivors' guilt, and torn between the desire to heal and the need to kill in order to protect people.

This internal struggle makes Kaladin one of fantasy's most interesting heroes, and as he also needs to inspire loyalty from the people he commands, this adds an extra layer of nuance. Knight's martial arts-heavy work in Into the Badlands and Ms. Marvel make him an ideal actor to handle the action scenes, while he often plays characters with difficult backstories, proving him capable of taking on the role of a man with the world on his shoulders.

Shallan Davar is one of the central characters in The Stormlight Archive, and a fan favorite who has inspired considerable debate over her potential casting. She is intelligent, witty, artistic, and complicated in a very human way, as most of Sanderson's characters are. Coming from a troubled family background, she uses humor as a shield, often projecting a confident and charming exterior as she struggles beneath the surface.

This description could easily apply to Stranger Things' Max, Sadie Sink's breakout role, which won her the Hollywood Critics Association TV Award for Best Supporting Actress in a Streaming Series, Drama. Sadie Sink's best movies and TV shows all showcase her ability to play a multifaceted character with emotional depth that shows in her face without the need for extensive dialogue, and Shallan feels like a continuation of these characters, but at a more mature level.

Practically unstoppable on the battlefield, the highprince of Alethkar was once a ruthless warrior whose fearsome reputation as "the Blackthorn" preceded him. The fantasy genre is full of archetypes like this, but Brandon Sanderson's Cosmere books stand out for the way they turn these tropes upside-down, and the character we meet is no longer a warlord. Dalinar Kholin is now a very introspective man, and casting him requires an actor with more than just an imposing physique.

At first glance, Dalinar is similar to Khal Drogo, Jason Momoa's character in Game of Thrones, but the role needs elements of Momoa's other characters to be a successful casting. While best known as an action star with a commanding screen presence, Momoa's performances in Dune and See showcase more of the range that he is not always assumed to have, and playing out Dalinar's redemption arc would be a good opportunity to highlight his more subtle acting skills.

The friendly and charming prince is the opposite of what a reader would expect the son of the Blackthorn to be, but Adolin Kholin's fan-described "golden retriever energy" has made him one of the most popular characters in the Cosmere universe. Adolin is so important that he is one of the few characters that Brandon Sanderson has personally fan-cast. However, while Sanderson suggested Alex Landi for the role, he has since aged beyond the character.

Dallas Liu already resembles a younger Landi and did his own stunts in Avatar: the Last Airbender, where he played Prince Zuko, also a fan favorite character. Zuko's journey from villain to hero is one of the best storylines in the fantasy genre, and Liu has both the charm, humor, and physical fighting training to convincingly play Adolin, including his complicated relationship with his father.

With a former warlord as a father and a celebrated swordsman as a brother, Renarin Kholin has a lot to live up to, and the responsibility weighs on him, despite his growing knowledge that he has skills of his own. Renarin's story is one of the most unsettling in the Cosmere universe, with the younger prince becoming a Truthwatcher, which drastically changes his demeanor from a gentle, reserved, and studious boy into one who can see elements of the future.

Any actor playing Renarin must be able to capture the shyness and anxiety that Sanderson has confirmed he has, before also portraying deep and complicated wisdom far beyond that of most other characters. Sean Kaufman's performance in The Summer I Turned Pretty showcases a seemingly outgoing character experiencing moments of vulnerability, and he could expand on this role while being believable as Dallas Liu's younger brother.

The scholarly Jasnah is one of the most intelligent and politically influential women in the Cosmere universe, and her radical honesty makes her polarizing among fans. Any actor playing Jasnah must be able to straddle the line between her admirable insistence on speaking her mind and her willingness to suggest the unthinkable, as she must remain likable even while suggesting options that frighten career politicians.

Florence Faivre was captivating during her short scenes as The Expanse's Julie Mao, even while being seen through cameras and the eyes of other characters. Mao's rebellious streak as she breaks from her highly influential family hits similar notes to Jasnah's arc, despite the stories being drastically different genres and with different final fates. Faivre can convey intelligence and conviction without needing to overact, and this is likely to be the key to keeping Jasnah fascinating, even to viewers who do not like the character.

Michelle Yeoh's career-defining movies began by showcasing her work as a talented martial artist, but her nuanced character acting has taken center stage in recent years, with Everything Everywhere All At Once and Wicked both focusing on her ability to make even an exaggerated character feel real. Yeoh may still be defined by her stunts and action work, but playing the Cosmere universe's Navani Kholin would give her even more to work with, as the character has one of Roshar's leading scientific minds.

Even in book form, Navani feels vivid and three-dimensional, negotiating with kings in a political firestorm one moment and being absorbed in an experiment the next. Her dedication to her family sets her as the Kholin matriarch, but to put her on the sidelines as merely a wife and mother does not do her justice. ‘Here There Are Blueberries’ Broadway Review: Evil As Banal As A Bowl Of Fruit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Here There Are Blueberries, the exceptional Pulitzer finalist docu-drama that opened Off Broadway two years ago to great admiration, finally makes its Broadway debut tonight at the Barrymore Theatre. The play, about a mysterious real-life cache of one-of-a-kind Auschwitz photos submitted to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in 2007, remains as intriguing as ever – and with source material as grotesquely mesmerizing as these candid snapshots, how could it not?

In fact, the sheer emotional heft of that photo album and everything it suggests dominates Blueberries to an extent that the play itself can seem rather thin at times, not entirely unworthy of the material at hand but not on equal footing either. Revelations about the banality of evil carry a force no matter how familiar we are with the concept, but familiar we are, and Blueberries can feel somewhat naive at times in its presumption that no one in the audience has ever heard of Hannah Arendt.

But in the grand scheme of things, and in light of the historical tale being told here, those quibbles with dramaturgy shouldn’t diminish the real achievement that is Here There Are Blueberries. The play is vital and more often than not riveting. Watch on Deadline

Performed by an excellent cast – any production that features the great Kathleen Chalfant is an opportunity not to be missed – Here There Are Blueberries is, at its core, a detective tale. In 2007, a young Holocaust museum archivist named Rebecca Erbelding (Delia Cunningham) receives a letter at her D.C. office from a man who wants to donate a cache of WWII snapshots.

The offer isn’t unusual – the museum serves as a repository for such keepsakes – but the nature of his collection most certainly is. The photo collection long in the man’s possession is more than a rarity, it’s absolutely unique: The images are of Nazi officers – including some of the highest ranking and most notorious, from Mengele to Höss – in their living quarters just over the wall from the camp’s atrocities. Some are of an almost surreal vacation lodge a few miles away, where officers, SS guards and female office workers alike would be treated to days of rest and relaxation for jobs well done. One officer is given five days there for his murder of two Jews attempting to escape the camp.

Such images had never been observed in photographs before, and their discovery is nothing less than historic, generating headlines across the globe, with much of the public fascination falling on the young German female camp office workers enjoying their vacation lodge days and food-and-drink treats. “Here,” says the caption (in German) of one such photo, “there are blueberries.”

The play is structured as an investigation, lead by the young archivist, as the museum staff seeks to uncover the circumstances under which these snapshots were taken, who took them, who is pictured, and, perhaps most intriguingly, how on earth did they survive when all over such evidence was destroyed by the Nazis themselves as the regime fell?

One such question – how did the man who possesses the photos discover them? – is easily answered: A retired U.S. Army Lieutenant Colonel (Michael Cerveris) found the album in the abandoned apartment he briefly inhabited while stationed in Frankfort at the end of the war. Why he kept them in his possession, and did so secretly, is unknowable – he died shortly after making the museum donation.

Other questions posed by the photos are of a more philosophical nature. Did those seemingly carefree young women at the lodge have any idea that the office work they were doing contributed to the genocide next door? And if so, how could they?

Blueberries, for all its demands on facing harsh truths, plays a bit coy in feigning shock at the answers, as if Judgment At Nuremberg never existed, an excusable but still noticeable manipulation. A bit more difficult to overlook are other red herrings seemingly designed to give the play momentum, such as will the museum board accept the donation of the Nazi photos when its mission is to chronicle the victims, not the victimizers? It’s a stick man argument.

Blueberries is on sturdier dramatic ground when new characters – the relatives of the Nazis and camp workers in the photographs – begin arriving mid-show to offer their perspectives. The sister of an office worker sticks with the usual denials, but others are revelatory, not least the grandson of Rudolph Höss, who relates an unnerving tale of his family’s complicity and his own struggles with the violence he fears is an inheritance.

The grandson’s tale is riveting, as is Brandon Flynn’s performance, a stand-out in a uniformly excellent cast that, in addition to those mention above, includes Conrad Ricamora, Scott Barrow, Michelle Beck and Montego Glover, all playing a variety of roles. Derek McLane’s stark set design, dominated by the many tall-legged stand-up desks used by the museum archivists to study the photos, is stellar, as are the crucial projections (designed by David Bengali) that allows us to see what the archivists are seeing. The photos projected onto the walls would be transfixing even without the superb lighting design (David Lander) and sound design (Bobby McElver) that further an entirely earned mood of dread and even despair, never more so than during a late scene that finally gives all those unseen victims their moment in this story, and in a manner so wrenching it won’t be spoiled here except to say that another photo album enters the story, and its story of survival is every bit as astonishing as the first.

In the end, Here There Are Blueberries has sailed past its own here-and-there shortcomings to land on a final thought, artfully told and with clarity of conviction: Just imagine how many untold boxes of forgotten family memorabilia and grim relics are waiting to be discovered, with secrets as efficiently hidden as those sealed away in the human heart?

Tomorrow I will be struggling again, but that’s okay.

By 1983, Carradine had come a long way from his TV debut in the short-lived Western series "Shane." More well-known 1970s examples include "Cannonball," "Death Race 2000," and "Deathsport." In the 1980s, however, Carradine was basically churning out direct-to-video fare, and in "Lone Wolf McQuade" he came face to face with the king of schlocky DTV actioners, Chuck Norris.

The neo-western was directed by Steve Carver, who'd worked at Roger Corman's New World Pictures at the same time Carradine had fronted several of the studio's features in the 1970s. With "Lone Wolf McQuade," Carver paid homage to the Spaghetti Westerns that had helped move the Western genre into its revisionist era. As such, there was a self-aware aspect to the movie, which knew exactly what it was and wasn't about to apologize for it. The final showdown between its two stars was emblematic of its excess and was, it turns out, a lot more real than you might imagine. In fact, Carradine and Norris seemingly gave everything they had to their climactic battle and ended up injuring themselves.

"Lone Wolf McQuade" isn't the type of '80s actioner that gets mentioned alongside such legendary features as "First Blood" or "Die Hard." But it's also not the kind of movie that should be lumped in with the worst examples of the decades' bombastic nonsense. In fact, it's easily one of the best Chuck Norris movies, and in its unapologetic embrace of the uber macho ethos of the era and its reverence for the great Spaghetti Westerns, manages to turn it into a sort of paean to both the golden age of action cinema the influential films that preceded it.

That's not to say the film is some sort of artistic masterwork. This is, at the end of the day, a film in which Norris and David Carradine beat each other senseless for our entertainment. "Lone Wolf McQuade" stars the former as Texas Ranger Jim "J.J." McQuade, an ex-Marine who is the epitome of the '80s action stereotype: a lone wolf lawman who routinely flouts the rules and lives a solitary existence in the wake of his divorce. Oh, and he also literally has a pet wolf.

The film follows McQuade as he tangles with David Carradine's Rawley Wilkes, an arms dealer who also happens to be a martial arts expert. It seems Carradine's Kung Fu specialist is selling weapons to terrorists, and J.J. isn't about to let that go unpunished. When Wilkes captures his daughter, Sally McQuade (Dana Kimmell), he all but guarantees a confrontation with J.J., which is exactly what we get in the film's climactic moments. That particular battle is a protracted slog in which Norris and Carradine go all out.

In a 1990 issue of Psychotronic Video (via archive.org), David Carradine recalled his showdown with Chuck Norris in "Lone Wolf McQuade." Neither actor opted to use a stunt double, making for what sounds like a brutal on-set experience. "We worked on that fight for four days," he said. "We shot it for four days. According to the actor, he and his co-star needed canes and crutches by the end of shooting their fight, which in Carradine's recollection was the result of Norris wanting to outdo his famous showdown with Bruce Lee in 1972's "The Way of the Dragon." "We were actually trying to go beyond that," continued Carradine. "I think we did. I think photographically we didn't. I'm fairly confident in saying that Jim "J.J." McQuade vs. Rawley Wilkes was not, in fact, quite as memorable as Tang Lung vs. Colt. But it was an impressive ending to one of Norris' best films and reminder of the physical talent that had made Carradine a star a decade prior.

Traveling one light-day took the space probe 49 years. it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft is on track to set another historic record as its mission approaches the half-century mark. On Nov. 18 at 5:16 a.m. ET, the probe will reach a distance of one light-day from Earth, a journey of nearly five decades that places it farther from our planet than any human-made object in history.

It’s quite an accomplishment for a spacecraft built in the mid-1970s One light-day is the distance that light travels in a single day at the speed of light, which is approximately 186,282 miles per second. That translates to 25.9 billion kilometers or about 16.1 billion miles in a single day. At an average human walking speed of 3 mph, it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

Fortunately, Voyager 1 has been traveling a bit faster than that. It currently zips along the cosmos at a steady 37,763 mph, a speed it achieved in 1980 after a gravity-assisted Saturn flyby. It has been flying at that speed ever since.

The milestone comes 14 years after its previous one, when Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the point where the sun’s heliosphere ends, and entered interstellar space. It’s also 28 years after passing Pioneer 10 to become the human-made object that has traveled the farthest into space.

The historic milestone isn’t without its drama. Voyager 1 has been in space for nearly half a century, and in that time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done everything it can to extend the life of Voyager 1. That includes troubleshooting issues, performing maintenance and shutting down various systems to save power and extend the spacecraft’s life-span.

Several of those efforts took place in 2026. The team running Voyager 1 spent weeks shutting down three devices and powering on three low-power alternatives to reduce overall power usage. This initiative, which was internally called The Big Bang, was also performed on Voyager 2. The transition was completed in early September, giving Voyager 1 more time with its dwindling power supply while also keeping it warm enough to continue operating in the frigid conditions of interstellar space.

It was a tricky maneuver that required the team to simultaneously turn multiple instruments on and off. The difficulty of the maneuver was amplified by the fact that it took almost a full day for any signal to reach Voyager and another full day for Voyager to send a signal back, with hours of downtime while the spacecraft performed the operations.

All of this took place several months after Voyager 1 experienced a sudden drop in power, which necessitated shutting off one of its three remaining instruments still in operation. Voyager 1 now has only two instruments still in operation.

Making it a whole light-day away from Earth is likely going to be Voyager 1’s final milestone while it still has enough power to communicate with Earth. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They are essentially nuclear-powered batteries that generate energy by converting the steady heat from decaying plutonium-238.

When Voyager 1 first launched, its radioisotope thermoelectric generators produced about 470 watts of power. But plutonium-238 doesn’t last forever, and after 50 years in space, the RTGs now produce about 215 watts of power, with an average loss of about 4 watts per year. Most of that power is used to keep Voyager 1 up and running, which is why NASA has shut down eight of the 10 instruments that Voyager had when it launched.

At its current rate of usage and decay, Voyager 1 is expected to shut down its final science instrument in the 2030s and will eventually lose communication with Earth in 2036 due to insufficient power to send back a radio signal.

The same fate is expected for Voyager 2, Voyager 1’s twin, which has also spent half a century in space. While it is labeled the sequel, Voyager 2 actually launched 16 days before Voyager 1, and the two have spent nearly identical amounts of time traversing the cosmos. Thanks to its slower speed and trajectory, it likely won’t reach the one light-day distance until after its instruments shut down.

Thanks to the vastness of space, Voyager 1 will lose all of its power before it hits its next milestone, which is the beginning of the Oort Cloud, a vast, icy shell of comets at the edge of our solar system. It’ll take about 300 years for Voyager 1 to make it there.

Thanks to the near-perfect vacuum of outer space, Voyager 1 will continue to fly at its current speed even after it shuts down entirely. So it’ll still hit that milestone; it just won’t be able to phone home to tell us about it.

It has been four days since the Ocarina of Time remake was released, and dedicated fans have already made some fascinating discoveries. Not just easter eggs and subtle differences between the remake and the original, but hidden dialogue that many think suggests a Majora’s Mask remake is already in the works.

YouTuber Austin John Plays has posted a video in which he shares the codes for the upcoming Ocarina remake amiibo. The Young Link and Young Zelda amiibo, which will be released in 2027, seemingly to coincide with the launch of The Legend of Zelda movie, unlock in-game masks of the characters’ original N64 designs when scanned.

Those amiibo have the codes Mask 101 and Mask 102, but upon discovering this, John also found a third, unannounced amiibo with the code Mask 103. There is naturally no image of the mask tied to its codename; however, as is the case with the two announced masks, there are lines of dialogue attached to it.

NPCs will react to Link in unique ways if you approach them wearing the N64 masks when you can unlock them next year. It’s the lines of dialogue attached to that third mask that make it seem like we’re getting at least one more Ocarina remake amiibo, and the conclusion John and his viewers have reached is that somehow, some way, we’re getting a Majora’s Mask remake.

The dialogue attached to Mask 103 all boils down to those who see it being afraid of it, referring to it as weird, strange, and creepy. Noted in a Reddit thread covering John’s findings, one of the lines of dialogue attached to the mask comments on it scaring away monsters from whoever’s wearing it. That’s how Majora’s Mask functions in Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom.

My takeaway from that is that we will perhaps get a Skull Kid amiibo down the line that unlocks Majora’s Mask in the Ocarina of Time remake, and that wearing the mask will effectively unlock an easy mode where monsters are too afraid to attack you. However, most people think there’s more to it, especially since Majora’s Mask was the original Ocarina’s sequel.

If you’re not a little skeptical about all of this, like I am, then you seem to fall into one of two camps. Either you believe this discovery proves a Majora’s Mask remake is coming, or that this suggests it will be DLC rather than a full-blown remake sequel.

Despite my skepticism, I do think it would be a little silly of Nintendo if its next Zelda project isn't a Majora’s Mask remake. I’d also understand why it might want to give it a miss, or relegate it to Ocarina DLC. Majora’s Mask didn’t have nearly as big a cultural impact as Ocarina, so despite the overall success of Zelda in 2026, Nintendo might still be a little worried that a standalone Majora’s Mask remake might not have the same cache that Ocarina of Time carries.

Spy x Family Officially Announces New Season and Movie Coming Soon

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the best anime series in recent years is coming with a new season and a feature film. Spy x Family, the comedy-action manga created by Tatsuya Endo and published by Shueisha, is getting a fourth anime season and a new movie, both coming in 2027.

The announcement, shared on Oct. 8 at the Spy x Family Anime Extra Mission II special event and on the official Spy x Family website, came as a pleasant surprise for fans. Season 3 of the anime aired from October to December 2025, exactly two years after season 2 ended. If the timeframe remains consistent, fans can expect season 4 to air at the end of 2027. The franchise's second movie will come out in the same year, following the success of Spy x Family's first outing in cinemas, Spy x Family Code: White, released in 2023.

Spy x Family is a deceptively lighthearted story set in a fictional country resembling Cold War-era East Germany. The two countries of Ostania and Westalis maintain a fragile peace after years of conflict. Westalis' intelligence agency, WISE, sends its top agent, codename Twilight, to Ostania's capital, Berlint, to spy on Donovan Desmond, the country's top politician and former prime minister during the war years. To do so, Twilight has to build a fake family and use his "daughter" to approach Desmond's son in the prestigious Eden Academy. However, Twilight, now posing as esteemed doctor Loid Forger, doesn't know that the child he adopts from an orphanage, Anya, is secretly a telepath, and the woman he convinces to take the role of his wife is Ostania's best assassin, the "Thorn Princess" Yor Briar.

The series' comedy stems from three people living together while hiding their true identities. Airheaded Anya is one of the most adorable characters in anime, while Loid and Yor's marriage of convenience is slowly (too slowly!) developing into a real romance. Seeing this dynamic built on lies grow into a real found family is truly heartwarming, but Spy x Family hides a much more serious side. The themes of war and violence are never treated dismissively, and the series has delivered some of the best reflections on the topic seen in the medium.

One of the most important shonen series ever could return soon

An anime series coproduced by Wit Studio and Cloverworks debuted in 2022 to critical acclaim. The two animation companies also produced the movie Spy x Family Code: White, an original story not adapted from the manga, which sees the Forger family going on an unexpected adventure in a northern town. The movie grossed $95.7 million worldwide, which isn't surprising, considering the series' popularity. The Spy x Family manga has sold over 42 million copies.

Spy x Family is the rare series that can provoke a whole range of emotions in its fans. Wit and Cloverworks have done a great job with the animated adaptations, and it's great to see that they will be coming back for season 4 and, most likely, for the new movie, too. Yasuhiro Akamatsu will replace Yukiko Imai as the season's director, while Aya Takaha will handle the series' composition, and Kazuaki Shimada and will return for character designs.

Set in a remote Newfoundland fishing village, the six-part Netflix series Below follows a fisherman named Calvin Penney (Josh Hartnett), a widowed father of three who realizes that something is attacking townsfolk underneath the surface of the water. And Calvin understands better than anyone, having experienced a tragedy of his own at the age of 10 when his own father was dragged under while his family was attempting to relocate across the ocean. While the residents are being pushed to accept a buyout and vacate their land, Calvin crosses paths with marine biologist Dr. Fonda Howander (Mackenzie Davis) as she’s tracking the pattern of behavior that seems to be occurring anytime someone drinks the water and starts to experience mysterious symptoms.

Sure, tangling with a tentacled creature under the sea would be enough to terrify anyone. But when you learn that, in reality, it’s a giant squid doing whatever it takes to protect its young in waters that are proving increasingly inhospitable due to climate change, you find yourself identifying with it more than the wealthy family looking to extract resources without ever properly compensating in return. Although Calvin and Fonda do everything they can to help keep the village safe, they can’t reverse the environmental issues that got them there in the first place.

In this exclusive Collider Signature in-depth conversation, Davis discussed the monster horror story with a family drama at its core, why she loved playing Dr. Fonda, and how glad she was they didn’t try to throw in a love story. During the interview, she talked about why it was important to keep the look of the giant squid a bit of a mystery, the experience of working in Newfoundland, that dead flamingo, getting to throw a punch, how she felt about the ending, and why she’d be on board for another season. She also spoke about the possibility of continuing the story told in Station Eleven, voicing Big Barda for the upcoming animated Mister Miracle TV series, working with Jamie Dornan in the Netflix series The Twin, and figuring out what’s next.

COLLIDER: The thing that I love about Below is the same thing that I love about Station Eleven, which is that even though it has this bigger concept in its story, whether it’s something in the water or a devastating flu, at its heart is the relationships between the characters. Was that something that was important to you when you read the scripts and had conversations with the creative team? Does it have to be more than a monster story or more than a post-apocalyptic story to get you on board?

MACKENZIE DAVIS: Yeah, definitely. I don’t think it’s interesting to play with a vibe because it’s just so unrealistic. If you’re scared of a monster, you’re also scared for your family and trying to coordinate with them. The relationships are the only important part. It’s not about the object of the fear. It’s about how a community is dealing with that fear, especially with monster things because it’s not there. I never met the squid. If that’s the only thing that you’re interacting with, I think it would be a pretty hollow experience.

I actually love that we never really get a good look at the full squid out of the water. It keeps the monster mysterious.

DAVIS: Yeah, it’s just a surefire way to lose people when you get to hold the entire thing in your eyes and be like, “I don’t buy it,” rather than just these glimmers. And it makes it seem so enormous to never be able to see around the whole thing.

There is a family drama at the center of this, and you’re not a part of the Penney family, but you’re a part of this larger community family. What did you connect with most when it came to the community that exists in this?

DAVIS: Jesse [McKeown], who wrote the show, didn’t grow up in Newfoundland. He’s not a Newfoundlander, but he’s an honorary one. His wife is a multi-generational Newfoundlander. The script reflected a such an intimacy with the culture and the people, and such a devotion to them, and an appreciation and love for this place, that kept it away from any broadness that you couldn’t pin down and say, “But no, that’s real.” Even if it’s funny or it’s unusual to your ear hasn’t picked up this accent before, it’s accurate and it’s real, and it’s portrayed out of affection and admiration. It’s really easy to tell when you read a script if it’s coming from a place of intimate knowledge or just somebody from a great distance trying to set their story in a kooky town. This one felt extremely grounded.

That specificity is so appealing because you get to travel, not just physically travel to corners of the world with this job, but you get to just learn about these professions or cultures or places. You get to transport into these places, and it’s so exciting and satisfying and alluring to be like, “Oh, here’s a really specific, lovely place that I haven’t had a chance to spend time in or know about. And the person who’s telling me and us is, if not like the ultimate authority, then definitely an avid amateur who loves it very much.” It’s just so appealing when something feels incredibly specific.

Personally, I find the world under the sea and sea life to be fascinating and frightening. It feels like there are things living underwater that most of us will never see or even know about, but your character really seems to have quite an interest and a reverence for those things. How did you feel about that side of Dr. Fonda Howaner?

DAVIS: I think it’s the most expensive, spookiest, and full of potential world. It interests me much more than space or anything like that. I feel like the oceans are it. As for my character, she’s a marine biologist, so it’s her life. Her interest is not passing. It’s her whole life. And she comes from away, which is how people who are not from Newfoundland are referred to. She’s from the other side of Canada and is choosing this pretty isolated existence. She’s not very integrated in the community. She’s reluctantly thrust into this save the day mission, but she’s not really in the world. So, I think that passion that draws her away from all other contexts in her life is something I relate to. I know what it’s like to really love something and travel far and miss loved ones to keep pursuing it because it satisfies some other deep, throbbing passion.

I love that your character is a little bit weird. She’s got the weirdness of someone who is a lab nerd and loves studying things, which felt very real. Was all of that scripted, or were there bits of things that you wanted to bring to her?

DAVIS: I don’t have a list of anything. To Jesse’s credit, the character was written so much the way that she is on screen. Obviously, whoever plays the characters is going to add their own tweaks, but the big gestures about Fonda were very much written into the script. I had such a point of departure to add anything to, but it was really there in the data of the original script. I think she’s got a real difficulty with big social interactions. There was a lot of comedy in Jesse’s script, of her being continually grasped at by the community to occupy this doctor role and she’s like, “I’m a marine biologist.” There was just a funny, reluctant hero’s journey.

Since it feels like a place many people will never get to, what was the best thing about shooting in Newfoundland, and what was the most surprising thing about shooting there?

DAVIS: Even growing up in Canada, it felt very far away. It’s such a big country. When I was in Newfoundland, it felt further than being in London from my parents in Vancouver. There’s just something very isolated about it. The most surprising thing was that I didn’t know anybody from Newfoundland, so the incredible kindness and humor and generosity of everybody that I worked with was a constant gift. It’s quite a rugged, intense place to shoot. We had quite good weather while we were there. There were lots of things that we lucked out on. But it’s a brutal part of the world in some ways. The weather and the cliffs and the sea, there’s just this confrontation with nature that feels substantial and a bit daunting. The people who live there just love it so much and are so thankful to be in this magical place. Every day on the drive to work, somebody would just give a huge sigh who’s lived there for their entire life and be like, “God, it’s just the best place in the world.” That’s so nice to be around and such a rare quality, to not get cynical at some point. That was really lovely and a lovely aspect of shooting there.

I love a memorable character introduction, and we meet your character when she’s standing next to a dead flamingo. Was that always her introduction? How did you feel about that moment of humor?

DAVIS: It was walking barefoot down a hallway, and then there she is, next to the flamingo. For the first read of a script, it was a really nice, “Oh, I like this lady,” way into it. I felt great about it. The crazy thing, which I didn’t know until we were actually shooting the scene, is that it’s a real story. Jesse had heard of a friend of a friend who had a flamingo land on his house in Logy Bay, which is chilling. It’s getting into plague territory. It’s a funny visual. What I think the show handles so well is that there’s a lightness and levity, and there’s the genre stuff, but the heart of it is this deep existential sadness about what’s happening to the planet. I do think you have to smile through some things because, what are you going to do? If you’re not in charge of making the global changes necessary, then you do have to find some joy in it. And I think the show finds a really nice way of doing that.

There’s also just something heartbreakingly sad about this poor flamingo.

DAVIS: It’s bizarre. It shouldn’t be there. We had a crazy heatwave in London this summer. The world has just flipped on its axis a bit. It’s nice because it’s hot and it doesn’t get to be hot all the time in London. But there’s something really dissonant and dark about experiencing something so other than what you’re supposed to experience in that place. And a flamingo in Newfoundland, I would say, is one of them.

In Episode 6, you punch Natasha Henstridge, which feels like something that Dr. Fonda probably wouldn’t have done when we meet her at the beginning of this series, but she does after everything that’s happened. What was your reaction to that moment? How did you feel about her acting on impulse like that?

DAVIS: I loved it. It’s a bizarre moment for her, but there’s something so endearingly Canadian about acting on impulse, acting violently, and then before you’ve even withdrawn your hand, apologizing. I thought it was a really funny beat in the script. The show has a lot of genres that it’s playing with. At the end, the team saves the day, and people are having these moments of revelation and growth, but that are really sweet. I liked it.

After everything that happened, and after everything Calvin and Fonda did to help save the squid and her young, how did you feel about the squid dying and the babies being unable to survive in the warm water?

DAVIS: I loved it. We watch sci-fi, especially, as this salve that the worst things in the world can be on the horizon, but one chosen man, and sometimes woman, will be able to stop this global devastation in its tracks. We watch it in this strange, cathartic way that divorces us from reality. I thought it was quite a gentle way of doing it, but really heartbreaking to come to the end of this big, heroic journey where they come up with a big plan at the end and save the day. But then, it doesn’t matter what this one town did. There are these huge, existential, unimaginable problems, and it’s great to recycle, but it doesn’t matter if you recycle. That’s not going to offset drilling in the North Sea. It’s one of my favorite moments in the show, not because I’m in it, but because of the wry sadness of, “It’s just going to get worse. But also, let’s look at the horizon and appreciate how beautiful it is right now.” I think that’s a very sad thing to say, but it’s what it feels like to be alive right now.

DAVIS: Oh, my God, yeah. I thought it was so fun and truly a murderer’s row of lovely people that made the show. I’d do it in a heartbeat. I thought it was a dream. I can’t imagine doing it, just saying the lines out into a void. I got to record with Josh, and he was incredible.

DAVIS: Yeah, she’s really cool. I think it would be really fun to explore in live action. She’s really funny and strong and angry and has a very traumatic childhood. There’s lots of interesting stuff about her. Coming from complete unfamiliarity and now knowing her quite well, I think she’s incredible … For me, everything has been a discovery. And that’s what we’re using for our show.

You also have another Netflix series, The Twin, with Jamie Dornan. What made you want to be a part of telling that story? How did you find the experience of working with him while he was playing twins?

DAVIS: Great. Jamie is an absolute delight. I love him and he’s so fun to work with. The twin thing was fun and fine. Mostly, it was just one-on-one scenes with us, so I didn’t have to contend with the twinship a lot, but when I did, I was titillated. How did I come to it? I met with Jeremy Lovering, who directed the series, about it and I just adored him. The scripts were really compelling, and I’d be playing a Scottish woman in the Highlands, which felt like its own challenge. And the character felt like a challenge. It just seemed like a really unavoidably good experience, and it was.

What was it like to find two different relationships with the same actor within the same project?

DAVIS: Yeah, definitely. Luckily, they brothers are very distinct, so it wasn’t that confusing who I was speaking to at any given moment. It was exciting. It was more challenging for Jamie than for me, but it was interesting. It was amazing to watch someone do really great work, and how physical and how minor the adjustments can be, you’ll completely understand the difference between the characters that you’re speaking to.

What I loved about the relationship between your character and Josh Hartnett’s character in Below is that typically, in this type of story, there would be some sort of romance and you’d have to take time away from the story to have a relationship. And this series doesn’t have that, but there is a really layered dynamic between your characters. What was it like to figure that out and find that with Josh Hartnett?

DAVIS: You can probably attest to this, but not every man you meet, you think that maybe you’ll fall in love with him. It’s more a convention that’s placed on us. I’m not going to be like, “I wonder if he wants more kids.” I loved that about the script. It wasn’t hinted at. These two are just people who are solving a problem together, and have an appreciation and affection for each other, but they’re thrust together out of necessity, and they don’t then need to have a romance. It wasn’t a thing to navigate it at all because I have many friendships like that. The convention of people always falling in love feels like the more foreign thing to me. But Josh made it very easy. He’s as lovely as everyone would like him to be. It’s easy to like solving a mystery with him. It’s funny how programmed we are to want that thing to happen. At the end of it, it was like, “Is there something here?” But we were like, “No, there’s not.” You’re just so used to that narrative that your body chemistry works to draw it out in scenes. I love that it’s not a part of the story because there are more important things at hand.

They really have nothing in common and probably never would have ended up as friends otherwise.

DAVIS: It’s like friends you meet when you travel. You can be extremely close and have a real intimacy, and it’s not that that isn’t a real experience, but it’s the context of the place and the circumstances that makes the relationship so intense. When you try to just hang out casually, you’re like, “Oh, I actually needed there to be a life-or-death catastrophe on the horizon for us. I don’t know what to say to you outside of that.” I think that’s so funny.

It goes beyond a simple creature feature.

Do you know what you’re going to do next, or are you someone who doesn’t have to know right away?

DAVIS: I neither like nor dislike that. If there were tons of things that I wanted to do and they were all lined up, that would feel quite safe, I imagine, in a good way where I’m like, “Okay, I have my whole year planned.” But I’ve never felt that there were that many things that I really wanted to do. I’m much more about sitting with it and then using that as a test of character to be present and not anxious and just believe in the process. I like to create little learning lessons out of things in my life, so that’s probably why I’m so thankful. I’m in the stage of not knowing right now. When I was younger, I used to feel a lot more anxious about not knowing all the time, but I just don’t anymore. I think it’s good to wait until you’re really compelled to do something. That’s how I like to work, even if it’s a bit painful in the periods where you’re like, “I’d really like to be compelled right now.” I also like to be busy with other things. I don’t like the feeling of not being active, so I have to fill my life with a lot of other things.
