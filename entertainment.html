YouTuber Finds Ocarina Of Time Remake Dialogue Tied To A Majora's Mask Amiibo

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It has been four days since the Ocarina of Time remake was released, and dedicated fans have already made some fascinating discoveries. Not just easter eggs and subtle differences between the remake and the original, but hidden dialogue that many think suggests a Majora’s Mask remake is already in the works.

YouTuber Austin John Plays has posted a video in which he shares the codes for the upcoming Ocarina remake amiibo. The Young Link and Young Zelda amiibo, which will be released in 2027, seemingly to coincide with the launch of The Legend of Zelda movie, unlock in-game masks of the characters’ original N64 designs when scanned.

Those amiibo have the codes Mask 101 and Mask 102, but upon discovering this, John also found a third, unannounced amiibo with the code Mask 103. There is naturally no image of the mask tied to its codename; however, as is the case with the two announced masks, there are lines of dialogue attached to it.

NPCs will react to Link in unique ways if you approach them wearing the N64 masks when you can unlock them next year. It’s the lines of dialogue attached to that third mask that make it seem like we’re getting at least one more Ocarina remake amiibo, and the conclusion John and his viewers have reached is that somehow, some way, we’re getting a Majora’s Mask remake.

The dialogue attached to Mask 103 all boils down to those who see it being afraid of it, referring to it as weird, strange, and creepy. Noted in a Reddit thread covering John’s findings, one of the lines of dialogue attached to the mask comments on it scaring away monsters from whoever’s wearing it. That’s how Majora’s Mask functions in Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom.

My takeaway from that is that we will perhaps get a Skull Kid amiibo down the line that unlocks Majora’s Mask in the Ocarina of Time remake, and that wearing the mask will effectively unlock an easy mode where monsters are too afraid to attack you. However, most people think there’s more to it, especially since Majora’s Mask was the original Ocarina’s sequel.

If you’re not a little skeptical about all of this, like I am, then you seem to fall into one of two camps. Either you believe this discovery proves a Majora’s Mask remake is coming, or that this suggests it will be DLC rather than a full-blown remake sequel.

Despite my skepticism, I do think it would be a little silly of Nintendo if its next Zelda project isn't a Majora’s Mask remake. I’d also understand why it might want to give it a miss, or relegate it to Ocarina DLC. Majora’s Mask didn’t have nearly as big a cultural impact as Ocarina, so despite the overall success of Zelda in 2026, Nintendo might still be a little worried that a standalone Majora’s Mask remake might not have the same cache that Ocarina of Time carries.

Spy x Family Officially Announces New Season and Movie Coming Soon

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the best anime series in recent years is coming with a new season and a feature film. Spy x Family, the comedy-action manga created by Tatsuya Endo and published by Shueisha, is getting a fourth anime season and a new movie, both coming in 2027.

The announcement, shared on Oct. 8 at the Spy x Family Anime Extra Mission II special event and on the official Spy x Family website, came as a pleasant surprise for fans. Season 3 of the anime aired from October to December 2025, exactly two years after season 2 ended. If the timeframe remains consistent, fans can expect season 4 to air at the end of 2027. The franchise's second movie will come out in the same year, following the success of Spy x Family's first outing in cinemas, Spy x Family Code: White, released in 2023.

Spy x Family is a deceptively lighthearted story set in a fictional country resembling Cold War-era East Germany. The two countries of Ostania and Westalis maintain a fragile peace after years of conflict. Westalis' intelligence agency, WISE, sends its top agent, codename Twilight, to Ostania's capital, Berlint, to spy on Donovan Desmond, the country's top politician and former prime minister during the war years. To do so, Twilight has to build a fake family and use his "daughter" to approach Desmond's son in the prestigious Eden Academy. However, Twilight, now posing as esteemed doctor Loid Forger, doesn't know that the child he adopts from an orphanage, Anya, is secretly a telepath, and the woman he convinces to take the role of his wife is Ostania's best assassin, the "Thorn Princess" Yor Briar.

The series' comedy stems from three people living together while hiding their true identities. Airheaded Anya is one of the most adorable characters in anime, while Loid and Yor's marriage of convenience is slowly (too slowly!) developing into a real romance. Seeing this dynamic built on lies grow into a real found family is truly heartwarming, but Spy x Family hides a much more serious side. The themes of war and violence are never treated dismissively, and the series has delivered some of the best reflections on the topic seen in the medium.

One of the most important shonen series ever could return soon

An anime series coproduced by Wit Studio and Cloverworks debuted in 2022 to critical acclaim. The two animation companies also produced the movie Spy x Family Code: White, an original story not adapted from the manga, which sees the Forger family going on an unexpected adventure in a northern town. The movie grossed $95.7 million worldwide, which isn't surprising, considering the series' popularity. The Spy x Family manga has sold over 42 million copies.

Spy x Family is the rare series that can provoke a whole range of emotions in its fans. Wit and Cloverworks have done a great job with the animated adaptations, and it's great to see that they will be coming back for season 4 and, most likely, for the new movie, too. Yasuhiro Akamatsu will replace Yukiko Imai as the season's director, while Aya Takaha will handle the series' composition, and Kazuaki Shimada and will return for character designs.

Set in a remote Newfoundland fishing village, the six-part Netflix series Below follows a fisherman named Calvin Penney (Josh Hartnett), a widowed father of three who realizes that something is attacking townsfolk underneath the surface of the water. And Calvin understands better than anyone, having experienced a tragedy of his own at the age of 10 when his own father was dragged under while his family was attempting to relocate across the ocean. While the residents are being pushed to accept a buyout and vacate their land, Calvin crosses paths with marine biologist Dr. Fonda Howander (Mackenzie Davis) as she’s tracking the pattern of behavior that seems to be occurring anytime someone drinks the water and starts to experience mysterious symptoms.

Sure, tangling with a tentacled creature under the sea would be enough to terrify anyone. But when you learn that, in reality, it’s a giant squid doing whatever it takes to protect its young in waters that are proving increasingly inhospitable due to climate change, you find yourself identifying with it more than the wealthy family looking to extract resources without ever properly compensating in return. Although Calvin and Fonda do everything they can to help keep the village safe, they can’t reverse the environmental issues that got them there in the first place.

In this exclusive Collider Signature in-depth conversation, Davis discussed the monster horror story with a family drama at its core, why she loved playing Dr. Fonda, and how glad she was they didn’t try to throw in a love story. During the interview, she talked about why it was important to keep the look of the giant squid a bit of a mystery, the experience of working in Newfoundland, that dead flamingo, getting to throw a punch, how she felt about the ending, and why she’d be on board for another season. She also spoke about the possibility of continuing the story told in Station Eleven, voicing Big Barda for the upcoming animated Mister Miracle TV series, working with Jamie Dornan in the Netflix series The Twin, and figuring out what’s next.

COLLIDER: The thing that I love about Below is the same thing that I love about Station Eleven, which is that even though it has this bigger concept in its story, whether it’s something in the water or a devastating flu, at its heart is the relationships between the characters. Was that something that was important to you when you read the scripts and had conversations with the creative team? Does it have to be more than a monster story or more than a post-apocalyptic story to get you on board?

MACKENZIE DAVIS: Yeah, definitely. I don’t think it’s interesting to play with a vibe because it’s just so unrealistic. If you’re scared of a monster, you’re also scared for your family and trying to coordinate with them. The relationships are the only important part. It’s not about the object of the fear. It’s about how a community is dealing with that fear, especially with monster things because it’s not there. I never met the squid. If that’s the only thing that you’re interacting with, I think it would be a pretty hollow experience.

I actually love that we never really get a good look at the full squid out of the water. It keeps the monster mysterious.

DAVIS: Yeah, it’s just a surefire way to lose people when you get to hold the entire thing in your eyes and be like, “I don’t buy it,” rather than just these glimmers. And it makes it seem so enormous to never be able to see around the whole thing.

There is a family drama at the center of this, and you’re not a part of the Penney family, but you’re a part of this larger community family. What did you connect with most when it came to the community that exists in this?

DAVIS: Jesse [McKeown], who wrote the show, didn’t grow up in Newfoundland. He’s not a Newfoundlander, but he’s an honorary one. His wife is a multi-generational Newfoundlander. The script reflected a such an intimacy with the culture and the people, and such a devotion to them, and an appreciation and love for this place, that kept it away from any broadness that you couldn’t pin down and say, “But no, that’s real.” Even if it’s funny or it’s unusual to your ear hasn’t picked up this accent before, it’s accurate and it’s real, and it’s portrayed out of affection and admiration. It’s really easy to tell when you read a script if it’s coming from a place of intimate knowledge or just somebody from a great distance trying to set their story in a kooky town. This one felt extremely grounded.

That specificity is so appealing because you get to travel, not just physically travel to corners of the world with this job, but you get to just learn about these professions or cultures or places. You get to transport into these places, and it’s so exciting and satisfying and alluring to be like, “Oh, here’s a really specific, lovely place that I haven’t had a chance to spend time in or know about. And the person who’s telling me and us is, if not like the ultimate authority, then definitely an avid amateur who loves it very much.” It’s just so appealing when something feels incredibly specific.

Personally, I find the world under the sea and sea life to be fascinating and frightening. It feels like there are things living underwater that most of us will never see or even know about, but your character really seems to have quite an interest and a reverence for those things. How did you feel about that side of Dr. Fonda Howaner?

DAVIS: I think it’s the most expensive, spookiest, and full of potential world. It interests me much more than space or anything like that. I feel like the oceans are it. As for my character, she’s a marine biologist, so it’s her life. Her interest is not passing. It’s her whole life. And she comes from away, which is how people who are not from Newfoundland are referred to. She’s from the other side of Canada and is choosing this pretty isolated existence. She’s not very integrated in the community. She’s reluctantly thrust into this save the day mission, but she’s not really in the world. So, I think that passion that draws her away from all other contexts in her life is something I relate to. I know what it’s like to really love something and travel far and miss loved ones to keep pursuing it because it satisfies some other deep, throbbing passion.

I love that your character is a little bit weird. She’s got the weirdness of someone who is a lab nerd and loves studying things, which felt very real. Was all of that scripted, or were there bits of things that you wanted to bring to her?

DAVIS: I don’t have a list of anything. To Jesse’s credit, the character was written so much the way that she is on screen. Obviously, whoever plays the characters is going to add their own tweaks, but the big gestures about Fonda were very much written into the script. I had such a point of departure to add anything to, but it was really there in the data of the original script. I think she’s got a real difficulty with big social interactions. There was a lot of comedy in Jesse’s script, of her being continually grasped at by the community to occupy this doctor role and she’s like, “I’m a marine biologist.” There was just a funny, reluctant hero’s journey.

Since it feels like a place many people will never get to, what was the best thing about shooting in Newfoundland, and what was the most surprising thing about shooting there?

DAVIS: Even growing up in Canada, it felt very far away. It’s such a big country. When I was in Newfoundland, it felt further than being in London from my parents in Vancouver. There’s just something very isolated about it. The most surprising thing was that I didn’t know anybody from Newfoundland, so the incredible kindness and humor and generosity of everybody that I worked with was a constant gift. It’s quite a rugged, intense place to shoot. We had quite good weather while we were there. There were lots of things that we lucked out on. But it’s a brutal part of the world in some ways. The weather and the cliffs and the sea, there’s just this confrontation with nature that feels substantial and a bit daunting. The people who live there just love it so much and are so thankful to be in this magical place. Every day on the drive to work, somebody would just give a huge sigh who’s lived there for their entire life and be like, “God, it’s just the best place in the world.” That’s so nice to be around and such a rare quality, to not get cynical at some point. That was really lovely and a lovely aspect of shooting there.

I love a memorable character introduction, and we meet your character when she’s standing next to a dead flamingo. Was that always her introduction? How did you feel about that moment of humor?

DAVIS: It was walking barefoot down a hallway, and then there she is, next to the flamingo. For the first read of a script, it was a really nice, “Oh, I like this lady,” way into it. I felt great about it. The crazy thing, which I didn’t know until we were actually shooting the scene, is that it’s a real story. Jesse had heard of a friend of a friend who had a flamingo land on his house in Logy Bay, which is chilling. It’s getting into plague territory. It’s a funny visual. What I think the show handles so well is that there’s a lightness and levity, and there’s the genre stuff, but the heart of it is this deep existential sadness about what’s happening to the planet. I do think you have to smile through some things because, what are you going to do? If you’re not in charge of making the global changes necessary, then you do have to find some joy in it. And I think the show finds a really nice way of doing that.

There’s also just something heartbreakingly sad about this poor flamingo.

DAVIS: It’s bizarre. It shouldn’t be there. We had a crazy heatwave in London this summer. The world has just flipped on its axis a bit. It’s nice because it’s hot and it doesn’t get to be hot all the time in London. But there’s something really dissonant and dark about experiencing something so other than what you’re supposed to experience in that place. And a flamingo in Newfoundland, I would say, is one of them.

In Episode 6, you punch Natasha Henstridge, which feels like something that Dr. Fonda probably wouldn’t have done when we meet her at the beginning of this series, but she does after everything that’s happened. What was your reaction to that moment? How did you feel about her acting on impulse like that?

DAVIS: I loved it. It’s a bizarre moment for her, but there’s something so endearingly Canadian about acting on impulse, acting violently, and then before you’ve even withdrawn your hand, apologizing. I thought it was a really funny beat in the script. The show has a lot of genres that it’s playing with. At the end, the team saves the day, and people are having these moments of revelation and growth, but that are really sweet. I liked it.

After everything that happened, and after everything Calvin and Fonda did to help save the squid and her young, how did you feel about the squid dying and the babies being unable to survive in the warm water?

DAVIS: I loved it. We watch sci-fi, especially, as this salve that the worst things in the world can be on the horizon, but one chosen man, and sometimes woman, will be able to stop this global devastation in its tracks. We watch it in this strange, cathartic way that divorces us from reality. I thought it was quite a gentle way of doing it, but really heartbreaking to come to the end of this big, heroic journey where they come up with a big plan at the end and save the day. But then, it doesn’t matter what this one town did. There are these huge, existential, unimaginable problems, and it’s great to recycle, but it doesn’t matter if you recycle. That’s not going to offset drilling in the North Sea. It’s one of my favorite moments in the show, not because I’m in it, but because of the wry sadness of, “It’s just going to get worse. But also, let’s look at the horizon and appreciate how beautiful it is right now.” I think that’s a very sad thing to say, but it’s what it feels like to be alive right now.

By the end of these episodes, Calvin is closer to his kids and he’s mended his relationship with Wade, but there are these new blocks of ice out in the water and new questions about what’s going on. Obviously, this is a limited series and the story has concluded, but if you got a call that there was more story to tell, would you want to return to this character and this world?

DAVIS: Oh, my God, yeah. I thought it was so fun and truly a murderer’s row of lovely people that made the show. I’d do it in a heartbeat. I thought it was a dream. If you can justify it, and the opportunity presents itself, then we must because there’s actually a good idea. At the end of shooting last year, Josh [Hartnett] had a really good pitch for a Season 2. I’m curious about that. Time will tell. I’m open to anything. I just love working with nice, talented people, and this really satisfied that.

When one of my co-workers spoke to the showrunner for Station Eleven back when it came out, he had said that he felt there was more story to tell that could come from that world. How would you feel about that? Would you want to return to that character and tell more story in that world?

DAVIS: Yeah, I love that world. I think at the time I didn’t because I was like, “What is the next story?” The story is about Kirsten and Jeevan, in the book and in our show. But I think there’s a world of many characters and stories and people who are lost and seeking each other, or what happened in the interim years, maybe the younger Kirsten. I think there are many stories to tell. I’m not sure where you’d go with me as the adult Kirsten and Himesh [Patel] as the adult Jeevan because we got that lovely conclusion to it. But (creator) Patrick [Somerville] has got one of the great minds on the planet, so if anyone could figure it out, he would do it. So, I wait and see. I haven’t heard anything, so it might be a longer wait than one may think.

DC Studios recently announced that you’re a part of the Mister Miracle animated series and voicing a character there. How did you get involved with that?

DAVIS: I got involved because they reached out while I was shooting in Newfoundland, and I met with them and put my voice down. I was not familiar with anything, just because I have a very limited field of focus and I just didn’t know about Tom King and I didn’t know about Mister Miracle and Big Barda. And then, I read the scripts and Tom’s graphic novel. Are you familiar with it?

A little bit now because I’ve been reading up on it myself.

DAVIS: It’s so beautiful. It’s very dark. Well, not dark, but it’s dealing with true pain and depression. It’s placed in this superhero world, but it’s about a couple and a suicide attempt and a marriage and loving somebody and holding the pain that they’re in. And then, there is this greater context that they’re in. But Tom has just written the most exquisite, painful, grounded show that happens to be about these two superheroes. It’s kind of crazy.

Does that feel like you have freedom to play? Are you recording fully solo, or do you get to record with Josh Hutcherson at all?

DAVIS: I’m recording with Josh, always. I think it would be hard. It’s such a two-hander of this couple dealing with a traumatic event and trying to navigate the healing of that and their marriage continuing. It’s quite a dense thing. I can’t imagine doing it, just saying the lines out into a void. I got to record with Josh, and he was incredible.

Obviously, there’s also always the possibility that you could play the character in live action, especially with James Gunn saying that that’s always the plan when they cast people. Is that something you’d be interested in doing? Is it a character you think would be fun to explore in live action?

DAVIS: Yeah, she’s really cool. I think it would be really fun to explore in live action. She’s really funny and strong and angry and has a very traumatic childhood. There’s lots of interesting stuff about her. Coming from complete unfamiliarity and now knowing her quite well, I think she’s incredible … For me, everything has been a discovery. I believe Big Barda and Mister Miracle were less prominent characters in the DC world, and then Tom King wrote this extraordinary graphic novel a few years ago that really brought a lot of attention to them. And that’s what we’re using for our show.

You also have another Netflix series, The Twin, with Jamie Dornan. What made you want to be a part of telling that story? How did you find the experience of working with him while he was playing twins?

DAVIS: Great. Jamie is an absolute delight. I love him and he’s so fun to work with. The twin thing was fun and fine. Mostly, it was just one-on-one scenes with us, so I didn’t have to contend with the twinship a lot, but when I did, I was titillated. How did I come to it? I met with Jeremy Lovering, who directed the series, about it and I just adored him. The scripts were really compelling, and I’d be playing a Scottish woman in the Highlands, which felt like its own challenge. And the character felt like a challenge. It just seemed like a really unavoidably good experience, and it was.

What was it like to find two different relationships with the same actor within the same project?

DAVIS: Yeah, definitely. Luckily, they brothers are very distinct, so it wasn’t that confusing who I was speaking to at any given moment. It was exciting. It was more challenging for Jamie than for me, but it was interesting. It was amazing to watch someone do really great work, and how physical and how minor the adjustments can be, you’ll completely understand the difference between the characters that you’re speaking to.

What I loved about the relationship between your character and Josh Hartnett’s character in Below is that typically, in this type of story, there would be some sort of romance and you’d have to take time away from the story to have a relationship. And this series doesn’t have that, but there is a really layered dynamic between your characters. What was it like to figure that out and find that with Josh Hartnett?

DAVIS: You can probably attest to this, but not every man you meet, you think that maybe you’ll fall in love with him. It’s more a convention that’s placed on us. I’m not going to be like, “I wonder if he wants more kids.” I loved that about the script. It wasn’t hinted at. These two are just people who are solving a problem together, and have an appreciation and affection for each other, but they’re thrust together out of necessity, and they don’t then need to have a romance. It wasn’t a thing to navigate it at all because I have many friendships like that. The convention of people always falling in love feels like the more foreign thing to me. But Josh made it very easy. He’s as lovely as everyone would like him to be. It’s easy to like solving a mystery with him. It’s funny how programmed we are to want that thing to happen. At the end of it, it was like, “Is there something here?” But we were like, “No, there’s not.” You’re just so used to that narrative that your body chemistry works to draw it out in scenes. I love that it’s not a part of the story because there are more important things at hand.

They really have nothing in common and probably never would have ended up as friends otherwise.

DAVIS: It’s like friends you meet when you travel. You can be extremely close and have a real intimacy, and it’s not that that isn’t a real experience, but it’s the context of the place and the circumstances that makes the relationship so intense. When you try to just hang out casually, you’re like, “Oh, I actually needed there to be a life-or-death catastrophe on the horizon for us. I don’t know what to say to you outside of that.” I think that’s so funny.

It goes beyond a simple creature feature.

Do you know what you’re going to do next, or are you someone who doesn’t have to know right away?

DAVIS: I neither like nor dislike that. If there were tons of things that I wanted to do and they were all lined up, that would feel quite safe, I imagine, in a good way where I’m like, “Okay, I have my whole year planned.” But I’ve never felt that there were that many things that I really wanted to do. I’m much more about sitting with it and then using that as a test of character to be present and not anxious and just believe in the process. I like to create little learning lessons out of things in my life, so that’s probably why I’m so thankful. I’m in the stage of not knowing right now. When I was younger, I used to feel a lot more anxious about not knowing all the time, but I just don’t anymore. I think it’s good to wait until you’re really compelled to do something. That’s how I like to work, even if it’s a bit painful in the periods where you’re like, “I’d really like to be compelled right now.” I also like to be busy with other things. I don’t like the feeling of not being active, so I have to fill my life with a lot of other things.

Remembering Sebastián Lelio, an Auteur Who Centered Women’s Stories With Style and Sensitivity

The Chilean filmmaker, who passed away on Friday aged 52, leaves behind a diverse but cohesive body of work including 'Gloria,' 'The Wonder' and the Oscar-winning 'A Fantastic Woman.'

When it was announced, in 2017, that the Chilean writer-director Sébastian Lelio was to helm a U.S. remake of his 2013 breakout film “Gloria,” admirers of the original — and indeed of Lelio himself — had cause for concern. English-language do-overs of world cinema successes frequently go awry; furthermore, we had the cautionary tale of Dutchman George Sluizer, who directed a hopeless 1993 remake of his own impeccable 1988 chiller “The Vanishing,” to remind us that even having the original filmmaker in charge was no guarantee against a travesty. Related Stories ‘I Play Rocky’ Star Stephan James on Paying Tribute to Carl Weathers with His Red Carpet Look: ‘I Can Literally Walk in His Shoes Yet Again’ Man Arrested on ‘Miami Vice ‘85’ Brooklyn Set After Brandishing a Knife

The news came at a crucial moment in Lelio’s career. The first of his two films to premiere in 2017, the vibrant, heartfelt transgender character study “A Fantastic Woman,” was riding a wave of festival and critical success that began with a Best Screenplay win at the Berlinale, and culminated with an Oscar for Best International Feature (or Best Foreign Language Film, as the award was still called then) — the first ever win for a Chilean film in the category, sealing a resurgence of the country’s cinema also being led by such contemporaries as Sebastián Silva and Lelio’s own producer Pablo Larraín.

The second, “Disobedience,” was Lelio’s English-language debut — another story of queer identity, but an altogether different tonal and stylistic proposition. A sober portrayal of an illicit romance between two women from North London’s strict Orthodox Jewish community, it was sensitively observed and beautifully acted by leads Rachel Weisz and Rachel McAdams, with a restraint that was occasionally countered by moments of frank, charged sensuality. The contrast between “A Fantastic Woman” and “Disobedience” confirmed Lelio as a particularly empathetic and versatile teller of women’s stories — and “Gloria,” a warm and unsentimentally joyous comedy of a fiftysomething divorcée’s reawakening, was also good one. But why go back, when his career was barreling forward?

As it turned out, Lelio knew better than his doubters. The remake, which eventually premiered in 2019 as “Gloria Bell,” hewed close to the template of the original, largely inheriting its pleasures and poignancies. But if that makes it sound redundant, it proved a canny and fascinating exercise in performer-as-auteur influence: It was clear that Lelio, self-evidently a champion of women on screen, was interested to see how a wholly different actress would change the tone and temperature of his original story.

A lot, we learned. Paulina García was a funny and resilient force of nature in the 2013 original; in “Gloria Bell,” Julianne Moore brought a yearning, lonely fragility to the material that gave even the film’s uplifting trajectory an undertow of melancholy. The remake got less than its due — Moore, at the very least, deserved some awards attention that year — but it sits beside “Gloria” as an illuminating companion piece rather than a competing interpretation.

I lead with the two Glorias because their respective successes improbably prove a truth about Lelio, a restlessly curious and compassionate filmmaker who passed away, far too soon, on Friday, aged just 52: Even when ostensibly making the same film twice, he never made the same film twice.

His 2005 debut “The Sacred Family” was confident and a bit scrappy, its handheld jaggedness far removed from his later polish, its droll comic tale of a bourgeois man falling for his son’s girlfriend also distinct from the searching feminist portraiture that would define his later career. But it was lively and promising, made in a semi-improvised fashion that he carried forward into his next, more complex film, the troubled youth study “Navidad,” which made its way into the 2009 Cannes Directors’ Fortnight program. But his third film, 2011’s “The Year of the Tiger,” was a considerably more sophisticated effort: an exquisitely understated exploration of faith, guilt and trauma in the wake of Chile’s devastating 2010 earthquake.

“The Year of the Tiger,” which competed at Locarno, was too soulful and richly considered a work on its own terms to be seen as a bid for arthouse-major status — but that’s how it worked out anyway, even if the film didn’t get quite the global exposure it deserved. But by the time “Gloria” came along two years later, the word on Lelio was out and expectations were high: The film was selected for the main competition at Berlin and was an immediate, ebulliently received crowdpleaser. If Larraín had established himself as the dark prince of new Chilean cinema with spiky, perverse, politically bristling works like “Post Mortem” and “No,” Lelio was poised to become its great humanist: his touch light but not soft, his perspective probing but not abrasive.

“A Fantastic Woman,” four years later, delivered on that promise. Propelled by trans star Daniela Vega’s extraordinary, emotionally unguarded performance, its story of a grieving trans woman spurned by the prejudiced family of her dead lover arrived at a vital tipping point for the visibility of transgender narratives and artists in popular culture, and delicately essayed an arc of emphatic, even triumphant self-definition amid a climate of isolation and rejection — all without feeling glibly or opportunistically issue-driven. It was to Lelio’s credit that he was fairly self-effacing in his promotion of the film, letting Vega — with whom he had closely developed the protagonist, aligned with her experience — be its chief ambassador and spokesperson.

“A Fantastic Woman” earned Lelio the most acclaim and kudos of his career — though his finest film as a director, to my mind, was comparatively shortchanged. A period drama set in 19th-century Ireland and adapted from a novel by “Room” author Emma Donoghue, “The Wonder” didn’t outwardly look or feel much like a Lelio joint — with its austerely repressed milieu shot, by Ari Wegner, with such a damp, chilly verdigris finish you could practically sense moss growing on the screen as you watched. Yet its story of feminine resolve and dueling moral and spiritual convictions meshed well with the director’s interests, as did Florence Pugh’s tenacious, stout-hearted performance; the film’s earthy folktale trappings stood in exciting contrast to its stark formalist construction.

If “The Wonder” was an ambitious and grandly realized reach, I was less sold on the huge stylistic swing of Lelio’s most recent film, last year’s political musical “The Wave,” which returned him to his homeland. Inspired by a national spate of #MeToo-related feminist student protests that shut down universities across Chile in 2018, it was rhetorically heavy-handed but composed and choreographed with dynamic aplomb, positively thrilling to the spectacle of massed bodies in motion: I wrote in my review that it made one eager to see Lelio tackle more musical fare on screen, never thinking that the film would be his last.

In one of “The Wave’s” more playful flourishes, the fourth wall is broken to acknowledge Lelio’s own presence as the director of the movie — and to ask, pointedly, what a male director was doing on this expressly feminist project anyway. It’s a wink-wink joke that more seriously encapsulated the generous, conscientious, community-minded approach that Lelio brought to his filmmaking: His auteur signature was subtly distinctive, yet he never made himself the star of his work.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

By now, it's no secret that Apple TV has a knack for releasing great science fiction; you can pretty much choose one at random, and it will still be a great choice. Saying one of them is the best feels even a little unfair to the others, so we have to raise the bar and look beyond the screen and individual taste, focusing instead on what kind of accomplishment it represents. All that considered, it's almost impossible not to choose Foundation, the adaptation of Isaac Asimov's seminal sci-fi saga. Before its release, these books were considered straight-up unadaptable, so, in many ways, the greatest achievement of Apple TV's Foundation isn't just being an incredible work of science fiction, but that it exists at all, really.

Between original series and adaptations, science fiction has had a boom in productions ever since the dawn of the streaming age. The one work every fan of the genre had pretty much accepted would never get such treatment was Foundation. Even after its announcement back in 2018, everyone only believed the series was actually real when it premiered via Apple TV in 2021. That's because no other work in sci-fi has a scope as huge as Foundation — it was just too much to believe it could ever be made into a TV series.

Set in a future where humankind has conquered the cosmos, the original book comprises many smaller tales depicting humanity's efforts to overcome a thousand years of dark ages. The single common thread is Hari Seldon (Jared Harris), founder of psychohistory, a field of study that uses probability and psychology to predict future events on a galactic scale. Seldon devised a plan for future human leaders to follow, thus reducing the dark ages from tens of thousands of years to a single millennium.

It's a fascinating story and an easy read, filled with equally interesting characters. However, most never interact due to their respective stories being set decades and even centuries apart. This is a major roadblock for any kind of visual adaptation, because focusing on just one of them would mean neglecting centuries of human history in that universe. It just couldn't be done, and those centuries of human history are the whole point of Foundation. Something had to drastically change for these novels to fit the TV framework.

When translating a work of literature into visual media, it's always necessary to make some changes, including, sometimes, some severe ones. Modern television usually demands strong protagonists with whom the audience can sympathize, so, when David S. Goyer and Josh Friedman started working on adapting Foundation, this was one of the very first things to tackle — that and a way to unify and streamline the original narrative, so it could fit the episodic and seasonal framework.

Instead of multiple separate storylines spread out across the timeline, the series brings them together with time jumps and some classic sci-fi narrative devices. Thus, Apple's Foundation takes the main characters in the early Asimov books and turns them into actual protagonists, each with their own complexities and development arcs. Instead of two or three small chapters, then, characters like Gaal Dornick (Lou Llobell), Salvor Hardin (Leah Harvey), and even Seldon himself have central and much larger roles.

Despite its scope, though, the books are quite simplistic in worldbuilding early on, as the Foundation itself is still struggling to consolidate. Sci-fi television demands something greater, though, so the Apple series has grander and more cinematic settings than the books. Every planet has a complex culture and background, and even things like Seldon's vault, where his recording reveals the next steps of his plan every few decades, become central plot devices tied to the protagonists, keeping the story straight and fluid.

There was no way to make Foundation work as a series if it was an exact match with its book counterpart, and a significant part of why it's so good is how it allows itself to go beyond Asimov's original writings. The best example is the Genetic Dynasty, an entirely new idea created for the series. In the books, the Galactic Empire's decay is what causes the dark ages, but it's the series that actually goes into why that is, since the original focuses mostly on the other side, the Foundation.

For that, the series introduces one of the most brilliant concepts in recent sci-fi: the Genetic Dynasty. It perfectly sums up the notion that the Empire is so stagnant, to the point where every emperor is a clone of the same man, Cleon I. Brothers Dawn (Cassian Bilton), Day (Lee Pace), and Dusk (Terrence Mann) are essentially different versions of the same person, each struggling to establish their own identity while living under the shadow of their predecessor. Another great example is Demerzel (Laura Birn), whose role is considerably expanded from the books, made in the Apple adaptation into a central figure in the Empire's political machinations, with a twist coming straight from the author's wider Robot universe.

These additions give Apple's Foundation an identity of its own without losing sight of the ideas that made the original novels so influential. Ultimately, that's what makes it such a great adaptation, focusing more on understanding and expanding on the potential of the original story rather than simply adapting it. Bringing Foundation to television was always going to require some creative liberties, but the series shows just how much can be achieved when an adaptation understands what makes its source material special.

Foundation is available to stream on Apple TV.

The Mandalorian is, by a wide margin, the single most important Star Wars show ever made. Nothing can take away the magic of those first two seasons or the impact they had on the franchise at large. 25-Episode Netflix Adaptation Of A Classic Book Series Delivers Perfect Worldbuilding

Worldbuilding is one of the most important ingredients in any TV show, but it’s easier with some genres than others. For fantasy and sci-fi, spectacular elements like magical kingdoms and distant planets make for great foundations to build lore on. Comedies and dramas grounded in the real world, however, have much less to work with. Making the familiar feel distinctive is no easy task.

Across three seasons between 2017 and 2019, it created a setting with more personality than many fictional universes that can boast elaborate maps and centuries of invented history. Its secretive world feels vast and governed by peculiar rules that make it seem quite unlike our own reality despite being set there. Based on Daniel Handler’s 13 novels, published under the name Lemony Snicket, A Series of Unfortunate Events follows the three Baudelaire orphans as they bounce between adopted parents and foster families all while pursued by the murderous Count Olaf (Neil Patrick Harris). It has the sinister humor and theatrical atmosphere of a quirky fantasy, yet there’s no magic or supernatural elements to be found. Netflix's A Series of Unfortunate Events may take place in the real world, but it doesn’t feel like it. As far as the aesthetics are concerned, A Series of Unfortunate Events creates its own world by making everything and everywhere look and feel slightly wrong despite being familiar. Its exaggerated sets make ordinary environments like a cheerily decorated living room seem capable of swallowing children whole. The unease this creates isn’t helped by the fact that the adults treat the obvious environmental dangers as perfectly reasonable.

The visual elements of A Series of Unfortunate Events’ worldbuilding are helped by the show’s deliberately scrambled relationship with history. There’s fashion, architecture, and technologies from various technologies, and this is once again presented as totally normal. Telegraphs and walkie-talkies, for example, are both treated like contemporary devices. The modern appears side-by-side with the antique in ways that technically could happen in the real world, but obviously aren’t the norm.

Patrick Warburton’s Lemony Snicket is the final masterstroke that ensures the reality presented by the Netflix show feels unique. He delivers his solemn narration like someone documenting an existing world, complete with personal regrets and information he cannot yet share. Every digression suggests another story happening beyond the children’s immediate predicament. Snicket’s musings make it clear that this world has a past just as rich as its present, making the absurdity of A Series of Unfortunate Events feel bizarrely plausible.

The convincing worldbuilding of A Series of Unfortunate events is definitely an achievement. However, the world of the Baudelaire orphans wasn’t created for the series. It came from Daniel Handler’s novels. This doesn’t make the creative vision of the show less noteworthy, however. Quite the opposite. Just as much as Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events stands out for its small-screen worldbuilding, it’s also an example of a near flawless literary adaptation. The Netflix show completely understands why the A Series of Unfortunate Events books are so beloved.

Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events succeeds as a show because it recognizes that the appeal of the books extends far beyond eccentric locations and elaborate misfortune (a mistake made by the divisive 2004 movie). The world of Handler’s books is a thematic exploration of children’s frustration with adults who possess authority without the intelligence or compassion to deserve it. It’s a setting whose central logic is that bureaucratic procedures are the governing power in all things, even when it blatantly puts the lives of children at risk. Any successful adaptation needs to bring this thematic core to screens, which the Netflix series definitely did.

The recurring failures of characters like Mr. Poe makes the world of both the A Series of Unfortunate Events Netflix show and books terrifying without requiring anything supernatural. The fact that those responsible for protecting Violet, Klaus, and Sunny repeatedly refuse to see danger that readers can is an essential part of why Handler’s novels are so engaging. The Netflix series recognized the importance of this, and that it was much more essential than the timeline of specific events or established visual details.

The show itself proves this, too, since the A Series of Unfortunate Events TV show makes multiple substantial changes to the timeline of the narrative. For example, it brings the Baudelaire’s connections to the shadowy V.F.D. organization forward so that their influence can be felt from the moment Violet, Klaus, and Sunny meet Count Olaf. Many other TV adaptations of books would have been lambasted for making such a huge alteration to the established story. A Series of Unfortunate Events gets away with it because the change helps to support a small-screen version of the narrative whose spiritual heart is a flawless recreation of everything that makes the source material so beloved.

Before ‘Band of Brothers,’ Tom Hanks Produced a Forgotten HBO Miniseries That Aged Perfectly

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the most impressive miniseries of all time is 2001's Band of Brothers. Executive produced by Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, the series is a compelling war drama that follows Easy Company, a part of the U.S. Army's 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment during World War II. Taking home 20 Emmy nominations and seven wins (including one for Outstanding Miniseries), Band of Brothers still holds up as being an authentic and powerful depiction of extraordinary historical events. But this wasn't the first time that Hanks had a hand in producing a show revolving around a true story. Just three years before, Hanks would also produce an HBO miniseries that is a must-watch today, especially for sci-fi fans who love the great beyond.

In 1865, science fiction writer Jules Verne penned a novel called From the Earth to the Moon. More than a century later, a team of Hollywood heavyweights would borrow the title for a 12-part television series depicting some of our country's most famous astronauts. The 1998 series was produced by Hanks, Ron Howard, Brian Grazer, and Michael Bostick — figures who created some of the most influential films of the 1990s. The miniseries tells the story of the Apollo program, which NASA ran during the 1960s and 1970s.

Although the episodes are dramatized, with stunning visuals and special effects by Ernest D. Farino, each episode explores one of the risky missions these astronauts undertook. Hanks introduces each episode, and then the viewer gets to dive into each mission, which is told from different perspectives. This allows every episode to showcase a variety of themes. A particularly brilliant installment is "The Original Wives Club" in Episode 11, which was directed by Oscar winner Sally Field. This episode shows how the wives of these astronauts lived through the Apollo program, leading to a behind-the-scenes look at the stress they endured while their husbands were put in danger, as well as the pressure they experienced in such public-facing positions.

From the beginning stages of the Space Race with the Soviet Union to a pseudo-documentary-style narrative in Episode 12 of the final lunar Mission (Apollo 17), every hour is as gripping as the last. While we're able to celebrate the triumphs of these astronauts as they travel where no man has before, From the Earth to the Moon never shies away from also depicting the tragedies that befell these men. We get to witness the horrific fire onboard Apollo 1 and the infamous Apollo 13 disaster, which ensures that the series is always honest in showing how truly dangerous these missions were. Although it's horrible to think of these traumas occurring to real people, the authentic storytelling makes the series even more harrowing and fascinating to watch.

The writers of From the Earth to the Moon were also intent on not just rehashing events that had already been depicted in movies prior to its release. For example, the 1983 film The Right Stuff portrayed the events of Project Mercury, so it's just briefly summarized in Episode 1. And then there's the 1995 movie Apollo 13, which was also directed by Howard and starred Hanks.

To avoid any repetition, and to ensure viewers still felt invested in the storytelling, From the Earth to the Moon tells the story of the mission from the eyes of the media covering the event. This type of creative storytelling allows the miniseries to feel fresh. While there were a few characters invented for the series, enough famous real-life figures, including Jim Lovell (Tim Daly), Buzz Aldrin (Bryan Cranston), Neil Armstrong (Tony Goldwyn), and Alan Shepard (Ted Levine), allow the show to fall into a familiar, yet captivating authenticity.

At the time of its release, From the Earth to the Moon was a critical darling, bringing home 17 Emmy nominations and three wins, including Outstanding Miniseries (just like Band of Brothers would do a few years later). It would also cement Hanks as someone who could tell historical stories in a respectful and intriguing way (paving the way for future projects such as The Pacific and Masters of the Air).

What makes From the Earth to the Moon so compelling nearly three decades later is its ability to capture both the wonder of space exploration and the enormous personal risks behind humanity's greatest achievements.

King Ghidorah's Origin, Powers, & Godzilla Movie History Explained

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The release of Godzilla Minus Zero will further solidify King Ghidorah's status as the greatest villain in the history of the Godzilla franchise. Hitting theaters worldwide on November 5, the upcoming sequel marks the ninth time a version of the three-headed golden dragon has butted heads with Godzilla in live-action. Ghidorah already had a richer history than all other Godzilla villains, with Mechagodzilla being the only one that came remotely close.

Over the last several decades, Godzilla has accumulated an impressive rogues' gallery made up of a diverse lineup of monsters. He's saved humanity from a giant lobster, a prehistoric dragonfly, all sorts of aliens, and even beings created from his own DNA. And some, like King Ghidorah, had to be defeated more than once. Because of his popularity, the monster has been revisited on multiple occasions, across numerous continuities. As a result, there are several different versions of King Ghidorah in the overarching Godzilla franchise.

Most movies that have included him maintain a high degree of consistency in terms of how he's portrayed. However, the King Ghidorah character is incredibly nuanced, much like Godzilla himself. Some films have offered different explanations about his origins and what his powers are, thus handing a level of flexibility to Godzilla Minus Zero for its take on the King of the Monsters' archenemy.

Although far from the first monster to fight Godzilla, he served as an early opponent to the King of the Monsters, fighting him for the first time in Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster. Released in 1964 as the fifth entry into the franchise and a direct sequel to Godzilla vs. Mothra, the movie used King Ghidorah to faciliate Godzilla's first-of-many team-ups in his cinematic history.

In the movie, Mothra has to convince Godzilla and Rodan to stop fighting and join forces to drive away King Ghidorah, an incredibly powerful alien monster who wiped out the alien race living on Venus and has since set his sights on Earth. Following Ghidorah defeat at the hands of Godzilla and Rodan, the villain returned for a rematch with the same two monsters in 1965's Invasion of Astro Monster.

In Destroy All Monsters, Ghidorah had an even more difficult path ahead of him when he had to fight Godzilla, Rodan, Mothra, plus an assortment of other kaiju. His next role, though, which came in 1972's Godzilla vs. Gigan, evened the odds by having Godzilla and his ally, Anguirus, square off against Ghidorah and Gigan.

19 years passed before Ghidorah was utilized again. The Godzilla franchise was rebooted in 1984 with a darker, less kid-friendly feel. Several of his allies and villains were gradually reintroduced into the franchise, with Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah giving Godzilla and Ghidorah their first one-on-one fight in 1991. The movie also famously gave Ghidorah a new mechanized upgrade, courtesy of the human characters - Mecha-King Ghidorah.

Ghidorah's next two movie appearances were significant departures from the past. Ghidorah filled a heroic role in Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack, a movie in which Godzilla was the true villain. In Godzilla: Final Wars, Toho introduced Keizer Ghidorah, who wasn't technically King Ghidorah himself, but a considerably more powerful member of the same species.

Legendary's Monsterverse tried its hand at Ghidorah by making the villain of the 2019 Hollywood production, Godzilla: King of the Monsters. Recreating the monster cast of Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster by featuring Godzilla, Rodan, Mothra, and Ghidorah, the film saw the latter attempt to terraform the planet. Naturally, it was up to Godzilla to stop him.

Nearly every movie appearance from Ghidorah - including his first and only Hollywood film - has defined Ghidorah roughly the same way. Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster established Ghidorah as an alien threat, an origin that every movie except Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack has kept. Being a film where Ghidorah was characterized as one of three benevolent guardians of the Earth, it remains an outlier in regards to the kaiju's portrayal.

Typically, his alien origins are important to the plot, such as when aliens controlled him in Invasion of Astro Monster and Destroy All Monsters. Legendary's King of the Monsters, for its part, didn't discuss it at length but heavily implied that his nature as an extraterrestrial being was the catalyst behind the destructive path he carved.

One particular movie went much deeper with his origin story. In Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah's telling of it, he doesn't begin the story as "King Ghidorah." Rather than exist as a singular entity, Ghidorah was originally a multitude of harmless, golden dragon-like creatures called Dorats. Hailing from a distant planet, the Dorats were collectively transformed into King Ghiodrah when time travel was used to make them take the place of Godzilla at the moment that he was to be bombarded with nuclear radiation during World War 2.

Similar to the situation with his origins, depictions of King Ghidorah's powers are largely consistent as well. Each of Ghidorah's three heads are capable of firing blasts of yellow lightning known as "gravity beams." They're not as powerful as Godzilla's atomic breath, but through continuous use of his gravity beams from all three heads, Ghidorah can indeed use them to inflict serious pain on Godzilla.

Godzilla: Final Wars' central villain, Keizer Ghidorah, possesses a stronger variation of the gravity beam called Destroyed Thunder, which actually allowed him to manipulate gravity.

Just as it is with Godzilla and his atomic breath, the gravity beams are the signature weapon of King Ghidorah. But he has other ways of fighting Godzilla too, such as his ability to fly. He can scoop up kaiju with his talons and has enough physical strength to carry them into the air before dropping them from extreme heights.

As for how dangerous that makes him, that tends to vary. Even though Godzilla had help on his side in Ghidorah's first three movie appearances, it was never proven that he wouldn't have been able to beat him alone. And Godzilla vs. Gigan did demonstrate that Godzilla could win a fair fight against him. The same was true of the 1991 and 2001 versions of Ghidorah seen in Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah and Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah respectively.

Based on how his fights with Godzilla typically went, King Ghidorah is an incredibly powerful Godzilla villain, but not quite as strong as Destoroyah or SpaceGodzilla. But his relative, Keizer Ghidorah, has a strong case for being the most powerful Godzilla villain of all time. In any case, there's every reason to expect an intense matchup before watching Godzilla Minus Zero.

The 10 Best Whodunit Movies in Film History

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There are very few genres that reward an attentive audience quite like a traditional whodunit. From the moment a body is discovered, or a will is read, or a dinner party takes a sinister turn, the contract between filmmaker and viewer is clear: someone here is lying, everyone has a motive, and the truth is usually hiding in plain sight. Deeply rooted in the golden age of detective fiction, these movies have given the genre a life of its own, and the best ones, such as The Thin Man, Murder on the Orient Express, and Clue, offer audiencesmore than a cryptic puzzle.

The greatest whodunits are exercises in precision, balancing a cast of colorful suspects, a tight-knit setting, and a reveal that feels both shocking and inevitable. Some are faithful to the traditions of a drawing-room mystery while others bend and break the rules entirely, but the one thing that unites them all is the commitment to the thrill of answering one question: who did it? From shadowy black-and-white classics like Otto Preminger's iconic noir Laura to sleek modern reinventions such as Knives Out starring Daniel Craig, these are the ten best whodunit movies in film history!

René Clair's adaptation of the world's best-selling mystery novel, And Then There Were None, written by the Queen of Crime Agatha Christie, undoubtedly earns its place among the greatest whodunits by doing what the genre does best: trapping its characters, and its audience, in a puzzle with no way out. The film follows eight strangers who receive an invitation from an anonymous host to a home located on a remote island, where they are picked off one by one, following the grim pattern of a nursery rhyme.

And Then There Were None endures because of its flawless sense of escalating dread. Clair balances genuine suspense with sly, darkly comic touches and lets the tone shift between menace and wit without ever losing control. The ensemble cast, including Barry Fitzgerald, Walter Huston, and Louis Hayward, gives each suspect a distinct personality, making every accusation feel plausible, and every death lands with weight. The movie also pioneered the "closed-circle" setting structure, which countless other mysteries would later borrow, ultimately proving why And Then There Were None is an influential cornerstone of the traditional whodunit.

Robert Altman's Gosford Park is an Oscar-winning love letter to Jean Renoir's classic French film, The Rules of the Game, and features an outstanding ensemble cast of notable stars as well as a witty, cleverly constructed whodunit with real dramatic bite. The movie centers around several British aristocrats and their servants who travel to a countryside estate for the weekend, where their plans of relaxation and fun turn into a hunt for a killer after their gracious host is found murdered.

Altman’s roaming camera and layered soundscape make you feel like an eavesdropper, catching fragments of gossip from both social classes that matter more than they first appear, making Gosford Park one of the best upstairs-downstairs movies. The ensemble cast, including Maggie Smith, Helen Mirren, Michael Gambon, and Clive Owen, delivers uniformly superb performances, while Stephen Fry’s bumbling Inspector Thompson gently parodies the genre’s detective tradition. Part Agatha Christie homage, part social satire, Gosford Park respects the whodunit’s pleasures while expanding what the form can say, making it an intriguing puzzle with real emotional and intellectual weight.

John Guillermin’s Oscar-winning classic Death on the Nile is a glittering, sun-soaked whodunit that proves Agatha Christie’s formula thrives with the right cast and setting. Aboard a luxurious steamer cruising Egypt, the famous Belgian detective, Hercule Poirot (Peter Ustinov), must solve the shocking murder of an heiress, Linnet Doyle (Lois Chiles), who is shot dead during her honeymoon, and nearly everyone aboard had reason to want her gone, most notably the jilted Jacqueline de Bellefort (Mia Farrow), who has been stalking the newlyweds.

Ustinov brings warmth and wry humor to Christie's famous sleuth, while Anthony Shaffer’s screenplay preserves the novel’s ingenious plotting. The solution depends on precise timing, misdirection, and a deception that few viewers spot, yet it feels fair on a second viewing. The suspects are a showcase of acting royalty, including David Niven, Bette Davis, George Kennedy, and Angela Lansbury, who each deliver showstopping performances. Between its collection of familiar faces and its alluring murder mystery shrouded in scandal, Death on the Nile is a murderously clever and gorgeously mounted whodunit that remains a benchmark for the all-star mystery, and is a must-see for any fan of the genre.

The Last of Sheila is a perfect whodunit written by Anthony Perkins and Stephen Sondheim​​​​​, who famously staged elaborate scavenger hunts and murder games for their Hollywood friends, turning their real-life pastime into a razor-sharp mystery. A year after his wife was killed in a hit-and-run, a Hollywood producer, Clinton Green (James Coburn), invites six friends to spend the week doing an elaborate scavenger hunt while aboard his yacht. As each guest receives a card bearing a secret, one by one the secrets are uncovered, and the clues begin to point toward real guilt.

What truly sets The Last of Sheila apart from other classic whodunits is its intricate plotting and the script’s insider wit about Hollywood phoniness, which adds bite. Herbert Ross directs with glossy, sun-drenched menace, and the cast is a delicious collection of acid-tongued egos including Raquel Welch, James Mason, Dyan Cannon, and Ian McShane, who all engage in a bitchy, quotable banter that makes the film as funny as it is twisty. Decades before Clue and Knives Out, The Last of Sheila stood as the prime example of how a whodunit could be both a brilliant game and a savage comedy, deeming it to be a significant contribution to the genre.

Rian Johnson's hit Knives Out undeniably revitalized the whodunit for a new generation of fans and did so by honoring the rules of the genre while gleefully bending them. Daniel Craig stars as a master detective, Benoit Blanc, who investigates the recent death of a wealthy crime novelist, Harlan Thrombey (Christopher Plummer), who is found dead after celebrating his 85th birthday. As Blanc sits down with Thrombey's extensive family of eccentric relatives, each of them proves to be a potential suspect with something to hide or something to gain.

Rather than withholding the truth until the final scene, Johnson reveals the central secret early and then builds suspense from a different question: will the guilty party be caught? That inversion keeps the story fresh while still delivering the satisfying twists that fans expect, and nearly every clue pays off on rewatch. The entire cast is irresistible, notably Ana de Armas, who anchors the film with real heart as the kind-hearted nurse Marta, while Jamie Lee Curtis, Chris Evans, Toni Collette, and Michael Shannon make the Thrombey clan deliciously awful. Funny, stylish, and genuinely clever, Knives Out stands proudly beside Christie’s classics while feeling entirely modern and original.

Instead of borrowing from the pages of great writers, director and screenwriter Jonathan Lynn took the popular board game, Clue, and, with no established story, turned it into one of the most gleefully inventive whodunit mysteries ever made. Set during a stormy night in 1954, six blackmailed guests arrive at a New England mansion for a dinner party hosted by the mysterious Mr. Boddy. When their questionable host turns up dead, the butler, Wadsworth (Tim Curry), leads them on a frantic search through the house as the body count keeps climbing.

The film’s boldest stroke is its trio of endings, which parody the genre’s tradition of the grand explanation while delivering a satisfying solution. Each version recasts earlier scenes, and the screenplay’s tight plotting makes the whole madcap structure feel fair. The comedic ensemble is a dream as Curry’s wired, fast-talking Wadsworth establishes the chaos, while Madeline Kahn, Eileen Brennan, Martin Mull, Michael McKean, Christopher Lloyd, and Lesley Ann Warren all land unforgettable moments. Despite being an initial flop at the box office, Clue has since become a beloved cult classic celebrated for treating the whodunit as both a genuine puzzle and a wild, farcical romp.

Otto Preminger’s noir classic Laura is a haunting whodunit that begins as a traditional murder mystery and gradually turns into a fascinating character study of obsession, control, and deception. Dana Andrews stars as a homicide detective, Mark McPherson, investigating the recent murder of an advertising executive, Laura Hunt (Gene Tierney), who was brutally gunned down in her home. As McPherson talks to Hunt's friends and associates, he finds himself falling hopelessly in love with the beautiful victim.

Laura is widely known for its mid-way plot twist, which upends everything and reframes the second half of the mystery with an unexpected cleverness, ultimately setting it apart from other classic whodunits. The suspects in Laura are a wonderfully poisonous lot, notably Clifton Webb, whose performance as the caddy but elegant columnist and Laura’s mentor, Waldo Lydecker, earned him an Academy Award nomination for Best Supporting Actor. Joseph LaShelle's Oscar-winning cinematography immerses audiences in an almost beautiful nightmare, while David Raksin sets the tone of a dreamlike atmosphere with his sweeping score, which has since become one of the most recognizable film melodies ever written.

W. S. Van Dyke’s pre-Code comedy mystery The Thin Man is the film that ultimately proved a whodunit could be as much about the detectives as the crime itself, making it a landmark entry in the film genre. Adapted from Dashiell Hammett’s novel, the movie follows William Powell as a retired private eye, Nick Charles, who is dragged back into sleuthing when an eccentric inventor, Clyde Wynant (Edward Ellis), vanishes, and his former secretary is found murdered. While Charles would rather be drinking, his wife, Nora (Myrna Loy), is thrilled by the prospect of a real case.

The mystery itself in The Thin Man is deliciously tangled, but Nick and Nora Charles are the film's secret weapon. Powell and Loy, who starred in thirteen movies together, have effortless chemistry, trading quips over cocktails with a playful affection that was revolutionary for its time. Their banter not only makes the film endlessly rewatchable, but it also set the template for the witty, happily married sleuthing couple who have been imitated ever since. Albert Hackett and Frances Goodrich's screenplay keeps the clues moving at a breezy pace, while the finale, a dinner party where Nick gathers everyone to expose the killer, is a classic quality of the quintessential whodunit. Smart, sophisticated, and consistently funny, The Thin Man spawned a slew of hit sequels and, even decades later, remains an undisputed landmark of the genre.

Billy Wilder’s Oscar-nominated classic Witness for the Prosecution is a courtroom whodunit adapted from Agatha Christie’s play of the same name that builds up to one of the most celebrated twist endings in film history. The movie follows ailing barrister Sir Wilfrid Robarts (Charles Laughton), who takes on the defense of Leonard Vole (Tyrone Power), accused of murdering a wealthy widow who had named him in her will. The case looks grim until Vole’s German wife, Christine (Marlene Dietrich), is called to testify against him, turning a seemingly open-and-shut case into a labyrinth of a mystery.

The film’s brilliance lies in how it turns the courtroom into a stage for misdirection. Every cross-examination, every shift in testimony, feels like a clue, and Wilder’s script, co-written with Harry Kurnitz, keeps the audience second-guessing who is lying and why. The final reveal is so audacious that the film’s closing credits famously ask viewers not to spoil it. Laughton is magnificent as the cantankerous Sir Wilfrid, whose sparring with his nurse between scenes provides delicious comic relief. Power is slippery and charming as Vole, while Dietrich is mesmerizing, delivering a performance of cool, calculated ambiguity that steals the entire show.

Sidney Lumet's adaptation of Agatha Christie's famous novel, Murder on the Orient Express, is the gold standard of the classic whodunit, and ends with a twisted reveal that remains shocking and morally provocative decades later. Stranded by a snowdrift in 1935, the luxurious train becomes a sealed room where the notorious Belgian detective, Hercule Poirot, played by Albert Finney, must unravel the stabbing of an unpopular businessman, Edward Rachett (Richard Widmark), whose past hides a terrible secret.

The film’s greatest asset is its dazzling ensemble. Ingrid Bergman won an Oscar for her small but unforgettable turn, while Lauren Bacall, Sean Connery, Vanessa Redgrave, and Anthony Perkins fill every compartment with star power. Finney’s fastidious, theatrical Poirot, buried under heavy makeup and a fussy accent, is a delight and earned him an Oscar nomination, making him the only actor to receive a nomination as Christie's infamous detective. Entertaining, handsomely crafted, and endlessly rewatchable, Murder on the Orient Express is a landmark murder mystery that set the template for every star-studded whodunit that followed, essentially solidifying its place at the top of the list.

“No Safe Waters” played in the Cannes Film Festival’s Immersive Competition this year, with Liu narrating and producing alongside Singer. It follows the original 2024 VR version of “The Pirate Queen,” which earned an Emmy nomination.

Liu says Cheng Shih was intelligent and strategically brilliant, and won respect through negotiation and leadership “in a world designed to exclude women entirely.” In Liu’s view, the pirate leader defies nearly every stereotype historically placed on Asian women in storytelling. “She was politically astute, ruthless when necessary, deeply strategic, and completely self-determined. Stories like hers remind us that history has always contained powerful, complex women – they were never centered in mainstream narratives,” Liu tells Variety. “That’s beginning to change, and I think audiences are hungry for stories that feel more expansive.”

Where the first outing was built for a headset, the new piece fills a shared room with projection, sound and movement. Audiences stand among storms and fleets as dragons move beneath their feet.

Liu does not appear on screen. She says voice carries a different kind of responsibility in immersive work, guiding atmosphere and movement at the same time. “In film, the camera captures a glance or physical gesture to communicate subtext, but here the voice can help create emotional architecture,” she says.

Singer has spent a decade on “The Pirate Queen,” the last three years of it with Liu, and says the pair have co-written a graphic novel, are preparing the international tour and are developing a new game. “I feel really lucky to be making it with Lucy. She’s thrown her heart and soul into this alongside me, and getting to build this world together has been one of the most special parts of the journey,” she says. “I love seeing how one story can take on a whole new life across different formats, each offering audiences a completely different way to experience it.”

Liu says the project’s ambition, and the importance of telling Cheng Shih’s story with nuance and scale, is something she and Singer both care about deeply. “We’re building ‘The Pirate Queen’ as a larger storyworld together, so it was never just about lending my voice to one experience,” she says. “As a producer, I wanted to help support the integrity of the story and the larger creative vision. A friend who went through the program before her told her to make the most of every opportunity. “So that’s what I intend to do! I’m really looking forward to meeting other filmmakers and finding ways to take over the world together,” she says.

The 15 Best Comedy Movies of the Last 25 Years, Ranked

📅 Dec 23, 2024  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

From slapstick to deadpan humor, comedy reigns as one of the most versatile film genres, always offering something for every kind of movie fan. Since the early days of movies, comedy has been one of cinema's first popular genres, serving as a humorous escape for audiences; today, it remains just as cherished, if not more so.

There have been hundreds of monumental comedies but, when it comes to the last decade and a half, there are a handful of titles, such as Wedding Crashers, Borat, and Bridesmaids, that rank higher than the rest. Even though there are countless comedies that successfully raked in the laughs, hits like The Hangover, The 40-Year-Old Virgin, and Anchorman: The Legend of Ron Burgundy far exceeded moviegoers' expectations with outrageous scenarios and non-stop laughter.

We're the Millers is a raunchy comedy starring Jason Sudeikis as David Clark, a small-time weed dealer who, after being robbed of his product, must travel to Mexico to pick up his supplier's latest shipment. To cross the border without arousing suspicion, Clark recruits a fake family: his stripper neighbor, Rose (Jennifer Aniston); a no-nonsense runaway, Casey (Emma Roberts); and a socially awkward teenager, Kenny (Will Poulter), leading them on a wild, unexpected road trip.

We're the Millers was released when R-rated comedies were thriving, and despite mixed reviews, the movie was a box office success, grossing over $200 million. The cast's chemistry is the real heart and soul of the movie, with each bringing their own unique style of comedy to the table and essentially keeping audiences invested from start to finish. Even though the film's humor is initially rather aggressive, the movie gradually turns into a story of misfits who become a family, creating a warmth that softens the crude jokes and outrageous scenarios.

21 Jump Street is a hilarious buddy-cop comedy based on the hit '80s show of the same name and stars Channing Tatum and Jonah Hill as rookie cops, Greg Jenko and Morton Schmidt, who go undercover as high school students to try and shut down a dangerous drug ring. Compared to other comedies at the time, 21 Jump Street stands out because it knows exactly the kind of movie it is and constantly pokes fun at Hollywood's resilience in reboots and remakes, subtly winking at the audience without becoming smug.

21 Jump Street was a major success, with many critics praising both Hill and Tatum for their performances. The bromance between Hill and Tatum's characters is what ultimately carries the movie, specifically the role reversal, which sees Tatum's character, a former popular jock, struggle to fit in while Hill's character, a former nerd, thrives with his classmates, essentially giving the movie real emotional stakes. It also didn't hurt the movie to have a few unexpected cameos by original stars of the show, Johnny Depp and Peter DeLuise, who bring the perfect touch of nostalgia to the film.

What We Do in the Shadows is a mockumentary horror comedy that follows a group of vampires and roommates, Viago (Taika Waititi), Vladislav (Jemaine Clement), and Deacon (Jonny Brugh), who try to navigate their way through the modern world while also showing a recently turned human, Nick (Cori Gonzalez-Macuer), some of the perks of being undead. The movie was both a critical and commercial success, and in 2019, it was adapted into a television series by FX and ran for six successful seasons.

At the time, vampires were popular subjects of the entertainment world, but unlike the rest, What We Do in the Shadows delivers a fresh and clever take on the concept that ultimately sets it apart from the crowd. The central joke never runs out of fuel, and the premise of immortal, centuries-old vampires who are petty, vain, and bad at modern life is one with endless variations. They struggle to get into clubs without being invited, they argue about chores, and what to wear for a night out, which audiences can surprisingly relate to on a certain level.

The 2003 comedy Old School earns a place on the list because it captured the best of the early-2000s "Frat Pack" era and turned a simple premise into a comedy that people still quote on a regular basis today. The movie follows an attorney, Mitch Martin (Luke Wilson), who, after discovering his fiancée's infidelity, tries to start over and moves into a house that happens to be near a local college campus. As Mitch settles into his new life as a bachelor, his friends Frank (Will Ferrell) and Beanie (Vince Vaughn) decide to turn his place into a party pad and eventually start their own fraternity.

While the premise of Old School seems a bit absurd, the concept of being able to relive your glory days with all the recklessness and lack of good judgment is irresistible to audiences, making it one of the best comedies of the last 25 years. The entire cast is an endless stream of comedy gold, notably Ferrell, who delivers a career-defining performance as Frank "The Tank", while Vaughn's fast-talking charm and Wilson's low-key sincerity round the trio out. Quotes, such as "You're my boy, Blue!" and "Ear muffs!", have since become staples in pop culture, with many fans still quoting the movie even decades later.

The 2008 comedy Tropic Thunder is a ruthless and fearless satire of Hollywood that also happens to be relentlessly funny. Ben Stiller directs and stars as a washed-up action hero, Tugg Speedman, who is cast in an epic Vietnam War movie alongside an intense method actor (Robert Downey Jr.), a drug-addicted comedian (Jack Black), and a rapper with aspirations for the big screen (Brandon T. Jackson). When production is delayed, the director decides to send the actors into the jungle, where they will be filmed by hidden cameras, leading to a series of unfortunate events that eventually land the unknowing group in a real war zone.

The greatest strength of Tropic Thunder is its unwavering commitment to the bit. The fake trailers that open the film, the unhinged jokes, and Oscar bait all show a movie that is unafraid to push the boundaries even if it involves taking shots at the industry itself. While the entire cast delivers sidesplitting performances, Tom Cruise's eccentric performance as the profane, bald studio boss Les Grossman nearly steals the show. Even though the movie earned some backlash, no amount of criticism can diminish how bold, outrageous, and endlessly quotable Tropic Thunder remains to this day.

Simon Pegg stars in the action comedy, Hot Fuzz, as an elite police officer in London, Nicholas Angel, whose successful track record makes the rest of his unit look bad, ultimately resulting in him being transferred to a small rural village. Once Angel arrives in his less desired area, he's soon assigned to investigating a series of brutal murders, adding a bit of an exciting silver lining to his situation.

Hot Fuzz is a hysterical homage to the traditional buddy cop film featuring the humorous dynamic of Pegg and Nick Frost, who originally caught audiences' attention with the parody film, Shaun of the Dead. The duo has a unique chemistry that is both comfortable and natural, conveying a convincing comedic duo that is one of the best in recent years. Aside from a few negative reviews, Hot Fuzz was overall well received by audiences and critics, who commended the film's use of clichés and puns of the traditional buddy cop genre.

South Park creators, Trey Parker and Matt Stone, return to the big screen with the puppetry comedy, Team America: World Police, which is an over-the-top satire on the traditional action film. The film follows a special counterterrorism force that recruits a Broadway actor to help them save the world from Kim Jong Il and his coalition of terrorists and Hollywood's elite.

Inspired by the British science-fiction series, Thunderbirds, Team America had no problem catching audiences' attention with its unusual use of puppetry and miniature effects, which conveyed Parker and Stone's signature style of comedy and antics. The movie has been regarded as one that will both offend and incite laughter in audiences, showing zero hesitation about the levels of offensive insults and exaggerated depictions of famous names. Team America earned generally positive reviews from critics, who praised its bold comedic formula of offending everyone on an equal scale.

Funny man, Judd Apatow, made his directorial debut with the outlandish romantic comedy, The 40-Year-Old Virgin, which stars Steve Carell as a middle-aged electronics employee, Andy, who is mortified when his co-workers discover he's still a virgin. Unwilling to let it go, Andy's co-workers help him find a woman to take his virginity, but as they set him up with disaster after disaster, Andy manages to find a woman who is more than just a one-night stand.

While the initial plot of the film seems a bit vulgar, The 40-Year-Old Virgin does have a sentimental side and a series of outstanding performances by an all-star cast, including Seth Rogen, Jane Lynch, and Paul Rudd, that are impossible to deny as comedy gold. The 40-Year-Old Virgin takes an uncomfortable, sensitive subject and makes it approachable for audiences with its genuine and lighthearted humor, making it one of the best comedies in the last decade and a half.

The mockumentary, Borat, was a cultural phenomenon that became one of the most talked-about movies of the year, earning a reputation for its improvised humor and overly offensive scenarios by Sacha Baron Cohen. Cohen stars as the titular character, a reporter from Kazakhstan who travels to the United States to make a documentary. Throughout his journey, Borat manages to piss off and offend everyone he crosses paths with, resulting in an unpredictable series of events.

Borat is noted for Cohen's improvised interactions with people who are genuinely under the impression that he is from another country and filming a documentary about America. This leads to uninhibited, uncensored, and outlandish scenes that are the definition of unhinged comedy. Surprisingly, Borat earned positive reviews from critics, including Peter Travers of Rolling Stone, who stated, "You won't know what outrageous is until you see Borat. High-five!"

Kristin Wiig stars as a single woman, Annie, whose life is an utter mess, ranging from having to move back in with her mom to losing her bakery. When her best friend, Lillian (Maya Rudolph), gets engaged, she's asked to be her maid of honor, which she graciously accepts, but she has her work cut out for her as she finds herself competing against Lillian's new, wealthy, and elite friend, Helen (Rose Byrne).

Bridesmaids is hands down one of the funniest and most entertaining movies of the last 25 years that accurately conveys the classic frienemy dynamic between women in an honest and hilarious light. While the movie is full of unforgettable, laugh-out-loud moments, it also has immense heart and, in the end, essentially reveals that everyone has their own batch of insecurities and problems in life.
