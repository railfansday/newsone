The Assassin: Freddie Highmore’s 12-Part Thriller Officially Returns On AMC+

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Freddie Highmore's 12-part assassin thriller officially returns on AMC+ with six new episodes, a deadly conspiracy, and an explosive mother-son reunion. After its season 1 premiere in late 2025, Highmore's first TV follow-up since The Good Doctor ended in 2024 offers an intriguing blend of action thriller and dark comedy, with critics and fans alike calling it pulpy, quirky, and loaded with on-screen chemistry. If you're a fan of Highmore's previous television projects, like Bates Motel, this is one of the best new series to press play on this weekend.

Freddie Highmore's thriller series The Assassin just released its season 2 premiere in the United States on Thursday, November 19, exclusively on AMC+. The series, which premiered on Prime Video in the UK earlier this month, is created by two of the producers of Prime Video's Emmy-winning comedy series Fleabag, making The Assassin worth checking out this week. The series earned a strong 72% critic score on Rotten Tomatoes and is praised for its striong leading duo of Highmore and Keeley Hawes, best known for Orphan Black: Echoes and Bodyguard.

Without getting into spoiler territory, The Assassin season 2 picks up right where season 1 leaves off after a shocking twist ending that completely changes the framework of the series. In the first season of The Assassin, Highmore's protagonist Edward meets up with his estranged mother, Julie, finding her on a Greek island living the life of a retired assassin. Edward is an investigative journalist searching for answers from his mother, specifically about their family history and their distant relationship. However, things quickly go sideways when Julie's past life comes back to haunt her, presenting new threats for both her and Edward.

These new dangers force Edward and Julie to escape from their island getaway, sending them deep within a global conspiracy that directly relates to Julie's profession as a contract killer. Edward and Julie must find a way to work together and survive despite their damaged mother-son relationship and conflicting personalities, driving mysteries and conflict from both external and internal sources.

The Assassin carries a similar dysfunctional parental dynamic to Dexter: Resurrection, in which a son must come to terms with the reality of their parent's crimes and must determine where they stand on this undeniable truth. It is also reminiscent of Netflix's hit spy thriller/dark comedy series Black Doves, which just returned for its 6-part sophomore season earlier this month as well.

The Assassin is far from your standard action thriller due to its complicated mother-son dynamic that is very much the emotional core of the story. That said, nothing is complicated about the on-screen chemistry between Highmore and Keeley, who are not only the backbone of the narrative, but the most entertaining characters throughout The Assassin.

Given Julie's history, she often comes off as a no-nonsense leader who takes swift, even deadly, action, while Edward's profession as a journalist makes him inquisitive, cautious, and a deep thinker. Because Edward and Julie are quite opposite in their personalities, their relationship defies typical familial standards in interesting ways. The situation between Julie and Edward only becomes more nuanced in The Assassin season 2, as new secrets are revealed that have the potential to ruin the efforts they made in season 1 to make amends.

Ultimately, The Assassin is one of the most unique thriller series on streaming right now, combining action, emotion, and humor to create a can't-miss viewing experience on AMC+.

Forget Bahoya: Daniel James Set to Push for January Leeds Exit

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Leeds United have had a mixed start to their season in the Premier League, and Daniel Farke has made some strange decisions in the eyes of the fans.

The Whites claimed a 1-0 win over Tottenham Hotspur just before the international break to move the team up to ninth in the table, as they snapped a run of just one win in their previous nine fixtures against the north London club.

Despite their healthy position in the Premier League table, Leeds have won just three of their ten games so far this season in the top-flight, and have scored just 10 goals. But the manager has continued to persist with his safety-first style of football, leaving a number of attacking talent out of the side to ensure there is more defensive stability.

Dominic Calvert-Lewin continues to be the focal point of the attack, and has rewarded the manager with a healthy return of four goals in those 10 games, while his strike partner has often been Noah Okafor, who has scored twice himself.

But aside from those two players, everyone else is more defensive minded. A midfield three usually consisting of Ethan Ampadu, Ao Tanaka, and Anton Stach has produced just one goal and one assist combined this season, while the back five is made up of traditional defenders.

There has also been frustration at a lack of opportunities for summer signing Jean-Matteo Bahoya, who has featured for just 11 minutes this season, despite arriving as a highly-rated and skilful attacker. But while there has been speculation that he could find his spell at Elland Road being cut short, despite an option to make the move permanent next summer, he isn't alone in being ostracised from the side.

Fellow winger Daniel James had been a regular under Farke for much of his Leeds career, but this season he has been very much a bit-part player.

The Wales international has featured for just 117 minutes across four appearances this season, making just one start in the Premier League. As expected, that means he is yet to contribute to a goal or assist, and there is believed to be growing frustration at his situation, despite having signed a new contract last year.

Speaking to Football Insider, expert Mick Brown revealed that the former Man Utd winger could push for a move away in January as a result of a lack of opportunities.

"Daniel James is in a bit of a strange position at Leeds, it's hard to know where he stands," he said.

"He is a valuable asset to have coming off the bench, because he's quick, he's direct, he can put defenders under pressure, and he's good to have when you're chasing a game.

"But despite all of those things I've said, notice there's nothing there about his quality as a player, and that's where the doubts are.

"Physically, he's great, he offers a lot in that department, but his actual skill level has always been in doubt and I'm still not convinced he's a Premier League player in that respect.

"He's done well at Leeds, he's played an important role for them, but now he's not starting games and it looks like they're moving to a level where he won't be needed.

"From his point of view, he is not happy with his role, he wants to be starting games at this stage of his career, and he doesn't just want to be sitting on the bench and coming on every now and then.

"So there is every chance that, if things don't change soon, he could push for a move away because that's what it might take for him to move on."

While James was a key figure in the team's promotion to the top flight, his performances in the Premier League have been far from the level needed or expected.

His pace is a clear weapon to have in the squad, but he isn't a wing-back and he doesn't have the technical quality or physical attributes to be able to play centrally. He currently finds himself behind Harry Wilson, Brenden Aaronson, Lukas Nmecha, Okafor and Calvert-Lewin in those roles too, which makes keeping him around a bit pointless.

The player is clearly unhappy with his situation and wants to go, and while Farke doesn't want his squad to become weaker, the club should be looking to cash in, make some money from him, and reinvest that into the squad for a player who can actually help them.

A new striker is likely to be a priority, but if the club want to bring in a more attack-minded wing-back option that could help too. Either way, it's obvious that James hasn't got a long-term future at Elland Road, and January seems to be a perfect chance for both parties to go their separate ways.

james turrell fills new york gallery with floating cubes and glowing walls of light

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

the legendary light artist brings floating cubes and luminous walls to almine rech's tribeca gallery in new york.

James Turrell has filled Almine Rech’s Tribeca gallery in New York with luminous forms that seem to extend from the walls and hover in midair. Titled Something Old, Something New, Something Borrowed, Something Blue, the exhibition brings together over fifteen works, and even includes new installations that revisit some of the artist’s earliest experiments with light and perception.

A rectangular opening glows violet against a darkened wall. Elsewhere, a luminous cube appears to float where two walls meet, with its sharp edges suggesting a solid object suspended in space. The room itself becomes difficult to read as the light alters viewers’ sense of depth and distance.

The show is on view through January 30th, 2027, and marks Turrell’s first presentation with Almine Rech in the United States. It arrives nearly four decades after their first collaboration in Paris, and returns to ideas that have occupied the artist since the 1960s.

Among the new works is the installation Shallow Space, part of a series in which James Turrell uses illuminated apertures to create unexpected depth within a wall. At first, the luminous surface can appear completely flat, like a field of color. As the viewer’s eyes adjust, the opening reveals an actual space beyond the wall, although its dimensions remain difficult to judge.

In Raemar II (2026), a glowing rectangular form casts violet light across the surrounding surfaces. Color spreads beyond its edges and gradually dissolves into the room. From a distance, the work resembles an illuminated painting, although its relationship to the wall is far more ambiguous.

The exhibition also introduces new pieces from the artist‘s Glassworks series, which uses precisely programmed LEDs behind etched glass. Colors change gradually across these luminous surfaces, and the apparent depth of each work shifts with them. Some passages of light appear dense and opaque, while others seem to recede far beyond the physical dimensions of the piece.

Another new installation, Afrum Violet (2026), returns to James Turrell’s Cross Corner projections. A sharply defined violet form occupies the intersection of two walls, where the geometry of the room helps create the illusion of a three-dimensional cube. The surrounding surfaces absorb a softer wash of purple, while the central form retains its remarkably precise edges.

Turrell began experimenting with these projections in 1966 at the former Mendota Hotel in Ocean Park, California, where he converted the building into a studio. By directing light onto the walls at specific angles, he discovered that a simple projection could appear to have weight and volume. A shape formed by light could become surprisingly convincing as a physical presence.

Six decades later, the strange effect remains. From one spot, the illuminated planes read as a solid cube. Move through the room, and the relationship between the shape and its architectural setting begins to change. The installation asks viewers to spend time with something as familiar as a corner and reconsider exactly what they are seeing.

UK and EU to agree youth mobility scheme, BBC understands

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Watch: Burnham says he wants 'best deal for Britain' at UK-EU summit

Up to 100,000 young people from the EU would be allowed to travel, work and study in the UK per year under a new youth mobility scheme set to be agreed on Friday, the BBC understands.

The same number of young Britons would be allowed into Europe under the scheme, expected to be approved at the UK-EU summit in Brussels. Downing Street has not commented on the details, but said talks were "down to the wire" on Thursday.

The prime minister has said a "Channel action plan" on immigration could also be struck, while negotiators are seeking agreements on food trade rules and carbon emissions pricing.

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has accused Andy Burnham of "reopening the divisions" of the Brexit referendum.

A new youth mobility scheme would aim to make it easier for young people to work and travel both in the EU and UK.

The UK already has schemes in place with 13 non-EU countries including Australia, Canada and India.

In 2024, the EU proposed allowing 18 to 30 year-olds to work or study in the EU and UK for up to four years.

Labour, which was then in opposition, rejected the deal - arguing it amounted to reinstating freedom of movement.

Since coming to government, the party has suggested it would consider a more restricted scheme with a time limit, a cap on the numbers coming to the UK and a ban on participants from claiming benefits.

The UK's request to limit numbers has proved to be a sticking point in negotiation ahead of the summit, along with the EU's request that EU students should pay the same tuition fees at UK universities as domestic students.

Speaking to broadcasters on Thursday evening, Burnham was asked if there were any circumstances in which the UK would walk away from a deal on Friday.

"There would always be those circumstances because I'm in this to get the best deal for Britain, always," he said.

"But what's in it for us is the possibility of lower food prices and I can't turn my back on that, lower costs for business, cutting red tape, and a really big win potentially of more collaboration on immigration - a Channel action plan - which is a really big deal if we can land that tomorrow.

"So I hope people can understand why I have pushed this because it could be a really good deal for Britain."

Burnham previously said the UK and EU could link up their law enforcement efforts, including sharing more data and criminal records of foreign nationals.

The prime minister's spokesman has said if a deal were secured, the government would introduce legislation to Parliament next week to implement changes.

Te Araroa: more muddy boogeymen

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In order to walk Te Araroa you must accept mud into your heart (and shoes and socks and blisters). You also must never trust roots for they are deceivers and false friends (slippery, so slippery). The dome is no exception to these rules. You will never escape the mud until you have completed the trail.

Dome day dawned and I was NERVOUS. I had been thinking about a zero for about a day and a half before that, but had kept pushing on because I wanted to stay with my group. I also made the biggest mistake of my life, not having a coffee.

The dome began with fields of mud, and our group split up pretty early into the day. It was the kind of terrain that we all prefer to navigate solo, it’s no fun when people are watching you slip and slide up and down a muddy hill, or you feel like you’re holding others up. I was feeling so rough mentally, and my hiker hunger had really started to kick in the night before, so I spent the morning mentally cataloguing all the food I had left and whether it would be enough to get me to the dairy in Puhoi. I was exhausted physically and mentally before the day even really began. But I kept going.

When I told people about walking Te Araroa I talked a lot about wanting to build resilience and the ability to keep going even when I really didn’t want to. Some part of me knew this was the first moment where I could start to learn that. So singing broken clips of songs to myself and sometimes even counting my steps, I trudged on.

I caught up with Sinèad at the end of the first big hill and descent section, and we walked the road section together. I fantasized about going to one of the open houses along the road and asking them for a coffee. I didn’t think they’d let my muddy hiker self in so I kept moving.

When we approached the next big, forested hill section of the day we found a fellow hiker, Xavier, eating half his lunch. I immediately sat down and ate a handful of every one of my accessible snacks. The others took off and I spent a further ten minutes talking myself into continuing. I knew that on the other side of this hill was a campsite where, if I really wanted to, I could stop for the night. I also knew that in less than a kilometre I’d hit the 500km mark.

I felt so sleepy, not just tired but burny-eyes-sleepy. I thought desperately about the single sachet of instant coffee deep in my food bag and sang “just keep swimming” to myself. By the time I was about ¾ through this hill I was once again mentally inventorying my meals, trying to decide if maybe I could break this section into two days.

Then I heard some girls ahead of me and like a puppy seeing its people again after a long work day, I perked up, thinking it was my friends. Unfortunately it was just two very clean-looking, white-sneakered day hikers.

Though they weren’t who I was hoping to see, the whiteness of their sneakers told me that from here, the trail would be more chill. I pushed on and eventually I could hear Sinèad’s accent floating through the trees. And there was our friend, Zoe, getting water from the stream! I crossed the stream and climbed up to the campground where I was delighted to find there was a picnic table so I wouldn’t have to sit on the ground.

I laid out my very wet tent to dry (still thinking about staying there), drank the last of my water, and filtered some into my mayo-turned cold soak jar to shake up my last instant coffee sachet. Sinèad said she watched me essentially reboot as soon as I started drinking the coffee.

It was 1:30 at this point, we’d been walking for six and a half hours, and I didn’t really have a sense of how much further there was to go. I decided to take a long lunch and to eat two wraps, food inventory be damned!

And then my saviours began to arrive. First was another set of day hikers, the older of whom helpfully told me I seemed very tired (I did not hold back when I agreed with him). And then Annabel, who I had been worried about because she normally walks at a similar pace to me and I hadn’t seen her all day.

After some lecturing about my choice to not wear boots, the day hikers gave me a bag of pumpkin seeds, dates and ANZAC biscuits. The snacks were enough to convince me to put my wet, muddy socks back on and walk the final five or six kilometres to the dome cafe.

Annabel and I climbed the third and final hill of the day which was actually suddenly easy terrain, and then descended a really well-graded, touristy track. I honestly felt like I was flying on that terrain and having Annabel around to chat to made it go really quickly. After eleven hours of walking (including the two hour lunch I took) we made it to camp. The dome cafe was an oasis, it had a bathtub and yes I did sit down in it. I pitched my tent and we all took turns having a hot shower and I think that place saved my life. My mashed potatoes have never tasted better. This was a big day of learning for me. Mostly that I can do it even when it’s a tough day.

I hope my work colleagues enjoy seeing my muddy sock water

Anchor me by The Mutton Birds. It should be noted that I did not choose this. It was unceremoniously bestowed upon me by the “here’s a song you didn’t want stuck in your head” gods. But it would be disingenuous to choose anything else because it is what was mostly going through my mind when I wasn’t inventorying my food or dreaming of coffee.

Busan-Selected Director Anoop Lokkur Turns His Own Tenant War Into Bengaluru Dark Comedy ‘Melgade Mane Kelgade Mane’ at India’s WAVES Film Bazaar

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Indian-Australian filmmaker Anoop Lokkur, whose debut “Don’t Tell Mother” premiered at the Busan International Film Festival in 2025, is turning his own standoff with tenants who wouldn’t leave his family home into “Melgade Mane Kelgade Mane” (Upstairs Downstairs), a dark comedy in the Co-Production Market at WAVES Film Bazaar.

The Kannada and English-language film follows Karthik, who returns from Melbourne, along with his wife and young daughter, to look after his ailing father. He expects to take over the top floor of the house in an older Bengaluru neighborhood where his family has lived for three generations. The tenant, government contractor Ramesh, had agreed to move out, then changes his mind. Polite negotiation gives way to a tit-for-tat war as legal routes go nowhere, the power gets cut, property is damaged and access is blocked. Related Stories Hybe Names Melissa Victor-Burkhardt Executive VP of Global Artist Services Former Cineworld CEO Mooky Greidinger Leads Acquisition of Showcase Cinemas in U.K., Inks Major Imax Deal

Lokkur says that in 2025 he returned to Bangalore with his wife and their 1-year-old daughter, planning to settle upstairs in his parents’ home, but the tenants stayed on after their lease ran out and the family ended up crammed into his parents’ part of the house. “The whole situation was frustrating and unpleasant. What followed were months of tension, passive aggression and even thinking of ways to get them to vacate. The irony, though, was that only a couple of years earlier, my wife and I had been forced out of a rented home ourselves.”

Having been on both sides of the door, he says, he grew curious about ownership, about what it means to call a place home and about how hard people will push to come out on top. “I realized there was a dark comedy in this situation that was universal, and the best way to deal with my frustration was to turn it into a film.”

The project is in development, with Lokkur and Mikayla Henke producing through Papunu Films. The budget is $1.3 million, with $156,000 in place, and the team is looking for international co-producers, financiers and sales agents in Goa.

“The film is built like a tennis match. Your sympathies keep switching between the two families, until you’re no longer sure who to cheer for,” Lokkur says. “I’m interested in how ego quietly takes over and how ordinary decent people justify each small act of revenge until they no longer recognize themselves. It’s also a film about Bangalore, a city changing so fast that old neighborhoods and family homes are disappearing.”

Henke says she went through the dispute alongside Lokkur, and that with the family living it every day, it was the obvious choice for their next film. “‘Don’t Tell Mother’ was a tender and very personal film. On the surface, ‘Melgade Mane Kelgade Mane’ (Upstairs Downstairs) might look like the opposite: darker, bolder and funnier. But it comes from the same place, a close eye on family and the things people struggle to say to each other. This time, we just want to take audiences on a much wilder ride.”

“Don’t Tell Mother” played in the A Window on Asian Cinema section at Busan, was nominated for best youth film at the 2026 Asia Pacific Screen Awards and had its Australian premiere at this year’s Sydney Film Festival. Lokkur’s short “Long Distance,” which Henke produced, screened at the Melbourne International Film Festival and Palm Springs International ShortFest before Canal+ acquired it. Henke was also the first producer on “Don’t Tell Mother.”

“While it’s set in Bengaluru, it’s a universal story about housing that people everywhere can relate to and we believe it can travel well beyond India,” the producers say.

WAVES Film Bazaar runs as the market component of the International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa.

Christie’s tepid 21st-century sale caps week of blockbuster auctions in New York

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Christie's auctioneer Yü-Ge Wang sells the top lot of the sale auction house's 19 November auction in New York, Jean-Michel Basquiat’s Untitled (Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads) (1983) Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Wrapping up this autumn’s week of marquee evening sales in New York, Christie’s 21st-century auction on Thursday (19 November) proved rather lacklustre, bringing in $117.7m ($145.9m with fees). That tally fell short of Christie’s pre-sale expectations, which do not account for fees, and projected a total take in the range of $126m to $192m. It was also a lower result compared to the auction house’s last two equivalent sales in New York—namely the $132m ($162.6m with fees) generated across 42 lots last May, and the $99.5m ($123.5m with fees) brought by 45 lots one year ago.

Just five of the 56 lots offered Thursday night failed to sell, for a slim buy-in rate of 9%. Two lots were withdrawn at the last moment, including a Jeff Koons sculpture estimated to bring between $3m and $5m. Twenty-four lots were backed by third-party guarantees and nine were backed fully by house guarantees, representing around 59% of all the works offered. Auction records for three artists were broken over the course of the night.

Roy Lichtenstein, Small Wall Explosion, 1965 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Business began with a bang at Christie’s Rockefeller Center salesroom: the first lot was Roy Lichtenstein’s Small Wall Explosion (1965), executed in porcelain enamel on steel and perforated steel relief to mimic the Ben-Day dot style from his paintings. It sold to a telephone bidder for $625,000 ($800,000 with fees). From an edition of six, it hailed from the collection of Antonio Homem Sonnabend, the adopted son of the late and legendary dealer and collector Ileana Sonnabend (1914-2007) and who ran his mother’s namesake gallery in its waning years. Antonio Homem died earlier this year, at age 86.

Another of the 19 Sonnabend lots from Sonnabend, Ashley Bickerton’s expansive wall relief Commercial Piece #3 (1990), covered with gallery and fashion-house logos and comprised of anodised aluminium, wood, leather, acrylic and rubber, sold for $120,000 ($153,600 with fees). Both the Lichtenstein and the Bickerton were backed by third-party guarantees.

Andy Warhol, Campbell's Soup Can (Turkey Noodle), 1962 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Price points for the Sonnabend trove zoomed higher with Andy Warhol’s iconicand hand painted Campbell’s Soup Can (Turkey Noodle) (1962), which sold for $7.3m ($9m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. Another Lichtenstein, the painting Large Spool (1963)—a razor-sharp depiction of the titular object that has been featured in multiple gallery and museum exhibitions—squeaked by on the tails of a third-party guarantee at $7m ($8.6m with fees) against an ambitious estimate of $10m to $15m.

Still in the Sonnabend lots, Jeff Koons’s toy-like Hulk (Friends) (2004-12), in polychromed bronze and standing nearly 6ft tall, found a new home for $3.3m ($4.1m with fees). And another Warhol, redolent with Hollywood celebrity, The Kiss (Bela Lugosi) (1963) in silkscreen ink on white paper, sold to another telephone bidder for $3.3m ($4.6m with fees).

A petite Jasper Johns oil painting on cardboard, Numbers (1957), measuring just 8.5in by 6in and rich in shades of grey, made $2.6m ($3.2m with fees). It was featured in the artist’s first solo exhibition at the Leo Castelli Gallery in 1958. Ileana Sonnabend acquired it from the Castelli Gallery in 1959, the same year of her divorce from Leo Castelli.

The Sonnabend group of 19 lots, including three examples of important 20th-century French decorative art—among them a Jean Prouvé Trapéze table from around 1956 that hit $1.3m ($1.6m with fees)—all in tallied $33.3m ($41.9m with fees), compared to Christie’s pre-sale expectations for the group of $35m to $55m.

Illustrious consignor names continued with works from the collection of the late Museum of Modern Art trustee and patron Agnes Gund sold to benefit the Gund Art Foundation. They included Robert Ryman’s immaculate, small, square all-over white composition Untitled (1961), which brought $2.9m ($3.6m with fees). Vija Celmins’s photorealist oil painting Untitled (Ocean) (1990), which featured in the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art’s traveling retrospective in 2018-2020, went for $5.7m ($7.1m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. It also was backed by a third-party guarantee.

Vija Celmins, Untitled (Ocean), 1990 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Gund acquired Alexander Calder’s hanging metal mobile The Eye in the Black (1963) from the Perls Galleries in New York in in 1967. On Thursday night it went to an online bidder for $2.8m ($3.5m with fees). Cindy Sherman’s Untitled Film Still #5 (1977), also widely exhibited and written about by major critics, sold for $320,000 ($409,6000 with fees). Gund had acquired it at Sotheby’s New York in November 2000 for a hammer price of $75,000.

Gund’s contemporary taste bordered on the encyclopaedic, as evidenced by an early Robert Rauschenberg work, Untitled (Gold Painting) (1953), in gold leaf on fabric and paper collage on canvas. The diminutive work, at 8in by 8in, fetched a sizeable sum, $1.7m ($2.1m with fees). Gund acquired it at Sotheby’s in New York in May 1994 for a $55,000 hammer price.

A modestly scaled Lichtenstein work on paper in coloured pencil, graphite and paper collage, Artist’s Studio with Model (Study) (1974), sold to the art adviser Ralph DeLuca for $950,000 ($1.2m with fees).

Jim Nutt, Loom, 1999 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

A standout from the Gund trove, a work she described as “my Mona Lisa”, was another modestly-scaled work, by Jim Nutt, founder of Chicago’s Hairy Who group. Titled Loom (1999) and measuring 15in by 14in, the portrait of a woman with a penetrating gaze set a new auction record for Nutt’s work when it more than doubled its high estimate of $350,000 and ultimately sold for $850,000 ($1m with fees). The ten lots sold to benefit Gund’s charitable foundation tallied $20.3m ($25.7m with fees), squarely within Christie’s pre-sale estimate of $16m to $24m.

Though not part of the Gund Art Foundation offerings, Alice Neel’s double portrait David and Catherine Saalfield (1982) hailed from Agnes Gund’s family and brought $1.6m ($2m with fees). Gund acquired it directly from the artist the year it was painted—not surprising since the sitters were her young adult children.

Alice Neel, David and Catherine Saalfield, 1982 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

The appeal of buying a piece of the legacy of storied dealers and collectors did not fare so well for a work that had belonged to the late gallerist Marian Goodman. Bidding on Gerhard Richter’s Abstraktes Bild (1990) stalled at $5.7m, against an estimate of $6m to $8m, and the lot failed to sell. As Richter’s longtime New York dealer, Goodman acquired the lushly surfaced squeegee painting directly from the artist and exhibited the painting in a gallery group show the year it was made. (A second Richter from another consignor fared better, with that somewhat larger Abstraktes Bild from 1991 bringing $7.6m, or $9.4m with fees.)

Among the works from more anonymous consignors, Richard Prince’s famous Nurse series made a splash when Everglade Nurse (2003), executed in acrylic and inkjet print on canvas, sold for $3.1m ($3.9m with fees). It last sold at Sotheby’s New York in November 2008 for $3.4m with fees, a rather flat return after 18 years.

Large-scale paintings destined to test living room wall space were also in evidence with Christopher Wool’s untitled black-and-white abstraction from 2009, measuring more than 8ft tall. It brought $2m ($2.5m with fees). George Condo’s more than 9ft-wide Double Heads in Blue and Silver (2014), realised $2.4m ($3m with fees). The Condo hailed from the collection of Stefan Edlis and his wife Gael Neeson, who donated 44 works cumulatively valued at more than $400m to the Art Institute of Chicago in 2015.

The top lot of the evening, unsurprisingly, was Jean-Michel Basquiat’s quirky and text-rich composition, Untitled (Leonardo and his Five Grotesque Heads) (1983), which hammered to another anonymous telephone bidder for $28m ($32.8m with fees) against a sky-high estimate of $30m to $50m.

Jean-Michel Basquiat, Untitled (Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads), 1983 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Evidently inspired by Leonardo da Vinci’s 1493 pen-and-ink drawing on paper A man tricked by Gypsies (also known as Five grotesque heads), from the Royal Collection Trust at Windsor Castle in London, Basquiat’s freestyle interpretation in oil stick and acrylic against a burnt orange background crackles with the artist’s terse way with wordplay and actually features eight heads in all.

Measuring 80in by 80in, the painting has no exhibition history, at least none acknowledged in Christie’s online catalogue entry—though it was included in the Whitney Museum of American Art’s 1992 Basquiat retrospective. The catalogue entry for the Whitney exhibition listed the owner as Zurich’s Bruno Bischofberger, an early and important champion of the artist. It last sold at auction at Sotheby’s London in October 2005, when it was the top lot at £736,000 ($1.3m) including fees against an estimate of £400,000 to £600,000. Sotheby’s at the time identified the buyer as “US Trade” and the current consignor acquired it from that entity in 2006.

Julia Jo, One Fell Swoop, 2026 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

The evening ended on a sweet note with Julia Jo’s large-scale abstraction One Fell Swoop (2026), donated to benefit the New York-based Coalition for the Homeless. It set a new auction record for Jo’s work, fetching $320,000 ($409,600 with fees). (The night’s other new auction record was for the work of the Spanish artist Antonio Santín.)

Apart from that feeling, the outlook was not as rosy. “It’s been a very long week, with a lot of art, and I think people are tired,” DeLuca said. “The theme of the week is that estimates are a little aspirational.”

The long week’s last burst of activity will be a pair of day sales at Christie’s on Friday (20 November), after which the US art industry will mark a pause to observe Thanksgiving before migrating south for Art Basel Miami Beach.

6 Funniest Far Side Comics Starring the Devil

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Gary Larson’s loved sending people to Hell

In the 1980s and 1990s, nothing in the funny pages was quite as dark as The Far Side. Lucy van Pelt pulling the football away from Charlie Brown as he was about to kick it is about as dark as things got in Peanuts. And in Garfield, the lazy cat’s darkest running gag also revolved around a kick — when he’d kick Odie the dog off a table.

Meanwhile, The Far Side featured jokes about hangings and murder — death was a regular punchline. Larson also liked to make light of whatever came after death. Occasionally he’d make a joke about Heaven, but he clearly found Hell far more amusing. Many Far Side comics take place in the underworld — or some specific version of it — with Satan a recurring figure in his comic strips. Few characters in The Far Side appeared more than once, but the Devil himself made many appearances.

In this comic from June 11, 1988, a pizza gets delivered to Hell and Satan is clearly unhappy about it. “First of all, this is going straight back — and I’ll just have a little chat with whoever placed the order,” says Satan. The best little detail in the comic strip is the slogan on the pizza box. It says “Jerry’s Pizza: We Deliver Anywhere” and Jerry clearly lives up to her word.

I can understand the Devil’s annoyance. Pizza is one of the greatest pleasures on Earth, which means that pizza is absolutely disqualified from being served in Hell, even though there’s a perfect-looking pizza oven literally everywhere you look.

A recurring source of humor in Gary Larson’s comic strip was to take a common phrase or idiom and interpret it as literally as possible. In one comic from 1992, he picked apart the common phrase “ride off into the sunset” by depicting a burnt-up cowboy that had literally ridden into the sun. In another comic from 1985, he played on the phrase “dressed to kill” by showing two sharks in fancy evening attire.

In this comic from 1982, Larson dissected the phrase “To Hell and back” by turning Hell into a mere tourist destination. In it, a man is showing off slides from his vacation, which include a picture of his wife standing with the Devil. Especially fun here is how happy the Devil is, while the man’s wife looks bored by the encounter.

While Hell showed up in several Far Side comics, it wasn’t always the same Hell, or at least it didn’t appear to be. Several comic strips depicted very specific, different forms of Hell, each of which was specifically designed to torture a particular resident of the underworld. For example, this comic from 1990 has the caption “Bowler’s Hell.” It shows the bowler having made an impossible-to-hit split, with the Devil saying “Whoa! Another split?... What a bummer!” Clearly, endless splits like this would be the worst possible punishment for a bowler.

If a bowler’s version of Hell is endless splits, a classical conductor's version of Hell would have to be an orchestra filled only with banjos. Larson seemingly found banjos to be obnoxious instruments as a number of his comic strips feature them in a less-than-flattering light. In another, more Earthly Far Side comic, a different conductor is horrified by the sound of a banjo in his orchestra as he shouts “Stop! Stop! What’s that sound? What’s that sound?”

Still, it seems that, for Gary Larson’s musical tastes, banjos rank higher than accordions, which received even harsher treatment.

This Far Side comic contrasts Heaven and Hell. In Heaven, you receive a harp when you die, but in Hell, the instrument given out to all new inhabitants is an accordion. I seriously wonder if any accordion or banjo players were ever offended by Gary Larson’s repeated attacks on their instruments.

In yet another specific version of Hell, dogs who have been sentenced to eternal damnation are forced to work as their arch-nemesis: mailmen. Originally, I’d assumed that this comic strip was a parody of the movie All Dogs Go to Heaven, a truly beautiful animated film directed by Don Bluth. But the years don’t add up as this comic strip comes from 1986 while All Dogs Go to Heaven is from 1989.

And while I’m not the kind of person to get into a theological debate between any two parties, I think Don Bluth is right about where dogs go in the afterlife, whereas Larson is totally wrong about any dog going to hell.
