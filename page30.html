Apple’s head of camera and Photos software has retired, per report

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

According to a new report from Bloomberg, Apple’s head of camera and photo software retired last month. Jon McCormack spent eight years at Apple and “oversaw the Camera and Photos apps and the underlying technology that determines how users capture, view, and edit their pictures and video.”

Mark Gurman reports that Apple hasn’t named a new vice president to replace McCormack, who reported to software chief Craig Federighi. “Instead, his former deputies are assuming his responsibilities under other executives,” the report says.

In his role, McCormack oversaw “close coordination between software engineers and Apple’s camera hardware and chip teams.” He’s credited with last year’s redesigned selfie camera and other projects:

His work included the redesigned selfie camera introduced with the iPhone 17 lineup last year. This year, Apple added a variable-aperture camera to the iPhone 18 Pro lineup, giving users more control over lighting and depth of field. The company also introduced a customizable Camera app interface with professional controls.

Bloomberg describes McCormack as a “strong opponent of using generative AI to create or alter photographs,” a philosophy that created tension as Apple embraced AI tools in the Photos app. His retirement, however, “wasn’t related to a disagreement or clash with Apple over its AI strategy,” the report says.

McCormack spoke in depth about iOS 27 features like Clean Up, Extend, and Spatial Reframing in an interview with Tyler Stalman in June. He emphasized how the features “preserve the integrity of the moment.”

New Homes Offer More Affordability for Buyers: Report

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With affordability remaining an issue at large in housing, new homes continue to represent a tempting alternative in a challenging market.

The latest data from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) found that affordability for new homes actually improved more than that of existing homes in Q3, focused on both home prices and rates.

The Q3 NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index (CHI) found that families earning the national median income of $106,800 needed 33% of their income to buy a median-priced new home, compared to 36% of their income for a median-priced existing home.

Low-income families (those who earn only 50% of the national median income) need 66% of their income to buy a new home, compared to 73% for an existing home.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens noted that this was the “widest affordability advantage (for new homes) since the CHI began,” as builders “are working hard to offset rising costs.”

The report also noted that this is due in part to the difference in price between new homes ($400,400 median price) and existing homes ($416,700 median price), called out as the widest national median price gap since the CHI began.

The other attribution for the new home “affordability advantage” is the incentives and deals offered by builders.

Owens called out NAHB’s recent data on builder confidence, which saw 43% of builders offer price cuts in October—the highest share since 2020. Additionally, 65% of builders offered sales incentives.

A recent Realtor.com® report also found that many buyers purchasing new homes are seeing lower mortgage rates due to builders offering buy downs or other deals with mortgage lenders. The average starting mortgage rate of a new-home buyer was 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers.

While new homes are offering better affordability than existing homes, overall housing affordability is still very much challenged.

For both new and existing homes, families earning the national median income remain fairly cost-burdened (paying more than 30% of their income for housing) or severely cost-burdened (paying more than 50% of their income for housing).

Of the 175 metro areas tracked in the report, 87 are cost-burdened by housing, and eight are severely cost-burdened. Leading the way among the eight severely cost-burdened markets is San Jose, California, where families use 79% of their income on housing.

Owens outlined that in order to move housing affordability in the right direction, “policymakers must do more to reduce red tape, address labor shortages and fix supply chains so builders can expand the nation’s housing supply.”

“As builders contend with rising construction costs, tighter lending standards and persistent labor shortages, policymakers should remove regulatory obstacles, enact permitting reform and create a stronger business environment that enables builders to produce more attainable, affordable homes,” added NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

Newborn stars are hiding in the red threads of a neighboring galaxy

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Webb peers into N159, where dusty filaments shelter some of the youngest stars in a neighboring galaxy.

Today’s Image of the Day takes us inside a cloud where stars are being born in a neighboring galaxy.

In this view from the James Webb Space Telescope, pale blue gas fills broad cavities, orange pillars jut from their edges, and dark red threads wind across the middle.

Some of the youngest stars in the scene are still tucked inside those threads.

The cloud, known as N159, lies about 161,000 light-years away in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

Webb’s image spans about 230 light-years. Across that distance, some stars are still gathering the material they need to grow, while others already shine fiercely enough to change the shape of the cloud around them.

Start with the pale blue areas. They trace hydrogen gas exposed to intense ultraviolet light from massive stars.

That radiation can knock electrons loose from hydrogen atoms. As the electrons rejoin hydrogen nuclei, the gas gives off light of its own.

A bright patch can therefore reveal where massive stars are affecting their surroundings, even if the glow itself comes from the gas.

Those stars also send streams of particles into the cloud. Over time, these stellar winds can help hollow out cavities and sculpt the denser material along their edges.

Look closely at where the blue meets the orange. Small pillars extend into the open spaces, marking places where pockets of dense gas and dust have persisted as material around them has been cleared away.

The bright knot on the left is the Papillon Nebula, an unusually compact pocket of glowing hydrogen that the Hubble Space Telescope first resolved in 1999.

In Webb’s wider view, it takes up only a small part of the frame. Its intense glow draws the eye, but the quieter areas around it hold some of the best clues to where stars are forming now.

One of the best places to zoom in is just above the blue cavity on the right. Thin red strands cross this part of the frame.

They trace molecular hydrogen, a form of the gas in which two hydrogen atoms are bound together.

Deep inside dense strands like these, gas and dust can shield developing stars from the radiation and winds of their more powerful neighbors.

Webb’s Near-Infrared Camera records light at wavelengths beyond what our eyes can see.

Some of that light gets through dust that blocks visible starlight, revealing points of light buried in the cloud.

Image processors assign visible colors to observations made through different filters.

Here, those colors distinguish the red threads of molecular gas from the orange glow of carbon-based molecules and the pale blue of ionized hydrogen.

Astronomer Nolan Habel and colleagues compared the brightness of more than 300,000 points of light across ten infrared filters.

Some gave off more infrared light than expected from a star alone. That extra light can come from surrounding dust, which absorbs energy from the star and radiates it back into space.

Many of the sources the researchers identified as possible young stars lined up with dusty strands and clouds of molecular gas.

In a preprint describing the observations, the team reported that the eastern part of N159 contains more developed groups of stars, while the western part shows signs of more recent star formation.

Some groups may be less than half a million years old. That difference suggests that star formation unfolded in stages across the cloud.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has a smaller proportion of elements heavier than helium than the Milky Way does.

The early universe was also poorer in those elements, which built up over generations of stars.

Studying a region like N159 gives astronomers a chance to see how stars form with a mix of ingredients closer in some respects to what was available long ago.

The image also captures a change that is easy to miss at first glance. In one part of the cloud, stars have already opened clearings around themselves.

In another, dense gas still shelters stars that are just getting started. Given time, much of that gas will disperse, leaving the stars exposed.

For now, the thin red strands mark places where some of the newest stars in this corner of a neighboring galaxy are still taking shape.
