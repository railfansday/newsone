NFL Panic Meter: While other NFC contenders loaded up at deadline, Rams and Lions were quiet

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The theme of the trade deadline seemed to be contenders being more aggressive than ever before in their pursuit of a Super Bowl, particularly in the NFC.

That's what made the inactivity of the Detroit Lions and Los Angeles Rams a bit startling.

The Seattle Seahawks, Chicago Bears and San Francisco 49ers all made trades to help themselves now. The Green Bay Packers did not, but they're getting Micah Parsons back soon and they're relying on that being a big midseason addition. The 49ers did the least of that group, but they're also having the best season in the conference and starting to get a little healthier. And we don't need to count anyone in the NFC East or NFC South as a contender.

The only move the Rams or Lions made was Detroit trading a sixth-round pick to the Jets for safety Andre Cisco, who was a part-time starter for a bad New York team.

Let's start with the Rams, because they had the biggest need and their inactivity goes against their normal mode. The Rams have lost Puka Nacua and Davante Adams to injuries, and don't know if they'll be back before it's too late. They're dreadfully thin at receiver for the next few weeks, at least. The Rams have gone from preseason Super Bowl favorite to probably a slight underdog to even make the playoffs. Yet, they did not make a trade before the deadline. The Rams could argue, like the Packers, that adding Myles Garrett back is their in-season addition. But that doesn't help at receiver, and it's hard to believe the Rams have championship upside building a passing game around three or four tight ends and nothing else. It seems like the Rams put three-quarters of their chips in the middle, then folded.

The Lions probably could have used a cornerback or linebacker nearly as much as the Rams needed a receiver, but they did nothing other than a low-cost move to add Cisco. Detroit's defense probably isn't good enough to win a championship, and their fans will be frustrated after seeing other teams, particularly in the NFC North, make moves. They were clamoring for a cornerback for weeks and Jack Campbell's injury left them with a weakness at linebacker. Those areas of need still exist.

The Rams have a "forget them picks" attitude but it still has a limit. They gave up a lot to get Garrett and Trent McDuffie in the offseason. They might have also privately seen a team that is slipping further from being a Super Bowl contender and didn't want to chase their losses. The Lions didn't want to pay the escalating prices to make a huge move on the back end of their defense, and while that might be prudent for the long haul, Detroit fans don't want to be patient anymore. Maybe the Lions or Rams still make a deep playoff run, but they'll have to do it against teams that got much better over the past couple weeks.

Panic meter: The Rams and Lions are still good, but everyone around them got better.

Sean McVay's Los Angeles Rams did not make a trade before the deadline on Tuesday. (Photo by Patrick Smith/Getty Images)

The biggest secondary domino from the trade deadline moves happened with the Las Vegas Raiders. Trading Kirk Cousins to the Minnesota Vikings kicked off the Mendoza era. The No. 1 overall pick will be the team's starter going forward.

The timing isn't perfect. The next two games for the Raiders are against the Seahawks and Broncos, two of the toughest defenses in the NFL. The Broncos game is at Denver, which is a tough place to play.

It could be rough for Mendoza early on. The Raiders had to make the move when the Vikings offered a third-round pick for a 38-year-old quarterback who was probably starting only two more games for them anyway. Two games won't ruin the rookie but he's in for a welcome to the NFL moment or two.

Panic meter: Mendoza will be fine but expect some early struggles.

Rhamondre Stevenson has an injured tendon in his knee, TreVeyon Henderson has been out with a murky injury and that led people to believe the Patriots could trade for a running back. That didn't happen.

No running backs moved at the trade deadline. The Colts were reportedly asking way too much in return for Jonathan Taylor (more on Indianapolis in a moment) and perhaps the prices of others like Tony Pollard were egregious too. But now the Patriots move forward for a while with Corey Kiner as the lead back. That will put more pressure on Drake Maye, who is coming off a poor game against the Packers. The dream of contending in the AFC East is slipping away. Will the Patriots be a playoff team?

Panic meter: The Patriots should still sneak in the playoffs, but a running back trade would have been nice.

The Colts traded an eight-time Pro Bowl guard, a three-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle. a six-time Pro Bowl receiver and a 25-year-old receiver who has shown promise, and somehow they didn't get a first- or second-round pick back for any of them.

The Colts got a lot of mid-round picks back for Quenton Nelson, DeForest Buckner, Keenan Allen and Josh Downs, but they didn't seem to maximize the value of any of them other than perhaps Allen. Buckner's return of a fourth-round pick seemed quite light given what some other defensive tackles went for. And the Jonathan Taylor situation was just odd.

The Colts reportedly received offers of a second-round pick and more for Taylor and passed. Taylor is a great player but he's a running back on a team with a long rebuild ahead of it. It made little sense to hold onto him. But the Colts, who don't have next year's first-round pick due to the Sauce Gardner trade, don't have much of a plan.

Panic meter: The Colts have a long rebuild ahead and this isn't a good sign it'll go well.

The Bengals can argue that trading the 10th overall pick of the draft for defensive tackle Dexter Lawrence II in the offseason was their big move. But some more might have been nice.

The Bengals are getting an MVP level season from quarterback Joe Burrow, their offensive line is better than it has been in years and the defense has been competent. That defense could be better though. Couldn't they have outbid the Broncos for Chargers linebacker Daiyan Henley, who went for fourth- and fifth-round picks?

The Bengals' ownership has always been ripped for being cheap and maybe that was part of the team making no moves before the deadline. Either way, it's a team that should have Super Bowl hopes and might regret not doing anything to improve itself with another trade.

Panic meter: The Bengals are good but might ultimately find they're a piece or two away.

Your Brain Keeps the Code It Writes. Not the Code It Pastes.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your brain remembers what it makes. It barely notices what it reads.

That's not a productivity quote. It's one of the most reliable findings in memory research, and it has a name: the generation effect. If you write code for a living, and lately you've been letting an assistant write a lot of it, it's worth knowing about.

In 1978, Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf at the University of Toronto ran a set of experiments that sound almost too simple to matter. Half the time, people read a word pair in full. The other half, they got the first word and a fragment of the second, and had to fill in the rest. Think "hot" and "c___", where you supply "cold".

Same words. Same time on the page. The only difference was who produced the answer.

People remembered the words they produced better. Every time. Across five experiments, on recognition tests, free recall, cued recall and even confidence ratings, the "generate" condition beat the "read" condition. The authors noted the effect showed up at high levels of reliability "even in experiments employing only 12 subjects." You can read the original paper here.

They also admitted they didn't fully know why. "It is concluded that the generation effect is real and that it poses an interesting interpretative problem."

A single lab finding can be a fluke. This one wasn't.

In 2007, Sharon Bertsch, Michael McDaniel and colleagues pooled the research into a meta-analysis in Memory & Cognition: 86 studies, 445 effect sizes, more than 17,000 participants. The average benefit of generating over reading was about 0.40 standard deviations, or in their words, "almost half a standard deviation." That's a solid, ordinary-sized effect. Not magic. Not nothing.

A larger review in 2020, led by Matthew McCurdy at the University of Illinois Chicago, went through 310 experiments and found the effect held on every type of memory test they looked at. It also found something useful. The less constrained the generation, the bigger the payoff for recall. Filling in one obvious letter helps a little. Building the answer with more of your own thinking helps more.

There's one catch in the 2007 data that I find the most telling. When the material was nonsense, like made-up nonwords, the generation effect nearly vanished (an effect size of 0.05). Generating only helps when there's something already in your head to connect the new thing to. Your brain isn't rewarding effort for its own sake. It's rewarding you for wiring the new fact into what you already know.

Here's where it gets uncomfortable for developers.

Early this year, Anthropic published a randomized trial (full paper on arXiv) with 52 mostly junior engineers. All of them used Python weekly. None of them knew Trio, an async library. They built two small features with it, then took a quiz on the concepts they'd just used.

One group had an AI assistant in the sidebar that could produce correct code on request. The other group coded by hand.

The AI group averaged 50% on the quiz. The hand-coding group averaged 67%. The time saved by the AI group wasn't statistically significant. The biggest gap was on debugging questions, which is the exact skill you need when the generated code is wrong.

A field experiment in a Turkish high school, published in PNAS in 2025 by Hamsa Bastani and colleagues, found the same shape with nearly a thousand math students. A plain ChatGPT-style tutor boosted practice scores by 48%. When the AI was taken away for the exam, those students scored 17% lower than students who never had it. The researchers saw students using it as a "crutch," asking for answers and copying them.

Neither study is a generation-effect experiment in the strict sense. Different tasks, different tests. But they rhyme with it. When something else produces the answer, you end up in the "read" condition, even if your name is on the commit.

The Anthropic paper has the hopeful part. Using AI didn't automatically mean a low score. The people who did well asked follow-up questions, requested explanations, or asked conceptual questions while writing the code themselves. The ones who handed over the whole task got some speed and lost the learning.

Same in the PNAS study. A version of the tutor with guardrails, which nudged students toward working things out, mostly removed the harm.

So the tool isn't the problem. Who does the generating is.

You don't need a new system. You need small moments where your brain produces the answer before it sees one.

Guess the code first. Before you ask an assistant for a function, write the signature and a rough version, even a wrong one. Then compare. You've turned a read into a generate.

Retype, don't paste, the first time. When you're learning a new API, type the example from the docs by hand once. It's slower. That's the point.

Explain the diff in your own words. After accepting a suggestion, write one line in the commit message about why it works. Not what it does. Why.

Ask the AI to quiz you. Instead of "write this," try "give me a hint, not the answer." It's the guardrail version of the tutor, applied by you.

Close the tab and rebuild it. After reading a good explainer on, say, structured concurrency, close it and write three sentences from memory. Then check what you missed.

Every one of these is just the 1978 experiment again. A word with a gap, and you fill it in.

I build a small app called Recl, mostly because I kept saving good explainers and never thinking about them again. It turns what you save, like a talk, an article or a thread, into a short summary and then brings it back later as a question you have to answer yourself. That "answer it yourself" part is the generation effect doing the work, not the summary. You can see some example saves in the public library.

But you can get most of the benefit with a notebook and a little discomfort. Next time an answer appears instantly, try making it first.

Toyota and Subaru hit the ‘sweet spot’ with affordable electric SUVs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Toyota and Subaru’s new electric SUVs are catching on with buyers, offering various options and prices ranging from about $35,000 to $45,000, or the “sweet spot” of the US market.

The US electric vehicle market rebounded in the third quarter with 262,649 EVs sold, according to Kelley Blue Book estimates, up 6.1% from Q2 and the strongest performance of the year.

Although nearly every automaker has sold fewer EVs so far this year than in the same period in 2025, as buyers rushed to claim the $7,500 tax credit before it expired, a handful of brands are seeing considerably higher demand.

Outside of Rivian, Toyota and Subaru are the only brands that have higher year-over-year EV sales in the US through the third quarter.

Combined, Toyota and Subaru have sold nearly 50,000 EVs through the first nine months of 2026, more than double compared to the same period last year.

Toyota alone has sold nearly 35,000 electric vehicles, up 183.4% YOY, while Subaru sold 14,630, up 46.7%. And that’s not even including the additional 12,676 sold under the Lexus brand, up 137.4% YOY.

While the two Japanese automakers were infamously slow to transition to battery electric vehicles, the timing worked in their favor.

After launching the heavily updated bZ in late 2025, previously called the bZ4X, Toyota’s electric SUV suddenly became one of America’s most popular EVs. Through Q3, it was the fourth best-selling EV in the US, trailing only the Hyundai IONIQ 5, Tesla Model 3, and Tesla Model Y.

Starting at $35,000, the Toyota bZ is one of the most affordable EVs in the US. It also offers up to 314 miles of range in the FWD Plus trim, which starts at $37,980.

Toyota and Subaru now each sell three electric SUVs, which share the same platform and technology.

With prices ranging from roughly $35,000 to about $45,000, Stephanie Valdez Streaty, director of industry insights at Cox Automotive, said (via Bloomberg) Toyota and Subaru’s new EVs are affordable and hit the “sweet spot” of the US car market.

Toyota also sells the smaller, sporty C-HR, starting at $37,080, and the larger, more rugged bZ Woodland, which starts at $45,380.

Subaru, on the other hand, sells the updated Solterra ($38,495, plus the smaller Uncharted ($34,995) and midsize Trailseeker ($39,995).

Karla Fisher, a Marine and retired engineer, told Bloomberg that the Subaru Trailseeker was her “most favorite car of all time.”

While she considered buying an EV before the $7,500 federal credit expired last September, none met her requirements at the time. Fisher wanted at least 7″ of ground clearance, 250 miles or more range, and a price that was similar, or even less than a comparible gas-powered vehicle.

The Subaru Trailseeker offers all of that and starts at under $40,000. Toyota and Subaru’s electric SUVs all start at about $45,000 or less, which is even less than the average new vehicle.

According to the latest Kelley Blue Book figures, the average price for a new vehicle in the US reached a record $51,109 in October, up 2.1% YOY.

Subaru spokesperson Dominick Infante said the affordable EV prices are mainly thanks to its partnership with Toyota. “We do believe there’s an EV future,” Infante added.

Next year, both brands will add a three-row electric SUV with the Toyota Highlander EV and Subaru Getaway.

Patrick Stewart died on Oct. 28, meaning that his performance as Professor Charles Xavier in Avengers: Doomsday will be the last of his long career. Stewart first played the telepathic mutant in Bryan Singer’s X-Men in 2000, leading the superhero team in the fight against Ian McKellen’s Magneto. The two actors had met decades before at the Royal Shakespeare Company, but they became close friends after working together on the X-Men films. The actors reunited for Avengers: Doomsday, and Anthony Mackie said he and his The Falcon and the Winter Soldier co-star Sebastian Stan were so excited about it that they embarrassed themselves.

“The day before [Stewart] passed, I was talking to Sebastian for some reason – I talk to Sebastian Stan a lot – and we were laughing about when we were on set,” Mackie told Polygon during a video interview. “Sir Patrick and Sir Ian come on set, and they hug, and they just stand there and talk in full costume. Despite the awkward first impression, Mackie said Stewart was great to work with.

“He was so kind, and he was just so easy to be around,” Mackie said. “He was very personable. At that level, at that stage in his career, I just didn't know what to expect. I was very happy and very honored that we had the opportunity to work with him.”

Mackie has been part of the Marvel Cinematic Universe since 2014, playing Sam Wilson as partner to Chris Evans’ Captain America. Wilson then took on the mantle himself after Avengers: Endgame, officially donning the Captain America suit in The Falcon and the Winter Soldier. McKellen played one of Marvel’s most iconic villains, but Mackie discovered on set that he doesn’t keep up with the franchise.

Pompliano Says Bitcoin Spot ETFs Are Safer and More Convenient Than Hardware Wallets for Bitcoiners Holding Over $10,000

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pompliano’s comments on Bitcoin hardware wallets and Bitcoin ETFs come at a time when more people show interest in BTC amid security threats.

Bitcoin expert Anthony Pompliano today tackled a crucial question that serious Bitcoin investors often ask. The question is: between hardware wallets and ETFs, which one offers the best outcome for securing and getting exposure to Bitcoin?

In an interview released early today, the celebrated crypto analyst answered questions savvy investors want answered about Bitcoin self-custody versus Bitcoin ETFs.

Today, Bitcoin adoption grows at a remarkable rate. As more investors seek to invest in the cryptocurrency, there are two major ways to do so: gaining exposure through Bitcoin hardware wallets (self-custody) or Bitcoin ETFs.

In the podcast, Pompliano admitted that using spot ETFs is more secure than storing Bitcoin in a hardware wallet. According to the crypto expert, spot ETFs offer an accessible approach, providing investors with a simplified, regulated way to buy and invest in Bitcoin through their existing brokerage accounts. As Pompliano stated, ETFs stand out because they emphasize the importance of regulation in providing investor protection and market integrity.

Pompliano further recognized spot ETFs as the safest and the most convenient option for investors holding between $10,000 and $10 million worth of BTC. He said these investment vehicles offer greater liquidity than directly holding BTC on hardware wallets. They provide investors with diversified exposure to stock exchanges and on-chain markets.

However, he said convenience comes with caveats, as ETFs provide indirect exposure to Bitcoin. This means that investors don’t actually own the underlying Bitcoin. Also, ETFs might not provide investors with the same level of control as directly owning BTC in self-custody wallets.

This highlights the advantages of Bitcoin hardware wallets, which allow customers to hold BTC directly, giving them greater control. However, this level of control comes with the responsibility of managing private keys, asset control, and security risks.

The debate between self-custody and ETFs comes at a time when the crypto industry continues to experience increasing security breaches, with the latest and largest one being the Binance infiltration.

Such unfortunate hacking events compel customers to rethink how to secure their BTC while still accessing strong liquidity. The self-custody approach offers direct ownership and control of BTC, though it requires technical know-how and involves greater security risks. On the other hand, ETFs offer a simple, regulated way to gain exposure to the asset, with diversified liquidity. They, however, come with some limitations.

Report: Giants called Falcons about a potential Bijan Robinson trade

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Giants didn’t make any big deals at the trade deadline, but they tried to make a very big one.

The Giants called the Falcons to see whether running back Bijan Robinson was available in a trade, according to Jordan Raanan of ESPN.

That would have been a blockbuster, but it’s unsurprising that the teams didn’t make a deal. The Falcons made a big investment in Robinson this offseason, signing him to a three-year, $66.8 million contract extension, and from all accounts they view him as one of the most important pieces of their franchise.

There’s no word on what the Giants were willing to offer, or whether the Falcons engaged at all, or simply told the Giants, “Not interested.”

The Giants did make one big trade this season, acquiring J.J. McCarthy, who has played well enough for the Giants to win their first two starts and remain in first place in the NFC East. Acquiring Robinson would have been a strong sign that the Giants believe they’re serious contenders, this year. That the Giants even tried showed that coach John Harbaugh meant it when he said they’re a team trying to win now, not build for the future.

‘Train to Busan’ Director Yeon Sang-ho on AI Thriller ‘Paradise Lost’: ‘Something as Mysterious and Unknowable to Us as a Black Hole’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After taking 'Colony' to Cannes earlier this year, the Korean filmmaker discusses AI, grief and family, and why a tight budget and a short shoot sharpened his latest film.

In Musan City, a decaying small town that exists only on screen, a grieving mother puts on a pair of AR glasses and spends time with her 10-year-old son. He has been gone for nine years, one of the children on a camp bus that vanished. Then the real boy comes home, grown up and a stranger, and the comfort the glasses offered starts to turn into something far more frightening.

That is the setup of “Paradise Lost,” the new psychological thriller from Yeon Sang-ho, the director of “Train to Busan” and the series “Hellbound.” It sits at the opposite end of the scale from “Colony,” which Yeon premiered in the Midnight Screenings section at Cannes earlier this year. “Paradise Lost” went on to Toronto’s Special Presentations section, where Yeon had also taken last year’s micro-budget “The Ugly,” and then screened as a Gala Presentation in Busan. Screenings sold out at both festivals. Related Stories Tom Hanks Returning to Broadway After 14 Years for New Play 'This World of Tomorrow' 'You've Got Mail' Reboot in the Works at Warner Bros.

Kim Hyun-joo, who worked with Yeon on both seasons of “Hellbound” and on “Jung_E,” plays the mother, Ryu So-young. Bae Hyeon-seong (“Hospital Playlist”) takes his first big-screen lead as Sun-woo, the son who returns. Yeon wrote the screenplay with Ryu Yong-jae, drawing on an original idea of theirs that author Oh Seong-eun turned into the novel “Black Inferno.” Wowpoint produced, and CJ ENM, which presents the film with Wowpoint, handles distribution. The film has sold to 118 countries, with Well Go USA handling North America, where it released Nov. 6.

“On one level, AI is something mechanical or technological. But when you look more closely, we can see what is beneath it. I think it is ultimately what we share as human knowledge and also collective intelligence,” Yeon tells Variety.

As he argued around “Colony,” that pooled knowledge is now beginning to threaten our individuality, he says, the very qualities that make each of us human. “The relationship between a mother and her son is perhaps one of the most deeply personal and intimate relationships we can have,” Yeon continues. “We can never truly know what lies in the depth of another person’s psyche.”

The film borrows its title from Milton’s epic poem. “Just like the story where the first of mankind are expelled from paradise, I thought that the loss of paradise is precisely where the human identity begins,” Yeon said in the film’s production notes.

He has used AI as a storytelling device before. In 2023’s “Jung_E,” an AI recreation allowed a daughter to free her mother from an idealized, self-sacrificing image, something he says would not be possible in the real world. “With ‘Paradise Lost,’ I wanted to take a much more grounded approach, using AI as a point of contrast, I would say, to explore something that is uniquely human,” he says.

AI may be built on humanity’s shared patterns, he adds, but it can also behave in very unpredictable ways, beyond our ability to fully control or comprehend. “In that sense, I think AI itself represents an uncharted territory. Something as mysterious and unknowable to us as a black hole.”

Where “The Ugly” followed a son digging into his father’s buried past, “Paradise Lost” swaps in a mother and son.

“I think the family is the very first form of society that we encounter as human beings,” Yeon says. “And because the family is our first experience of society, it plays a fundamental role in shaping much of our sense of self and also our personal identity.”

“The Ugly” was shot for KRW200 million ($150,000). “Paradise Lost” was also made lean, for KRW500 million ($374,000) over just 14 shooting sessions. Yeon leaned on Kim, whom he knew understood his directing style extremely well after several projects together. The schedule meant few takes and limited locations.

“But when you think about it, many of the most legendary films in Asian cinema were made under similar constraints,” Yeon says. “In a way I found that those limitations allowed me, and allowed us, to explore the material more creatively, and they also demanded an extraordinary level of focus and concentration from everyone involved.”

Korea’s theatrical industry is going through a major transformation, Yeon says, and some feel the market has shrunk considerably. “But I don’t necessarily see that as an obstacle to making good films. A smaller industry doesn’t automatically mean fewer great films will be made,” he says. Making “Colony” and “Paradise Lost” in the same year gave him firsthand experience of two radically different scales, which he hopes will help him lead productions of any size with greater confidence.

International sales covered the film’s net production budget ahead of release. Among Korean distributors, CJ ENM is one of the most active in working with overseas partners, Yeon says, and that experience counts. “Whether a Korean film is a box office success or not, whether it is a mainstream commercial production or something with a more niche sensibility, I believe there are audiences somewhere in the world who will appreciate it,” he says.

Making a film available everywhere is not enough on its own, he adds. “Global streaming platforms already release films simultaneously across multiple territories, using algorithms to recommend content based on individual preferences. But I don’t think we have necessarily established just how effective that approach is at helping films find their audiences.”

Yeon has two projects in post-production. The first is a Japanese-language Netflix series, based on the manga “Parasyte: Tamiya,” on which he serves as screenwriter and showrunner. It moves one of the original’s villains, Tamiya Ryoko, to the center of the story. “What I found particularly fascinating is the way Tamiya, who is a parasite rather than a human being, gradually tries to understand humanity,” he says.

The second is “Devotion,” a horror feature due next year. It grew out of the way people now put versions of their identities on display and hide behind the personas they build. Its Korean title, “Yeto,” is a Buddhist term for an imperfect world marked by suffering, the opposite of the realm of ultimate bliss.

For a filmmaker who thinks being human starts with the loss of paradise, it is a fitting next stop.

Maybelline updates ‘Maybe’ tagline as beauty perceptions evolve

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Miley Cyrus is leading a new global brand platform promoting changes meant to be more inclusive and empowering for women.

Maybelline is reworking one of its signature brand assets as it tries to adapt to an evolving landscape for beauty and cosmetics. The L'Oréal-owned marketer’s “Maybe she’s born with it” tagline became a fixture of its advertising beginning in 1991, but consumers in 2026 are applying makeup for different reasons.

The company has attempted to modernize “Maybe” in the past. It was at one point dropped for the largely forgotten “Make It Happen” but eventually brought back. Maybelline revamped the “Maybe” tagline for the TikTok era two years ago. The latest overhaul appears more substantial, changing the underlying meaning of the copy, and is described in the release as permanent.

Over half of surveyed global respondents today see beauty as a way to build confidence compared to 26% who see it as a means of looking more attractive to other people or appearing younger for longer, per McKinsey findings cited by Maybelline. The suggestion is that makeup can be something less superficial and more empowering and inclusive.

Tweaks to the marketer’s tagline try to recognize these trends by adjusting the phrasing to sound less like a question being asked about a single woman’s look. Instead, the new “Maybe” is positioned as a declaration of the possibilities all women can achieve by leveraging makeup as a form of self-expression.

“With 'Maybe.', we're building on our legacy and inviting women everywhere, at every stage of life, to explore the possibilities within them,” said Maybelline Global Brand President Sandrine Jolly in a statement.

Maybelline enlisted Cyrus, an ambassador who’s previously voiced the “Maybe” tagline, to lead its new “The Beauty of Infinite Possibility” platform. Cyrus was selected for the effort because her own career has entailed a constantly shifting identity, moving from child star to hard-partying club song purveyor and then back to her country roots.

Cyrus released her latest album, “Bass Persuades,” in September, which could draw additional interest to the Maybelline partnership. “Bass Persuades” sees Cyrus again dabbling in the dance pop and electronic music genres.

Products featured in the campaign include Maybelline’s Serum Lipstick, Lash Sensational Sky High Mascara and Instant Eraser Concealer. Maybelline will partner with additional women in the worlds of music and elsewhere to share their stories of using makeup as a form of expression and reinvention.

Consumer products, the division of L'Oréal that Maybelline sits under, grew 4.3% on a like-for-like basis in Q3, lagging other segments like dermatological beauty and professional products.
