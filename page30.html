This is my review of the most popular mid-range Marriott credit card in that portfolio, the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless® Credit Card. It’s a card that many Marriott guests will likely want to have, if eligible.

Let’s dive into the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card’s details to see whether it’s worth it and who it makes the most sense for.

The Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card has a $95 annual fee. You can add authorized users at no extra cost. That’s an incredibly reasonable annual fee, given the amount of annual fee inflation we’ve seen over the years on other cards.

The Bonvoy Boundless Card has no foreign transaction fees, so it’s an excellent card for purchases abroad. That means you can earn rewards on your foreign spending, without having to pay any fees that will offset those rewards. In particular, it’s a good card to use for foreign Marriott spending.

While I wouldn’t recommend using the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card for your everyday spending, there are potentially some situations where it could make sense. Let’s go over the rewards structure of the card, including the bonus categories and other incentives to spend.

The Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card offers 6x Bonvoy points for spending at Marriott-family hotels globally. For many people, this will be a worthwhile bonus.

Since I value Bonvoy points at 0.7 cents each, that’s the equivalent of a 4.2% return on hotel spending. While there are other great cards for hotel spending, some people will want to use this as their card for Marriott spending.

Keep in mind this is on top of the standard points that you can earn for Marriott Bonvoy hotel stays. At most brands, Bonvoy members earn 10x points per dollar spent, and that’s before elite bonuses, which range from 10-75%. So as a Bonvoy Silver member paying with the card (the minimum status you get with this card), you’re looking at earning 17x points per dollar spent at most brands, minimum.

The Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card offers 3x Bonvoy points on dining, gas station, and grocery store purchases globally. This only applies to the first $6,000 spent on combined purchases in those categories annually, and after that, you’ll earn 2x Bonvoy points on those purchases.

Since I value Bonvoy points at 0.7 cents each, the 3x points is like the equivalent of a 2.1% return on eligible gas, grocery, and dining spending. While there are still more rewarding cards for spending in the categories of dining, gas stations, and groceries, this is about as good of a rewards structure as you’ll see on a Bonvoy credit card.

The Bonvoy Boundless Card offers 2x Bonvoy points on all other eligible purchases. I don’t consider 2x points per dollar spent to be especially compelling since I value that at a return of 1.4 cents per dollar of spending, and there are better cards for everyday spending.

How would I approach the value of Marriott points? As mentioned above, based on my valuations, I consider Bonvoy points to be worth 0.7 cents each. I’m not going to suggest that Bonvoy points are my favorite points currency — they’re not — but the reality is that Marriott has a huge global portfolio, and many of the properties are quite decent.

Generally you’ll find that Bonvoy award stays cost anywhere from 10,000 to 200,000 points per night, though some hotels do fall outside of that range. You can maximize value by taking advantage of the fifth night free benefit, which is open to elite members.

This is an extreme example of value, but take Hotel Cala di Volpe, a Marriott Luxury Collection hotel in Sardinia, which is the brand’s most expensive property in summer. I just pulled up a five night redemption (including a fifth night free) for 776,000 Bonvoy points. By my valuation, that’s like $5,432 worth of points.

Meanwhile the cash rate at the hotel is 4,161 EUR ($4,639) per night, so those same five nights would cost you $23,195. Talk about huge savings!

One unique thing about Bonvoy points is they can also fairly efficiently be converted into airline miles. You can transfer your rewards to dozens of different airline programs, and a vast majority allow a 3:1 transfer ratio. On top of that, you get a bonus of 5,000 airline miles for every 60,000 Bonvoy points you transfer. In other words, with most programs you can convert 60,000 Bonvoy points into 25,000 airline miles, which is potentially a decent value.

One of the primary reasons to consider getting the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card is how the card can help you earn elite status in the Bonvoy program. Status starts to get valuable around the Bonvoy Platinum tier, when you become eligible for perks like complimentary breakfast and valuable room upgrades.

One of my favorite perks of having the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card is that you get 15 elite nights toward status annually just for having the card.

Note that several co-branded Marriott credit cards offer this, and you can only receive the benefit once per year on a personal card. In other words, if you have three Marriott personal cards, then you wouldn’t get 45 elite nights.

However, you could earn 15 elite nights from this personal card and 15 elite nights from a business card, so that you’d earn 30 elite nights in a year. The ideal setup would be to have a personal card in conjunction with the Marriott Bonvoy Business® American Express® Card (learn more).

This makes it more attainable to earn Platinum status, which requires 50 elite nights. If you had the Bonvoy Boundless Card, you’d only need 35 more elite nights, and if you also had the business card you’d only need 20 more elite nights.

Note that if you really want to maximize elite nights with personal credit cards, the Marriott Bonvoy Brilliant® American Express® Card (learn more) offers 25 elite nights per year, which is the most elite nights you’ll get with any Marriott credit card.

The Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card offers one elite night for every $5,000 spent on the card. Any elite nights earned this way will be credited to your account within eight weeks of the last day of the calendar month in which you qualify.

Personally, I think spending $5,000 to earn one elite night is a lot, but everyone’s situation will differ, and for some people, this could make sense if you’re otherwise close to earning status. This is the only Marriott credit card that offers bonus elite nights for spending.

Perhaps this is a bit redundant, but just for having the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card you receive Silver elite status in the Bonvoy program. Now, don’t get too excited, as this doesn’t exactly come with that many benefits.

Marriott Silver elite status ordinarily requires 10 elite nights, and comes with perks like 10% bonus points on hotel stays, priority late check-out, and free Wi-Fi. So it’s better than nothing, but that’s about all I can say.

If you have the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card, you can earn Marriott Gold elite status if you spend $35,000 on the card in a calendar year. This status ordinarily requires 25 elite nights. Bonvoy Gold elite status gets you benefits like 25% bonus points on hotel stays, 2PM late check-out, a room upgrade subject to availability, and more.

In addition to the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card spending bonus categories and ability to earn elite status, there are some other major perks to having the card. This is where the card’s annual fee becomes really easy to justify, in my opinion.

The real perk that makes the Marriott Bonvoy Boundless Card worthwhile is that you get a free night award every year on your account anniversary, valid for a one-night hotel stay at a property costing up to 35,000 points.

It’s also possible to top off Marriott free night awards with up to 25,000 additional points to redeem at more expensive properties. This means you could redeem a free night award plus 25,000 points to stay at a hotel costing 60,000 points per night.

This is an exceptional deal, and I think this card is worth having. Marriott is the world’s largest hotel chain, so regardless of your preferred hotel program, just about everyone may find themselves in a situation where this could come in handy.

I consistently redeem this certificate at hotels costing $250+ per night. Based on my valuation of 0.7 cents per point, I’d consider the free night award to be worth around $245, more than double the annual fee… that’s awesome!

10 Best HBO Shows With Less Than 20 Episodes, Ranked

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Since the dawn of television’s golden era in the late ‘90s and early 2000s, HBO has been intrinsically affiliated with the very fabric of small-screen brilliance, with such series as The Sopranos, Game of Thrones, and Succession compelling viewers year after year and season after season. However, it is easy to forget that some of the premium network’s best productions didn’t necessarily need (or get) expansive runs to present their stories.

Ranging from universally acclaimed miniseries that stand among the greatest productions in the history of the medium to criminally underrated titles that were canceled years before their due, each of these TV shows exhibits HBO’s trademark excellence with no more than 20 episodes. Spanning from war zone dramas to post-apocalyptic dystopias, period crime thrillers, and even to the awkwardness and charm of real-world romance, these 10 series highlight both the brilliance and the vastness of HBO.

Abruptly canceled following its second season — falling victim to the merger of WarnerMedia and Discovery, Inc — Raised by Wolves never got the chance to truly thrive the way many wished it would, but the series still holds an enthralling thematic might within its high-concept sci-fi premise. After Earth is decimated by war, two androids are tasked with raising humanity’s children on a foreign planet. They find their assignment growing increasingly complex and treacherous when the colony begins to fray due to religious differences.

Buoyed by the creative involvement of Ridley Scott as both executive producer and occasional director, the series delivers an arresting mixture of bold world-building, unsettling atmospheric minimalism, and thought-provoking storytelling. It aired just 18 episodes across its two-season tenure, and yet it has only grown in relevance since it was axed in 2022 courtesy of its attention to A.I.’s limited view of humanity and the at-time destructive role religion plays in society.

Ever since beginning as a crime novel series penned by Erle Stanley Gardner, Perry Mason has been a cult figure of the genre, appearing in radio shows and television films, and, most notably, a CBS series that ran for nine seasons through the ‘50s and ‘60s. Unfortunately, HBO’s bold re-imaging of said series didn’t fare quite so well, lasting just two seasons and a total of 16 episodes before being canceled by the network in 2023.

Spearheaded by Matthew Rhys’ typically sublime lead performance, it follows the origin story of the famous criminal defense attorney, Perry Mason, focusing on his humble beginnings as a low-fee private eye scouring 1930s L.A. while struggling to overcome trauma tied to his service in WWI and a recent divorce. While Season 1 occasionally feels as though it is being torn between different ideas, Season 2 finds more clarity in its storytelling, which in turn allows for the detail of the period production and the power of the performances to thrive.

A major candidate for being the single most underrated miniseries HBO has ever produced, The Night Of evolves with magnetic precision over the course of its eight-episode run to encompass everything from murder mystery angst to courtroom suspense and prison drama. It is chiefly a scorching deconstruction of the American justice system, revolving around the trial of Nasir (Riz Ahmed), who stands accused of murdering a woman he was partying with after waking up to find her stabbed to death.

From the claustrophobic intensity of its first episode, The Night Of absorbs viewers as a relentless yet agonizingly patient descent into the darker side of the legal system, from the overwhelming power of the police department to the terrifying environment in Rikers Island. Powered by great performances from Ahmed and John Turturro, an astonishing script, and viscerally immersive direction, The Night Of endures as one of HBO’s finest ever crime productions.

It feels as though romantic-dramedy entertainment is having a niche moment in pop culture, with the genre’s ability to switch between awkward and confronting home truths and outrageous, resonant hilarity giving it a distinct appeal. It is a shame, then, that Looking either pre-dated this trend, or simply wasn’t picked up by it, with the grounded HBO production following video game designer Patrick (Jonathan Groff) as he navigates life and the gay dating scene with his two friends in San Francisco.

The series received high praise for its performances and its intimate mumblecore approach, both of which imbue it with a sense of unvarnished realism that is honest, nuanced, and clever enough to find the imperfect beauty in life’s ugly moments. While it does celebrate gay love, it isn’t about making a fantastical and contrived illustration of it. Instead, characters struggle with rent, job opportunities, dating mishaps, and social pressures while trying to find their own place in the world, making it a tenderly human drama, and one that deserved to go beyond its two-season, 18-episode tenure.

Running as a biographical descent into an American reporter’s time in Japanese newsrooms in the late 1990s, Tokyo Vice is a fascinating mixture of neo-noir crime, journalism drama, and culture-clash intrigue that delivered two compelling seasons before being canceled in 2024. Ansel Elgort stars as Jake Adelstein, an investigative journalist who relocates from Missouri to Tokyo, where he is taken in by Hiroto Katagiri (Kan Watanabe), a veteran of the newspaper’s organized crime squad. From there, Adelstein begins a disturbing descent into the city’s neon-lit underbelly.

Filmed entirely on location in Tokyo, the series holds a certain authenticity that is quite palpable, especially with executive producer and pilot episode director Michael Mann establishing a simmering, slow-burn mood that revels in looming shadows and reflective glass. Both seasons received widespread praise for their setting, storylines, aesthetic, and characters, and the fact that Season 2 was a strong expansion on what the series achieved in Season 1 makes it all the more unfortunate that HBO did cancel it prematurely.

A stunning depiction of modern warfare, Generation Kill finds its dramatic pull in the realism of bureaucracy and miscommunication in combat. Based on Evan Wright’s non-fiction book of the same name, it unfolds as an embedded reporter with Rolling Stone joins the US Marine Corps’ 1st Reconnaissance Battalion on the eve of the Invasion of Iraq in 2003. Handled by the creative team behind The Wire, the war miniseries flaunts no patriotic agenda as it immerses viewers in the disorganized chaos of the battlefield.

Prying beneath the genre’s surface-level action intrigue, Generation Kill revels in the long-winded boredom that plagues soldiers between assignments. The battalion’s cynical humor and crude antics are as much an escape from inactivity as they are a coping mechanism for the horrors they witness. Featuring an impressive ensemble cast, incisive thematic ideas, and a compelling sense of ground-level intimacy, Generation Kill delivers seven episodes of eye-opening war drama that eschews Hollywood sensationalism in favor of unvarnished, unflattering reality.

Yet another underrated masterpiece from HBO, Station Eleven is perhaps a victim of unfortunate timing more so than anything else. The post-apocalyptic miniseries is a hauntingly beautiful meditation on artistic enrichment, humanity’s values, and the power of community, but its setting—the ruins of civilization 20 years after a devastating flu virus decimates mankind—wasn’t what many viewers were looking for at the height of pandemic anxieties in 2021. The shame is that Station Eleven is one of the most compelling and deftly brilliant miniseries of all time.

Based on Emily St. John Mandel’s novel of the same name, it revolves around a group of traveling actors who venture between settlements to perform Shakespeare for the survivors. Between flashbacks to the outbreak and some confronting encounters with a violent cult, Station Eleven is firmly grounded in the sense of optimism and good faith that Kirsten (Mackenzie Davis) and her troupe both present to the surviving world and receive in kind from people wanting to believe in goodness. Complimented by a procession of layered performances, rich character-focused drama, and eerily gorgeous production design, Station Eleven presents 10 episodes of post-apocalyptic intrigue in a manner that it has never been seen before.

Given its style, themes, and the creative duo of David Simon and George Pelecanos, We Own This City is easy to view as a spiritual sequel to The Wire, one that combines the classic HBO crime series’ unflinching rawness with a terrible true story of police corruption and institutional rot. Jon Bernthal is exceptional as Sgt. Wayne Jenkins, the golden boy of the Boston Police Department’s Gun Trace Task Force whose leadership is a rotten mixture of swaggering charisma, moral corrosion, and predatory enabling of his colleagues’ unlawful actions.

The six-part series is mercilessly complex, with the near 20-year span of Jenkins’ career being explored in non-linear flashbacks in the form of an FBI investigation into the GTTF’s illegal conduct. The decision to frame the story in such a way is ingenious, allowing not only for seamless transitions between different chapters of Jenkins’ career, but for an all-encompassing examination of police corruption, the cultural reasons why it thrives, and the helplessness of people who want to combat it being stranded in inefficient and failed bureaucratic systems. Furthermore, it explores how the ill-fated “War on Drugs” impacted police work and political pressures, analyses how corrupt cops can be brought to justice, and yet it covers every major character with a sense of humanity, refusing to delve into heroes and villains.

Not only the greatest miniseries of recent years, Chernobyl also stands tall among HBO’s finest achievements of all time. With just five episodes, the faultless limited series explores the aftermath of the Chernobyl nuclear power plant disaster of 1986, ranging from the trauma endured by the unsuspected first responders to the sacrificial heroics of many who did their part to contain the radioactive fallout, the enormity of the operation required to ensure the incident didn’t grow even more catastrophic, and the corruption and ineptitude of the Soviet leaders who tried to conceal the truth from the world.

The miniseries is laced with a visceral, horror-like tension that combines the awful historical event with an unrelenting sense of stomach-churning dread. Both the precision and the astonishing scope of the screenwriting play a hand in that, but so do the mesmerizing period production design, the thematic emphasis on truth and accountability, and the incredible ensemble cast led by Jared Harris, Stellan Skarsgård, and Emily Watson. The end result is an unblemished masterpiece of television drama, a harrowing and eye-opening examination of a horrendous tragedy and the leadership that brought it to fruition, and a truly iconic triumph from HBO.

2026 marks the 25th anniversary of Band of Brothers’ premiere on the small screen, and even with how drastically television as an entertainment medium has changed in that time, the 10-part war drama has endured as a timeless masterpiece. Created by Hollywood titans Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, it follows “Easy” Company through their campaign in WWII, beginning with their training regimen and their drop into Normandy, covering the many battlefields they fought on the front lines, and ending with their uneasy stay in Austria as the war comes to a close.

Imposing with the sheer scale and might of its production, Band of Brothers excels at realizing each of the battles it depicts with gravitas, instilling a sense of terrible awe in viewers as it combines the horrific brutality of combat with the deeply humane, character-driven emotions of the soldiers’ journey. It is often heartbreaking, oddly humorous, and always harrowing, especially with the real Easy Company veterans giving their own testimonies about their experiences in the war. It delivers 10 outstanding episodes of war drama, and it remains one of the finest feats in the history of television.

Lamar Jackson reportedly expected to miss 6-9 weeks, is telling teammates he'll return this season

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Baltimore Ravens quarterback Lamar Jackson is determined to return before the end of the 2026 NFL season. Jackson, who is expected to miss between six and nine weeks, reportedly told teammates to "just stay alive" ahead of his return, according to ESPN.

Jackson reportedly sustained a fractured and dislocated thumb during the team's Week 8 game against the Buffalo Bills. Jackson, who was making his first start since Week 4, was injured on his first passing attempt of the contest after his thumb made contact with the hand of Bills defender Greg Rousseau, who was trying to disrupt the pass.

Jackson left the game, and was replaced by Tyler Huntley, who also got injured in the contest. On Thursday, the team placed both Jackson and Huntley on injured reserve. Huntley's injury — turf toe — is said to be season ending.

The team kept things closer to the vest with Jackson, not revealing the extent of his injury Thursday. When asked whether Jackson would miss the rest of the season, Ravens head coach Jesse Minter merely said "we'll see."

Given the timeline, Jackson's return is far from guaranteed. If he's needs more than a minimum stay on injured reserve, Jackson would be looking to come back in early January. At that point, the team would either be in Week 18 or the playoffs.

Reaching the postseason without both Jackson and Huntley could prove difficult. A loss to the Bills in Week 8 dropped the team to 4-4 on the season. With both players sidelined, the Ravens turned to third-stringer Joe Fagnano in Week 9. He struggled during a 33-10 loss to the Jacksonville Jaguars, dropping the Ravens to 4-5.

Before that game, however, the team signaled it planned to contend down the stretch. On Wednesday, it was reported the team acquired veteran Jacoby Brissett in a trade with the Arizona Cardinals. Brissett wasn't ready to play Thursday, but will likely move into a starting role as soon as he's up to speed.

Whether Brissett can keep the team afloat long enough for Jackson to return remains to be seen. It should help, however, that the Ravens' next four opponents are currently under .500.

It's imperative the team come out of that stretch with a strong record. Baltimore closes out the season with four straight AFC North games, all against teams with winning records.

Amsterdam police have arrested 150 to 200 protesters at a Microsoft data center site on suspicion of trespassing. According to a Facebook post by the authorities [machine translated], tools designed for breaking into the site were seized from the protesters, who were then allowed on their way to protest peacefully. However, it appears that some members of the group decided to enter the construction site “with violence” anyway, forcing the police to intervene and leading to their arrest. There were also some allegedly stolen items from the construction site found among the belongings of some of the protesters, plus damage and vandalism done to the data center site.

This protest is a continuation of the action that started last Friday, where protesters breached the Microsoft data center, which was being owned by Pure Data Centers. It is also the first of many organized by the “Stop Dirty Data Centers” coalition across Europe, with actions planned for Amsterdam, Dublin, Warsaw, Denmark, and several other cities across the continent. The Dutch publication NOS [machine translated] identified the exact group at the site today as Fossielvrij, who are against the use of fossil fuels in data centers such as the jet engines that Elon Musk installed at xAI’s Mississippi data center.

“Gas prices are going through the roof, and new houses are being postponed because of the overcrowded power grid. Yet tech billionaires like Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos are building energy-guzzling, hideous data centers,” the group said on its website. “We can take back the power of Big Tech by pressing the pause button now. Give us the opportunity to democratically decide what we do and do not use AI for, so that it is not at the expense of our living environment and everything that makes our lives beautiful. Meet Gayane Umerova, the Woman Putting Uzbekistan’s Arts and Culture Scene on the Map

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s been a landmark year for Uzbekistan. The country held its inaugural biennial in the ancient city of Bukhara last fall; 10 Soviet modernist buildings in its captial, Tashkent, were named UNESCO World Heritage Sites over the summer, making them the youngest structures in the world to receive that protected status; and its first permanent contemporary art museum, the Centre for Contemporary Arts Tashkent (CCA Tashkent), opened in September.

Upon meeting Gayane Umerova—the woman behind Uzbekistan’s cultural awakening—one of my first questions is whether she was surprised by the biennial’s success.

“My expectations are always very high, so it didn’t really surprise me. It was exactly what I envisioned,” Umerova says. What did surprise her: that out of the 1.8 million visitors to the Bukhara Biennial, half were domestic. “That’s a big shift in society. For someone to travel from Kokand to Bukhara [some 460 miles away], that would never really happen unless you’re visiting relatives. Now people see Bukhara not only as a historical city, but also as somewhere exciting to discover.”

Her official titles include Head of the Department of Creative Economy and Tourism of the Administration of the President of the Republic of Uzbekistan, as well as Chairperson of the Uzbekistan Art and Culture Development Foundation (ACDF), an organization she founded in 2017 to support the country’s visual artists, writers, theatermakers, musicians, and choreographers. (We’re meeting in her office at the ACDF headquarters in Tashkent, a space tastefully decorated with sleek wooden furniture made by Levantine designer Nada Debs in collaboration with Uzbek artisans—another one of Umerova’s ideas.) She additionally serves as Chairperson of the National Commission of Uzbekistan on UNESCO Affairs under the Cabinet of Ministers, was awarded France’s Order of Arts and Literature in April 2025, and is a LACMA Global Ambassador. It’s no wonder that when I ask how she spends her free time, she retorts that she has none—though she does relish family time with her husband and three daughters.

“Because Uzbekistan is not a country where you have a lot of private collectors building museums or architectural sites, everything has to start with the state support and then rise within society,” Umerova explains, crediting the flurry of activity in Tashkent—like that in Doha or Riyadh—to the attention and investment of its leadership, namely Uzbekistan’s president, Shavkat Mirziyoyev.

Uzbekistan’s large (and growing) subset of young people also makes the country ripe for a cultural renaissance. As of July 2026, more than half of Uzbekistan’s 39 million people are under 30. “You see it often in countries like Uzbekistan or Saudi Arabia, where there is a young population that they drive you,” Umerova says. “You meet people and see they’re creating things from nothing, and that’s really what motivated me to be in the art world.”

Much of Umerova’s desire to strengthen her country’s cultural ecosystem stems from her own childhood in Tashkent. With an aunt who was an artist and grandparents who collected antiquities, she grew up surrounded by the arts, though her first dream was to become an architect. Her father tried to steer her elsewhere, worried that she would be crowded out of such a male-dominated field: “He didn’t want me to end up behind the scenes of these great projects, like a secretary at a big firm.” Nevertheless, Umerova’s passion for architecture has come full circle, from her UNESCO advocacy to her partnerships with firms like the Paris-based Studio KO—which designed the CCA Tashkent—and Japanese starchitect Tadao Ando, who is currently at work on the new National Museum of Uzbekistan, scheduled to open in the capital in 2028.

Umerova received much of her education in the United Kingdom, discovering in Europe what she hoped to create at home. “I’d never seen museums like the Pompidou, which was so extravagant to me at the time,” she recalls. “I was inspired not just by the arts but the buildings that house them. I felt like I would love to do something like that in my own country.” Back in Tashkent, she served as a senior curator at the Art Gallery of Uzbekistan from 2008 to 2017, the year she started the ACDF.

The majority of Umerova’s roughly 50-person team at the ACDF has lived or studied abroad. “I think we have the stigma of not hearing or seeing our country on the map, but now the younger population really wants to own that they’re from this country,” she says. She believes it has become a “collective ambition” to strengthen the country’s arts-education infrastructure from a young age. (In service of that goal, the ACDF was behind the renovation of the Nationwide Children’s Library, which has operated out of a 19th-century mansion in Tashkent since 1965.)

This demanded, in part, a reframing of what even constituted art. “In Uzbekistan, there are a lot of artisans, but they just see the traditional way of getting their work to people, for example, through fairs. They don’t really see themselves as artists. They only see themselves as makers.” She traces that sensibility back to the fall of the Soviet Union, when, “for all Central Asian countries, it was about self-identity,” and “everything which was contemporary or modern was seen as foreign.”

While Umerova acknowledges the value of promoting Uzbek crafts, from woodcarving to papermaking, she sees no reason why contemporary art and design can’t also thrive. In 2022, the ACDF formed a partnership with the Michelangelo Foundation, marking the first time a Central Asian country joined its Homo Faber Guide, a curated digital platform spotlighting master artisans and ateliers around the globe. Uzbekistan also sent its first representatives to the Venice Architecture Biennale and the Venice Art Biennale in 2021 and 2022, respectively.

A testament to Uzbekistan’s success in Venice is London-based Uzbek artist Aziza Kadyri, who combines suzani, traditional hand-embroidered textiles, with AI. She participated in the Uzbekistan National Pavilion at the 2024 Venice Art Biennale, and last year she was commissioned for the Bukhara Biennial. (Visitors to the Victoria & Albert Museum in London can see a site-specific installation in which Kadyri responds to the museum’s historic suzani collection until January 10, 2027.)

Venice’s Biennale model has long inspired Umerova. “It’s like a living city with art,” she says, emphasizing the convenience of having most sites within walking distance of each other. With restoration at the heart of the Bukhara Biennial (the Historic Centre of Bukhara was added to the World Heritage List in 1993), Umerova conceived a master plan to ensure that the art and the impending tourist boom would not harm the city, the most complete medieval city in Central Asia. The inaugural edition saw local artists, such as ceramicist Abdulvahid Bukhoriy and metal engraver Jurabek Sidikov, transform a prayer room in a restored historic madrasa (a center of Islamic education), while participating international artists ranged from Egyptian multidisciplinary artist Wael Shawky to British sculptor Antony Gormley.

Of course, Uzbeks can now get their contemporary art fix year-round at the CCA Tashkent. Curated by Sara Raza, who specializes in postcolonial and post-Soviet art, the debut exhibition, “Hikmah”—Uzbek for “wisdom”—brings together works by artists near and far. The painstaking restoration of the building, originally constructed in 1912 as a diesel station, has also made a powerful statement about the potential of adaptive reuse.

Exterior of the Centre for Contemporary Arts Tashkent (CCA Tashkent).

The CCA Tashkent, Uzbekistan’s first permanent contemporary art museum, is housed in a former diesel station.

Conscious that modern buildings are often considered less precious than ancient sites—and therefore more vulnerable to being torn down—Umerova spent five years researching and applying for Tashkent’s Soviet modernist buildings to be granted UNESCO status. She also advocated for about 160 mosaic panels adorning residential and public buildings in Tashkent and other areas to be added to the National List of Real Estate Properties of Tangible Cultural Heritage in 2024. After an earthquake destroyed much of Tashkent in 1966, these mosaic designs were embedded into new constructions, becoming a distinctive part of Uzbekistan’s Soviet-era architectural landscape. Under this heritage status, the mosaics are protected from being dismantled, painted over, or covered with advertising.

Several more museums are underway in Uzbekistan, most notably the new National Museum, Tadao Ando’s first major project in Central Asia. The architect tells Vogue via email that he hopes the 40,000-square-meter institution “will become a spiritual anchor for its people and serve as a vibrant cultural center.” Its collection, currently housed in the State Museum of Arts (which will become a state museum of architecture—fitting, as it was one of the 10 buildings to earn UNESCO protection this year), is especially important to Umerova, as she often visited it with her parents and on school trips. The ACDF is also working on the Jadids’ Legacy Museum in Bukhara and the Silk Road Museum in Samarkand. They are projected to open in 2027 and 2029–30, respectively.

A rendering of the National Museum of Uzbekistan.

Umerova’s efforts have also reached American institutions. New York’s Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum loaned a monumental aerial sculpture by Nadia Kaabi-Linke, a Tunisian-Ukrainian artist based in Berlin, for the CCA Tashkent’s inaugural exhibition; and Umerova teases a major collaboration with the Smithsonian in 2029 that will have been 10 years in the making.

“Gayane Umerova is leading the cultural strategy for Uzbekistan with an uncommon combination of inspiring vision, executive follow-through, and a fine sense of what art means for human thriving and cohesion as well as social and economic development,” Dr. Mariët Westermann, director and CEO of the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation, told Vogue via email of Umerova’s “broad-spectrum” approach.

Umerova’s hope is that Uzbekistan will not just be a place more travelers want to visit, but one that they keep coming back to. “We want people to see our country on repeat,” she says. “Not just once in a lifetime.”

Mike Vick, Norfolk State lose after his order was ignored

📅 2026-11-08  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Like a growing number of former NFL players, Mike Vick has become a college football coach. Unlike many college football coaches, a direct order from Vick wasn’t followed on Saturday.

With 1:34 to play during Saturday’s game between Vick’s Norfolk State and North Carolina Central, Norfolk State led, 28-23. Norfolk State had the ball, facing second and five from the 50. North Carolina Central had no timeouts remaining.

While a pair of knees wouldn’t have fully exhausted the remaining time, Vick told the offense to take a knee in victory formation. Two straight kneel-downs would have resulted in a punt with less than 15 seconds to play.

Instead, Norfolk State lined up in shotgun formation, the quarterback ran to the left, a defender hit him, and the quarterback fumbled.

North Carolina Central recovered at the Norfolk State 37. Five plays later, North Carolina scored a touchdown, on a 26-yard touchdown pass, making the score 29-28. (The two-point conversion attempt failed.)

Twenty-seven seconds remained. Norfolk State drove in position to attempt a game-winning field goal. The 37-yard try missed wide left.

“I don’t know what happened at the end of the game,” Vick told reporters after a loss that dropped Norfolk State to 4-6 on the season. “I put my headset down. I called victory formation. All we had to do was take a knee, kneel the clock out. That’s just situational football. I don’t know what happened in regards to that. I looked up and we was running the ball, and then I seen the fumble. But my final instructions was victory formation. That’s when you kneel it out. And they had, I think, zero timeouts left and the game was over practically.”

“Well, I put my headset down, and I took my headset off because all we had to do was just run it three times, or two times, or kneel it out. That’s called victory. I don’t know what happened. A run play was called. I looked up, I seen us running the ball. I don’t know what happened. I really don’t. I don’t know what the play call was. I don’t know why we even ran that play.

“People will look at that, and that’ll go on as a teaching tape for a lot of teams across the country for years to come. I hate it had to be us. But I called victory formation. Just letting everybody know that. I called victory.”

While the strategy wouldn’t have completely exhausted the clock, it was the right move. And, even if it wasn’t, the coach gave the order. The order wasn’t followed.

So what happened? Vick said he had taken off his headset and then later realized that a run play had been called. The fact that the offense went straight into shotgun formation suggests that it wasn’t the players going rogue. Apparently, offensive coordinator Brian Sheppard decided to ignore Vick’s directive.

Indeed, a somewhat chaotic postgame video (posted by Vick on Twitter) includes Vick telling his players “it was supposed to have been victory.”

The video of the moments before the play in question shows the offense gathered at the numbers near the Norfolk State bench, speaking to a group of coaches who were still wearing headsets. The coach without a headset (who appears to be Vick) walked to the sideline before turning around and returning to the meeting.

It’s clear that the coaches and players were having a discussion. From Vick’s perspective, there was nothing to discuss.

And while Vick claims his instruction was ignored, he seemed to be a little too nonchalant at a time when he should have realized that something more detailed than telling the quarterback to line up under center, receive the snap, and take a knee was being planned.

If Vick believes Sheppard or other coaches overruled the head coach, Vick should be issuing pink slips immediately. It’s possible, though, that Vick himself bears the bulk of the blame for not noticing that something other than the implementation of victory formation was coming.

The Best Energy Stock to Buy With Oil Near $105 Isn't an Oil Producer

Oil prices are high today, but also highly volatile; you can sidestep the commodity risk with this high-yield energy investment.

Enterprise Products Partners (EPD +1.12%) operates in the energy sector, but oil prices aren't the main determinant of its success. That's an important distinction today as you consider energy stocks, with oil prices trading around $105 per barrel. It wasn't too long ago that oil was less than half that. Here's how Enterprise avoids the commodity risk that buying an energy producer would expose you to.

The broader energy sector is generally broken into three parts: the upstream (production), the midstream (pipelines), and the downstream (chemicals and refining). The upstream and the downstream are heavily impacted by commodity prices. The midstream simply connects the upstream to the downstream and the rest of the world. Midstream businesses like Enterprise charge fees for the use of their energy infrastructure assets, so the volume flowing through their systems is more important than the price of what is being moved.

Compared to the upstream and the downstream, the midstream is actually pretty boring. And Enterprise is one of the most boring midstream operators around. While it is among the largest pipeline businesses in North America, it has long focused on slow and steady growth. That's highlighted in its distribution, which has increased annually for 28 years. That's basically as long as it has been publicly traded.

Distribution growth tends to be in the low- to mid-single digits, but slow and steady is hard to complain about in a sector known for volatility. Meanwhile, Enterprise has an investment-grade-rated balance sheet, and its distributable cash flow covered its distribution by 1.7x in 2025. There is material room for adversity before a distribution cut would be considered here. And it has $6.5 billion in capital investment planned through 2029, so more slow-and-steady growth is a far more likely outcome.

All that said, the single factor likely to be most interesting to investors is Enterprise's roughly 6% distribution yield. That's six times what you would get from the broader market today. While upstream and downstream investments rise and fall along with oil prices, you can comfortably sit back and collect a well-above-market yield that has grown steadily for decades. If history is any guide, oil prices will eventually fall from their lofty levels after the geopolitical conflict in the Middle East ends. If you own Enterprise, you probably won't care all that much if they do.
