Antarctic blue whales survived near extinction, but can they fully recover?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Antarctic blue whales are slowly recovering from whaling, but their population remains around 2% of its former size.

Several whale populations that were hunted hard in the 20th century have already climbed back to their old numbers. Antarctic blue whales, the largest blue whales on Earth, are not one of them.

Their numbers are rising again, but a new estimate suggests there are still only about 2% as many as there were before whaling began.

Since the last recorded catch in 1973, the population has grown nearly tenfold, from about 418 whales to about 4,018 in 2024.

The team got those numbers from a computer model rather than a head count, so each one comes with a lot of uncertainty.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington’s School of Marine and Environmental Affairs, led the work with colleagues in the US, Australia, Japan and South Africa.

It’s the first full assessment of these whales in more than 20 years.

“No whale was hunted quite to the extent that Antarctic blue whales were hunted,” Rand said.

In 1904, whalers killed 11 Antarctic blue whales. The hunt grew so fast that by 1930 they were taking 30,405 in a single year.

Breeding couldn’t keep up with a hunt on that scale. In many seasons, whalers removed between 10% and 40% of the whole population.

By the time the last recorded catch occurred in 1973, whalers had killed a total of 347,662 Antarctic blue whales.

The team estimates that about 206,000 Antarctic blue whales lived in the Southern Ocean before whaling, and that only about 418, roughly 0.2%, were left by 1973.

Even a ban didn’t stop the killing right away. The International Whaling Commission agreed to end the commercial catch of these whales in 1963, but illegal Soviet whaling carried on for about another decade.

Counting whales spread around an entire continent is hard, so the team pulled together three kinds of information, starting with the old catch records.

The second came from ship surveys, including three full trips around Antarctica between 1978 and 2004 that recorded every blue whale the crews saw. Japanese ships also surveyed part of the same waters from 1989 to 2008.

The third came from photographs, since each whale can be recognized from pictures of its sides.

By tracking how often the same whales reappeared in photos from 2003 to 2019, the team estimated about 2,889 in Antarctic waters for the 2018 to 2019 season.

The researchers then combined all three in one computer model. It estimated how many whales were alive in every year from 1904 to 2024.

The model’s best estimate for 2024 is 4,018 whales.

The estimated range runs from about 1,900 to 8,400 whales, and even the high end would be less than 5% of the population before whaling.

So the whales are coming back, but from an extremely low starting point.

The team estimates the population is growing by about 4.5% a year, slower than the 7.3% a year reported in the last assessment, published in 2004.

Co-author Trevor Branch of the University of Washington’s School of Aquatic and Fishery Sciences led that study.

The team traced the difference to updated Japanese survey numbers and the new photo-based count.

“I think this is relatively realistic for the kind of population growth we would expect from them,” Rand said.

If growth continues along the model’s path, Antarctic blue whales wouldn’t reach even 60% of their old numbers until around 2102.

The authors note that this date depends on how many whales lived there before whaling, a number that is itself uncertain.

Other whales have recovered much faster. Blue whales off western North America, gray whales in the eastern North Pacific and North Pacific humpbacks have all returned to or passed their pre-whaling numbers in the past decade.

But most of those populations weren’t pushed down nearly as far. Growth that starts from a few hundred animals takes a long time to add up.

“I think it’s probably an open question whether they will make it back to pre-whaling levels,” Rand added.

Antarctic blue whales feed mainly on Antarctic krill, small shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean. That food supply may have changed.

Large baleen whales dive deep, eat several tons of krill a day and excrete iron-rich waste near the surface.

Scientists think that waste feeds the tiny ocean plants krill depend on, so losing millions of whales may have meant less krill.

Blue whales returning now will likely face more competition for that krill. Humpback, fin and Antarctic minke whales eat it too, and they may be recovering faster.

Krill fishing is projected to grow, and earlier research has linked shrinking sea ice and warmer water to fewer krill.

The experience of southern right whales shows how recovery can stall.

Several of their populations are having fewer calves, with longer gaps between births, even though they’re still well below their pre-whaling size.

If the Southern Ocean can now support fewer blue whales than it once did, the authors wrote, the old population size may no longer be a realistic target.

The new numbers are probably on the low side. Ship surveys assume observers spot every whale directly in the ship’s path, but blue whales are often underwater, and some likely get missed.

Studies of other blue whale populations suggest those misses could make the survey estimates 4% to 25% too low.

The photo count assumes whales mix randomly around Antarctica each year. In fact, they return to the same ocean basin 54% to 64% of the time, compared with about 33% if they moved at random.

Both problems point toward more whales and faster growth than the model shows. Neither would change the main finding, which is that the population is growing but still far from recovered.

Keeping track is also getting harder, because full ship surveys around Antarctica have largely stopped.

Underwater microphones can pick up blue whale songs, but scientists still can’t turn the number of calls into a count of whales.

“Antarctica is a big place, and it’s not just one country’s ocean,” Rand said. “We really need more monitoring and continued monitoring to figure out what is going to happen to this species in the future.”

The authors called for more ship surveys, more photographs and more listening.

Without that work, researchers won’t be able to tell whether the recovery holds up as the Southern Ocean changes.

The full study was published in the journal Endangered Species Research.

Microsoft VP Scott Hanselman has revealed a new open-source GitHub project called gputaskman. The name neatly encapsulates the headline role of this tool, a GPU-focused task manager, but its scope might be broader than you expect. This new tool for Windows, Linux, and macOS can show per-process GPU usage, VRAM consumption, active runtimes, and many more stats. It also breaks out AI/LLM task-specific information.

On social media, Hanselman snappily makes clear the technical itch that gputaskman was designed to scratch. “There's a lot going on in my GPU,” wrote the prominent public face of Microsoft's developer ecosystem. “I'm making a GPU Taskman to get insights to where my GPU memory is going.”

There are several example screenshots of GPU Taskman in use by the Microsoft VP. They clearly show the kinds of data that are presented in the terminal-like display that pops up in the tool. Hanselman’s tweet shows that the most VRAM-hungry app on his RTX 4080S system is the desktop windows manager. That is selected in the list, and more details are shown for this task at the bottom of the window. Also, we see AI/LLM apps consuming VRAM, and a few Windows staples.

In a GitHub-embedded image, we see what details are broken out when the llama-server.exe app is selected in the list. The bottom of the window breakdown reveals that Qwen3-0.6B is being used, and there’s a breakdown of its weights, the KV cache, the number of parameters, and more. Very informative.

As the Microsoft developer has made portable Windows downloads of the latest v0.3.0 release of gputaskman, I couldn’t resist giving it a spin. Upon launch, I found that no tasks were using the discrete GPU in my laptop… so I fired up ye olde Unigine Valley GPU benchmark to stir the RTX 4060 from its slumber. I note the live-streaming charts showing GPU load, VRAM use, temperature, and so on are more prominent in my screenshot than in Hanselman’s examples. Perhaps he screen-shotted a previous version of the tool.

There’s a lot more information about gputaskman, its inner workings, and even its source code, on the linked GitHub repository. Frontier Hires A Wizz Air Executive As Two Leaders Walk Out

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Frontier announced Monday that Michael Delehant will join as Executive Vice President and Chief Operating Officer (COO) on November 30, 2026 according to AirlineGeeks. He most recently ran operational and commercial activities as a group Managing Director at Wizz Air, and before that he was chief strategy and network officer at Vueling; he also held several leadership roles at Southwest. He will report to Frontier CEO, Jimmy Dempsey.

The other half of the announcement is the exits. Trevor Stedke, Senior Vice President of Operations, and Alex Clerc, Senior Vice President of Customers, will both depart on November 13. Frontier didn’t provide a reason for either. Not all departures have a “spending more time with my family” boilerplate announcement provided and there’s nothing wrong with that. But  it’s telling that the person running operations and the person in charge of customers are both gone, unironically about two weeks before a new operations boss shows up for work.

James Fenner, who has spent more than a decade at Frontier and was most recently Vice president of Revenue and Loyalty, becomes Senior Vice President and Chief Revenue Officer. His job now covers pricing, revenue management, loyalty and network planning. Most of those make sense to me together with the exception of loyalty. Pricing and revenue management go hand-in-hand as does network planning. But with the Frontier getting a dedicated first class, and the loss of Spirit (so far), Allegiant/Sun Country merging, and the struggles JetBlue is facing and Frontier’s own revenue challenges – it feels like loyalty should be its own gig.

Bobby Schroeter becomes Chief Marketing Officer, overseeing the brand and customer experience. With the customers executive leaving, customer experience moves under marketing. I would have thought loyalty responsibilities would have fallen to CMO but I am apparently mistaken.

Frontier lost $104 million in the Q3 2026, compared with a $77 million loss a year earlier. Removing special items and the airline made $40 million on a record $1.36 billion in revenue. Matthew covered it the day the numbers dropped as a loss despite record revenue, and he found reason for optimism.

Dempsey took over after longtime chief, Barry Biffle, left suddenly in December last year and was named CEO in January. This CEO, on the job about 10 months, is swapping out the operations chief, the customer chief and handing the revenue chief and in essence reinventing the carrier. I want to applaud Dempsey for taking his time to evaluate before making such dramatic changes. Newly installed leaders often want their own people and to break from the past to do things their way and make their own mark. However, as long as it has taken to make these changes, many might have presumed that he felt he was able to make those changes with the current staff in place.

Hiring a Wizz Air executive indicates what Frontier thinks its future playbook is. Delehant comes from two low-cost European carriers, Wizz and Vueling, and those two airlines operate differently from each other and the market is different from Frontier. Vueling is owned by International Airlines Group, the same parent as British Airways, Iberia, LEVEL, and Aer Lingus. Vueling operates outside of the network carrier model with connections and oneworld partner feed but still benefits from the incredible buying power of IAG. Wizz does not.

But for those who have called the death of LCCs and ULCCs in the United States with the shut down of Spirit, the new Frontier could fly in the face of that. Further, as pointed out on this site before, based solely on flying people and things, Ryan Air might be the most profitable true airline (not loyalty program) in the world. It closed last year earning $2.4bn, and every carrier that placed above Ryan had a loyalty program and credit card tie-in.

One thing that has become very clear, is that first class in the United States simply must be offered to be successful. Frontier, Allegiant/Sun Country, and JetBlue have all added a first class product this year (JetBlue had Mint for long haul but its shorter haul domestic first class is genuinely new.) Spirit had just moved into this space too, separating its Big Front Seat product into a First Class cabin, then European-style Business Class (blocked middle seat) though the writing was already on the wall in Fort Lauderdale.

These changes at Frontier could foretell the future of the ultra low cost model in the United States, first class and all.

Whether Delehunt’s changes have the capability of increasing Frontier Airlines stock price will likely determine just how much further he goes. But he’s right to install his own leadership, and given the market segment, quite savvy to find executives with experience in the ULCC space and at least some overlap in the United States in Southwest Airlines. Frontier has a credit card, its loyalty division now shares a boss with revenue management, and a hub model at least in part so it’s not a like-for-like fit with Wizz or other ULCC models. But it’s not a miss either, and I am interested in seeing just how much of the Spirit vacuum Frontier chooses to operate.

Should You Forget IonQ and Buy These 2 Quantum Computing Stocks Instead?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

D-Wave Quantum and Rigetti are taking different approaches to developing quantum computers. Here's how their commercial progress and valuations stack up against IonQ's.

IonQ (IONQ +23.42%) may be a leading pure-play name in the quantum computing race, but it certainly isn't the only promising one. D-Wave Quantum (QBTS +18.01%) and Rigetti Computing (RGTI +19.29%) are also among the stronger picks in the space, and each brings distinctive technologies and growth opportunities to the table.

Could these two quantum stocks be better buys than IonQ right now?

D-Wave's biggest advantage is its focus on quantum annealing, a niche approach to the technology that is largely useful only for solving optimization and sampling problems. However, those types of problems come up frequently in a host of applications, from managing supply chains and logistics to materials science and chemistry. Customer adoption of its systems has been picking up, with more companies using D-Wave's quantum technology to improve their operations.

That's starting to show up in its financial results.

In August, the company delivered its Q2 numbers and reported that production applications accounted for a growing share of its quantum cloud computing revenue. It also expanded its commercial relationships during the quarter, including an agreement with AT&T to broaden the use of quantum computing in network operations.

Plus, with its acquisition of Quantum Circuits, the company has been developing a dual-platform strategy that combines its annealing approach with a more common gate-model quantum computing approach. That's important because gate-model systems have the potential to address a much broader range of problems in computing.

With that, D-Wave may appeal more to investors than IonQ. In fact, its technology is already being used in production applications, helping businesses optimize their operations. That gives it a more immediate path to monetization.

Of course, there are risks in owning a stock like D-Wave. Quantum annealing has its value, but it doesn't address every problem that general-purpose quantum computers could potentially solve. D-Wave is also still at an early stage of its journey toward a commercially viable product, so adoption still needs to translate into more sustainable revenue growth and profitability.

On top of that, with the stock trading at a price-to-sales (P/S) ratio of around 300, its valuation prices in a whole lot of hoped-for future growth. If the company doesn't deliver on those expectations, the stock could decline dramatically.

Still, the majority of Wall Street analysts covering the stock rate it a buy, with an average 12-month target price of $33.83, implying about 226% upside.

Meanwhile, another quantum stock is taking a unique approach to scaling quantum computers.

Rigetti offers a different opportunity: It's using superconducting qubits to build powerful, general-purpose quantum computers. It's also developing full-stack technology, spanning hardware, software, and the infrastructure needed to run quantum computers.

The company has also made meaningful progress expanding its commercial and research partnerships. In July, Rigetti announced an expanded collaboration with Hewlett Packard Enterprise and the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center. The project involves deploying Rigetti's Novera quantum system, allowing researchers to explore how quantum processors can work alongside traditional supercomputers.

Rigetti is also working to expand its international footprint, including plans to invest in the U.K. and deliver systems to research institutions. These relationships could help drive adoption of its technology and create opportunities for future commercial deals.

If superconducting qubits become the leading approach to scaling up quantum computers to a commercially useful size, Rigetti could be one of the biggest beneficiaries. Its chiplet-based design, in-house manufacturing, and federal government backing also make it an interesting way to invest in the next generation of quantum hardware.

However, Rigetti's path to potential success will depend on improving processor performance, scaling its quantum systems without compromising quality, and securing commercial demand. As a result, the company faces significant execution risk.

Its valuation also is approaching 300 times sales, so it, too, will have to deliver on extremely high expectations to generate the sales and profits necessary to justify its current share price and produce additional returns. Still, like D-Wave, the majority of analysts covering Rigetti rate the stock a buy, with an average 12-month target price of $27.69, implying about 183% upside.

Each company offers a different way to bet on how quantum computing will evolve, and each has its own path to growth. D-Wave stands out as an investment due to the progress it has already made in commercial applications, while Rigetti offers an approach to building scalable quantum computing hardware that could be useful in a broader array of applications.

For investors who prefer to own companies with nearer-term commercial traction, D-Wave looks like the stronger fit. The US Department of Treasury on Friday issued an order to cut cryptocurrency exchange MEXC Global off from the US’ financial system after accusing it of facilitating at least $16 billion in transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity.

The action is part of Operation Economic Outcast, a US Treasury campaign launched in August with aims to cut off financial entities that help Iran evade sanctions.
