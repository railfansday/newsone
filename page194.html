Cathie Wood 'always feels better when the market is worried' — here's why

📅 2026-11-08  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ark Invest CEO Cathie Wood is shrugging off this week's market jitters, telling CNBC on Friday that concerns among investors actually reinforce her optimism that further gains lie ahead.

U.S. equities sold off sharply on Thursday, reversing the initial rally seen in the wake of the midterm elections. AI-related stocks led the rout, sending the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite into correction territory. On Friday, tech stocks staged a tentative recovery.

Much of the sell-off on Thursday was driven by concerns that a Democrat-led Congress would lead to a slowdown in data center construction. But concerns about artificial intelligence – particularly when it comes to capital expenditure, cash flow, debt and circular financing – have sparked multiple bouts of volatility over the course of this year.

Wood, whose firm manages various funds focused on tech and innovation, told CNBC there was a "wall of worry" pervading financial markets right now. But her decades of experience as an investor give her a somewhat unconventional view of the state of play.

"I experienced the tech and telecom bubble, and you know there really wasn't much worry about it," she said. "It was the 80s and the 90s, and it was a bull market, and people were just assuming this would go to the moon, that is not how this [the AI trade] has evolved."

She explained that "from the beginning," many investors have been concerned about how much firms are spending on AI development, how rapidly the spending is taking place, and how the hyperscalers have moved from extreme levels of positive free cash flow to negative free cash flow.

"So it's been unsettling the markets for quite some time," she said. "I always feel better when the market is worried, because that tends to suggest a more durable bull market, and I don't think we've derailed this bull market."

In 2025, her flagship Ark Innovation ETF gained more than 35%, far outperforming the S&P 500's 16.4% rise and the Nasdaq Composite's 20.5% rally.

Asked on Friday whether markets were anywhere near the end of the current bull run, Wood replied: "I don't think so."

"I think we're in the middle of a technology revolution," she said, pointing to the development of technologies like robotaxis and AI agents.

One thing that did risk becoming a "showstopper," Wood noted, was the rising cost of capital.

Interest rates and bond yields have risen sharply this year as the U.S.-Iran war pushes inflation higher across the globe. Yields on corporate bonds have been rising too, with creditors demanding greater compensation for lending to companies.

"This correction is partly caused by what's going on in the bond markets," Wood said on Friday.

"I had been saying for quite some time that the wall of worry has been built by senior leaders in the financial world who were young during the bubble," she said.

"It's the elder statesmen and women of the industry who are keeping this wall of worry in place, and that's not bad. Now you can add the bond investor to that. Bond investors in the old days weren't necessary for technology. It was software. It was low fixed cost. It was viral apps, very high margins. [AI] is high fixed cost, so bond investors are experiencing or taking a journey into the technology world, really that they haven't very much before."

But she noted that AI hyperscalers were somewhat insulated by opportunities for growth.

"If a company thinks that taking on debt is going to accelerate its momentum, meaning its growth rate, it hardly matters what happens to interest rates for those companies," Wood explained.

Wood said her advice to investors in the current volatile environment was to identify their high-conviction positions and double down on them as the market shifts.

"When you see a rally in a volatile environment, take some profits. When you see a sell-off, you'll have the psychological wherewithal if you have taken profits to move back in," she said.

"That's what we do. So what we do during periods like this is concentrate towards our highest conviction names. So that's what we've been doing in the last week or so. Our SpaceX position has gone up. If anything, the controversy around data centers on Earth is giving data centers in space a bigger opportunity."

In 2027, Wood said she expects the equity rally to continue.

"This is two steps forward, one step back," she said of the post-election turbulence. "The revolution will become clearer and clearer next year, and I think the market will do well."

A rural community came together to take on big tech and won

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

A small Ohio town with just 6,600 residents has become an unlikely example of how communities can push back against the massive infrastructure powering the AI boom.

On November 3, voters in Upper Sandusky, Ohio, overwhelmingly approved a measure prohibiting the construction of large data centers within city limits. Nearly 77% voted in favor, following months of opposition to a proposed $900 million data center and natural gas power plant.

Organizers initially believed they needed 155 signatures to put the proposed ban on the ballot. They collected 301, only to discover that the actual requirement was higher and that many of their signatures had been rejected. With hours remaining before the deadline, they were still 20 valid signatures short, as accounted in substack post from the president of the national nonprofit Farm Action, Angela Huffman. I spoke to Ms. Huffman, one of the residents who helped organize the campaign, about how her community challenged a nearly billion-dollar development and what other towns facing similar proposals can learn from the experience.

Upper Sandusky sits in northwest Ohio, surrounded by farmland. Huffman's family has farmed about 10 miles outside town for six generations.

In June, Smartland Energy proposed developing approximately 146 acres near the northern edge of the city. The roughly $900 million project would have included data centers and a 150-megawatt natural gas power plant.

For residents, the proposal raised questions about electricity, water, infrastructure and the long-term future of their community.

Upper Sandusky was already dealing with infrastructure questions surrounding a major new soybean processing plant. Another industrial development raised concerns about how much additional demand local systems could handle.

Huffman, whose work at Farm Action focuses on corporate power in agriculture and rural communities, had already been examining the potential impact of data center expansion nationally.

When asked about the proposed data center, Huffman said, "We saw it as a threat to our rural way of life." She added that everyone had questions, "what's this doing to our water supply? How is this going to impact our electricity bills?"

Residents also questioned whether the city's existing industrial zoning rules were appropriate for a project of this size.

According to Huffman, people wanted to understand what the development would mean for their community before officials approved it.

The initial organizing effort began with something familiar to many small communities: a local newspaper article shared on Facebook.

Huffman told me that a resident shared coverage of the proposal from the local newspaper called, Daily Chief Union in a community Facebook group. The post attracted hundreds of shares, bringing the development to the attention of residents who might otherwise never have heard about it.

Huffman connected with the person who posted the article, and together they began organizing a response.

Their dedicated Facebook group eventually grew to approximately 700 members, a significant number for a town of 6,600 people.

Residents began attending public meetings, requesting government records and pressing city officials for information about the project.

Huffman tells Tom's Guide, "Many people were initially uncomfortable speaking publicly, particularly when it meant being quoted in a newspaper or potentially creating tension with neighbors."

Still, residents continued showing up. As opposition grew, Smartland Energy withdrew its zoning request, although the company continued efforts to build local support, including holding a public information event at a church. I asked Huffman if she attended Smartland Energy's information sessions and she said, "No, I'm not sure how many people did." Once the company withdrew the proposal, it didn't mean the development had been permanently abandoned, so residents continued organizing.

On July 6, Upper Sandusky City Council unanimously approved a six-month moratorium on data centers and associated power-generation facilities.

The temporary pause gave officials time to examine the implications of large data center developments and consider new regulations. It also gave residents time to pursue a longer-term solution.

Huffman tells Tom's Guide that the community had originally requested a 12-month moratorium. Although the city approved only six months, organizers recognized that the pause created an opportunity to put the question directly before voters.

Working with attorneys from Conserve Ohio, a statewide organization that has supported similar campaigns, residents drafted a charter amendment. The proposal would prohibit construction of data centers with an electrical demand or peak load exceeding 25 megawatts within Upper Sandusky's city limits.Huffman notes that having a lawyer is key for towns hoping to fight against big tech. She says their town's lawyer was pro bono, and encouraged communities to either find a lawyer that will offer their services for free or even pay for a legal assessment at the very least.

But even with a lawyer, to get it on the November ballot, residents needed enough valid signatures from registered city voters. That's where things nearly fell apart.

According to Huffman's account of the campaign, organizers were initially told they needed 155 valid signatures.

They collected 301 and submitted them on August 3, believing they had comfortably exceeded the requirement. But election officials accepted only 192.

Worse, organizers discovered that the city's charter actually required 212 valid signatures.

After months of meetings, organizing and legal work, the entire effort was suddenly in jeopardy because of a 20-signature shortfall.

With just hours remaining before the filing deadline, residents headed back out to collect more. On August 5, they submitted an additional 50 signatures. Officials accepted 37, bringing the final total to 229.

Nearly 77% of voters supported the ban. On November 3, Huffman and other organizers returned to their local polling place, this time to watch residents decide the outcome.

In her published account, Huffman describes arriving at 6:30 a.m. and remaining until polls closed at 7:30 p.m., spending the day outside holding signs and talking with voters.

The results were decisive. According to the Daily Chief Union, 1,893 voters supported the charter amendment, compared with 568 who opposed it.

The result followed months of uncertainty, including a withdrawn development proposal, a temporary moratorium and a petition drive that nearly missed its deadline.

Upper Sandusky wasn't alone. Other Ohio communities also approved restrictions on data center development during the November election, reflecting broader concerns about how these facilities are approved and who gets a say in the process.

The rise of generative AI has created enormous demand for computing infrastructure. Training and running advanced AI models requires powerful servers, cooling systems and significant electricity. As technology companies expand their operations, new data centers are being proposed across the country, including in rural areas with available land.

Developers and industry supporters argue that data centers can provide construction jobs, tax revenue and infrastructure investment.

But residents also want to know how much of that economic benefit will remain in their communities, particularly when projects require substantial investments in electricity transmission, water systems and other infrastructure.

During our conversation, Huffman identified several concerns that motivated residents in Upper Sandusky, including potential increases in electricity bills, water consumption, noise, environmental impacts and the possibility of changing the rural character of the community.

Tom's Guide contacted Smartland Energy to ask about the project's future, its potential economic benefits, projected resource requirements and the company's response to the election result. The company did not respond by the time of publication.

One of the most interesting parts of my conversation with Huffman was the credit she gives to the local newspaper. The Daily Chief Union covered Smartland Energy's original proposal, bringing attention to a development that many residents might otherwise have missed.

That reporting was subsequently shared on Facebook, where it helped connect people concerned about the project.

Huffman emphasizes how important local journalism remains in rural communities, particularly when major companies propose developments that could permanently alter the surrounding area.

In Upper Sandusky, newspaper reporting helped residents understand what was being proposed before decisions were finalized.

It ultimately became an early catalyst for the organizing campaign.

Although the November 3 vote restricts large data centers within Upper Sandusky's city limits, it doesn't prohibit development throughout Wyandot County.

Much of the surrounding area consists of unincorporated townships, some without comparable zoning protections.

Huffman says residents are now working with people in those areas to explore additional restrictions, including asking county officials to consider a moratorium and zoning changes.

That leaves an important unanswered question: Could Smartland Energy or another developer pursue a large data center just outside the city limits?

For now, the city charter amendment applies to qualifying large data centers within Upper Sandusky, not to every possible project in the surrounding county.

As AI companies continue expanding their infrastructure, more Americans are finding themselves in the middle of debates over data center development.

Upper Sandusky's experience shows how one community responded, beginning with a local newspaper article and a Facebook post before moving into public meetings, legal assistance and a ballot initiative.

For a town of 6,600 people, that's a significant outcome and one Huffman encourages other communities with proposed data centers saying, "You can do something about it. You're not alone."

Is Broadcom a Buy After Falling 9% in One Day?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The recent drop in Broadcom stock does not align with its fundamentals.

Broadcom (AVGO +0.74%) dropped by 9% as artificial intelligence (AI) fears continue to compound. Financing risks and questions about the ROI on AI investments dominated October, and midterm results are the new key item in November.

The growth stock was once approaching $500 and is now under $300 at the time of this writing. However, this drop does not correlate with the status of Broadcom's fundamentals. The dip looks like a good buying opportunity for long-term investors.

It's always good to look at a company's recent earnings results and guidance before making a decision regarding its stock. In this case, Broadcom delivered superb results.

Revenue jumped by 48% year-over-year, with AI semiconductor revenue doing the heavy lifting. That part of the business was up by 143% year-over-year and represented almost half of total sales.

Furthermore, Broadcom guided for AI semiconductor revenue to more than triple in its fiscal 2026 third quarter, which the company will report in early December. The momentum is not limited to 2026. The chipmaker is on record anticipating $115 billion in AI semiconductor sales in fiscal 2027, and that number is expected to double to $230 billion in its fiscal 2028.

Broadcom also expects more than $30 in earnings per share (EPS) in its fiscal 2028. That implies a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio below 10 at current levels. The fact that Broadcom guided for fiscal 2028 shows that chip sales are still heating up.

Democrat victories in the midterm do not mean that the AI buildout is canceled. Regulations may get stricter, and future tax breaks may be less generous, but it's extremely unlikely that Democrats order a complete halt for the entire industry.

The AI buildout isn't just producing data centers. It's also creating jobs, and data center developers are investing directly into communities.

Even if Democrats clamp down on data centers harder than expected, especially in Texas, tech giants have been investing in international data centers for years. For example, Alphabet recently announced a $15 billion investment in a Malaysian data center.

Hyperscalers have been more than happy to buy AI chips, including the ones that Broadcom creates. While Nvidia is the undisputed leader in the GPU industry, Broadcom has established itself as the firm leader in the Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) industry.

Whether large data centers are primarily built in the U.S. or international countries in the future remains anyone's guess, but all of those facilities will need chips. Tech spending isn't going down and continues to trend upward. Broadcom remains well-positioned in the AI boom despite the ongoing correction.

Matteo Thun designs Torre Velasca interiors using "building itself as the starting point"

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Alberto Strada (unless stated otherwise)

Italian architect Matteo Thun looked to the original materials and colours of Milan’s iconic Torre Velasca when creating its new interiors, which feature pink hues inspired by the tower’s dusky facade.

Thun has designed 72 flats in the upper floors of the tower, ranging from suites to duplex penthouses. He approached the interior design of the skyscraper, built in the 1950s by the architecture studio BBPR, with a focus on reinterpretation.

“We allowed the masters and the building itself to speak, imagining a way of living within it that would carry the building a little further into the future, adding a new chapter to its history,” Thun told Dezeen.

“We did not seek contrast: our design was informed directly by the materials and colours of Torre Velasca, and we chose not to intervene where we felt the architecture had already achieved a sense of completeness.”

His studio preserved original details, including doors, window frames, loggias and tiles in the bathrooms and kitchens. These were then juxtaposed with modern furniture and evergreen design classics.

“The interiors bring together new furniture designed by the studio and design references spanning the 1950s to the 1980s, including iconic pieces developed in new colourways in collaboration with historic Milanese companies,” Thun said.

“With a deep appreciation for craftsmanship and a strong attention to detail, these designs share an ability to evolve without losing their identity.”

The colour palette, too, was inspired by the original hues of the Torre Velasca.

In the living rooms, walls are covered in custom-made velvet wall coverings by textile company Dedar in a pale pink colour that resembles the pink plaster on Torre Velasca’s facade.

“The building itself was our starting point,” Thun said.

“The pink plaster, developed and patented specifically for the tower, together with the blue and green floor tiles found both inside and in the loggias, formed the basis of our colour palette,” he continued.

“We developed different gradations of these tones, opting for darker shades of green and blue to define the entrance areas and bedrooms. “

As well as the apartments, Thun also designed the interior of the reception, lobby and the corridors on the ground floor.

And while this was an interior project, Thun says it ended up becoming “a lesson in architecture” for his studio.

“Working with a building as distinctive as Torre Velasca challenged us to think and work first and foremost as architects,” Thun said.

“The challenge was not to reinvent the building, but to find ways to make its original character feel relevant today, while remaining true to BBPR’s vision,” he continued.

“In this sense, the project became a process of discovery, with the building itself serving as our greatest teacher. It was, in many ways, a lesson in architecture for us.”

Thun has previously designed the interior of the boutique hotel The Julius Prague and “hotel-like” rooms for a German hospital.

TL;DR: Leap, described by Solana Labs as a mobile trading app for tokenised stocks, ETFs, metals, currencies and crypto, launched in early-access beta on iOS on November 5. The app brings several asset categories into one interface, but each token’s legal rights and user eligibility still need separate examination.

Solana’s tokenised-asset ambitions are moving into another consumer-facing interface. In a November 5 announcement distributed by Solana Labs, Leap said it had launched an early-access iOS beta app for trading multiple kinds of assets represented onchain.

The offering brings tokenised stocks and exchange-traded funds alongside metals, currencies and cryptocurrencies, with the stated goal of providing market context, clearer execution and detailed profit-and-loss tracking. Leap says it was built by the team behind Dialect.

Putting assets in the same app can make switching between markets easier for users, but the underlying instruments may remain quite different. A conventional listed share, a tokenised entitlement to a share, a cryptocurrency and a claim linked to a commodity can involve different legal rights, custodians and market hours.

The announcement should therefore be read as an application launch, not proof that every listed security has been made directly transferable to every wallet or that all asset classes operate under identical rules.

NewsBTC examined the NYSE’s planned tokenised securities infrastructure, highlighting the importance of transfer agents, corporate actions and investor rights. Those questions still matter when trading takes place through a polished phone screen rather than an institutional desk.

Leap’s November 5 release describes early access on iOS, not unrestricted public availability across all devices and countries. Beta launches allow developers to test user experience and market integrations, but they also mean functionality, supported regions and service rules can change.

The company highlights informed trading, market information and precise P&L presentation. Those features can help users understand orders, yet the accuracy of a dashboard depends on price feeds, cost-basis tracking and the availability of underlying markets. A clear interface is a benefit only if it communicates the risks, fees and settlement terms faithfully.

Liquidity also deserves attention. A tokenised equity can be technically transferable on a blockchain while the market for it remains thin. Bid-ask spreads, redemption arrangements and the ability to exit positions can differ from the traditional market in the underlying asset.

The wider Solana ecosystem has been expanding its settlement and tokenised-market infrastructure. NewsBTC’s reporting on Solana’s delivery-versus-payment tool examines the institutional side of this effort. Leap is attempting to bring related activity to a consumer trading experience.

That does not by itself guarantee meaningful network revenue or token demand. App adoption should be measured through active users, actual trades, repeat usage and transparent liquidity conditions, rather than the breadth of assets named in a launch release.

The next useful disclosures will be supported jurisdictions, precise asset rights, custody arrangements, fee schedules, trading hours and the move from beta to wider distribution. Leap has put a new interface in users’ hands. The larger test is whether the financial products behind that interface are as clear and dependable as the app aims to be.
