GTA 6 Has Already Sold Nearly 8 Million Copies, One Data Firm Claims

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Grand Theft Auto 6Grand Theft Auto VIGTA 6PS5Rockstar GamesSalesXbox Series X/S

We are two weeks out from the start of Grand Theft Auto 6‘s global launch, and one data firm is claiming it’s already sold nearly 8 million copies. Not surprisingly, it also claims roughly three quarters of those sales are on PlayStation 5.

Data firm Sensor Tower has updated its GTA 6 sales tracker and currently has Rockstar Games’ latest open-world crime simulator at an estimated 7.71 million copies sold via pre-orders alone. That comes out to an estimated $751 million in revenue, with Sensor Tower claiming a breakdown of roughly 74 percent of copies sold on PS5 compared to 26 percent on Xbox Series X/S.

The trendline shows an uptick in the rate of pre-orders sold following the extended gameplay reveal on Netflix, and it sure seems like Sony’s exclusive marketing campaign for GTA 6 has helped give it a bump over Xbox. The fact that a base PS5 is cheaper than a base Xbox Series X could also be a factor. According to Sensor Tower, 1.97 million copies have been pre-ordered on Xbox compared to 5.74 million on PS5.

Given Xbox’s struggles this console generation, that number isn’t great, but it could be much worse. Especially when it has to resort to sticking hardware inside of an alligator to get around marketing prohibitions on talking about GTA 6 coming to Xbox Series X/S.

To put the overall estimate of 7.71 million pre-order sales in perspective, Cyberpunk 2077 reportedly amassed around 8 million total pre-orders in the run up to its 2020 launch. That number also already puts it just behind Resident Evil Requiem‘s estimated 8.5 million sales which, for now at least, are enough to make Capcom’s horror sequel the second best-selling game of 2026 in the U.S. so far.

And it’s just a small chunk of what some analysts are predicting for total launch window sales. Financial services firm Piper Sandler is predicting 45 million sales when GTA 6 comes out. Anything less might be seen as disappointing, even if it would still be well over the $800 million GTA 5 made during its first 24 hours. It’s going to be the biggest game launch ever. We’ll see if it ends up being big enough for Take-Two’s investors.

This California Tuning Shop Built an Insane Lexus GX–Toyota Tacoma Off-Road Pickup Truck

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The “Lexus TX1” is equal parts GX 550, Taco, and trophy truck.

The Toyota Tacoma is one of the most popular mid-size pickup trucks on sale in America today, with a wide range of variants. But what if there were a luxury version? What if it were built by Lexus? California tuning shop Meso Customs has brought an answer to that line of questioning with its 2026 SEMA Show build, and the results are surprisingly compelling. It’s called the Lexus TX1, and it is immediately recognizable as the forbidden love child of a Lexus GX 550 and a fourth-generation Toyota Tacoma.

As Toyota’s luxury wing, Lexus does not have a pickup in its lineup; premium trucks tend to sell best when they have non-luxury badges. That said, the TX1 features horizontal grille slats and a prominent silver Lexus badge that somehow all seem to blend in and work together well. On top of all that, a rally-style light bar on top of the windshield and a high-clearance metal front bumper show the TX1 means business.

The project started as a CGI rendering, according before evolving into a full build a year later, with Meso Customs using CAD designs and fabricated individual pieces to make the TX1 a reality. But this isn’t a simple body kit build, either. The Yuba City, California-based shop added a lift kit from King Off-Road Racing Shocks, 74Weld portal axles, and 38-inch Toyo Tires.

The rear end is equally stunning, with a straight-across LED light bar from the GX550 and a tucked bumper. The bed was actually custom-built to incorporate the shape of a GX hatch into the Tacoma shell, while Baja Designs lights were integrated underneath and to the side of the wing mirrors. Meso Customs even displayed the TX1 with a color-matched Honda Grom in the bed, to show just how practical a Lexus pickup truck could be.

Of course, the TX1 build was not officially sponsored by Toyota or Lexus, so don’t expect a luxury-style Tacoma derivative from the Japanese automaker anytime soon. Even so, the idea that Lexus could build a proper off-road pickup with a luxurious interior is one fun fantasy. And if you’re inclined to see it in person, Meso Customs currently has the Lexus TX1 on display at the Toyo Tires Treadpass exhibit at the 2026 SEMA Show.

What I Didn’t Tell You About My Flip-Flop

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I left off writing at the edge of the 100-Mile Wilderness. I planned two more posts, one about the Hundred-Mile, the last about Katahdin, but I couldn’t write them.

After Katahdin I was home, as I’d planned to be. I’d be back in Wisconsin a week, get another cortisone shot for my arthritic knee, spend time with my wife, see friends, then catch Amtrak back to Pawling and push south to Springer.

That was the plan, the one I told you about. But the truth is, by the time I hit Maine, I’d stopped believing it.

I had my reasons, and they were good ones.

Heading south would have meant finishing in December and a flip within my flop to get through the Smokies before snow turned the trail into a post-holing nightmare.

Then there were my spring classes to prep (I still teach), my wife’s birthday, an election to vote in, and one more Boundary Waters trip to take. Plus, I looked forward to resuming my barista job. I was excited about improving my latte art and counter skills.

Better to come back next year, I told myself. Better weather, more time, and a straight shot to Georgia. I would finish the first week of November. Perfect.

I’d thru-hiked the PCT and the CDT. I was fine doing the AT as a two-part LASH. I had nothing to prove.

Unlike Killington in 2024, getting off the AT this time wasn’t a surrender. It was a conscious choice. I would be back next summer, but I would return as a section hiker. And to my surprise, that mattered. A lot.

Many of us deal with depression after getting off trail. I am a member of this sad clan. On trail I made new friends, shared parts of myself that hadn’t seen the light of day in years, shed pounds, and became crazy-fit while eating like a joyful pig.

Being on trail expanded my life and gave it purpose by shrinking it. The daily routine of miles and chores, the resupply stops of splurge and cleansing, and the slow accretion of accomplishment.

But now, back home and staying, that bigger life has abruptly ended. The better version of myself, the one who crushed miles despite the bad joints, is slowly fading.

I’ve been following Guru on Instagram, the guy in the kilt I hiked the 100-Mile Wilderness and summited Katahdin with. Seeing his photos of autumn sunsets, fall-stripped trees and leaf-strewn trails, I can smell the damp leaves, feel the cold air. I think, “That could have been me.”

Getting through the Whites showed me what my old body could do. My confidence only grew as I clambered through the Mahoosucs, the Bigelows, and hard sections with no name. I flew over the roots and rocks of Maine, nearly ecstatic. When I climbed Katahdin, I was at my peak.

There are no guarantees I’ll come back and finish. Not for lack of desire, but because of my waning ability. My arthritis, my stamina, and who knows what other shoe might drop between now and next July.

I now believe I should have kept going. It’s not just that I can’t trust my body; it’s like I broke a promise to myself.

When I set out to hike the Triple Crown in 2022, the subtext read “thru-hike.” My dad’s passing, and my AT attempt in 2024 that followed, changed the rules. It seemed that just finishing, no matter the method, was all that mattered.

I am a thru-hiker. An identity of which I am ridiculously proud. I’ve had my face wind-blasted raw below whooshing turbines outside Tehachapi, been chased by wildfires through Oregon, and dragged a norovirus-weakened body through the Cascades. On the CDT, I pushed through joint pain requiring oral steroids in the Wind River Range and dunked frost-stiffened shoes into the Gila River so I could jam my frozen feet into them. To get off trail 6,000 miles into my Triple Crown, even by choice, now feels like a betrayal of what I set out to do.

It took me a long time to write this post. I’d planned to keep writing my way to Katahdin, a story I still need to tell. But I had to come clean first. Thank you for following me this far.

Next time, I’ll tell you about the Hundred-Mile.

andrés jaque questions the politics of utopia in an age of permanent connectivity

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

from archigram’s technological utopias to today’s infrastructures of control, OFFPOLINN's founder explores how architecture can challenge the systems shaping our collective and environmental realities.

Utopias have a tendency to become less interesting as they become real.  At first, they are exhilarating. They overturn conventions, ridicule authorities, and embarrass the present. Their authors anticipate what everyone else has not yet learned to desire. But sometimes, they happen. Archigram. Its visions of technological freedom, mobile architectures, continuous connectivity, and cities emancipated from fixed institutions were among the twentieth century’s most energizing propositions. Their irreverence was inseparable from their architects’ intelligence and biological vitality. Young minds were having enormous fun imagining the demolition of established orders, their hearts racing at possible worlds.

Now consider permanent connectivity. Work finds us everywhere. Platforms promise choice while concentrating power. Technologies celebrate freedom while organizing surveillance, extraction, segregation, and uninterrupted availability. The promise of mobility encounters selective and segregation-making fortified borders. The promise of flexibility encounters a housing crisis that displaces millions. Sophisticated computation accompanies warfare and the destruction of cities. Networks promising universal knowledge also industrialize disinformation.

What once looked like liberation can become an infrastructure of domination. Utopia did not simply fail. Its realization changed the politics of its promises. A utopia does not carry its politics intact into the future.

Blaming Archigram would be absurd. But the resemblance is disturbing. What once looked like liberation can become an infrastructure of domination. Utopia did not simply fail. Its realization changed the politics of its promises. A utopia does not carry its politics intact into the future. Something else has changed. Today’s crises emerge through interactions among computational systems, biological processes, economic institutions, and planetary ecologies. Housing is shaped by financial algorithms, property regimes, migration, and bodies struggling to survive. Climate instability connects atmospheric processes, energy infrastructures, industrial production, governance and predictive computation. Biodiversity loss involves agriculture, logistics, microbial ecologies, and land finance. Warfare combines automated detection with the destruction of bodies, habitats, and infrastructures.

These are not separate technological, social, and environmental problems. They are posthuman enactments in which computation and biology, sensing and decision-making, are inseparable. We struggle to imagine our participation in transformations whose production exceeds what we can sense. Yet we participate constantly. This makes experimentation at the thresholds of sensing an architectural question.

Experimenting now means entering the conflicts through which reality is being produced, rather than imagining alternatives from a safe distance. It means challenging what computational systems recognize, what institutions exclude, what bodies endure, and what other forms of life make possible. It means transforming not only what we know, but who and what can produce knowledge, intervene in decisions, and redirect their consequences. The radicality of architecture lies in making reality itself a site of collective experimentation, where the power to sense, contest, and transform is not merely imagined or proposed but enacted and bodyenvironmentally lived.

Andrés Jaque is a Spanish architect, researcher, and educator, and the founder of the Office for Political Innovation (OFFPOLINN), an international architecture practice established in 2003 and based in New York and Madrid. His work approaches architecture as a political and environmental practice, examining the complex relationships between people, technology, ecology, and the built environment. Moving between architecture, research, performance, and exhibition-making, Jaque explores how design can make visible the social and environmental systems that shape everyday life. He is Professor and Dean of Columbia University’s Graduate School of Architecture, Planning and Preservation. His distinctions include the 2016 Frederick Kiesler Prize for Architecture and the Arts and the Silver Lion for Best Research Project at the 14th Venice Architecture Biennale.

Star Trek Cast A Real-Life American Hero In The Perfect Role

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In the image above, you'll see real-life Georgian politician Stacey Abrams, the minority leader of the state's House of Representatives from 2011 through 2017. She played the president of a united Earth in a notable 2022 episode of "Star Trek: Discovery."

The final episode of the fourth season of "Discovery" was called "Coming Home," and it wrapped up a story arc involving the mysterious Species 10-C, an alien species so cognitively and physiologically different from average humanoids that communication proved near-impossible. The species was so different, in fact, it took months of research to get them right.

Species 10-C was responsible for unleashing a widespread gravitational cataclysm called the Dark Matter Anomaly, which was unwittingly poised to do untold destruction on Earth and other worlds. A lot of diplomacy and panic were involved in trying to contact Species 10-C and explain to them what they were doing. /Film reviewed the season, and found it to be a tricky balance between the cosmic and the personal.

Throughout the season, there is chatter about evacuating Earth, and mention of the planet's president. In the 32nd century, when "Discovery" takes place, Earth will still be united, and will have a singular presidential figure overseeing its government. The president appears at the end of "Coming Home" to put a button on the whole season, reiterating that communication and diplomacy are very, very good indeed. It's wholly fitting that a real-life politician like Stacey Abrams should be willing to appear as that character.

Abrams famously ran for governor of Georgia in 2018, but narrowly lost to Republican Brian Kemp. She ran again in 2022, but lost to Kemp a second time. She has long been an outspoken advocate for voting rights, and founded the organization Fair Fight Action, which aims to fight against voter suppression. She's quite well-suited to appear in the futuristic utopia of "Star Trek."

The end of "Coming Home" involves Earth rejoining the United Federation of Planets, an organization that had fallen by the wayside in the previous few centuries. Stacey Abrams has a scene with Captain Michael Burnham (Sonequa Martin-Green), the show's lead character in which the President of United Earth is presented as somewhat regal, although Abrams, not an actress, brings a grounded officiousness to the role as well. When she says, "United Earth is ready right now to rejoin the Federation, and nothing could make me happier than to say those words," it sounds like a speech that a 21st-century Earth politician might say.

Martin-Green was blown away to be working with Abrams, something she gushed about in an interview with Deadline. She said:

"I'm still floored when I think about Stacey gracing us with her presence in our Season 4 finale. [...] We spent so much of our time fanning out and pretending to be cool in her midst. [...] She's a legend in the making and a civil hero, and she was an absolute delight to work with. [...] She wowed us with her charm, humility, and generosity, and she whipped out some acting chops too! [...] It was an honor for me as a Black woman to stand with her in the story. It's an experience I'll cherish for the rest of my life."

Abrams is cherished throughout the American political landscape. In 2019, she was selected to deliver the response to the President's State of the Union address, and she was even buzzed about as a potential running mate for presidential candidate Joe Biden. The New York Times cited Abrams as a major influence on boosting voter turnout in her state.

Stacey Abrams seems to believe that everyone should have a voice in American elections, which is a philosophy that falls very neatly in line with the overall egalitarian thinking behind "Star Trek." If anyone was to be present for Earth to rejoin the Federation, Abrams certainly fit the bill.

According to The Hollywood Reporter, Abrams has long been a fan of "Star Trek," and was well aware of the rise of Captain Michael Burnham. Abrams wanted to be involved in "Star Trek: Discovery" no matter what plotline was being explored. She posted a video to her Twitter/X account, and was quoted as saying:

"Whatever the story was, I wanted to be a part of it because watching Michael Burnham come into her power as captain, watching this party-crew reclaim itself — not only in a new era but in a new space that is so reminiscent of what they remembered but so different — that, to me, speaks of why we do the work we do in politics. [...] It's about giving people hope and giving them a better life and creating this vision of what is possible."

Abrams has long stood up for justice, fairness, and equality. One can find ample evidence online tracing her stances on issues like criminal justice reform, her belief in the power of public education, and her need to address her state's maternal health crisis. She possesses a progressive standpoint politically, which is fitting for the overall progressive attitudes of "Star Trek, so this was smart casting. As we mentioned in our own article ranking the best "Star Trek" guest stars, Abrams and "Star Trek" both remind us that the future can be better.

A Meeting With Elizabeth Holmes

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Posted November 4, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

Like many Americans, I recently watched You Can See Everything, the documentary that follows Elizabeth Holmes through the weeks before she reported to prison. I left the theater fascinated and bewildered. Throughout the movie, I kept asking the same question the filmmakers seem to be asking: Am I being deceived, or am I missing something because I don't know enough?

I have asked myself that question about Holmes before. In July 2013, I was the Deputy Commissioner for the New York City Health Department, where I oversaw the city's infectious disease programs and the public health laboratory. City Hall asked me to meet with a promising new lab-testing company; a few weeks later, I joined an hour-long video call with Holmes, her team, and our lab director.

I still have my notes. Holmes and her colleagues told us that their company, Theranos, had developed a more convenient way to test blood in small amounts that would cut testing costs by 30 percent.

A wide range of tests had been validatedFederal and New York State regulatory approvals were completeThey secured a Department of Defense contract. Testing was scheduled to launch at New York Walgreens pharmacies within months.

My colleague and I asked repeatedly about the underlying science and data. Every time, we got the same answer: they could not disclose business secrets, but everything worked and was approved. Rest assured.

When the call ended, my colleague and I were dumbfounded. Nothing we knew about collecting specimens, validating tests, or getting regulatory approval made their story plausible. And yet I doubted myself. Holmes spoke with such composure and certainty and had such powerful connections and investors that I kept wondering: Did she know something about science or lab regulations that I didn't?

We offered to evaluate the device in our lab before any wider use. Neither side ever followed up. When the Wall Street Journal published its first investigation into the company in October 2015, I called my colleague, and we both said the same thing: "We knew it!" Most of what she said in that meeting was not true.

After watching the movie, I kept thinking about that incident and how it raised a hard healthcare problem: knowing when to trust your own expertise and when to defer to someone else's.

We assume that people who sound certain know more than we do. This is particularly common when checking a person’s claims is difficult or costly. That describes the Theranos meeting almost exactly. We could not check her claims because she said her data was confidential, and she spoke with absolute certainty.

While the lack of data made me suspicious, I fell prey to something known as “the halo effect,” where people with credibility in one domain are often thought to be credible experts in other domains. Theranos had funders and board members who had extremely prestigious backgrounds in government and the military. Even though none of them had a background in laboratory medicine, I assumed they had done sufficient due diligence on the technology.

A big challenge in health: The body is so complex that none of us can be experts in everything. As a physician, if I order an MRI on a patient, I rely on the radiologist to interpret it correctly. When I order a blood test, I rely on laboratory technicians to use validated methods and equipment. I could not do my job if I had to personally verify that everyone knows what they are doing and is doing it correctly. The need to trust others is even greater for patients, because most of them will not be medical experts.

The problem, of course, is that humans are imperfect. Medical errors are, unfortunately, quite common. Someone did something wrong—errors of commission; someone forgot to do something—errors of omission. And, because medical knowledge is always growing, something correct 10 years ago may be incorrect now. I always encourage people to ask their healthcare providers questions and, with major health decisions, to seek second opinions.

How do you decide when and whom to trust? Here are a few questions you can ask whether you are a patient talking to a doctor or interpreting health claims online.

First, is this an area where you have real knowledge and experience? If so, give your own judgment more weight than you are inclined to, even when the person across from you is more confident than you are.

Second, does their authority come from the relevant field? A famous name, a large social media following, or impressive business credentials do not mean they understand how a vaccine works or what a blood test measures.

Third, is the other person offering evidence or only certainty? Consider asking, “What is the evidence that supports this approach? Is this what expert guidelines recommend?” Trustworthy experts can explain their reasoning or point you to high-quality studies or published guidelines to check their claims.

Fourth, has anyone independently checked? Has it been approved by the FDA or another government regulatory agency? Or do major professional organizations recommend it?

We need our health experts and institutions to see everything—the methods and the data—to make sure medications, vaccines, and other health interventions are safe and effective. But when someone asks for our trust and won’t back it up with data, we need to consider trusting our own judgment or asking for a second opinion.
