Rio Ngumoha ready to make England splash as he spells out Ballon d’Or ambition

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Fearless winger has forced his way into Thomas Tuchel’s plans and believes he can become best player in the world

Rio Ngumoha is fearless, exciting and ready to make his mark as an England player. The Liverpool winger narrowly missed the cut for the World Cup squad last summer but he impressed during the pre-tournament camp in Florida and has forced his way into Thomas Tuchel’s plans after an excellent start to the season.

There is an opportunity for the 18-year-old to advance his case with Bukayo Saka injured and more experienced players left out for the Nations League games against Croatia and Spain this week. Ngumoha, who is dangerous on both flanks, has a chance of starting when Croatia visit Wembley on Thursday and would love to test himself against Lamine Yamal when England face Spain in Madrid on Sunday.

“What he’s doing changed football for young players,” Ngumoha says of Barcelona’s 19-year-old winger. “He’s phenomenal. He’s one of the best in the world. Seeing what he can do so young inspires me and all the other young players in how we can just play freely. The way he plays, it’s like it’s his pitch. It’s a good challenge.”

Ngumoha has rarely been afraid to test himself. One of the few times he has shown trepidation was when he was a small boy, impressing at grassroots level, and was asked to play with an older age group. “I was six, maybe five, playing for a local team,” he says. “I got an assist and I could hear my mum on the side saying well done. Then they started saying that I was playing with the older players and I just panicked and started crying.

“I walked off the pitch because I’m so scared because everyone was older than me. Then I realised after I could definitely have competed with them, so I’ve toughened up a bit.”

It has been a winding journey from those games in east London. Ngumoha travelled across the capital to attend Chelsea training sessions after school. It meant getting home late but it was a necessary sacrifice for a player who could always count on the support of family and friends.

England have called up the Bournemouth defender James Hill with the usual centre-back pairing of Marc Guéhi and Ezri Konsa ruled out of Thursday’s game at home to Croatia.

It is a first senior call-up for the 24-year-old Hill, who played for the under-21s and under-20s. Guéhi withdrew after sustaining an injury in Manchester City’s Premier League defeat at Nottingham Forest on Saturday, leading to Dan Burn being summoned on Monday, and Konsa will not be able to face Croatia as he follows a fitness programme. The Arsenal defender is expected to be available for Sunday’s game in Spain.

Hill has played 15 times this season for Bournemouth, whom he joined from Fleetwood in 2022. He will be competing with Jarell Quansah, Jarrad Branthwaite and Burn for a spot in central defence. Press Association

Ngumoha was pushed by his older brother, James, who now acts as his agent. He smiles as he thinks about his family. It would be easy for Ngumoha to let fame go to his head. He caused a stir when, aged 16, he marked his Premier League debut by scoring a sensational last-minute winner for Liverpool against Newcastle last season, but he did not get carried away. He leaned on his support network, worked on blocking out the noise and paid little attention to comments on social media.

There is so much buzz around young players now. Arsenal are coping with the hype around Max Dowman and Liverpool have to be protective of Ngumoha, who caused a controversy when he joined them from Chelsea’s academy in 2024.

Chelsea fumed at the deal and a tribunal ordered Liverpool to pay a minimum of £2.8m but Ngumoha was set on the move because he saw a clearer route into the first team at Anfield. “I feel like I needed a new challenge,” he says. “Liverpool gave me a new pathway. The plan that they set for me was straightforward. It was a no-brainer. They just said if I prove myself then they know that I would definitely be a star for them in the making.”

Ngumoha moved north. He lives with his brother and his mum, Liz. “The hardest thing for her has been moving up to Liverpool,” Ngumoha says. “She left her job to move up. She worked in a school. But I think she’s enjoying it as well. She goes back whenever she can. But she does everything for me and my brother. She’s just amazing. I can’t thank her enough.”

Ngumoha smiles again and says his mum will definitely be reading this interview. She will be pleased to learn her son charmed the media with his warmth and intelligence. It does not feel brash when Ngumoha, who counts Neymar, Eden Hazard and Mohamed Salah as inspirations, says he can win the Ballon d’Or one day.

“Harry Kane winning the Ballon d’Or is definitely a big, thing,” he says. “It’s also helped English football now because everyone’s seen one of us win the Ballon d’Or, it is definitely encouraging many more people to want to be known as the best player in the world. For me, I would like to achieve it and I think I can do it.”

Ngumoha feels at ease. This is his third England camp. He remembers turning up for the first time and feeling too shy to speak to Kane. Yet Ngumoha starred on his debut, impressing as a substitute in a World Cup warm-up win against New Zealand in Tampa, to the extent that Tuchel wondered whether he should have picked the youngster in his tournament 26.

It has become a trend for wingers to check back and play the easy pass but Ngumoha always wants to beat his full-back. “I’m quite fearless. If there’s an opportunity to beat my man I’ll always do it. It was the way I came up, playing in cages near my house. I’ve never really been told to stop. I like getting people off their seat.”

Ngumoha will hope to provide the ammunition for Kane. He raves about England’s captain, praising Kane as a person and his dedication in training. Ngumoha has been watching closely and he soon felt the nerves disappear. “It was from the first training session. The shyness goes from the first time you touch the ball and step on the pitch. Then you realise it’s just football and you can play the way you play.”

AI’s future starts to look surprisingly old-school: theCUBE’s KubeCon day one keynote analysis

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The cloud-native community that turned Kubernetes into an enterprise standard is now being asked to carry artificial intelligence into production. As the underlying technology looks more alike from one company to the next, the hunt for domain-specific AI value is spotlighting how AI is put to work – and how to do so safely.

That shift is on display this week in Salt Lake City, where the Cloud Native Computing Foundation has added a dedicated AI inference and agentic track to its flagship conference. The plumbing built for microservices is proving to be the substrate for AI factories and agents, along with a renewed focus on tried-and-tested security and business principles, according to John Furrier (pictured, left), co-founder and executive analyst of theCUBE Research.

“The AI-native world that we’ve been covering and following and living — AI factories, the rise of agentic, which involves coding and now tasks — are all based fundamentally on the substrate of cloud-native technologies,” Furrier said. “This community is the heart and soul for the AI generation.”

Furrier spoke with theCUBE’s Rebecca Knight (right) as a part of a keynote analysis at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon NA, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how enterprises can secure and govern AI agents while turning proprietary knowledge and workflows into measurable business value rather than simply maximizing token consumption. (* Disclosure below.)

Kubernetes has matured from a niche orchestration project into the reference platform for major clouds and neoclouds, and enterprises are now standardizing on it. That maturity is what makes it useful for AI, which needs the same control planes, scalable data layers and telemetry the community already worked out for microservices, according to Furrier.

“Kubernetes got boring, which means it’s working,” Furrier said. “A lot of things that are happening in cloud-native that were involved in microservices in Kubernetes world are actually the same challenges in the AI world.”

Those lessons now apply to a pressing question: how much freedom enterprises will give AI agents inside real systems. Agents that cause trouble are usually poorly instructed rather than malicious, and the answer lies in context, identity and guardrails, according to Furrier. Perimeter-style security thinking is expected to make a comeback, along with a move away from token consumption as a measure of value and toward tangible business outcomes.

“We’re gonna get back to an era of, ‘Build something, show me what you built, present it for value,'” Furrier said. “If it’s worth something, there’s an exchange for value.”

Rio Ngumoha has revealed why he chose to leave Chelsea and move to Liverpool, branding the decision a “no-brainer”.

The teenage winger joined Chelsea’s academy - world-renowned for its youth development - at the age of eight and became one of the club’s most-prized starlets, with academy coaches previously stating that they believed Ngumoha could win the Ballon d’Or.

But at 16, Ngumoha left Chelsea and joined Liverpool, who oversaw the last of his development in junior ranks before watching him burst onto the scene at senior level.

Chelsea were disgruntled with the nature of Ngumoha’s exit and Liverpool were told in February to pay an initial £2.8m in compensation by a tribunal, with potential for that sum to rise to £6.8m with various add-ons.

Nevertheless, with Ngumoha now a regular under Liverpool boss Andoni Iraola after a breakout season last term, the 18-year-old has explained the reasoning behind his transfer decision and asserts he has no regrets over the switch.

“I feel like I needed a new challenge,” he said. “I feel like if I stayed at Chelsea... not saying that I wouldn't have got to the first team as quick, because I believe in my ability, but I feel like Liverpool just gave me a new pathway.

“The plan that they set for me was straightforward really. For me, it was a no-brainer going to Liverpool, and they just said if I prove myself, because they know how much ability I had, then they know that I would definitely be a first-team player and definitely a star for them in the making.

“I think going to Liverpool so far has been the right choice.”

While Ngumoha only crossed paths with the senior Chelsea team a handful of times during his time at Cobham, he reflects on an interaction he had with Raheem Sterling - who was similarly once a teenage prodigy - when training with the seniors.

“I trained with them only a few times but Raheem Sterling pulled me to the side a couple of times and told me he could see I had talent but I should never zone out in the game and I should always be in the game,” he said.

“The best players are always in the game in the last minutes. If you look at him and Mo [Salah], they’re always there until the crucial moments. Learning off them was important.”

Ngumoha has featured 14 times for Liverpool so far this season across all competitions, notching two assists which both came in a standout Champions League performance in the 4-3 win over Villarreal.

He was hailed by pundits and fans alike after that match, with Liverpool supporters chanting his name as he was substituted.

“Like I always say, Liverpool, we have the best fans in the world, especially Anfield on Champions League nights against big teams,” Ngumoha added.

“The way it roars, I've never heard anything like it. It's special. It's definitely special to play in front of them.”

Ngumoha earned his second call-up to the England squad for the November international break, again entrusted by Thomas Tuchel as the youngest player in his roster.

He could make his first England start in the coming days as the Three Lions see out their Nations League group-stage campaign, first with the visit of Croatia on Thursday before travelling to Madrid to face World Cup winners Spain on Saturday.

The latter will present him the opportunity to share the pitch with another winger sensation in Lamine Yamal, who was pipped to the Ballon d’Or by England captain Harry Kane last month.

Asked about that prospect, Ngumoha said: “I think what he’s doing changed football for young player. He’s phenomenal. He’s one of the best in the world.

“Seeing what he can do so young inspires me and all the other young players in how we can just play freely. The way he plays, it’s like it’s his pitch. It’s a good challenge.”

Archaeologists Found 2,500-Year-Old Battle Lines From a Conflict No One Knew Ever Happened

The Iron Age palisade likely blocked an invading army. But who built it—and who were they afraid of?

Archaeologists sifting through portions of Denmark’s western peninsula ahead of a highway expansion keep uncovering remnants of the area’s past, often in small snapshots: a grave here, an axe there, or maybe sometimes even the foundation of an ancient home. They certainly weren’t expecting to find the remains of battle lines in West Jutland, indicating a conflict they didn’t know ever transpired.

“The find came as a complete surprise to us,” Michael Alrø Jensen, curator at Museum Vest, said in a translated statement. “It points to previously unknown conflicts in West Jutland dating back to the earliest part of the Iron Age.”

The find showed up via a 2,500-year-old line of pits, which experts believe fighters created during the Iron Age to block hostile incursions along an ancient West Jutland thoroughfare. The discovery came as archaeologists from the museum examined fields along Route 11, between Varde and Korskro, before the Danish Road Directorate began a highway expansion.

Experts dubbed the battle lines, which were found beneath a field near Schaefergården, a “post-hole alignment,” likely from around 500 B.C.E. The lines feature a long series of closely spaced holes in which one end of pointed wooden posts were buried to form a defensive palisade.

“Our best theory is that there was a fear of an invading army arriving from the north via the ‘Drivvej’ route, and that attempts were therefore made to control and block the passage,” Jensen said.

More evidence may still lie buried at the site. Experts note that the current Route 11 alignment between Varde and Korskro matches the ancient “Drivvej,” or Drove Road, that was the main transportation line through West Jutland for centuries. The modern road project adds an additional lane to the two-lane highway and shows how closely the ancient road—whose origin date remains unknown—matches the modern setup.

Still, the find raises questions. Archaeologists and historians don’t know who developed the defensive structure—or why. The team hopes that additional work in the area may offer fresh clues about the ancient mystery.

Attempts to pinpoint answers to a 2,500-year-old question got a bit muddy as the excavations revealed artifacts from a range of eras in spots that hadn’t been searched previously. Archaeologists now have evidence of human activity along the road from the first Stone Age farmers, who buried their dead in small mounds through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and into the Viking Age (including the discovery of a 4,500-year-old battle axe buried alongside a Stone Age warrior).

“What we are most excited about is that we have found traces from so many different periods within a relatively small area,” Jensen said. “We have suddenly gained a wealth of new pieces that provide much greater insight into how people lived and moved about in this region over several thousand years.”

Every new discovery provides nuance to the historical record, whether the remains of a house, farm, or grave. Museum Vest hopes to learn more by excavating additional burial mounds. The various discoveries in the region together form a narrative of humans’ transformation of the land, first into farming plots and then into villages. “Once our work is complete, we hope to be able to offer the local community a more cohesive narrative about the area they live in,” Jensen said.

He joined Bell Labs in 1961 as a communications researcher, and his early work there focused on speaker identification technology. During the 1960s he helped develop linear predictive coding, a powerful method for analyzing and compressing speech. LPC is now the most widely used method for encoding speech at a low bit rate.

Atal developed increasingly sophisticated low-bit-rate speech-coding techniques. They included multipulse LPC, which improved the quality of compressed speech.

He also worked with his colleague Manfred R. Schroeder to develop code-excited linear prediction, a method for producing high-quality speech at very low bit rates. CELP became a foundational technique in modern speech coding, letting digital communications systems carry voice using less bandwidth. Later in his career, he led the acoustics and speech research departments at Bell.

The ice giants' skewed magnetic fields are a window into the high-pressure, high-temperature environment deep inside Uranus and Neptune.

The posided magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune may stem from extreme conditions lying within their deep interior. Unravelling the mystery behind the magnetic fields of these worlds is giving us new insight into the little-known interiors of the ice giants.

Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus and Neptune, the Voyager 2 spacecraft which spend past Uranus in January 1986 and Neptune in August 1989. Voyager 2 found that their planetary magnetic fields were, to put it bluntly, wonky. Uranus’ magnetic field was offset from its rotational axis by 59 degrees and isn’t even centered on the planet’s core, but instead is only two-thirds of the way down. Neptune is similar, with a disordered magnetic field offset by 47 degrees and centered halfway between the core and its visible cloud tops.If we compare these planets to Earth, which has a stable magnetic field tilted by just 11 degrees to our planet’s rotation, we can see how extreme the magnetic fields of the ice giants are.

Various explanations for the strange magnetic fields of the ice giants have been put forward over the years. Uranus as a whole is tilted by 97 degrees and effectively rolls around the sun, and speculation is that billions of years ago it was knocked on its side by a collision with a long-lost Earth-sized planet. Could this collision have also skewed its magnetic field?

This might be a plausible explanation were Neptune’s magnetic field also not similarly skewed without having experienced a collision. Clearly, whatever the cause, it must be intrinsic to the nature of the two ice giant planets. Now, a new study by scientists in the U.S. and the UK, based on computer simulations and Voyager 2’s measurements of the two planets’ magnetic fields, may have found a solution.

The solution to the weird magnetic fields of the solar system's ice giants may come down to how planets generate their magnetic field through the dynamo effect. This arises from the swirling, convective motion of an electrically conducting liquid within a rotating body, which is able to generate and maintain a powerful magnetic field.

Inside Earth, the electrically conducting fluid is the molten iron in the outer part of our planet’s core, and the magnetic field that it generates has clear north and south poles – what we call a dipole, a phenomenon that we can also see in the two magnetic poles of a simple bar magnet.

However, the conditions inside Uranus and Neptune are somewhat different. As ice giants, they contain a lot of volatiles – ammonia, hydrogen, helium, methane, and water surrounding a probable rocky core. Furthermore, Uranus and Neptune are substantially larger than Earth, with 14.5 and 17 times the mass of our planet, respectively. This increased mass creates crushing conditions within the interior of the ice giants.

"Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, pressures and temperatures become extremely high," research team leader Chi Yan of the University of Texas at Austin, who led the new findings, said in a statement. "Under those conditions, which can cause water molecules to break apart into ions, the material may become increasingly electrically conductive with depth."

Given the increasing electrical conductivity and the compositions of the two planets, the computer simulations were able to replicate the bizarre magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune. This showed how the magnetic-field structure co-evolves alongside changes in the fluid motion driven by convection and the temperature inside the planets.

It also seems that this co-evolution might not yet be over. The simulations indicate that the magnetic fields of both planets can change quite significantly in the space of just a few decades.

"Earth’s magnetic tilt drifts by only a degree or so over 50 years," said Yan. "Our models show the ice giants can behave very differently. In some stretches, the tilt swings by tens of degrees within decades. In others, it shifts only slightly over the same span."

NASA have prioritized a new mission to return to Uranus in the early 2040s, while the China National Space Administration’s Tianwen-4 mission launching to Jupiter in 2029 will deploy a small probe to carry onwards to Uranus via a gravitational slingshot. Until then, scientists are coming up with creative ways to not have to rely only on the 40-year-old Voyager 2 measurements of the magnetic fields.

For example, Tom Stallard of Northumbria University in the UK, who is a co-author with Yan on the new research paper, has been leading James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations of the faint infrared auroral lights on both Uranus and Neptune. Aurorae are generated when charged particles on the solar wind interact with a planet’s magnetic field, driving some of those charged particles into the atmosphere above the magnetic poles. (Neptune actually has a quadrupolar component, muddling its defined north and south magnetic poles.) As such, the location of aurorae is a good indicator of what’s going on with a planet’s magnetic field.

"We are busy working on what’s contained within these observations, but the shape and structure of the aurora, as well as other weird patterns in the ionosphere [upper atmosphere], promise to provide us with a way of understanding how the magnetic fields have changed, [which is] an important stepping stone," said Stallard.

The hope is that by combining JWST’s observations of the aurora with Voyager 2’s magnetic-field measurements and the results of the computer simulations, it will be possible to estimate the configuration of the magnetic fields on Uranus and Neptune today and see how they compare to the 1980s.

This would provide an experimental test to help show whether the new model describing the magnetic fields is on the right track, although we won’t know for sure until a new mission arrives in the ice giants’ backyard.

The findings were described on 3 November in the journal Communications Physics.

TL;DR: Spot Bitcoin ETFs lost a net $201.0 million on November 9, following $430.2 million on November 5 and $411.9 million on November 6. BlackRock drove Monday’s withdrawals, while one smaller fund reported inflows. The figures measure fund creations and redemptions, not the intentions of every Bitcoin investor.

The latest fully reported Farside figures put the consecutive negative sessions at roughly $1.04 billion in aggregate. Thursday’s $430.2 million loss was followed by Friday’s $411.9 million and then Monday’s $201.0 million. That progression shows persistent selling pressure, although Monday’s net outflow was smaller than either of the previous two sessions.

Our previous report on Friday’s fund activity documented a similar pattern of issuer concentration. Going back further, the November 2 breakdown shows why it is worth examining individual ETF lines rather than reading the aggregate as a single institutional vote.

On November 9, ARK Invest and 21Shares’ ARKB showed a $9.9 million outflow. The positive contribution from MSBT offset part of the selling elsewhere. Several other funds showed no daily net creation or redemption. That does not mean no shares changed hands on exchanges; secondary-market trading and flows into or out of a fund’s underlying Bitcoin exposure are different measures.

BlackRock’s fund is a large and closely watched route into Bitcoin for investors using traditional brokerage and portfolio systems. Its persistent redemptions can therefore affect the tone of market commentary disproportionately. But the identity of an ETF’s end investors is not published in a daily flow table, and a withdrawal cannot automatically be attributed to hedge funds, pensions or retail holders.

The distinction between net flows and assets under management matters as well. A fund’s reported assets can fall when Bitcoin’s price drops even without corresponding redemptions. Conversely, share creations can occur on a day when the asset price weakens. Analysts who blend these concepts can make demand appear stronger or weaker than it actually was.

The next test is whether the large-issuer selling eases over several completed reporting sessions. One positive day would interrupt the streak but would not, by itself, demonstrate that buyers have regained control. It is also worth watching whether inflows broaden across products or remain confined to a single issuer.

These numbers are current through the November 9 US trading session, not an estimate of November 10 while trading is still under way. A better measure of sentiment combines a completed ETF flow series with spot volume, derivatives positioning and the changing rates outlook. For now, the latest evidence is that listed-fund demand remains weak even as the scale of net redemptions has moderated from last week’s peaks.

Talking Point: With Big Walk, The AI-In-Game-Dev Debate Just Got A Lot Murkier

"The line must be drawn here! This far, no further!"

Please forgive the Captain Picard First Contact quote (it's been a couple of weeks since Patrick Stewart's death and only now can I mention his most famous character without welling up) - I promise it's relevant as an illustration of an admirable, principled stance.

In the Borg, good ol' Jean-Luc had a well-defined enemy to engage, with the threat of humanity's assimilation providing a pretty clear line between good and bad. There are larger philosophical questions about the hive mind and our aversion to Star Trek's cube-riding automatons, but for the purposes of this article, let's assume we're all in agreement: Borg=baaad.

Back in the real world, though, that line between right and wrong often isn't so easy to define. Today there'll be a lot of commentators in the video game space, players and developers alike, longing for a better understanding of another encroaching, all-consuming entity.

House House's belated disclosure that LLMs were used during the development of indie hit Big Walk pours fuel on an already raging debate and brings questions over permissible AI use in game dev right back into the discourse - specifically the differences between generative AI for player-facing audio-visual assets and writing versus coding and other less obvious uses.

News Big Walk Devs Issue Statement Over AI Usage

This latest flashpoint followed a statement released by a subset of IGF Award jurors explaining why they won't be voting for games developed using any form of generative AI.

Last year, Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 — which swept The Game Awards with nine wins from 13 nominations — raised similar questions after the Indie Game Awards disqualified Sandfall Interactive's game upon discovering AI had been used in the development pipeline.

I've voiced my distaste for GenAI, and it remains a massive no-no - professionally and personally. I'm fortunate to not have corporate overlords mandating its use around these parts, which certainly isn't the case for some other outlets, nor for many creatives in the gaming space. The gulf in quality between the human-written word and generated bilge is still enormous and easily identifiable, and for now, the same is broadly true of GenAI visual artwork, too.

What this Big Walk situation highlights, however, is how difficult it can be to discern in other places, and the increasing pull it has on even the most principled developers.

The phrase gets bandied around a lot, but it's fair to call Big Walk a proper 'critical darling'. It garnered "Universal Acclaim" back in August, with an impressive 91 Metascore. Alex gave it a "Great" 8/10 after reviewing the Switch 2 version for us - technical hiccups took the shine off it for him, but he still loved the co-op experience. And by September it was already Nile's GOTY.

As someone who didn't play Big Walk at launch, its place in my GOTY-catch-up priorities may have shifted, but wherever you land on the GenAI spectrum, the consensus among critics and players is as unanimous as it gets: Big Walk is an excellent video game.

There are many issues to consider — not least where AI use crosses from tool to theft — but an important one relates to how this game in particular has had a profound effect on many players. Rejecting something flagged as 'suspicious' on principle is easy, especially with so many brilliant games releasing each week. But something you've played already and internalised and loved? From a dev team you respect?

For some, this revelation will feel like when a beloved artist/actor/writer/musician is revealed to be problematic, but we're not talking about illegal behaviour or allegations here.

If House House's statement is to be taken at face value, the team used a chatbot to find a solution to a coding issue with Unity, the third-party engine the game is built on. People online with real chops have already investigated the specifics of House House's single example:

That's the one example the devs offer in their statement, and the language around further usage feels vague. The impression it gives is of a team searching for a solution to a specific problem using common tools, as opposed to vibe-coding huge swathes of the game.

Now, I've never programmed anything in my life, but I can appreciate that efficient code must be an art in itself. Is it any less of an art because that code is invisible to players? Then again, to me, generating great chunks of game code feels very different from using an LLM to squash a bug or find a solution when third-party documentation fails. Is one usage as 'bad' as the other, or is this slippery slope, gateway-drug territory?

(Perhaps if any programmers are reading, they'd like to share thoughts in the comments - as the headline says, this is very much a talking point and I'm keen to explore with as much nuance as the internet will permit.)

My point is that seeing exactly where to draw that line is increasingly difficult when AI tools of all types become ubiquitous. A blanket rejection of anything inevitably now means excluding not just an increasing number of games, but also artistically significant, important ones.

And with executives like former Nintendo of America president Doug Bowser advocating the use of AI for indie teams to improve workflow, it's also easy to understand how a developer might turn to the tech. The whole topic is getting greyer by the day, much to the pleasure of evangelists, I'm sure.

Like it or not, this situation forces AI sceptics to reconcile their positive feelings for the game. House House estimates "well under 0.1% of Big Walk’s code" has any trace of generative AI, but to paraphrase a certain Starfleet captain again, "How much does it take before it becomes wrong, hmm?" That is the question.

For some players who simply don't care, it's a non-issue. Some may reluctantly cut the game from their lives and GOTY lists. I suspect that others, though, disappointed or otherwise, will start reconsidering where they personally draw the AI line.

And if House House hadn't disclosed this, would anyone have ever known? I'd guess not. And if a celebrated indie team turned to AI as a problem-solver, how many other devs aren't disclosing similar usage? And how many sure-as-hell won't do so after this?

Lots of questions, no clear answers, just murk. How unsatisfying. I've heard 'ends-justify-means' sentiments online — 'hey, if the game is good, does it matter?' — which feel unsavoury given the documented, quantifiable damage AI is doing to the games industry and the wider world. It's that damage which has IGF jurors abstaining from voting from Big Walk this year: an understandable, admirable stance.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It was always going to be big, but with a week until release, Rockstar has now confirmed that GTA 6 is the most pre-ordered digital game in PS Store history.

Confirmation comes courtesy of the PS Blog, in a post from Sony’s Tim Turi.

“GTA 6 is now the most pre-ordered game in the history of PS Store. Given this unprecedented demand, we’ve joined with Rockstar Games to allow players to start pre-loading the game nine days early, so they’re ready to play on launch day. We’ve also adopted a rolling midnight launch by country, beginning at 12:00AM NZDT in New Zealand and running across the globe as respective countries hit midnight on 19th November.”

You can learn more about when you’ll be able to play in our dedicated GTA 6 release times guide, so keep that bookmarked for 19th November.

Digital pre-orders for GTA 6 have been available since 25th June, so Rockstar has had a long time to build up numbers.

It’s worth reiterating that physical versions are following a “code-in-box” format, so there’s less incentive to purchase at retail, too.

(Although in a recent Push Square poll, a remarkable 23% of our readers did say they’d be plumping up for the boxed version.)

Record-breaking pre-orders like this are significant as the PS Store has been selling digital games for over a decade now; that it’s set a new record with over a week to spare and hype still building is remarkable.

While we don’t have actual numbers, Rockstar’s said they’re “exceeding” its external expectations.

Analyst firm Sensor Tower predicts 9.69 million pre-orders have been placed, with an astonishing 75% of those sales commanded by the PS5 version.

It’s worth noting that this milestone comes at a time of deep unrest among PS5 players, who’ve continuously spammed the platform holder with proclamations of ‘No Disc, No Buy’ in the aftermath of its decision to stop shipping physical games in 2028.

Sony recently reported year-over-year increases to its monthly active users, during a period in which enthusiasts mobilised a “blackout” of its platform.

Irrespective of fan frustration, it looks like Rockstar’s latest open world is days away from registering the biggest digital launch in industry history.
