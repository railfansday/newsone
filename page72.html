9 Bodily Changes That Happen a Month After Taking Testosterone

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

TRT can bring big changes fast—and some might surprise you.

TESTOSTERONE GOES BEYOND building muscle. T helps you maintain muscle mass, facial and body hair, sex drive, and red blood cell production. So when your T levels start to decline—which happens naturally in your thirties—there’s a whole range of symptoms like low libido or just feeling blue.

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) can help raise T levels. After a couple of weeks to a month, you’ll start to see some changes, some big and small. Some pleasant and not so pleasant. Below, urologists explain what happens to your body a month after taking testosterone.

IF YOU HAVE low T, your doctor will conduct some tests to see if you’re a good candidate for the therapy. Ryan Smith, MD, urologist specializing in men’s health at the University of Virginia Health, says a doctor will perform two blood tests in the morning (on different days) when testosterone is at its highest.

Low is considered under 300 nanograms per deciliter (ng/dL) by some accounts and under 264 ng/dL by others. Doctors also factor in any symptoms you’re having, like trouble getting erections or orgasms, fatigue, or just feeling blah.

Nowadays, testosterone is more accessible without a prescription, with many people only having to complete an online assessment. Leaving out a doctor’s approval and supervision, however, can be harmful, says Justin Dubin, MD, urologist at Baptist Health.

In a 2022 study that Dr. Dubin co-authored, researchers found that many online platforms are not providing testosterone therapy in accordance with the American Urological Association and the Endocrine Society guidelines. Using a secret shopper, the researchers found that the platforms offered therapy to men who didn’t meet the guidelines and didn’t mention the risks or benefits of testosterone therapy.

Working with a doctor ensures that you’re prescribed testosterone only when you need it and that you’re taking the right dose. Then, they can work with you to make sure your testosterone levels respond appropriately. Doctors like to recheck everything every six months to make sure you’re not having any negative side effects.

A number of testosterone options are available. You can choose between injectables, topical gels, creams, under-the-skin pellet therapy, pills, and nasal sprays. You can learn more about them here.

Each comes with its own set of side effects, so it all comes down to your personal preference, health situation, and what your insurance covers. You can read about the real-life experiences of guys of different ages and body types and their experiences with taking testosterone.

IT CAN TAKE a few weeks to a month or two to see your symptoms improve after starting testosterone replacement therapy, says Dr. Smith. Here are some of the side effects—some obvious and some not—that come with testosterone therapy.

When you’re low on testosterone, you might notice your sex drive dip. But when you take TRT, the treatment reactivates the androgen receptors in the part of your brain that controls desire, says Abraham Morgentaler, MD, associate clinical professor of urology at Harvard Medical School and author of Testosterone for Life. Regaining a healthy sex drive is one of the biggest benefits of testosterone replacement therapy, he says.

TRT may also make your erections more satisfying, though this isn’t the only piece of the puzzle. Erections also rely on healthy nerves and blood flow. That means that testosterone therapy by itself won't always help erectile dysfunction.

Muscles respond to testosterone and starting TRT can increase muscle mass, says Dr. Morgentaler. That’s because testosterone activates the androgen receptors in muscle tissue to stimulate growth.

The effect of a testosterone replacement dose on your muscles, however, is fairly minimal. To make the most of this muscle-building benefit, you’ll need to be doing your part through strength training and possibly raising the amount of protein you consume (depending on your current intake). Don’t know where to start? Check out MH’s four-week muscle building guide.

Some men also report fat loss. While testosterone doesn’t directly incite fat loss itself, part of it may be thanks to the uptick in muscle mass. The more muscle you have, the higher your basal metabolic rate (BMR). That means your body will burn more calories at rest.

Another indirect way testosterone can help with fat loss is motivation to keep pushing once you see results. Jed Kaminetsky, MD, urologist at NYU Langone Medical Center, testosterone can increase your energy levels, making it easier to get up and sweat it out.

Fatigue is a common symptom of low T. “When we treat them, a lot of men will say that their energy has improved,” says Dr. Morgentaler.

Scientists studying testosterone aren't sure exactly how the hormone plays a role in energy—just that it does. One of the prevailing hypotheses Dr. Morgentaler can get behind is that testosterone somehow affects your mitochondria (that organelle you learned in high school is the powerhouse of your cells). The theory is that “testosterone turns them on, so they're more productive in terms of creating the energy that the cells need.”

Ronald Tamler, MD, PhD, associate professor of medicine at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, says changes in energy may also be tied back to androgen receptors. “If they don't get sufficient input, that can cause fatigue,” he says. Bringing testosterone levels back up can help reverse this.

Experts don't have a definitive answer to why testosterone impacts mood so deeply—after all, “the brain is a complicated thing,” says Dr. Tamler. But the positive effect of TRT on mood is a potential life-changing benefit.

An old but highly referenced clinical trial in 2003 looked at how testosterone gels affected middle-aged men with treatment-resistant depression and low T. After 8 weeks, the men showed greater improvements in the psychological aspects of depression (sadness, guilt, and anxiety) than those given a placebo. The men taking testosterone gels also showed nearly the same rate of improvement in somatic aspects of depression (improvements in sleep, appetite, and libido). A more recent review of 27 clinical trials involving 1,890 men with low T found that TRT provided a significant reduction in depressive symptoms, especially at higher doses.

One of the most important things to know before starting TRT is that the treatment decreases sperm production and may cause infertility.

Testosterone might affect the size of your testicles, too. "Most of the size of the testicle is dedicated to making sperm, so when you're making less sperm, the testicles get smaller," says Dr. Morgentaler.

Sperm production may or may not go back to normal even after stopping treatment, says Dr. Kaminetsky. One study found increasing age and longer TRT linked to lower chances of normal sperm recovery.

Some people notice a little swelling in their feet and ankles because testosterone can encourage your body to hold onto excess fluid, says Dr. Morgentaler.

This isn't a big deal for most people, he says, and swelling is more common if you're taking a non-daily treatment like an injection, when you're getting a higher dose of T in one sitting.

Testosterone replacement therapy may change your skin type—for better or for worse, says Dr. Morgentaler.

Testosterone can increase oil production, which isn't necessarily a bad thing. Oil production is important for healthy skin, so you may actually end up with a better complexion. However, too much oily buildup can lead to breakouts.

While Dr. Morgentaler says testosterone-induced breakouts aren’t common, they are more likely to occur in men with a history of acne. Skin changes may also be more common if you’re taking testosterone injections versus other modes of administration since you're getting a higher dose all at once.

In all men—whether you’re taking T or not—some testosterone is converted into the hormone estradiol, a form of estrogen. In men who naturally have more breast tissue, testosterone can be converted into estradiol, which may stimulate breast tissue to grow.

This is called gynecomastia, says Dr. Morgentaler, and it's not as common as other side effects. If gynecomastia does happen, your doctor will likely stop treatment for a month or two to allow your breast tissue to go back to normal. Then, you’ll resume T along with a drug that blocks the conversion of testosterone to estradiol.

If you’re going the topical testosterone therapy route, one issue to watch out for is transference with women and children.

Darshan Patel, MD, assistant professor of urology at the University of California San Diego’s Men’s Health Center, says testosterone from gels or creams can be transferred through skin-to-skin contact or from clothing and absorbed by someone else.

“You really have to be careful to wash your hands well and not expose others,” adds Dr. Smith. Contact with excess testosterone can affect children’s genitalia and cause premature pubic hair development and aggressive behavior.

At a tight-security screening at New York's Lincoln Center Thursday night, the first lady's longtime collaborators celebrated how the Brett Ratner-helmed show allows her to tell her own story.

In late January, Amazon‘s Brett Ratner-directed Melania documentary debuted at the Kennedy Center in Washington amid crowds of demonstrators in the wake of the fatal shootings by ICE agents of Alex Pretti and Renée Good in Minneapolis. The president had also sparked backlash for his efforts to overhaul the Kennedy Center, including adding his name to the exterior, which are still playing out in court.

Thursday night, Melania: The Series, a two-episode follow-up, though part of the initial $40 million project, consisting of bonus footage, debuted at New York’s Lincoln Center. Those involved with the film and series downplayed any significance of the change in venue, noting the president and first lady’s ties to New York. But if there were demonstrators, they weren’t seen or heard by those inside the tight-security screening at Alice Tully Hall, though whether that’s due to people moving on or the premiere location remains an open question. The screening came just two weeks after the 2026 midterm elections saw Democrats score historic, surprise victories in a referendum on the president that will result in a change in party control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate.

Still, Trump’s longtime adviser Marc Beckman previously teased that the docuseries, now streaming on Amazon’s Prime Video, would be a “total different experience, completely new.”

Speaking to The Hollywood Reporter ahead of the Melania: The Series premiere, Beckman explained how the filmmaking team determined what would go in the documentary and what would be for the series.

“We decided that we would bifurcate and isolate certain stories that we thought were suitable for the series that weren’t necessarily appropriate for the film,” Beckman said. “The reason they’re suitable for the series is because it gives us more time to unpack more of her storyline, more about her family, more about her career, more about philanthropy and also what happens right after the inauguration when she takes on the role of being first lady of the United States again.”

Melania: The Series producer Fernando Sulichin, who took on the same role on the documentary, added that the film “was about going into the inauguration, and this one is first lady in action and what she’s about, more intimate — not the preparation of becoming the first lady but being the first lady.”

Beckman likened the approach to what Trump’s team did with her 2024 self-titled memoir Melania.

“With the trade edition of the book, we tell the story and we have certain photos in it, and then the collector’s edition, it’s a completely different experience so the fans could get both and enjoy them,” he said. “That’s the same thing here.”

Though it was initially expected that the docuseries would arrive on Amazon’s Prime Video when the documentary began streaming in March, Beckman said that was “just the media speculating,” and insisted the timing of the series’ release was in keeping with the licensing deal with Amazon he negotiated for the film and series.

“The timing for the contract is exactly what we’ve done,” he told THR on the black carpet Thursday night. “I negotiated the contract, so trust me, there’s no delay.”

While the documentary film, which was released in theaters in late January, followed Trump in the two weeks leading up to the start of her husband’s second term as president, the docuseries, in which Trump answers softball questions about her daily routine, interests and beliefs, first focuses on her role as a wife and mother, including her relationship with the president, followed by an episode centered around her and the president’s trips to North Carolina in the wake of Hurricane Helene and Los Angeles after the 2025 wildfires.

The film, coming in the wake of Trump’s best-selling memoir, was seen as part of a launch of a lifestyle brand. The doc and series’ $40 million Amazon purchase sparked backlash that the tech giant was trying to curry favor with the White House, criticism likely to continue with the project’s latest extension.

And while the Lincoln Center screening boasted Trump’s signature black-and-white color palette, complete with a black carpet and white tables and flowers, as well as tickets and popcorn boxes featuring the first lady’s face, Beckman steered questions about a possible lifestyle company back to Trump’s humanitarian efforts, specifically her work brokering a ceasefire in Oleshky, Ukraine and her Fostering the Future initiative to provide resources and educational opportunities for foster kids.

“I think right now what she’s really proud of is how she’s helping children and people throughout the world,” Beckman said. “What she’s doing in America is so much more important than a lifestyle brand.”

And before the docuseries premiered, Trump spoke, from a black-and-white podium bearing her name, about the Ukraine ceasefire, connecting it to her own personal ethos and the series’ focus, and episode titles, of “Family & Trust” and “Pride & Honor.”

“We live in a world that never stops moving, but life’s greatest moments belong to those who can stand still,” she began. “In calmness, we can see beauty. In peace, we can see humanity. My work is demanding, and sometimes the challenges I face seem impossible. Still, even in the most difficult circumstances, I find the drive to achieve the impossible. I rely on my own strength and character, with conviction, courage and reckless determination. I found a way forward where others would not.”

“For a brief moment, the world stood still for Oleshky. Eight hundred lives were saved. After so many years of fighting, the ceasefire brought a little bit of sunshine into lives which had been in the shadows of fear and war. The moment was very powerful for me because fighting and conflict have no value to the families on either side. Peace is rational,” she continued. “In the Oleshky ceasefire, we see the power of family, the restoration of trust and the courage of young children who do not back down in the face of war. Family, trust, pride, honor are the values we all hold in our hearts; they are also the foundation of the personal story I’m going to share with you tonight as part of Melania: The Series.”

As Trump teased that the series offered the opportunity to “experience [her] world through a new lens,” Trump’s current and former colleagues said they hoped the show, like the documentary, let Trump tell her own story.

President Trump’s first-term adviser Kellyanne Conway said conceptually that the documentary and series were “so amazing.”

“She’s obviously a very private person who has a very public life and in my view a very positive impact and that story does not get told,” Conway told THR. “And that’s OK, so let her tell her own story.”

Fashion designer Hervé Pierre, who created the first lady’s inaugural gowns for the president’s first and second ceremonies, added, “She’s not mysterious, but she’s not all over the media all the time and I think when she does something like she just did with [Russia and Ukraine], it’s not, like, fluff. It’s a very specific mission. So I think it’s important. I hope we will discover something that we don’t know about her. And I think sometimes it’s better for her to [express something in her own words] than to have an interpretation of what she’s doing. At least you have a direct line.”

And what sort of things might viewers discover about Trump that they don’t already know?

Pierre, who’s long worked with the first lady, offered a couple of insights.

“She cries when she laughs,” he said of her tendency to tear up at humorous moments. “She’s very happy. I’ve worked with difficult women for 30 years in haute couture. She never, ever raised her voice or [had attitude]. She’s always in a good mood — it’s fascinating.”

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The three officers were seen on video beating up music producer Michel Zecler in his studio

Three French police officers have been convicted of beating up a black music producer six years ago in Paris in what the court upheld as an aggravated circumstance of racism.

The three were also found guilty of signing a falsified police report stating that the producer - Michel Zecler, 47 - had violently resisted their attempt to arrest him.

Their lies were exposed because the whole scene at Zecler's recording studio was caught on CCTV, revealing the producer had suffered some 30 punches, kicks and baton strikes while offering little resistance at all.

Aurelien Lapersonne, 29, Philippe Tondeux, 50, and Pierre Plantier, 37, all received five year sentences, with different amounts suspended.

Lapersonne had three years of his sentence suspended and Tondeux three and a half years suspended. Plantier had four years suspended, and will serve his one year with an ankle monitor.

All were told they could never serve in the police again.

The case became a cause cÃ©lÃ¨bre for critics of the police, who say it exposed systematic racism among officers. With police again facing criticism for alleged use of excessive force in stopping recent protests, the verdict was hotly awaited by Zecler's supporters.

Much depended in the two-week trial on Zecler's own testimony that the officers called him a racial slur during the beating.

This was denied by the three policemen, who in court admitted the beating and the faked report but denied there was a racial motivation. But the court preferred the evidence of the producer, because - unlike the police - everything else he said was verifiably true.

Because the racist element was accepted by the court, the sentences were toughened.

The case was heard by a panel of five judges.

The incident took place during the Covid lockdown in 2020. Zecler was accused by the officers for not wearing a mask when doing so was obligatory as he approached his studio on foot.

They later claimed his behaviour aroused their suspicions, and they thought he might be carrying drugs. No evidence of this emerged.

After Zecler entered the lobby, the police followed - without a warrant - and tried to bring him outside. Zecler refused to go out, and the beating ensued. Zecler cried for help and some people appeared from the basement and pushed the police outside.

The officers then smashed a glass pane and sent a tear-gas grenade into the building, forcing those inside to come out - after which Zecler was again hit. He was then arrested.

During the trial Zecler said had it not been for the CCTV video, he would have been in court not as a victim but as a potential criminal.

In their last words before the court, all three police officers apologised for what they had done. Tondeux said he was sorry "for the image of the police that I have given".

This Is What Was on the Menu at the First Thanksgiving in 1621

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Somehow, turkey has spent the last four centuries getting all the glory.

For a meal that has been discussed, depicted and reenacted for more than four centuries, the first Thanksgiving has left us surprisingly few details about what anyone actually ate. We know there were roughly 140 people, three days of feasting and exactly five deer. Beyond that, the historical record is rather unhelpful. Apparently, nobody thought to write down the menu.

The gathering took place in Plymouth in the fall of 1621, when about 50 English colonists celebrated their first successful harvest alongside 90 Wampanoag, including their leader, Ousamequin, better known to history as Massasoit. It was a meeting between two communities whose relationship was far more complicated than the cheerful scene that would eventually find its way into American classrooms. Although they had formed an alliance earlier that year, cultural differences and tensions of an English settlement on Wampanoag land were hardly going to disappear over dinner.

And while the English had much to celebrate in bringing in their first harvest, they owed a great deal of that success to Indigenous knowledge. The Wampanoag understood the region’s growing seasons, its waterways and the foods that could be grown and gathered in the surrounding landscape. Their traditions had helped sustain communities there long before the colonists arrived.

The two surviving English accounts of the gathering offer only a few details about the feast itself, but taken together with what historians know about the food traditions of both communities, they give us a rather interesting picture of what may have been on the table.

Edward Winslow, one of the English colonists who attended the gathering, recorded that the Wampanoag went hunting and returned with five deer, which they presented to the colony’s leaders. It’s one of the few specific foods mentioned in his account and an especially substantial contribution to a celebration that lasted three days.

Deer were an important source of food for the Wampanoag, who also made use of their hides, bones and other materials. In England, however, deer hunting was largely reserved for the wealthy, making venison something most ordinary people rarely got to enjoy. The meat may have been roasted over a fire or simmered in a hearty venison stew, although Winslow neglected to tell us how it was prepared. We know exactly how many deer arrived, but what became of them in the kitchen remains a mystery.

Roast turkey has become so thoroughly associated with Thanksgiving that it’s difficult to imagine the holiday without one occupying the center of the table. The trouble is, we don’t actually know whether turkey was served at the original celebration.

Winslow wrote that four Englishmen went hunting for wildfowl and returned with enough to feed the colonists for nearly a week. What he didn’t do was tell us which birds they shot. Wild turkeys were present in the region, but so were ducks, geese and other game birds, all perfectly suitable for roasting over a fire. There’s every possibility that turkey was on the menu, but nothing to suggest it enjoyed the distinction we’ve since bestowed upon it. For all we know, the centerpiece was a duck, and we’ve spent the last few centuries giving credit to the wrong bird.

Corn was one of the most important foods available to both communities, although the English were relatively new to growing it. The Wampanoag had cultivated maize for generations, developing agricultural practices suited to the region’s soil and growing seasons. Their knowledge was enormously valuable to the colonists, whose first attempts at establishing a food supply had been anything but fruitful.

One traditional Wampanoag preparation was nasaump, a thick porridge made from ground corn, sometimes cooked with other ingredients. Corn could also be ground into meal for simple breads, early relatives of the cornbread we bake today, or used to thicken stews and make other hearty dishes.

The waters around Plymouth were full of food, provided you knew where to look and how to catch it. The Wampanoag had long fished the region’s rivers and coastline, gathering shellfish and catching everything from bass and cod to eels. Clams, mussels and lobsters were plentiful too.

The English were beginning to take advantage of these resources by 1621. William Bradford, the Plymouth colony’s governor, wrote of the colonists catching substantial quantities of cod and bass during the summer. A freshly caught fish could be roasted or boiled, while clams and mussels might be cooked over a fire. Lobster was another plentiful local catch that was enjoyed regularly. The variety of ocean fare available might have made for a Thanksgiving spread that looked rather more like a seafood boil than the feast we know today.

Squash and pumpkin were important crops in Wampanoag agriculture, and their ability to keep well after harvest made them especially useful as the colder weather set in. Both could be roasted, boiled or added to stews, much like we still prepare winter squash today, though without the benefit of a balsamic glaze.

The English called pumpkins pompions, a name I think we abandoned prematurely. They were familiar with the vegetable, although the varieties growing in New England differed from those they might have seen at home. A pumpkin dish would have been entirely plausible at the harvest celebration, but anyone hoping for a slice of pumpkin pie would have to wait almost two centuries. The colonists had limited supplies of wheat flour, butter and sugar, and the familiar custard-filled pie wouldn’t appear in American cookbooks until 1796.

The Wampanoag grew beans alongside corn and squash, three crops that complemented each other remarkably well. Cornstalks supported climbing beans, while squash vines spread across the ground and helped shade the soil. The combination was as useful in the garden as it was on the plate.

A traditional Wampanoag stew called sobaheg combines ingredients like corn, beans, squash and meat or fish, depending on what’s available or in season. The combination of corn and beans also appears in succotash, another dish with Indigenous New England roots that remains an American favorite.

The forests and wetlands of New England offered plenty beyond cultivated crops. Chestnuts, walnuts, hazelnuts, acorns, wild grapes and cranberries were among the foods the Wampanoag knew how to gather and prepare, each with its own season and uses.

Nuts could be eaten plain, stored or ground into meal, while fruits could be enjoyed fresh or preserved for later. Acorns required particular attention, since their natural bitterness had to be leached out with water before they could be used in cooking. It’s one of the few ingredients we’ve more or less surrendered entirely to the squirrels.

Of all the wild fruits available, cranberries have perhaps the most recognizable place on a modern Thanksgiving table. Their natural tartness eventually found a friend in sugar, giving rise to the cranberry sauce that’s become nearly as much a part of the holiday as the turkey itself.
