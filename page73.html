Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. Related Articles Celebrating the Inaugural Art Week NYC, Organized by ARTnews, Art in America, and Artforum New Museum Inks Multiyear Partnership with Video Art-Focused Vega Foundation

On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

Why PicS Stock Cratered Today

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Investors are fleeing following the company's financial report.

Shares of PicS (PICS -26.02%) plunged out of the gate Tuesday morning, falling as much as 31.6%. As of 1:35 p.m. ET, the stock was still down 26%.

The catalyst that sent the Brazilian fintech specialist lower was the company's financial report, which investors found wanting.

PicS, the parent company of PicPay, reported its third-quarter results, which contained both good news and bad news. Revenue of 4.7 billion Brazilian reais (roughly $812 million) grew 73% year over year, fueling adjusted net income of 271 million reais ($46.9 million), which jumped 157%.

Other metrics seemed equally bullish, as PicPay's credit portfolio grew 88% year over year to 35 billion reais ($6 billion). Management also noted that revenue per client grew by 58%, and lower fixed expenses improved its adjusted efficiency ratio by 11 percentage points.

This all sounds like good news, so why is the stock plunging today? It all comes down to changes in the company's write-off policy. PicPay is reducing the period for delinquent accounts from 360 days past due to 120 days past due, citing its "more mature portfolio and robust historical data." That will result in a one-time charge of 325 million reais ($56.2 million) in the fourth quarter. As a result of the new policy, PicPay adjusted its full-year net income guidance to 733 million reais ($126.7 million), down from 1.06 billion reais ($182.8 million).

The growing credit portfolios among fintech companies in Latin America have caused some investor concern. Since many of these accounts have limited histories, it's difficult to assess the delinquency or default rate in the event of a regional or global recession. Moreover, given the history of inflation in that geographical area, those concerns carry greater weight.

PicS also has a limited history, having only gone public in early 2026. Since its debut, PicS stock has shed 73% of its value. On the other hand, at just 3.5 times forward earnings, there isn't much growth baked into its valuation. For investors who have the stomach for a little risk and the potential for significant volatility, PicS might be worth a look.

Disappointment, but some important perspective.

Those were the emotions felt by Chris Billam-Smith when reflecting on the third defeat of his professional career against Cheavon Clarke earlier this month.

The former cruiserweight world champion was dropped to the canvas and stopped for the first time in the pro ranks as Clarke repeated the outcome from when the pair met in their amateur days.

Although the loss is a stumbling block to Billam-Smith's ambitions, he felt it was important to put things into perspective.

On Tuesday, it was announced Irish boxer Lee Reeves had died aged 31, three months after he suffered serious injuries in a bout in Dublin.

Billam-Smith is also close friends with former gym-mate Raven Chapman, who is recovering after suffering a brain bleed over the summer in a sparring session.

"We had news recently of fighters ending up unwell and losing their lives," an emotional Billam-Smith told BBC Sport.

"I get to come home to my kids and other people haven't had that luxury so in the grand scheme of things, coming home healthy is most important.

"There's plenty of perspective to be taken and I've had a lot of that around me, so when I look at life as a whole, it's good.

"I think the ref got it (the stoppage) wrong in the moment but I'm not angry at him for that because his job is fighter safety in a brutal sport. Boxing wise I'm devastated and you never like to lose let alone get stopped."

Billam-Smith stopped as Clarke repeats amateur win

Billam-Smith won the WBO cruiserweight title in 2023 in front of 15,000 supporters at his beloved AFC Bournemouth's Vitality Stadium.

The following summer, he defended his title at Selhurst Park as he outclassed Richard Riakporhe at the home of Crystal Palace.

After losing a WBA and WBO unification fight against Gilberto Ramirez in late 2024, he then beat Brandon Glanton at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium before signing a deal with Zuffa Boxing and stopping Ryan Rozicki back in his hometown.

Although he now has three defeats to his name alongside 22 wins, Billam-Smith believes he still has more to give in the sport.

"The fight against Chev isn't the ideal way (to end his career) and I wouldn't want to go out like that," he added.

"I haven't properly thought about it and at the end of the day it will be my decision when I retire but people close to me, their opinions matter too."

Billy Bob Thornton isn't a stranger to the thriller genre. His breakthrough came in 1992, when he co-wrote and starred in the critically acclaimed crime thriller One False Move. Since then, he has received three Academy Award nominations in his career. And now one of his best movies - A Simple Plan - is streaming for free.

He wrote and directed the 1996 thriller Sling Blade and also starred in the lead role, Karl Childers, which earned him his first Oscar nomination as an actor. His script won the Academy Award in the Best Adapted Screenplay category that year.

While Sling Blade may have been a career-defining project for Thornton, two years later, he starred in an acclaimed but lesser-known crime thriller that led to his third Oscar nomination.

Directed by Sam Raimi, A Simple Plan features Thornton as Jacob, the odd older brother of Hank Mitchel, who discovered 4.4 million dollars and a plane with their friend Lou. The movie follows the three as they decide to keep the money.

Adapted from Scott B. Smith's novel of the same name, A Simple Plan's premise is very straightforward, but the escalation comes as a surprise. The movie is brilliantly paced, with the first half loaded with psychological tension and suspense and the second half filled with action and consequences.

Thornton's character, Jacob, is layered and tragic. He first appeared to be somewhat goofy and greedy, fast to side with Lou who proposed on taking the 4.4 million dollars. His brother Hank appeared to be the more law-abiding person, who was against stealing. However, as the film progresses, viewers will soon find the roles reversed.

A Simple Plan received high praise from critics, including Roger Ebert, when it came out in 1998. In addition to Thornton's Best Supporting Actor nomination, Smith's script also received a nomination for Best Adapted Screenplay. It's hard to imagine another crime thriller matching the brilliance of Sling Blade, but A Simple Plan comes very close.

Billy Bob Thornton's performance is nuanced and deliberate. He makes Jacob seem like the least likable and least intelligent brother at first, but there are moments where the characters manage to surprise viewers. Jacob has depth and a conscience, which makes his tragic ending heartbreaking.

What's even more uncanny is how much the Landman actor seems to reveal about Jacob and Hank's father through his delivery of dialogue. He peels back the layers of an easily overlooked, normal-day guy and reveals the person underneath, who is good-natured, kind, observant, and almost too disillusioned and witty for his own good. Even though Jacob isn't the lead character, he manages to steal all the spotlight.

A Simple Plan officially joined Tubi's free streaming platform in November, in addition to Pluto TV, Paramount+, and YouTube subscriptions. Despite the 1998 neo-noir crime thriller being a masterpiece, it's often overlooked in both Sam Raimi and Billy Bob Thornton's filmographies.

Several factors have contributed to its lack of hype. Known for directing horror and action movies such as Evil Dead and the original Spider-Man trilogy, Raimi has a kinetic visual style. The film's slow-burning, atmospheric style takes a detour from the director's usual high-energy, flashy aesthetics.

It also came out two years after Fargo, which established a cultural touchstone by blending dark comedy with a snowbound Midwestern crime aesthetic. A Simple Plan is often considered a darker and bleaker cousin of the Coen brothers' film. While A Simple Plan was critically acclaimed, the movie wasn't a huge box office hit. It struggled in theaters and grossed only $16 million.

3 AI Infrastructure Stocks to Buy Hand Over Fist if the Market Pulls Back

Investors have been selling AI stocks as if the build-out were about to stop. The companies wiring, connecting, and building the data centers just said the opposite. Here are three I'd keep buying on any dip.

Wall Street spent the past few weeks selling artificial intelligence (AI) stocks as if the data center boom was about to stall. The companies actually doing the building keep saying the opposite.

The Nasdaq Composite fell into a correction on Nov. 5 after the midterm results raised fears of new limits on data centers. By Nov. 6, the PHLX Semiconductor index sat roughly 40% below its June high. Then Nvidia (NVDA -1.50%) CEO Jensen Huang resigned on Nov. 12. The Nasdaq still gained 2.3% last week, closing Nov. 13 at 25,136.27.

That bounce could easily fade. If it does, these are the three AI stocks I'd be buying with both hands, because none of them depends on which chip wins.

Eaton (ETN -0.40%) makes the electrical gear that takes power from the grid and safely feeds it to racks of servers: switchgear, breakers, and power distribution systems. Its data center revenue rose about 65% last quarter, and data center orders more than doubled over the past 12 months.

What I like most is the profit side. Eaton has been racing to add factory space, and 18 of its 25 capacity projects are now complete. Operating margin in its Americas electrical business, the share of each sales dollar left as profit, jumped 2.3 percentage points in a single quarter to 29.8%.

Eaton is also reaching closer to the chip itself. Its Boyd Thermal unit, which makes liquid cooling systems for AI servers, roughly tripled its backlog from a year ago. Management raised its full-year forecast for the third time this year, to $13.70 to $13.80 in adjusted earnings per share. At $352.97, the stock trades about 26% below its 52-week high. I'd buy it now and buy more on any dip, because the expensive part of the build-out is behind it and the payoff is just starting.

Corning (GLW +1.21%) is best known for the glass on your phone, but these days its biggest business is optical fiber, the hair-thin glass strands that carry data between servers. Optical sales jumped 44% to $2.38 billion last quarter, and sales of its products for generative AI more than doubled.

CEO Wendell Weeks said that AI growth is outpacing growth in graphics chip shipments. In plain English, every new AI server needs more of Corning's fiber than the one before it.

Corning also signed four long-term, multibillion-dollar customer deals last quarter, including with Verizon Communications and AT&T, and says those agreements share the cost of building new capacity. That's a major reason Corning generated $2.27 billion in adjusted free cash flow in the first nine months of 2026 while continuing to expand.

Even so, the stock closed at $114.08 on Nov. 13, about 58% below its 52-week high. Management expects fourth-quarter core earnings per share of $0.96 to $1, up about 36% from last year. For a company aiming to double its annual sales pace from $20 billion to $40 billion by the end of 2030, that's a price-to-earnings ratio I'm happy to pay.

Someone has to physically install all of that equipment, and Emcor (EME +0.56%) is a leader in electrical and mechanical construction, the wiring, piping, and cooling inside big buildings. Its remaining performance obligations, meaning signed work it hasn't finished yet, hit a record $18.57 billion, up 47% from a year ago. The biggest jump came from data center work, which requires heavy electrical systems and increasingly complex cooling systems.

The fun detail is cooling. AI chips run so hot that the piping and cooling work inside these buildings keeps growing. In Emcor's mechanical business, revenue from network and communications jobs, which are mostly data centers, reached $985.2 million last quarter, up from $432.1 million a year earlier.

Emcor raised its 2026 forecast to $34.50 to $35.50 in adjusted earnings per share and added $500 million to its stock buyback program. On Nov. 13, it agreed to buy P1 Construction, a Kansas contractor that builds hyperscale data centers and expects up to $650 million in revenue this year, paying with cash on hand. It can afford to, with just $6 million in total debt at the end of September.

Shares closed at $659.36, about 31% below their 52-week high. That's a bargain for a company whose signed work grew 47% while sales grew 29%, and it's the one I'd reach for first.

The market is pricing these three stocks as if the build-out ends in a few months. Their order books, signed contracts, and new factories say they'll be busy well into 2027, and that gap is exactly what I'd want to buy into if the market drops again.
