‘Second ball’ may be a Sunday league mantra but Yates showed its top-flight relevance

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Midfielder’s old-school attributes came to the fore during Nottingham Forest’s win against Manchester City

Modern football is more systematised and data-driven than ever but some things never change. Winning the first ball is important but the second ball – the Holy Grail for Sunday league footballers – is often more so.

Nottingham Forest’s 3-1 win against Manchester City last weekend featured a classic example. Forest led 2-1 inside the last 20 minutes but City, lifted by Rayan Cherki’s sweet half-volley, were pushing for an equaliser.

Another City attack comes down their right: after Jair Cunha’s clearing header from a deep Matheus Nunes cross, the ball loops up in the air before descending inside the home penalty area.

Ryan Yates, Forest’s old-fashioned midfield general, is being pressed from behind by the Argentina international Enzo Fernández as the ball drops. Yates stands his ground, using his strength to hold off his opponent’s muscular challenge. Fernández tries to impose himself, too, but ends up on the turf.

Not only does Yates win that crucial one-on-one, by cleverly improvising an overhead kick to shift the ball away from danger he inadvertently begins a counterattack that arguably changes the course of Forest’s season.

Liam Delap shepherds the ball to Morgan Gibbs-White. Enzo Maresca’s team are hopelessly exposed and Gibbs-White devours the space in front of him with a driving run.

He lays a pass off to Delap, who takes one touch before swerving a sensational shot into the far corner for 3-1. It is a memorable move that will surely compete for goal of the month. But none of it would have been possible without the dirty work of Yates: a show of strength and desire from the captain at the other end of the pitch.

Oliver Glasner understands the importance of the moment and sprints to celebrate with his players. After a barren scoring run Delap suddenly has two goals in one afternoon. The home fans are delirious. Twelve seconds after Yates won the duel, Forest have a two-goal cushion rather than a desperate fight to maintain their lead.

The second ball is a pleasingly old-school term but remains of paramount importance. Visit your local amateur club and you are likely to hear it being shouted as a motivational mantra.

At grassroots level it’s a reminder to teammates that finesse and creativity are all very well but it’s hard work, desire and a refusal to let your opponent get the better of you that win matches.

And it remains part of the elite game’s lexicon. Premier League managers sometimes point to an upcoming opponents’ second-ball prowess – a back-handed compliment if ever there was one. The hidden meaning: “They feed off scraps at set pieces. They don’t create from open play.”

Kick off your evenings with the Guardian's take on the world of football

After seeing Southampton Under-21s lose to Wealdstone in the National League Cup a couple of years ago, I mentioned to the then Saints manager, Simon Rusk, that his goalkeeper had been shouting “second ball” throughout.

“To be honest,” Rusk replied with amusing frankness, “I’m not sure some of them even know what second ball means.”

Managers like relying on data. They like predictable outcomes. They prefer to remove risk, reduce chaos and exert control over as much of the game as possible. But while the outcome of a one-on-one duel can never be certain, the consequences can often be far-reaching. That’s why second balls matter.

Against City Yates was named in Forest’s starting lineup, and given the captain’s armband, for the first time this season. Glasner, asked how he had motivated his players, revealed: “Ryan Yates spoke. I played more than 500 games as a player, I’ve managed, I don’t know, 500 games … and I’ve never heard such an inspiring speech.”

Glasner’s captain backed up stirring words with action. If there was a sense Yates’s star was fading – that his attributes of graft, tenacity and box-to-box drive were losing currency – then it was emphatically extinguished at the City Ground.

Win the first one if you can. But it’s the second ball that often makes the difference.

Michael Carrick has been sacked by Manchester United, with former Chelsea and Tottenham Hotspur boss Mauricio Pochettino waiting in the wings as his successor.

Just 173 days on from Carrick being given a two-year contract, having guided United to a third-placed finish and back into the Champions League as an interim, the decision to fire him was ratified at a crucial board meeting in Monaco on Wednesday.

Carrick arrived at United's training ground at 7.50am this morning and was dismissed. He left at 10.18am, less than two and a half hours later.

In a statement released at 10.37am, Carrick's sacking was confirmed. United's statement read: 'After a disappointing start to the season, the club has come to the extremely difficult conclusion that a change is necessary in order to achieve our ambitions.

'Michael is, and always will be, a Manchester United legend. Just as he did as a player, he has conducted himself with the highest standards of dedication and professionalism throughout his time back with the club. His leadership and achievements last season will always be fondly remembered.'

Sources close to United say the club's leadership believe their squad of players is far better than the performances and results under Carrick have indicated. They gave the under-fire manager a chance to turn things around against Chelsea, Roma and Aston Villa but ultimately felt the team were not on the right path.

Minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe, along with the Glazer family, who dialled in virtually from the United States, chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox reached a verdict in the south of France yesterday, all while Carrick remained in limbo back in Manchester.

Carrick took charge of training on Tuesday morning after it had emerged that Berrada and Wilcox were heading to Monaco to recommend his sacking and did so again on Wednesday when it transpired United were closing in on an agreement to bring in Pochettino as his successor.

The current United States national team manager reached a verbal agreement with Man United to take over on Wednesday. United then made a formal approach to US Soccer shortly after, with the club having to trigger the 54-year-old Argentine's £7million release clause.

The final nail in Carrick’s coffin came in Sunday's dramatic 4-4 draw against Aston Villa, which saw United stuck in the relegation zone in 18th position.

A defeat at Chelsea, in which Lisandro Martinez badly let his manager down by getting sent off, and a win over Roma to go with the draw against Villa, was not enough of a sign that it will turn around for the board to keep faith.

With Carrick's departure, it pushes the compensation paid out to coaching staff and executives over the last three years under minority owner Ratcliffe and Ineos close to £30million. Buying Pochettino out of his contract with the US national team will cost another £7m. Adding his large coaching staff will take millions more.

Matchgoing supporters will be sad to see Carrick go and made those feelings clear in recent games when he was loudly cheered at the end of matches, with the ire and frustration levelled against players and executives.

Optimism heading into the campaign was sky high given that no team in the Premier League performed better than Carrick's side between January and May.

But that level of success and dominance has long since evaporated after a shambolic start to the current campaign - and it has cost the 45-year-old his job just five months after the ink dried on a new deal.

It has been a season in which United won just three matches, one of which came against Azerbaijani minnows FK Sabah, and are yet to keep a clean sheet in the league all season.

Ukraine warns of fresh Starshield disruptions as U.S. envoys arrive in Moscow for talks with Putin

📅 2026-11-12  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine was investigating what he described as fresh restrictions on SpaceX's Starshield service over the country, after similar speed limitations were temporarily imposed last month.

"Our units have been recording significant disruptions to Starshield's operations over the territory of Ukraine for several days now – along the entire front line and in the temporarily occupied territories of Ukraine," he wrote on X early Thursday.

Starshield is SpaceX's satellite network for government and national-security use, built on Starlink technology.

CNBC has reached out to SpaceX and the White House for comment.

"The Ukrainian military have already investigated the circumstances, and these are not random errors but systematic speed restrictions," he said, adding that they are trying to establish the circumstances with the U.S.

Zelenskyy said the restrictions imposed an effective speed limit on terminals moving at speeds above 161 kilometers per hour (100 miles per hour), disconnecting anything that was moving faster and making it harder for Ukraine to intercept Russian jet-powered drones.

"There have already been strikes that got through because our interception systems cannot reach sufficient speeds and are unable to intercept in time."

On Oct. 14, Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of undermining the country's ability to defend itself by cutting connectivity speeds and thereby disrupting military operations. On October 24, Zelenskyy confirmed that systems had returned to their previous operating speeds.

According to The Wall Street Journal, SpaceX CEO Elon Musk ordered those restrictions, rendering some Ukrainian strike and interceptor drones virtually unusable. The newspaper later reported that U.S. President Donald Trump had asked Musk to reverse the move. Trump has denied that the White House ordered Starlink restrictions in Ukraine.

Musk initially appeared to pin responsibility for any restrictions on the U.S. government, writing on X that "Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government." He later claimed Ukraine had violated Starlink's terms of service, prompting the company to restrict its access to the high-speed satellite technology, something that Ukrainian officials have strongly contested.

Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense tech advisor to Zelenskyy, said at the time that Ukraine cannot use commercial Starlink for medium- or long-range drone strikes because the service does not operate over Russian territory and had imposed drone speed limits of 70–90 kilometers per hour for months.

On Thursday, Beskrestnov confirmed that "the speed of Starlink operation during movement was again limited."

"We did not rejoice for long," he said, adding that "any terms can be changed and discussed, instead of leaving our country without sky protection."

The comments came as U.S.-brokered peace talks wrapped up in Abu Dhabi on Wednesday and U.S. special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner arrived in Moscow for further talks with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

Witkoff and Kushner were met by Russian presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev as they arrived at Vnukovo Airport on Thursday morning, according to Russian state news agency Tass. The pair were expected to discuss a lasting resolution to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which has now raged for more than four-and-a-half years.

Later on Thursday, Witkoff and Kushner are poised to meet with Zelenskyy on the sidelines of a political summit held in Ireland.

Trilateral talks in the United Arab Emirates between Ukrainian, Russian and American negotiators ended earlier this week without a major diplomatic breakthrough on a ceasefire or territorial settlement.

A pricing strategy can look convincing in a spreadsheet and still break down during negotiations. Salespeople encounter objections the original research missed. Customers value features differently from what a product team expected. Discounts become routine, while the reasons behind them remain scattered across calls, CRM records, and approval requests.

Mondrio’s approach is to make those signals part of an ongoing process. Its platform analyzes quantitative data alongside qualitative information, including customer conversations and market feedback, to identify opportunities and recommend changes. Lessons from quotes, negotiations, and renewals then feed back into the next pricing decision.

That connection matters as AI changes the economics of software. A feature that invokes models or performs automated work can create variable costs each time a customer uses it. Packaging, usage allowances, and discounts therefore affect both the attractiveness of an offer and the cost of delivering it. Faster product releases also give companies more frequent reasons to revisit what they charge.

CEO and co-founder Gregor Biljardt said the opportunity lies in agents combining quantitative information with insights from conversations and markets. Mondrio is applying that combination to a commercial workflow in which an apparently small pricing decision can carry consequences across many subsequent deals.

The company’s Pricing Studio handles pricing design: packaging, pricing models, and price levels. Its published workflow moves through research, design, simulation, decision-making, and rollout, giving teams a way to test an offer before introducing it to customers.

The platform also lists features for benchmarking, pricing simulations, experiments, and customer migrations. These functions address different questions: which features belong together, which customer groups value them, and how a proposed change could affect an existing customer base.

AI personas provide a way to explore hypotheses, but their responses are simulated evidence rather than observed customer behavior. The useful feedback loop comes from comparing those hypotheses with research and actual commercial outcomes as offers reach the market.

Once a team approves a pricing decision, it can flow into Mondrio’s Deal Engine. That handoff is central to the product’s design: a new price list should influence the quotes customers receive, instead of remaining an analysis that sales teams must translate into their own tools.

Deal Engine covers execution, including quote creation, discount guidance, approvals, and the information collected as a deal progresses. Mondrio describes a workflow in which an agent builds a quote using the current price list, evaluates it against policy, and routes exceptions for approval.

The distinction between a recommendation and an authorized action is important. Quotes that meet a company’s rules can move through automated approvals; exceptions can be escalated with supporting context, such as the effect of a discount on margins. The platform’s listed capabilities also include a customer portal, quote engagement tracking, deal insights, and electronic signatures.

This gives the system a potential role beyond reducing the effort of producing a sales document. Repeated objections, unsuccessful offers, or discount patterns can become inputs to pricing design. These sources represent different views of the same commercial relationship. A CRM records the opportunity, a conversation platform captures what a buyer said, product analytics show usage, and billing records what the customer pays. Bringing those signals into pricing work can help teams assess whether an offer reflects customer value and actual delivery economics.

The catalog assigns integrations to different modules, so the presence of a connection does not mean every system supports identical functions. The broader technical proposition is to connect pricing decisions to the operational records that can explain their results.

Rafay Systems and Fortanix announced on November 11, 2026 that the Rafay Platform now orchestrates Fortanix Confidential AI workloads running on CNCF Confidential Containers (CoCo), extending their existing confidential virtual machine integration to Kubernetes pods. The joint offering is available now for neoclouds, sovereign AI clouds and enterprises.

The service addresses a gap in conventional encryption. Data can be protected at rest and in transit, but without confidential computing, data and model weights must be decrypted for processing in CPU and GPU memory. That creates an exposure on infrastructure operated by someone else, where people with privileged access could reach customer records, patient information, financial transactions or proprietary models.

CoCo, a CNCF Incubating project built on Kata Containers, runs each Kubernetes pod in its own lightweight confidential virtual machine. The CPU encrypts memory and, on supported hardware, GPUs can also be included in the trust boundary. Developers continue using standard Kubernetes workflows while workloads run inside hardware-based Trusted Execution Environments, which isolate code and data during processing.

Fortanix Confidential Computing Manager performs composite attestation, checking the integrity of CPU and GPU trusted execution environments, firmware and software before authorizing key release. Fortanix Data Security Manager, which the announcement describes as backed by FIPS 140-3 Level 3 hardware security module certification, releases encryption keys only to attested environments. If attestation fails, the keys remain protected, preventing the workload from accessing sensitive data or executing successfully.

Rafay supplies the operating layer around those security controls. Its blueprints install the Fortanix CoCo add-ons and keep them synchronized across confidential clusters. Operators onboard GPU infrastructure once and assign each customer an isolated tenant, while policies, access controls and quotas apply across tenants and clusters.

Administrators can package a confidential workload as a catalog item, called a SKU, in Rafay SKU Studio. End users then launch it on demand through Rafay Developer Hub without filing a support ticket. The workflow combines tenant-level service management with Fortanix’s verification of each protected environment before sensitive information is unlocked.

“Model owners want their weights hidden from operators and customers; enterprises want their data hidden from model owners and operators; and operators must prove they can see neither, even via another tenant or compromised software,” said Anuj Jaiswal, chief product officer at Fortanix.

The companies see several operating models for the service. Neoclouds and GPU cloud providers can offer confidential AI as a premium option backed by evidence that tenant workloads run in trusted environments and keys go only to verified workloads. Sovereign AI clouds can retain keys in an operator-controlled hardware security module, on-premises or as SaaS. Regulated enterprises can process sensitive data without exposing it to the infrastructure operator, while model providers can keep model weights encrypted until a customer environment passes attestation.

A Rafay technical post dated March 16, 2026 documented the companies’ earlier confidential virtual machine integration. In that workflow, Fortanix validated the environment and supplied keys after attestation, while Rafay provided repeatable service definitions, centralized multi-tenancy and self-service deployment. The new launch extends that division of responsibilities to CoCo-based Kubernetes pods.

Fortanix had also already announced Confidential AI support for CoCo in October 2026. That release described work on open-source plugins connecting its attestation and key-release products to CoCo, with SaaS, on-premises and fully air-gapped deployment options. The new development is Rafay’s available orchestration of those deployments alongside the existing virtual machine integration.

The launch therefore separates service operation from data access: Rafay handles the catalog, tenant isolation and governance, while Fortanix’s attestation determines whether decryption keys are released. For shared GPU infrastructure, that allows an operator to run and sell AI capacity without customers having to treat its administrative access as implicit permission to read their data or model weights.

The AI Industry Wants Our Trust, But It Needs Better Controls First

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In security, we don’t get to discover whether our controls work after something goes wrong. We test them. We challenge them. We assume someone will eventually find the weakness we missed.

So why should frontier AI get a different standard?

As AI systems become more autonomous and gain greater access to real-world systems, we’re seeing something that should concern anyone who has spent time on the defensive side of cybersecurity: capability is moving faster than control.

And speed is not an excuse for weaker security. It is the reason we need stronger security.

A recent assessment by nonprofit Guidelight AI looked at publicly available evidence of AI control practices across Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and xAI. None scored above a C+ overall.

That doesn’t mean these companies are reckless. It doesn’t mean additional controls aren’t operating behind closed doors.

But security doesn’t run on assumptions. If we’re being asked to trust increasingly powerful systems, we should expect evidence that the controls surrounding them can withstand the same scrutiny we would demand anywhere else.

When an AI agent behaves unexpectedly, gains unintended access or exposes a weakness, the story tends to focus on what the technology did.

A security practitioner wants to know: Was the activity logged? Did anyone see it happening? Were dangerous actions gated? Could someone stop the system? Was there a containment plan, and had anyone tested it?

Those questions aren’t unique to AI. They’re the questions we’ve been asking in cybersecurity for years.

Yet prevention and containment appear to remain weak spots in the emerging frontier AI control environment. Public evidence around gated actions, circuit breakers and emergency containment remains limited.

That should matter more than the novelty of the incident. When something fails, I want to know what failed, what stopped it from getting worse and what changed afterward.

Transparency is useful. Learning is better.

I don’t think the biggest near-term threat is AI suddenly inventing an entirely new category of cyberattack. The bigger problem is much more practical. AI can make the attacks we already understand happen much faster.

Research that once took hours can happen in minutes. Attack techniques can be chained together faster. Vulnerabilities can be identified and acted upon before a human-centered security process has made it through its first escalation call.

That’s a problem because much of today’s security infrastructure still assumes there will be time to respond.

If an attack unfolds rapidly, a response process built around tickets, meetings, approvals and manual escalation can become obsolete before anyone reaches step three.

And that’s where the AI industry has some work to do.

If frontier labs expect enterprises to rethink visibility, vulnerability management, architecture and incident response for the AI era, they should show us that they’ve done the same thing themselves.

I’ve spent my career around security testing, and one principle holds up remarkably well: intentions don’t prove security. Evidence does.

You find out what happens when the controls don’t behave the way the architecture diagram says they should. And you make sure someone has the authority to stop the system when necessary. Frontier AI should be held to that same standard.

That means adversarial testing of controls, measurable evidence that those controls work and clear authority for shutting systems down when something goes wrong.

Frontier labs cannot improvise containment after an AI agent acts outside its intended boundaries. As they train and test increasingly capable models, they must take responsibility for the actions those systems can take. That requires rigorous adversarial testing, pre-authorized safeguards, clear limits on agent authority and proven ways to intervene or shut a system down before a failure spreads.

Human judgment still matters. But the labs developing these systems must establish the boundaries, test whether they hold and remain accountable when they do not.

Their responsibility is not simply to demonstrate what an agent can do. It is to show that the agent can operate safely, that its behavior can be controlled and that new capabilities make the world more secure, not less.

I’m not arguing that we slow AI innovation to a crawl. I’m arguing that security has to keep pace with it. If we invest enormous resources in making AI more capable, we need comparable urgency around making it defensible.

But as frontier labs accelerate the training and testing of new AI models with cyber capabilities, they must keep defenders in mind. First, by helping them detect and respond to threats faster. More importantly, by keeping those systems from becoming threats themselves. That responsibility also requires accepting outside scrutiny.

Independent review can’t become something companies embrace only when it supports the story they want to tell. If frontier AI systems are powerful enough to change the threat landscape, their controls should be strong enough to withstand meaningful independent testing.

As a security practitioner, I’m willing to give it. But first, show me the controls.

High Fat Diet Linked to Higher Colon Cancer Risk, Early Research Shows

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In a new study, researchers report that a high fat diet may potentially increase the risk of colon cancer.

The researchers from Arizona State University said a diet high in certain fats can cause lasting changes in intestinal stem cells that lead to the development of tumors and the possibility of colon cancer.

The study was conducted in mice, but the researchers noted changes in the DNA of intestinal stem cells that may explain their growth and survival.

The researchers published their findings on November 12 in the journal Stem Cell Reports.

The researchers said the study is important because 1 in 25 people develop colon cancer in their lifetime. In the United States, that translates into about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer every year. About 55,000 males and 55,000 females are expected to die from the disease in 2026.

Experts not involved in the study say the research provides valuable information, but they have reservations about the results, as the findings do not yet apply to humans.

“The findings provide a potential mechanism linking diet to intestinal cancer development, but we need to be cautious about translating these results to humans,” said Dalia Perelman, MS, CDE, a research dietitian at Stanford University in California.

“We already have evidence from human studies that obesity and certain dietary patterns, particularly those high in processed meats and low in fiber-rich foods, are associated with increased colorectal cancer risk,” Perelman told Healthline. “This study adds a potentially important mechanism to investigate, but it does not establish that dietary fat itself increases colorectal cancer risk in humans.”

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, shared similar views.

“This study provides a better understanding at the cellular level of how a high fat diet may contribute to colorectal cancer development,” Thanki told Healthline.

“Animal studies are an important starting point for understanding biological processes, but we must be cautious about directly applying their findings to humans,” he added. “Mice and humans share many cellular and biological mechanisms, which makes these studies valuable in identifying potential pathways involved in disease development. However, human diets, genetics, metabolism, and environmental exposures are more complex.”

To reach their findings, the Arizona State researchers fed mice what the scientists described as a Western-style high fat diet for 6 months.

They then compared those results with those from mice given a low fat diet.

The researchers said the mice on the high fat diet gained more weight than the mice on the low-fat diet. They also developed more intestinal stem cells, and those cells grew at a more rapid rate.

They added that mice genetically predisposed to intestinal cancer who were fed a high-fat diet developed more and larger tumors.

The researchers noted changes in the DNA of intestinal stem cells that may help explain their increased growth and survival.

They also pointed out that stem cell behavior returned to normal after high fat diet mice were switched to a low fat diet, although some DNA changes persisted. After four weeks, approximately 18% of these changes remained.

When mice were returned to a high fat diet, the researchers said their intestinal stem cells responded more quickly and strongly than those of mice exposed to the diet for the first time.

They concluded that the study suggests that maintaining a healthy, low fat diet may help regulate intestinal stem cell growth and reduce colon cancer risk.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, RD, president of KAK Consulting and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine in Ohio, wasn’t involved in the study but highlighted a particular aspect of the findings.

“What I found particularly interesting was that even after the mice returned to a low fat diet, some of the molecular changes persisted,” she told Healthline. “This raises questions about how our dietary patterns over time may influence long-term health.”

Experts said these new findings are interesting but noted numerous caveats.

“One important takeaway is that dietary habits over time matter,” said Thanki. “Even if someone has consumed a high fat diet for years, making changes now may still provide meaningful health benefits.”

“I also think these findings are particularly important for children and adolescents, as establishing healthier dietary habits early in life may provide long-term benefits,” he added.

However, Thanki noted that not all fats are the same. “The health risks depend partly on the type of fat consumed, how much is consumed, and what foods make up the overall diet,” he said.

“Diets consistently high in saturated fats, particularly those containing large amounts of processed meats, fried foods, and highly processed products, can contribute to obesity, elevated cholesterol, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic dysfunction. These factors can also contribute to chronic inflammation and may increase the risk of certain cancers, including colorectal cancer,” Thanki continued.

Perelman said the diet used in the study was extremely high in fat, providing 60% of calories from fat, primarily from lard.

“We know that the health effects of dietary fats differ depending on their sources and fatty acid composition, and we cannot assume that a diet rich in unsaturated fats from foods such as olive oil, nuts, and seeds would produce the same effects,” she noted.

Perelman added that the researchers primarily studied stem cells from the small intestine rather than the colon.

“Although these tissues share some biological characteristics, they have important differences, and we cannot assume that the same changes would occur in the colon,” she explained.

Finally, Perelman noted that “the mice consuming the high fat diet also gained weight, making it difficult to separate the effects of dietary fat itself from those associated with increased body fat and other metabolic changes.”

Not all high fat foods are unhealthy. Some high fat foods that offer healthy benefits include:

“The goal shouldn’t necessarily be to eliminate fat because our bodies need healthy fats for normal functioning,” said Thanki. “Instead, people should focus on reducing saturated and trans fats while incorporating healthier unsaturated fats in appropriate amounts.”

Thanki had several dietary suggestions. Among them:

“Consistency matters more than perfection. Even modest dietary changes, maintained over time, can have meaningful health benefits,” Thanki said.

Perelman agreed. “The overall quality of the diet matters. Diets that are very high in fat may displace other foods and nutrients that have important health benefits,” she said.

“For colorectal cancer specifically, higher intakes of dietary fiber and whole grains have consistently been associated with lower risk,” Perelman noted.

“Therefore, rather than focusing exclusively on reducing fat, it is important to consider the overall dietary pattern and whether it provides adequate amounts of fiber-rich plant foods and other beneficial nutrients.”

Kirkpatrick agreed that the type of fat is what is important. “A Mediterranean-style diet that includes olive oil, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish can be relatively high in fat but is consistently associated with positive health outcomes,” she said.

“On the other hand, a diet high in saturated fat, processed meats, and ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of certain chronic diseases, particularly when it’s also low in fiber and other protective nutrients.”

Kirkpatrick also noted that many lifestyle habits contribute to a person’s overall health.

“Our health is influenced by so many factors beyond diet, including how we manage stress, our sleep habits, physical activity, genetics, and overall lifestyle,” she said.
