The lost years of Lamar Jackson -- and the greatness still within reach

Some years, Lamar Jackson's thumb hits another player and is fine. Some years, his ankle just gets hit rather than rolled up on. And those years, he has been -- and will be -- a player who can win an MVP and perhaps a Super Bowl.

Jackson's latest injury was almost too cruel to believe. After missing the previous three and a half games with a high ankle sprain, the two-time MVP was finally back. So, too, was the Ravens' hope. Jackson had looked phenomenal -- MVP-level phenomenal -- in new offensive coordinator Declan Doyle's system. Not only was he back, but he was back in a big spot: against fellow MVP Josh Allen in a crucial matchup vs. Buffalo on the first day of November, with Baltimore trying to end a two-game losing streak.

And then, on the Ravens' first play, Jackson hit his thumb on Greg Rousseau's arm. Jackson looked down at his injured right thumb, trotted toward the sideline and then took a knee.

"Oh boy," NFL on CBS analyst Ross Tucker said.

Medical personnel surrounded Jackson, draped a towel over his hand and brought him down the tunnel, off the field.

What more was there to say? Lamar Jackson, one of the defining players not just of the era but of NFL history, was hurt. Again. And it was serious. Again.

Jackson officially landed on IR with a thumb injury that will require surgery. It is "potentially" season-ending, and with the Ravens 4-5 and losers of four straight, it's looking like a lost season in Baltimore. This is the fourth time in the last six years the 29-year-old has missed at least four games. It has robbed the Ravens of their Super Bowl aspirations. It has robbed the NFL of a superstar. And it has robbed Jackson, a Heisman Trophy winner and twice an NFL MVP, of even higher levels of all-time greatness.

On the positive side, the thumb injury is the first ailment to land Jackson on IR. He has never had an ACL tear, an Achilles tear, etc. On the negative side, Jackson has racked up so many injuries of so many different kinds that it seems like something is constantly getting in the way, whether it's nagging at him or keeping him out of games. He even said the ankle pain entering the Bills game last week was merely "bearable," not gone.

Since the start of last season alone, Jackson has had hamstring, ankle, toe, knee, back, and thumb ailments. Not all of those kept him out, but they add up. He admitted he came back from last year's hamstring injury too early, impacting his play all season.

And yes, for what it's worth, it's bad luck. If Jackson's hand hits Rousseau at any other angle, he might not hurt his thumb enough to need surgery. If a diving Jermaine Johnson II doesn't awkwardly trap Jackson's ankle to the turf in Week 3, Jackson might not miss the next three games. Josh Allen and Patrick Mahomes have both had hundreds of similar plays and, with the exception of Mahomes' torn ACL last year, avoided injury for the most part. Sometimes, it's simply human anatomy, something Jackson cannot change.

For as prolific a runner as Jackson is, he does an excellent job avoiding hits on those plays. None of his injuries have been on runs. It has just been a constant parade of misfortune. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, the NFL rolls on, whether you're lucky or not.

Perhaps time and injuries have taken their toll and blocked our collective memory from how incredible Jackson has been. That would be truly unfortunate.

In 2019, Jackson's second year of the league, he threw 36 touchdown passes and ran for 1,206 yards. In NFL history, there are ...

In fact, only one other player ever has posted 1,200 rushing yards and merely double-digit passing touchdowns in a season: Spec Sanders of the New York Yankees (football version) in the All-America Football Conference in 1947. But he only threw 14 touchdowns. Jackson did things modern football has never known.

Jackson became the youngest quarterback to win MVP and joined Tom Brady as the only unanimous MVPs in NFL history. There are many ways to measure individual season greatness, but Pro Football Reference's "Approximate Value" is as good as any. Jackson had an approximate value of 25 in 2019, tied for the second-best season on record; LaDanian Tomlinson's 2006 season, in which he scored an NFL-record 31 touchdowns, is the only one better.

Jackson was healthy in 2020 and had another strong season, including a second straight 1,000-yard rushing effort. He has as many 1,000-yard rushing seasons as all other quarterbacks in NFL history combined (Michael Vick and Justin Fields both have one).

Then the injuries started. In 2021, Jackson was on pace for another 1,000-yard rushing season, and the Ravens led the AFC North when Browns linebacker Jeremiah Owusu-Koramoah dove at Jackson's ankles after Jackson released a pass. Jackson got carted off and didn't play in any of the final four games. The Ravens lost all four and missed the playoffs.

The next year, Jackson sprained his PCL when his left knee got driven into the ground on a sack by the Broncos' Jonathan Cooper. He ended up missing six weeks -- the final five of the regular season and a wild card game, which Baltimore lost.

This was a strange one. John Harbaugh initially wondered if Jackson could be back the next week and added "If not, it'll be sometime after that shortly." Then, Ravens officials were confident Jackson would return for the playoffs, per ESPN, leaving Jackson to essentially rule himself out -- and give his own medical update -- via social media.

In 2023, though, Jackson was healthy and marvelous once again. Under new offensive coordinator Todd Monken, Jackson set career highs in completion percentage (67.2%), passing yards (3,678) and yards per attempt (8.0). He was still excellent on the ground with a team-high 821 yards. He won MVP and was one vote short of doing so unanimously.

He was somehow even better in 2024, with 4,172 passing yards and 8.8 yards per attempt. He threw 41 touchdowns and just four interceptions. He ran for 915 yards. He had already reached unprecedented levels. Now, he was reaching new ones, as the first player with:

His 10.25 touchdown-to-interception ratio was also the best ever in any 30-touchdown season.

Somehow Jackson didn't win MVP. He was a first-team All-Pro, but MVP went to Allen, an extreme rarity. Jackson would have been the youngest three-time MVP in NFL history. That's how good the start was.

From 2019 to 2024, Jackson ranked third in passer rating and touchdown-to-interception ratio and fourth in yards per attempt. He was also fourth in touchdown passes despite missing significant time in two seasons. He was also eighth in rushing yards and first in yards per carry.

He was the Shohei Ohtani of football. No one can be this good at two different aspects, right? Wrong. Jackson was truly exceptional, and he seemed to be getting better.

He had nine touchdowns and zero interceptions on an absurd 9.6 yards per attempt through three weeks of 2025. Then came the hamstring injury, which started an avalanche of injuries. The hits haven't stopped coming since.

If two things stand out as Jackson's career shortcomings, it's injuries and playoff performance. The two are somewhat intertwined. In 2021, Baltimore missed the playoffs. In 2022, Jackson missed the playoffs. Last year, his injuries derailed the Ravens, but they somehow got a Week 18 win-and-in game against the Steelers. Tyler Loop missed a game-winning field goal attempt. It's looking like he will not add to his postseason ledger this season, either.

Had Jackson been healthy in even two of those four lost seasons, is he a Super Bowl champ? Or does he at least have more postseason wins?

Jackson is one of nine quarterbacks to win multiple MVP awards since the 1970 AFL-NFL merger. Of those nine, Jackson has the second-fewest playoff wins (two) before his 30th birthday, only ahead of the famously late-blooming Steve Young.

Wins, it is said, are not a quarterback stat, and that can be true. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, both his play and his team's have declined in the postseason. The Ravens are 3-6 in the playoffs since drafting Jackson in 2018 (3-5 with Jackson as the starter).

Jackson lost in his playoff debut in 2018, but the real disappointment came after his 2019 NFL season. With the Ravens as the AFC No. 1 seed, he had 365 yards and a touchdown passing and 143 yards rushing against the Titans, but he also had two interceptions,  lost a fumble, and failed on two fourth-and-short runs.

The next year, Jackson earned his first playoff win, getting a measure of revenge on the Titans in the wild card round. But one week later, Baltimore went quietly in a 17-3 loss to Buffalo, most remembered for Jackson throwing a terrible end-zone interception that Taron Johnson returned 101 yards for a touchdown.

His next playoff game came after his 2023 MVP campaign. He was excellent in a win over the Texans, but in the AFC Championship, he lost a fumble and had another awful end-zone interception, this time in the fourth quarter. The Chiefs won 17-10, and Jackson's two turnovers loomed large.

In 2024, the Ravens, supercharged by the Derrick Henry addition, were on the precipice of a return to the AFC Championship Game before falling to the Bills, 27-25. Jackson again had turnover issues -- a bad interception and an even worse fumble, the latter of which led the a Buffalo touchdown -- but that game is better remembered for a Mark Andrews fumble and, later, drop on the potential game-tying two-point attempt.

There have been tons of moments where supporting cast members have let Jackson down in crucial moments. Andrews' nightmare two seasons ago and Zay Flowers' fumble at the 1-yard line against the Chiefs certainly come to mind. There's a lack of game-changing plays from the defense: two turnovers forced in Jackson's seven postseason starts, and none in the past two playoff trips, when hopes were the highest. But it's undeniable that Jackson has been very poor in the playoffs, too.

The final stat -- turnovers -- is truly staggering ... and costly. Jackson's turnovers have cost the Ravens an estimated 6.86 points per game in the playoffs, basically a touchdown. The only quarterbacks with a higher rate out of the 26-player sample are Drake Maye, Justin Herbert, and Trevor Lawrence.

Unless there's a major turnaround, this stands to be the Jackson's fourth postseason-less season in the last six years. He can't fix a glaring hole in his resume if he can't even get there.

The quick answer is "yes." It might just look a little different.

The first order of business, assuming this season is over for all intents and purposes, will be Jackson's contract. Former NFL agent Joel Corry dove into that recently. Of note ...

Baltimore should absolutely want to keep Jackson, even with the injury history. Through three games this year, he led the league in yards per attempt and was on pace for his highest missed-tackle rate and highest yards per rush of his career, all while not running as much as earlier in his career.

They have also been truly unlucky with injuries. He was healthy in 2023 and 2024. Last year's hamstring and this year's ankle were unfortunate, but the thumb was a freak injury. Perhaps we should expect him to miss a few games every year from now on, but there's also a reasonable chance he doesn't.

Jackson will be 30 next year, but 30 is not ancient. John Elway won his first Super Bowl at 37. Matthew Stafford got his first at 34. Drew Brees was 31. Peyton Manning was 30.

Josh Allen is 30 and, like Jackson, hasn't been to a Super Bowl. Joe Burrow is about a month older than Jackson and hasn't won one.

A common budding misconception is that Jackson can't be a star as he gets older and he runs less and/or less effectively. But Jackson has proved to be a superb pocket quarterback. His development as a passer is as impressive as it gets. Since 2023, Jackson has the third-highest yards per attempt in the NFL on throws inside the pocket and released within three seconds -- aka without much improvisation going on.

Jackson may never reach the heights he was on the path to after being on the precipice of becoming the youngest three-time MVP ever in 2024. That's the sad truth about a player playing hurt and/or missing significant chunks of three years of his prime. Would he have another MVP? Two? A Super Bowl ring? We'll never know, and that's tough to swallow.

But there's a version of Jackson -- a little luckier, a little better in the playoffs, a lot better of a passer than he was his younger years -- that can still be a superstar for many years and get him back on a path that ends with him as an all-time great.

There seems to be a slew of warning signs pointing to the inevitability of the AI bubble bursting a lot sooner than most people think.

The controversial exit of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang (who happens to be recognized as the "high priest" of the global AI buildout) and the stock market volatility tied to companies that have made huge investments in AI are two of the bigger signs pointing to the AI bubble bursting. Plus, there have been plenty of major personalities in the tech and business worlds who have given their thoughts on it becoming a reality at some point.

One of those individuals is the Former Securities and Exchange Commission Chair, Gary Gensler, who sat down with CNN to give his own take on the possibility of the AI burst event coming to fruition.

Here’s what he had to say and why his prediction places it near the next presidential election.

Gensler, who ran the Securities and Exchange Commission from 2021 until 2025, told CNN that today’s AI infrastructure buildout has already crossed into bubble territory and will likely lead into a recession before the next presidential election. "It's already a bubble," the former Wall Street regulator stated. "The only question is if it's a soft recession or something more dramatic."

On the topic of all the big spending dedicated to strengthening the current AI infrastructure (which is said to have reached $800 billion a year by some estimates), Gensler pointed to past trends where major investments in technology eventually stagnate.

“History tells us that when you have a new technology and a significant investment ... you come to a point where it plateaus. It retrenches,” Gensler noted. “The only question is if it’s a soft recession or something more dramatic.” The previous instances he cited as big tech investments that eventually met steep declines included railroads, 1920s electricity and the 1990s dot-com boom.

As for when he sees the AI bubble bursting, he points to it going down sometime before the next presidential election. "I think we're going to have this repricing and a softening of the economy that happens before November 2028," he went on to say. "I can't say exactly when and how stormy, but I would be battening down some hatches."

Gensler is among the many other voices in the tech and investment industries who have given their thoughts on the possibilities of the AI bubble bursting and the resulting fallout. Gary Marcus, the former head of AI for Uber, argued to the Financial Times that today’s AI boom is a massive financial bubble driven by unsustainable economics and technological dead ends:

“If there is a deflation of the AI bubble, the optimists say that the new infrastructure will remain even if the companies do not — just as railways survived the 19th-century railway bust. However, this fails to reckon with the reality of depreciation (few pieces of silicon hold their value for very long because better chips inevitably come along) and the possibility that LLMs could be displaced by more efficient models less dependent on massive numbers of expensive AI chips. In placing massive hyperscaling bets, investors are setting lavish expectations about future earnings. But LLMs are not likely to replicate the near monopolies that have made the market power of current tech giants hard to assail. A better analogy for them might be airlines, which are hobbled by small margins, intense competition, high expenses and dependence on hardware created by outside vendors.” – @garymarcus in @FinancialTimesJune 26, 2026

The Canadian mining financier Frank Giustra was quite frank with his AI bubble burst prediction while speaking at this year’s New Orleans Investment Conference. “There will be a reckoning, and some of these companies will get destroyed," he said. During that same conversation, investor Grant Williams compared the disconnect between recognizing the stock market dangers attached to AI investments and taking proper precautions to a natural disaster.

"If we told you a hurricane was heading to New Orleans, everybody would take precautions," he explained. "The interesting thing about stock market bubbles is people don't really think about what can go wrong. They're still thinking about making money."

While many voices are speaking on the very real possibility of an AI bubble burst, there are business bigwigs who have made it clear that everyone shouldn’t be all that worried about it. During a sit-down interview with CNBC, Amazon founder Jeff Bezos tried to ease everyone’s fears about such a financially devastating event coming to pass. “Even if it does turn out to be a bubble, you shouldn’t worry about it because the bubble is driving investment and a lot of the investment is going to turn out to be very healthy,” he proclaimed.

Bret Taylor, the chairman of OpenAI and CEO of Sierra, spoke to The Verge and flat-out acknowledged that an AI bubble exists while still noting that it's totally fine. And while speaking to CNBC at the 2026 World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland, his AI optimism pointed to a scenario where the market will correct itself as weaker industry players and overfunded ventures will fall by the wayside.

“When everyone knows that AI is going to have a huge impact on the economy across a huge range of industries and workflows, money is plentiful,” Taylor noted. “I think over the next few years, you’ll see a correction, you also see consolidation, but I don’t think you can get innovation without that kind of messy competition.”

Gensler’s prophetic words about the AI bubble bursting before we even reach the next presidential election are alarming, yes.

But I can envision a world where potential candidates use that moment as one of their political talking points in a bid to ease back on all that AI spending, put more into the American populace and hold back on bailing out any of the AI companies that are hurting from such a traumatic event.

All signs point to something devastating happening to the AI industry at some point, especially with the huge backlash against data centers and the politicians who are looking to put in moratoriums on their continued development.

The Helius CEO says financial firms need confidentiality onchain and argues Zcash’s rise could push bitcoin to improve.

Privacy features are a must for bringing institutional finance onto Solana, according to Mert Mumtaz, co-founder and CEO of Helius.

"Institutions will not come onchain unless they have privacy and compliance guarantees," Mumtaz told The Block on Monday at Solana Breakpoint in London.

Mumtaz, whose company builds Solana infrastructure, said confidential lending was a big example. Financial institutions need to be able to transact without exposing private information, and moving onchain shouldn't mean accepting an inferior product, he added.

He compared it to the internet's shift from HTTP to HTTPS, which added stronger protections for sensitive information to help scale online commerce. Blockchain privacy should follow a similar path, he argued.

"Crypto without privacy is not crypto," Mumtaz said. This also extends to Zcash, which Mumtaz sees as a store of value that complements bitcoin.

"If you derive a store of value from first principles, Zcash looks very close to the ideal solution," he said.

Despite acknowledging bitcoin's longer history and wider distribution, he sees competition between the two benefiting users.

"I think it’s good for Bitcoin that Zcash is now getting traction," he said, adding that it could put pressure on bitcoin to improve.

Mumtaz was also optimistic about crypto infrastructure security amid a wave of hacks and exploits, including the worst in history — a $6.4 billion theft from crypto exchange Binance last month. He said these attacks expose weaknesses that developers can repair.

"Within a year, I think crypto will actually be much more secure than the alternative," he said. "I recognize it’ll be painful a little bit, but fundamentally speaking, this is what we’re prepared for."

Probably the most impressive thing about Dungeon Crawler Carl, though, is just how easy the books are to devour. Hardcore skiers can be difficult to shop for.

The more involved you become as a skier, the more specific your wants and needs get. Suddenly, the standard goggles no longer apply, and you choose your jacket not based on what looks cool but on what functions in the ways you need. If you have a dedicated skier in your life, it’s not always obvious what to gift them, especially since you’ll want them to use it and love it.

To help ease your mind, we’ve rounded up some of our favorite gift ideas for the people in our lives who eat, sleep, and breathe skiing: from powderhounds to corduroy snobs, this list has a little something for everyone. And we can guarantee it—we’re somewhat of ski hardos ourselves.

We all have that one friend who complains a little too much about being cold. But when your butt freezes the second you sit on the lift, can you blame them? That’s where insulated knickers come in. Throw them on over your base layers for extra protection your derrière didn’t know it needed. Wear them over your base layer for some extra warmth or on their own for a more loose-fitting base under your ski pants. These are the perfect gift for the skier you know who likes to get a lot of days in and stay nice and toasty on the hill. —Giorgia Menetre, Senior Social Media Strategist

Good goggles don’t need to cost a fortune. Dependable click-in, click-out interchangeable lenses are a staple for a reason—they’ll get you through just about every condition.  Smith’s OG reliable goggles offer a wide field of vision, ChromaPop lenses that make the view pop, and a flexible, comfortable frame. Lenses swap easily, with no worry of a magnetic slip-up. Don’t let another ski day go by letting the skier you love squint through bad light. —GM

These booties come with a high price tag, but they’ll be the gift hardcore skiers never forget. This updated compression boot from Normatec takes the pressurized, full-length boot system that has proven recovery patterns and adds heat. Dynamic air circulation throughout the boot’s treatment session helps muscles recover faster and reduces soreness. Remember how good it feels to put an achy and tired calf next to a hot tub jet? Now, imagine that level of recovery from the comfort of your couch. Boots come in three sizes and can fit a wide range of heights.

Please note that boots will ship the week of 11/23. —Jamie Aranoff, SKI Senior Editor

Hestra’s Fall Line mittens are consistently ranked as some of the warmest by our testers. The mittens have a cozy inner layer of brushed polyester and foam to keep hands insulated without overheating. The outside is leather that, once broken in, is soft and dexterous. Wrist straps ensure that the mittens stay with the skier, and not at the bottom of a chairlift. The mittens are on the pricier side, but will surely be a gift that skiers cherish for years. The same warmth also comes in a glove. —JA

Scrambled Eggs Get A Major Protein And Flavor Boost When You Add This Omega-Rich Ingredient

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When many of us think "hot breakfast," we tend to naturally gravitate toward thoughts of scrambled eggs. Aside from the fact that, when prepared correctly, they are tender, delicate, and fluffy (or custardy, depending on how you like them), eggs are considered the gold standard for protein, so they are quite wholesome as well. On their own, though, they can get a little monotonous, especially if you're making them every day, so it does help to add an ingredient here or there. And if you happen to enjoy omega-3-rich cold-smoked salmon, well, it pairs seamlessly with a classic scramble, boosting both the flavor and the protein content.

Once considered an expensive delicacy, smoked salmon is quite popular now in the U.S., and as a result, it's much easier to find at your local grocery store (even Aldi carries its own version, and it can be found at Walmart, too). Cold-smoked salmon, despite the fact that it tends to be packed in super-thin slices, is buttery and velvety. While it does taste fishy, there are also light, smoky notes that give it a deeply savory flavor, which works beautifully when combined with the mild egg scramble. Additionally, a serving (which will vary in size) might contain anywhere from 12 to 18 grams of protein, which is great if you have daily goals you want to meet or you're just looking to get more of the macronutrient in general.

Truly, the most difficult part about incorporating smoked salmon into your scramble is cooking the eggs to the right consistency without burning or browning them. All you have to do with the salmon is slice the ultra-thin pieces into slivers or small squares. Once your eggs have reached the texture you desire, you'll take the pan off the heat and fold in the salmon. That's literally it; you don't have to actually cook the salmon over direct heat because it's so delicate that the residual warmth of the scrambled eggs will gently bring it up to temperature.

Now, because smoked salmon is so light and thin, you might want your eggs to match that energy, so create a fluffy, soft texture with a bit of cream whisked in. Alternatively, you could take a page out of Bobby Flay's book and fold in a dollop of crème fraîche, or really up the decadence and melt in some cubes of cream cheese (and take care not to overcook anything, because smoked salmon with burnt, rubbery eggs? No thanks). Also, because all of the elements involved are either rich or thick, a sprinkling of something green, like fresh chives or dill, or even a little parsley or tarragon, would brighten things up, both aesthetically and in terms of flavor.
