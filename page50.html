5 Fall/Winter 2026 Wedding Guest Dress Trends You Can Still Shop

The season of celebration is upon us. With winter weddings kicking into full swing and the Resort and Spring collection deliveries just around the corner, now is the moment to make a final play for Fall/Winter 2026’s most coveted runway looks. After all, self-expression is the unofficial theme du jour, with designers embracing personality-driven dressing through bold colors and lush fabrics; so much of what we saw lends itself perfectly to formalwear. In the wedding guest dress department, there’s no shortage of ways to make an entrance, from full-on glamour to elegant twists on tradition.

Use the season’s defining trends your starting point. Art Deco made a glamorous impression on the runways, with geometric shapes and ornate embellishments taking center stage. Its offshoots, like plush velvet and fringe detailing, feel especially apropos for winter weddings; as does red, a festive and bold colorway that appeared this season across a range of silhouettes from designers like Carolina Herrera and Tory Burch. And for a bit of drama, we’re loving all things dark romance—think, sheer lace, decadent ribboning, and an all-over inky palette.

Before a new season takes over the fashion conversation, give Fall/Winter one last moment in the spotlight—and find your next wedding look in the process. Scroll down to shop our favorite dresses straight from the runways.

Maximilian Davis at Ferragamo, Peter Copping at Lanvin and Daniella Kallmeyer each offered exquisite interpretations of the style that feel destined for the dance floor—and give you every reason to stay until the last song. With their Art Deco-inspired silhouettes and unabashed glamour, these shorter styles are made for celebrations that sit just shy of black tie. To keep the look on the right side of modern sophistication, remember: the trick is to channel the era, not recreate it. Resist the temptation to over-accessorize; a considered approach to jewelry will let the dress do all the talking.

This time of year, red is basically a neutral; accessorize with glitzy metallics for the more festive ceremonies on your calendar. From Carolina Herrera and Fendi to Tory Burch, designers offered a compelling range of silhouettes to suit every sensibility and dress code. The most spirited interpretation came courtesy of Duran Lantink for Jean Paul Gaultier, whose voluminous sleeves were ever so slightly tempered in the commercial version of the dress. Its ankle length is best-suited for a black-tie optional wedding (or anything that leans more thematic).

Romance took a darker turn this season. Erdem, Simone Rocha, The Attico, and Altuzarra explored their own interpretations through inky lace, sheer chiffon, high collars, and dramatic bows. The effect is alluring, a little subversive, and imbued with just the right amount of mystery for an after-hours wedding affair. Here, makeup plays a more important role—keep your lipstick understated, choosing a muted neutral over anything crimson to avoid veering into vamp territory.

Whether draped, ruched, or ruffled, velvet brings an inherent sense of luxury to the wedding circuit. Its versatility makes it a fitting choice for everything from intimate ceremonies to grand affairs; the silhouette and hemline, of course, will determine its dress-code credentials. When it comes to styling, take a cue from Pieter Mulier’s Alaïa, where velvet gowns were paired with barely-there shoes and luminous, glowing skin. Jewelry, if any, should be kept to a minimum: A single, discreet earring is all the embellishment you need when the dress is this rich.

If velvet brought the opulence this season, fringe supplied the theatrics. Leave any bohemian preconceptions at the door: This season, the embellishment took on a decidedly more sophisticated character. Gabriela Hearst, Ulla Johnson, Akris, and Altuzarra each offered their own interpretation, from fluid strands tracing the contours of sleek evening gowns to exuberant cascades that transformed minimalist silhouettes into something way more fun. These are dresses best-suited to cocktail receptions and formal RSVPs, where a little theatricality is always welcome.

The Trump administration’s approach to cutting red tape is starting to pay off.

A rendering of the proposed Xe-100 nuclear reactor facility at Dow’s Seadrift plant in Texas.

Federal regulators have approved construction of four advanced nuclear reactors at a Dow chemical plant in Texas, the first approval of its type in 60 years and the next step in America’s red tape–free nuclear renaissance.

The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) issued a construction permit for the proposed plant featuring four Xe-100 small modular reactors (SMRs) at Dow’s Seadrift site in Texas — the first construction permit for a high-temperature gas-cooled reactor issued in the U.S. in more than half a century. It’s the most recent sign that President Trump’s cutting of nuclear red tape last year is paying dividends.

A Swing Through Montana’s Art Treasures

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Life on the range, Native spirits, Vienna, and Little Big Horn are pretty good raw material.

A “Greetings from Montana” postcard published by the E. C. Kropp Company, c. 1940.

Steven R. Shook Collection, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0

Today I’ll write about Big Sky Country — Montana. I’d never been. The Mountain States aren’t the place to go for Raphaels, Rembrandts, and Renoirs, but there are treasures to be found. What ought to be there? I very easily found my answer: Montana’s own Charles M. Russell, the perceptive, precise artist famous for his cowboy pictures but proudest of his scenes of Montana’s Chippewa, Blood Indian, and Lakota Natives. At 16, Russell (1864–1926) left school in Missouri to work on a sheep ranch in Montana. He lived under those spacious skies for the rest of his life, making thousands of works of art. A contemporary of Frederic Remington, he’s less known but just as good if not better.

Russell has a museum of his own in Great Falls, Mont., but he’s also got a suite of revelatory galleries at the Montana Heritage Center in Helena, my starting point as I explored the state from west to east. The Heritage Center, whose galleries are filled with art and artifacts from Montana’s Historical Society, opened late last year after a $160 million expansion and renovation. It’s wonderful. The Russell section is more or less chronological and a mix of art, hagiography, and atmosphere. I loved it. The entire new complex is great. It’s got a Russell section as well as multiple galleries on Montana’s history.

Best Laptops for Travel (2026): Which to Take on the Road

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As a laptop reviewer, I have plenty of devices to choose from when I travel for work. In some cases, my favorite overall laptop doesn’t translate into the one I want to travel with. When I’m squeezed into a plane, I’ll choose maximum battery life and portability over features like a bigger screen or more ports.

Fortunately, there are many great travel laptops, and you’ll still appreciate them when you aren’t on the road. Of the hundreds of laptops I’ve tested in my life, here are the ones I always choose to travel with.

If there’s an obvious pick for the best travel laptop, it’s the Dell XPS 13. It’s been the best compact laptop you can buy for years, and that’s even more true in its latest incarnation. The key dimension to pay attention to on the XPS 13 is the depth, which is crucial for air travel. At 7.9 inches, it’s among the smallest laptops you can buy. Those economy-class seat-back tray tables on airplanes are only around 10 inches deep, and while that’s technically enough to accommodate even a 16-inch MacBook Pro, whether you can actually open the lid and use the laptop is another question. The XPS 13 is even smaller than the MacBook Neo, despite having a larger screen.

The XPS 13 achieves this by using a 13.4-inch display (measured diagonally), compared to many of the 14-inch laptops on this list. Second, it has very narrow bezels around that screen, minimizing the device’s overall footprint. While 14-inch laptops have become more popular in recent years, that bigger display size also increases the overall footprint—and every millimeter counts when working on a plane. The XPS 13 still has enough room for your palms when typing (unless you have huge hands), and the keyboard is among my favorites to type on.

The other two major advantages for travel are weight and battery life. This little laptop only weighs 2.2 pounds, a full half-pound lighter than the 13-inch MacBook Air. You might forget you have it in your backpack while traveling.

Now, the XPS 13 isn't the most powerful laptop in the world—at least not the cheaper models. The standard version comes with only 8 GB of RAM and an Intel chip with only two Performance cores. It’s good enough for basic web tasks, but for real work, you might run into limitations. You can address this by opting for the 16 GB model (which keeps the price under $1,000) or upgrading to the much more powerful Core Ultra 7 355 processor. The latter is much more expensive and comes with twice the storage, but it doesn’t add extra weight or size. Regardless of the chip you choose, the XPS 13 gets absolutely fantastic battery life. It depends on what you're doing with it, but you could easily work through a long international flight without worrying about plugging in.

If weight is your primary concern, the Asus Zenbook A14 is another contender. Despite being larger overall than the XPS 13 (with a bigger screen), the Zenbook A14 weighs 2.16 pounds—a hair lighter than the Dell. That’s seriously impressive, and I still remember how astounded I was when I first picked it up. Asus debuts its “ceraluminum” material in the chassis, which gives you the sturdiness of aluminum with the lightness of plastic. It’s not quite as rigid as metal, but it’s not as flimsy as ultra-light laptops in the past.

If you’re less worried about working on the plane and more about lugging a laptop around, the Zenbook A14 is a good choice. The Zenbook A14 has several advantages over the XPS 13 that can be helpful on the go, including built-in ports like a headphone jack, USB-A, and HDMI. If you’re working out of a remote office or presenting to a client, these can definitely come in handy. The XPS 13 only has two USB-C ports and nothing else.

There are a number of different versions of the Asus Zenbook A14 floating around out there. I’d stay away from the 8 GB model Asus sells and opt for the 16 GB model instead. There’s also a newer model that uses the Snapdragon X2 Elite chip, but I don’t think it’s worth the extra $450—especially not for a laptop focused on travel.

The 13-inch MacBook Air also makes a great travel laptop. It’s relatively small, thanks to its 13.6-inch screen.

In terms of portability, its main advantage over the XPS 13 and Zenbook A14 is its thinness. At 0.42 inches, it’s the thinnest laptop out there—and it doesn’t stick huge rubber feet on the bottom to compensate like other laptops do, such as the Razer Blade 14. You might not think thickness matters much in a travel laptop, but if you’re squeezing stuff into a backpack, every extra millimeter matters.

The MacBook Neo is also worth mentioning here. It’s not quite as thin as the MacBook Air, but because its screen is 0.4 inches smaller, it has a more compact overall footprint. Battery life is the bigger issue when taking the Neo on a trip, as it doesn’t last as long as the MacBook Air or Dell XPS 13. The 8 GB of RAM limitation doesn’t affect its use as a travel laptop much, but it's something to consider for a work laptop in general. That said, it has a very pretty screen for a laptop at this price.

Budget laptops aren’t typically known for being particularly lightweight and travel-worthy. Most of them struggle, especially with battery life. The HP OmniBook 5 might be an exception. You can usually find it on sale for under $700, even with 16 GB of RAM and an OLED display.

The OmniBook 5 isn’t quite as compact as the MacBook Neo because of its larger screen, but it is also half an inch thick and weighs only 2.8 pounds. The Snapdragon X Plus also delivers solid battery life, plenty to feel comfortable traveling with. It’s not a laptop without compromises (like the poor touchpad), but it comes with 16 GB of RAM, unlike some other laptops at this price. That’s a solid deal if you need something to travel with on a budget.

Good question. Tablets can make for great travel devices because of their size and ease of use. If you need a proper keyboard and touchpad—and the app support to handle a typical workday—you’re probably better off getting a laptop.

The Surface Pro 12 is the two-in-one laptop I’d recommend first. It’s absolutely tiny for a computer, even though it runs the full version of Windows 11. Even with the detachable keyboard connected, it weighs only 1.8 pounds. The only real problem with bringing this on a plane is the kickstand, which adds some extra depth. It’s just small enough that it should fit while open on an airplane tray table, unlike the 13.8-inch Surface Pro model.

Of course, that kickstand is also a huge advantage for the Surface Pro 12 if you’re using it without the keyboard. While that would make work difficult, it’s ideal for watching movies. Set this up on your tray table with some movies downloaded, and you’ll be the envy of your aisle. And when you need to get some work done in a coffee shop or at a smaller table, you’ll be happy to have access to the keyboard and touchpad—both of which are very comfortable to use. Just know that it's sold separately.

The iPad Air, paired with the Magic Keyboard, avoids the Surface Pro 12’s kickstand issue. And with only an 11-inch screen, it’s perfect for use in small spaces—with or without the keyboard (note that there is also a 13-inch version). If you can accomplish all your work on an iPad, more power to ya! The iPad Air should give you everything you need, so long as you get the Magic Keyboard. If your travels involve more leisurely activities, the iPad Air is a great device for reading by the pool or for playing games.

One benefit of this latest version of the iPad Air is not only its performance (thanks to the M4 chip inside) but also the front-facing camera, which has been moved to landscape for more natural-feeling video calls. Of course, you could also opt for the 11-inch iPad Pro for extra performance (and the incredible tandem OLED screen). It’s also slightly thinner and lighter, though I don't think it's worth the significant price difference.

Lastly, there’s definitely room here for laptops with 360-degree hinges—a different class of two-in-one laptops. I haven’t tested a recent Windows device with this form factor that I truly loved, but there’s no question it works well on an airplane. Just fold the screen back with the keyboard face down, and you have the perfect screen for watching movies. The Lenovo Yoga 9i 14 is a gorgeous option, though it’s incredibly expensive right now. But if you can afford it, you’ll love the 2-in-1 action combined with one of the slickest designs imaginable. The Framework Laptop 12 is also worth considering. While it’s a bit thick and heavy for a travel laptop, its smaller screen, touchscreen, and 360-degree hinge make it a good option. It’s also one of the cheaper options available, and it’s user-upgradeable.

Whether you’re packing for a trip across the world or across the state, a proper travel laptop shouldn’t be heavy or overly large. That rules out 15-inch and 16-inch laptops, which are so big you can’t use them on airplane tray tables or at coffee shops. But even when you narrow it down to 14-inch laptops or smaller, there are three portability factors to consider.

When you’re on the go, you want a laptop that doesn’t constantly make you worry about finding an outlet. Bringing a laptop power bank can help, but the biggest factor in staying sane while traveling is a device that can stay on. Many factors affect how long a laptop can last, including CPU efficiency, battery wattage, screen brightness and resolution, and more.

Long-lasting laptops, such as the Dell XPS 13, will get you through a long flight. The processor is the main thing to look out for. Qualcomm’s Snapdragon X series of chips are battery-efficient, as are Apple’s M-series chips. Intel also has some highly efficient CPUs in its most recent lineup, though you’ll need to pay close attention to the model number, as it can vary wildly depending on the specific CPU you’re using. To dig deeper, read my extensive guide on How to Buy the Right Laptop, which breaks down different CPU options.

The size of seat-back tray tables varies. There’s no exact size because of various factors, such as the airline, aircraft, cabin, and seat. Remember: Just because a laptop can physically fit on a tray table doesn’t mean you’ll have space to open and use it. With that in mind, here are the general averages to keep in mind. Airlines do not list tray table sizes, but you can expect one in economy to measure around 16 x 9 inches, while premium cabins will have larger tables depending on the seat.

I’ve included both tablets and laptops in my recommendations, but your choice will depend on why you’re traveling with a device. If it’s for a vacation and you really only need a device to read, watch, and play on (perhaps with the occasional email check), a tablet works perfectly. Lounging with a tablet, whether on a plane or by the pool, feels much more leisurely than using a laptop. More importantly, it'll help you avoid dipping back into work mode. Whether an Android tablet, iPad, or Windows tablet, just pair it with a compatible keyboard case—and you’re good to go.

However, if you’re traveling for work (or need to work while traveling), a proper laptop will be better. The one you already have may work just fine, but frequent fliers know they prefer a more compact device. And more specifically, a small laptop with a keyboard and trackpad will work better on your lap or on small desks than a tablet.

As more Americans struggle to put food on the table, crowdsourcing money for groceries has become consumers’ balm to an ongoing affordability crisis.

Food-related GoFundMe fundraisers have increased 35% year-over-year in the platform’s English-speaking markets of the U.S., Canada, UK, Australia, and Ireland, including a 40% jump in the U.S., according to the company. The amount of money given to these campaigns increased 50% since the year before.

“The costs of regular things—food, rent, gas prices, obviously, particularly this year, and not just here but elsewhere in the world—have gone up, and folks are struggling in many cases to be able to afford all of that,” CEO Tim Cadogan told Fortune. “Things are more expensive, and a lot of people’s incomes have not moved up at a commensurate rate.”

This year’s growing fundraising demand for essentials is part of a larger trend that began during the pandemic, when the intersection of widespread job losses,health emergencies, and a transition to virtual community led more people to seek help online.

GoFundMe introduced the food, rent, and monthly bills tab in October 2020 in response to an increased need for necessities during the pandemic. Martin Lukk, a postdoctoral research fellow at the Data Sciences Institute at the University of Toronto, said while the company created the new category as a result of increased demand, it also was a catalyst for even more people seeking help through the platform.

“In response to an overwhelming number of campaigns for exactly these things, they invented this new category, and that seemed to usher in this new era of desperation that probably really didn’t exist during the pandemic,” Lukk told Fortune.

Fundraisers for essentials also increased last year, when the platform saw a 20% rise in the number of fundraisers to cover essentials from rent to utilities to groceries, which quadrupled the year before. The “monthly bills” category on the platform was among the fastest-growing, second only to individual support for nonprofits.

“We have been seeing that trend now really for about four years, really since the late stage of the pandemic had a lot of impacts on supply chains that’s flowed through,” Cadogan said.

There are also variables outside the economy that may have contributed to the shift toward more necessity-related campaigns, including more people simply knowing about GoFundMe.

Crowdsourced fundraising was documented as early as the 1700s, when the Irish Loan Fund gave small loans to low-income rural families who would pool resources together. The U.S. saw perhaps its most famous crowdfunding campaign in 1885, when publisher and lawmaker Joseph Pulitzer launched an effort to raise money for the pedestal on which the Statue of Liberty would rest. More than 160,000 donors would raise $100,000 in about five months. But the rise of GoFundMe, which was founded in 2010, coincided with the financial crisis tightening lending requirements, forcing artists and entrepreneurs to turn to the internet to raise money. Ventures soon expanded from creative endeavors to philanthropic initiatives and charity drives.

This year’s increase in fundraising correlates with ongoing affordability woes stoked specifically by the Iran war and tariff uncertainty. Americans now spend nearly half their income on food and housing alone, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)’s annual Consumer Expenditures Report published last month. Compared to the year before, food spending increased 4.6%, and housing expenditures climbed 5.8%, with the share of spending on these necessities reaching its highest in 15 years.

While much of that increase is related to rising costs, it’s also a result of stagnating wages. Minutes from the latest Federal Open Market Committee meeting found wage growth for most industries was lagging behind the inflation rate of 3.7% over the last 12 months. According to BLS data, the average wage growth in the U.S. over the last 12 months ending in September 2026 was around 3.1%. Inflation-adjusted wages and salaries fell 0.6% over the same period.

“I wouldn’t give up on social policies,” he added, “and at least to continue imagining a world where some of these could reduce the kind of desperation that we see flowing out onto GoFundMe.”

Your AI agent has a wallet. Who answers for its decisions?

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last week, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet, its second attempt at agentic payments after shelving in-chat checkout earlier this year. Its agents can now pay merchants on a user’s behalf, but every purchase still requires a human in the loop to approve it.

That safeguard will not last, and it shouldn’t. An agent that needs your approval for every purchase is a faster checkout button, not an agent. Once approval moves from the transaction to the instruction, payments lose the assumption they were built on: that a human decides, at the moment of payment, that this is what they want.

The industry is making rapid progress on how agents pay with existing payment methods. It has not yet solved what agents are allowed to decide, or who pays when they decide wrong. The companies that solve that problem will define the agentic economy.

Imagine asking your agent to plan a family vacation within a $5,000 budget. It books nonrefundable flights on the wrong weekend, a hotel 40 minutes from the beach, and accepts a counteroffer from the airline’s own agent that quietly strips out checked bags.

Every payment was authorized. Every merchant delivered exactly what was ordered. The trip is still nothing like what you asked for.

OpenAI’s terms leave delivery and returns with merchants and preserve existing card dispute protections. However, agents introduce a different kind of failure: a purchase that was properly authorized and fulfilled, but did not reflect the user’s instructions. Existing dispute mechanisms may offer remedies, but they do not clearly establish who should bear the loss when the failure lies in the agent’s judgment.

Authorization to spend is not authorization to judge. Today’s infrastructure only knows how to verify the first.

The industry is beginning to build verifiable mandates. Google’s Agent Payments Protocol, for example, uses cryptographically signed mandates to record what a user asked for and approved. That is necessary, but not sufficient. A signature can prove what was authorized. It cannot prove that the agent interpreted the user’s preferences correctly, acted in their interest, or was free of outside incentives. Closing that gap requires a broader idea of what the industry has started calling Know Your Agent. Today it means verifying whose agent is acting and what it was authorized to do. It must also mean verifying whether the agent exercised that authority as intended. That requires three layers.

First, mandates that capture intent. In practice, agent permissions still center on price limits, timing and product parameters. A meaningful mandate also encodes intent: the user’s priorities, the trade-offs they accept and reject, and the conditions that require escalation, such as “nothing nonrefundable without asking me.” Relevant constraints must be verifiable across the transaction, by the merchant’s agent, the payment provider and any later arbiter, without unnecessarily exposing the user’s private instructions. A spending limit tells the system how much. A mandate tells it what for.

Second, a liability framework. When an agent acts inside its spending authority but outside its mandate, the loss could land in many places: the user who gave an ambiguous instruction, the provider whose model misread it, the merchant whose agent extracted worse terms, the platform that ranked the options, or the payment provider that executed the transaction. No rule decides among them today, so every failure is likely to become a dispute. To earn consumers’ trust, the allocation should be settled in advance, for example by asking which party can prevent the harm at the lowest cost. Other industries have done this. The card industry’s EMV liability shift made the party that had not adopted chip technology bear certain counterfeit-fraud losses, creating a powerful incentive to upgrade. Product liability law allocates defects along the supply chain. Clear allocation also creates a market: risk that can be assigned can be priced and insured.

Third, a duty of loyalty. OpenAI is not taking a cut of Wallet transactions at launch. But business models evolve. Once an agent’s platform earns money from merchants, through fees, paid placement or advertising, the agent faces a conflict of interest its user cannot see. Financial regulation addressed a similar conflict for brokers and advisers through disclosure and best-interest obligations. Consumer agents need enforceable standards for whose interests they serve, how paid influence is disclosed, and how compliance with the user’s mandate can be independently verified.

AI labs, payment companies and commerce platforms are writing the agentic protocols right now. Private standards are well suited to identity, credentials and payment flows, because every participant benefits from interoperability. They are structurally weak on liability, because every party at the table would prefer the loss to land somewhere else.

I have worked on both sides of the rulebook, as chair of the FATF’s Risk Group and as a fintech executive. In both roles, the rules that worked were the ones that settled, in advance, who absorbs failure. Systems that answer that question scale. Systems that leave it open stall, or have the answer imposed by regulators after a visible failure, usually in terms written for the last crisis.

The companies that build mandates, clear liability rules and loyalty into their agents now will not be slowed down by it. They will be the ones consumers trust with the wallet. Card payments became global infrastructure because people believed that when something went wrong, someone would make it right. Agents need the same promise, across consumers, AI platforms, merchants and financial institutions that must trust decisions none of them fully controls.

Whoever makes that promise credible will do more than enable agentic payments. They will help write the rules of the new economy.
