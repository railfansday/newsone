Though, in my experience with recent iPhones, that was never particularly appealing. On-screen controls always felt faster and simpler, especially since I could adjust things like zoom without taking my hands off the phone itself.

ANALYSIS | How Miro Muheim and Albert Grønbæk were catalysts for Hamburg’s 3-1 win over Borussia Mönchengladbach

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Hamburg finally won again. On a cold Friday night at the Volksparkstadion, they beat Borussia Mönchengladbach 3–1. These three vital points, coming just before the international break, put them on 12 points, three points adrift of Werder Bremen, who occupy a European spot.

HSV also put in an impressive performance throughout the game. Some might argue that the result was influenced by the fact that Gladbach played with 10 men from the 49th minute onwards.

This did indeed impact the game. However, some important moments in the game may have been evident from the outset.

Kou Itakura’s red card actually seems predictable from the very beginning. The reason is simple. He repeatedly struggled when Albert Grønbæk tried to pass him on the right. Whether it was his own decision or that of the coaching staff, Itakura’s tendency to play as a close defender was only causing him trouble.

The problem is that Itakura is not a fast defender, yet he was playing on the side where HSV were targeting their attack. Unlike Philipp Sander, who played in the centre and used his close proximity and constant pressing to Patson Daka to good effect, Itakura is playing in the right space where his decision to press is not necessarily important.

This was especially the case when they couldn’t find a way to build up play with short passes, as HSV tended to send long passes directly forward, with Daka usually becoming the target. Constant pressing became the right approach.

With such an aggressive approach, it was only a matter of time before Itakura received his second yellow card after getting his first in the first half. He was sent off after just 49 minutes.

Miro Muheim’s goal also seemed destined to happen. He has always had a special talent for shooting, but in this game it was his positioning that made the difference.

From the early game, Muheim tried to move around to find the right space in which to receive the ball from his teammates. He always tried to become the free man for HSV.

He moved diagonally (inside or outside) and was constantly looking to become available in the final third, and so it was no surprise when he had the space to unleash his beautiful shot that it put HSV in the lead for the first time in the match.

Alongside Albert Grønbæk, Muheim was also the key to Merlin Polzin’s strategy of overloading HSV’s left side in the second half. This triggered the second goal – not to mention the fact that they were playing with an extra man.

This overload, combined with intelligent diagonal play, was key to HSV’s second goal. This diagonal play was also something that had already been signalled in the first half. The Red Shorts constantly moved the ball from the outside to the inside, especially when they were able to pull Gladbach’s centre-back out wide, opening up space in the half-space.

For that purpose of diagonal play and exploiting the half-space, Grønbæk was their specialist. He knew exactly how to position himself in the half-space to receive the ball and create opportunities. Their second goal came from a similar situation, with Grønbæk receiving a beautiful diagonal pass from Albert Sambi Lokonga before assisting the homegrown Shafiq Nandja.

What’s more, Grønbæk didn’t look like he was struggling at all when he received the ball while facing his own goal. He could still turn his body quickly, and it was this that caused Itakura to receive a red card.

His combination with Muheim, Sambi Lokonga and wing-back David Møller Wolfe on the left side caused Gladbach a lot of problems. In the second half, coach Melzin emphasised the overload on this side to support them.

Grønbæk later finished the game with a spectacular goal from outside the box, showing not only his shooting ability, but also his ability to contribute to many phases of the game.

He is not a fancy dribbler, but a player who creates opportunities through constant movement, good positioning and involvement in many phases of the game. When he found the space to shoot, it was the combination of these attributes that allowed him to do so.

Alexander Blessin is renowned for his ability to inspire his team to press high and play with intensity. Gladbach showed this approach in the first half, with their high press forcing HSV to play long balls frequently.

The problem was that the collective synergy wasn’t always there. This meant that the gap between their first pressing unit and the defensive line was sometimes too big, with the defensive line dropping too deep too quickly. This created a gap between the lines that HSV were able to exploit, which resulted in a goal.

When in the drop structure, the wingers or forward players were sometimes late to track back, so Gladbach were outnumbered.

These issues are, of course, solvable, as Gladbach have only been training under Blessin for a few weeks and are still in the process of adapting. And if we’re studying Blessin’s previous teams, it will create hope that Gladbach will improve structurally, as his teams were usually strong in collective defence.

Another area that Gladbach still need to work on, at least according to this game, is their finishing. Their goal came from a shot with an expected goal (xG) value of 0.05 (although the small deflection was higher). Meanwhile, they missed two big chances and plenty of other opportunities. If they had finished better, they could have made it 2-2 or even 3-3.

The return of captain Tim Kleindienst and perhaps Hugo Bolin, who is also injured, could improve their attack. So, once Blessin has a full squad, we will be able to judge Gladbach’s performance fully.

They need to be efficient and minimise unnecessary mistakes, like those made by Itakura against HSV. Their journey to escape the relegation spot will be tougher than before, especially as they need to face Borussia Dortmund, in-form Werder Bremen and Bayer Leverkusen in their next three games.

Scientists figured out how to use engineered bacteria to dissolve rocks faster and capture carbon dioxide

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Engineered ocean bacteria kept rust off olivine sand and sped up rock weathering, a slow process that pulls carbon dioxide into seawater.

As rock slowly wears away, it releases compounds that trap carbon dioxide and take it out of the atmosphere. The trouble is speed. Left alone, the process takes thousands of years, partly because iron in the rock turns to rust and coats the surface.

Now, a team led by Harvard researchers has engineered a common ocean bacterium to keep that rust from building up. In tanks of real harbor water, the microbes made crushed rock dissolve about three times faster than plain seawater did.

That’s a big jump for a process that has looked too slow to matter. So far the researchers have tested it only in lab reactors and a few glass tanks, not in the open ocean.

Neil Dalvie, a synthetic biology fellow in systems biology at Harvard Medical School, led the study with colleagues at the Wyss Institute and the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability.

Dalvie told Earth.com he wanted to highlight the interdisciplinary nature of the work.

“We learned about alkalinity and mineral measurements from geochemists in the rock weathering industry,” he said. “Very few people have both the skillsets for molecular biology, microbial engineering, and mineral processing.”

“Some people describe rock weathering as a silver bullet for climate change,” said Dalvie. “The problem is that it’s too slow.”

The team worked with olivine, a common mineral, in the form of sand. When olivine dissolves in seawater, it gives off magnesium, silicon and iron.

The magnesium is the useful part. It makes seawater more alkaline, and alkaline seawater takes up carbon dioxide from the air. This is held as bicarbonate, a form of carbon dissolved in water.

Because magnesium stays in the ocean for a very long time, the authors count that carbon as stored for good.

Iron is the problem. It doesn’t stay dissolved. It forms a layer of rust on the rock, and that layer slows any further dissolving.

The rust is one reason that natural rock weathering takes so long. Spreading crushed rock on farm fields or coastlines helps, but the authors note that the carbon captured that way has been hard to measure.

Many microbes already have a way to deal with rust. When they run low on iron, they release molecules that grab iron and make it dissolve in water. Biologists call them siderophores.

Once a microbe has enough iron, though, it stops making these molecules. And a microbe doesn’t need much.

The team tested this with Alteromonas macleodii, a marine bacterium that makes its own siderophore. The researchers ran 16 small lab reactors with different amounts of olivine sand and different flow rates.

The bacteria made large amounts of the molecule only when there was little rock and the water moved fast.

With more rock, the bacteria had iron to spare and quit. The authors concluded that the bacterium’s natural settings rule out steady siderophore production in a tank big enough for industrial use.

So the team rewired the bacterium. They replaced the genetic switch that turns siderophore production on only when iron runs short with one that keeps it on all the time.

Dalvie said the change itself was fairly easy. Showing that it worked was the hard part.

“The bacteria keep growing, the cells keep making siderophores, the rock keeps dissolving – it’s very hard to measure all these things at the same time,” Dalvie said.

In the small reactors, the engineered strain produced more than 100 times as much of the siderophore as the natural strain. Olivine dissolved about 2.6 times faster with the engineered cells than in reactors with no cells at all, while the natural strain made no clear difference.

Then came a bigger test. The researchers placed more than 8.8 pounds (4 kilograms) of construction-grade olivine sand in the bottom of 3-gallon (11-liter) glass fish tanks.

Unfiltered seawater from Boston Harbor flowed through each tank at about 1.6 quarts (1.5 liters) a day. Some tanks got a slow drip of nutrients, and two of those also got a fresh dose of the engineered bacteria once a week.

In those two tanks, magnesium came off the rock 3.1 times faster. The water also became more alkaline, which the team calculated as about 0.018 ounce (0.50 grams) of carbon dioxide removed from the air each day.

That’s about half the weight of a paper clip. The amount is small, but it was measured directly in the water rather than estimated from a model.

In earlier small reactors fed raw seawater, the engineered strain held steady for about a week before its numbers fell. That’s why the pilot tanks got a fresh dose weekly.

The strain’s staying power surprised the team. “We were actually surprised that our originally engineered strains were able to survive and perform for over a week in unfiltered seawater,” Dalvie said.

“We think that biofilms may help protect the bacteria from growth competition and viruses in the seawater.”

Bacteria have to eat, and making their food releases carbon dioxide too. So the team ran a life-cycle analysis, an accounting of the emissions from building and running a full-size version.

Sugar failed on both counts. Making it emits too much, and in the lab, feeding it to the bacteria left the water less alkaline, likely because of acidic byproducts. The researchers switched to acetate, a simple food that some companies now make from carbon dioxide itself.

Ordinary bacteria failed the accounting too. Feeding wild microbes enough to keep them hungry for iron would release far more carbon dioxide than the faster weathering captured.

With engineered bacteria and a carbon-neutral acetate feed, the model predicted 74 percent more net carbon removal than a tank of rock and seawater alone. The researchers assumed the 2.6-fold speedup from the lab would hold at that scale.

“We’ve found a way that it could work and, maybe more importantly, now we have a model to calculate what it would look like to implement this at scale,” he said.

Four of the authors, Dalvie among them, have filed a patent application on strains of bacteria engineered for siderophore production that keep rust off rock.

The pilot tanks didn’t stay fast. After about three weeks of feeding, dissolving and carbon uptake dropped in every tank.

Slimy films of microbes had built up on the glass and on top of the sand, especially in the tanks that got the engineered bacteria.

The films appeared to keep the water from mixing, and the researchers suspect they blocked the rock. A thin crust had also formed, and most of the dissolving probably happened in the top 0.4 inch (1 centimeter) of sand.

The authors suggest that breaking up that top layer from time to time could keep the rock working, but they haven’t tested it. Dalvie and his colleagues say the next steps are larger experiments, better nutrient mixes and more efficient bacteria.

Dalvie said a real coastline or mine would need “some sort of enclosure or tank” to make enough of the iron-grabbing molecules. Such a tank “would resemble existing wastewater processes,” he said.

The bigger hurdle, he said, is the food for the bacteria. “Green feedstocks like this need to be available at large scales,” Dalvie said, referring to the acetate made from carbon dioxide and electricity.

The study did not analyze the economics of capturing carbon dioxide. “The price of carbon needs to be high enough to justify a new biochemical process,” he stressed.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Biotechnology.

The Evolution Of Airplane Window Design

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Boarding a plane today means stepping into a highly structured, carefully designed world. Regardless of whether we are crammed into the last row of economy or stretching out in a first class suite, our human eyes always instinctively gravitate toward the exact same feature: the window. That small, oval piece of multi-layered acrylic and glass is our only physical connection to the absolute miracle of hurtling through the stratosphere at six hundred miles per hour.

As an industrial design engineer who spends an unhealthy amount of time thinking about cabin interiors, I can tell you that the window is the most psychologically vital component of the passenger experience. In this article, we will examine the fascinating evolution of the airplane window, why it looks the way it does, and why the aviation industry's obsession with replacing it with 'digital windows' is almost guaranteed to be an absolute disaster for how we feel when we fly.

To understand why the future of airplane windows is so concerning, you have to look at how much their engineering has evolved to keep us safe in the past. During the early days of pressurized jet travel in the 1950s, aircraft manufacturers were learning hard lessons about aerospace physics. Early commercial jets featured large, square windows that looked beautiful but structurally compromised the fuselage, leading to catastrophic failures.

Today, the industry is a highly competitive, data-driven machine built on optimizing weight, fuel burn, and aerodynamic efficiency. Every actual hole cut into the side of an aircraft requires heavy structural reinforcement, which adds weight and burns more jet fuel. While we might love the view, airlines and manufacturers look at windows as heavy, expensive liabilities.

This tension between structural efficiency and human psychology is pushing us toward a future of screen-based, digital windows. This is a modern approach that, while brilliant on a spreadsheet, entirely misses what human beings actually need to survive twelve hours in a metal tube. The shape of the modern airplane window is not a stylistic choice made by an interior decorator: it is an engineering mandate born from tragedy.

When the de Havilland Comet, the world's first commercial jetliner, took to the skies in the early 1950s, it featured large, square windows that provided passengers with glorious, panoramic views. However, engineers soon discovered a terrifying flaw in this design. When an aircraft cabin is repeatedly pressurized and depressurized during flight cycles, the stress of that expansion concentrates heavily on the sharp 90-degree corners of square windows.

This phenomenon, known as metal fatigue, led to microscopic cracks that eventually caused the aircraft to break apart mid-flight. The immediate solution was to eliminate the corners entirely, leading to the ubiquitous round and oval windows we see on every commercial jet today. By using a rounded shape, the stress of the cabin pressure flows smoothly around the window frame rather than building up in a single spot. It is a brilliant, life-saving piece of aerospace engineering.

However, creating these reinforced oval holes in a cylindrical aluminium or composite fuselage is incredibly complex. The window assemblies are heavy, requiring thick, multi-pane acrylic layers to withstand freezing temperatures, immense pressure differentials, and the occasional bird strike, all while being perfectly sealed.

This brings us to a massive, silent frustration for the modern passenger: the dreaded 'windowless' window seat. Because cutting holes in the airframe is so dictated by the structural ribs of the Original Equipment Manufacturer (like Boeing, Airbus or Embraer), the windows are placed at fixed, mathematically rigid intervals. But airlines want to squeeze as many seats into the plane as possible.

Using the modern seat-track system on the floor, airlines tend to compress the seat pitch with zero regard for where the actual windows are. This is why you often pay extra for a window seat, only to spend a six-hour flight staring at a blank, curved plastic wall where the seat rows have completely fallen out of sync with the aircraft's architecture.

In the last decade, we witnessed the first major disruption to the airplane window in half a century: the removal of the physical plastic window shade. When Boeing introduced the 787 Dreamliner in 2009, they debuted massive, electronically dimmable windows. Airbus, a while later in 2022, followed suit on the A350.

Instead of pulling down a piece of plastic to block out the sun, passengers press a button, and an electrical current passes through a special electrochromic gel sandwiched between the windowpanes, slowly turning the glass from fully transparent to a deep, dark blue. From an industrial design standpoint, this is a remarkable piece of technology.

It eliminates the mechanical failure of plastic shades snapping off their tracks or breaking, reduces cabin weight, and allows passengers to look outside without letting blinding, harsh sunlight flood the cabin and ruin the contrast of everyone's IFE screens. This feels like magic the first time you use it.

However, it also introduced a subtle, highly controversial shift in cabin dynamics: it transferred environmental control from the passenger to the cabin crew. In my opinion, this is not bad at all, since it lets airlines control the circadian rhythm of the majority of their guests and takes away the possibility of a single passenger disturbing the rest and sleep of a whole cabin of passengers just because they want to see the view.

This shift, however, profoundly affects the passenger experience. With electrochromic windows, the flight crew has a master override switch in the galley. On a daytime flight across the Atlantic, the crew can lock every window in the cabin to the darkest setting to force a sleeping environment, effectively trapping passengers in artificial twilight.

While this is great for minimizing jet lag and keeping the cabin quiet, it is deeply frustrating for the aviation geek who wants to see Greenland from 38,000 feet. Giving the passenger a personal window but electronically denying them the ability to look out of it perfectly illustrates the modern airline's desire to manage passenger behavior through cabin design.

If dimmable glass was a big evolution, the next big idea circulating in aerospace design circles is a total revolution: the complete elimination of physical passenger windows, replaced entirely by high-definition 'digital windows' (the fancy name for a simple screen) that show a live video feed from external cameras. We have already seen the first iterations of this in the industry.

Indeed, Emirates famously installed 'virtual windows' in the middle-aisle first class Game-changer Suites on its Boeing 777s. These are highly advanced, ultra-high-definition screens shaped exactly like airplane windows. They display a real-time feed from fiber-optic cameras mounted on the exterior of the aircraft, and are probably the only justifiable application (and a genius one indeed), to have digital window screens on an aircraft.

To an airline accountant or an aerospace engineer focused on sustainability and cost savings, a windowless fuselage is like the holy grail. If you remove all the windows, you can build a perfectly smooth, uninterrupted metallic or carbon-fiber tube. The aircraft becomes significantly lighter, massively more aerodynamic, and burns drastically less fuel. Furthermore, supersonic jet concepts, which are highly sensitive to drag and structural integrity, frequently feature windowless cabins as a baseline requirement.

Airlines would save millions on fuel and give the passenger a 4K screen that looks just as good as the real thing. However, for the passenger experience and well-being, that just doesn't cut it, as a screen is fundamentally not a window. When you look out of a real airplane window, your eyes focus on infinity. As you move your head, your perspective of the clouds and the ground shifts, a geometric phenomenon known as parallax.

A digital window, no matter how high the resolution, is a flat, two-dimensional light-emitting surface placed two feet away from your face. The camera on the outside of the plane captures one single perspective, and no matter how you move your head in your seat, the image on the screen remains static. Your brain instantly recognizes this physical disconnect, shattering the illusion of transparency.

From booking to upgrades to dining, Emirates' first class cabin defines the carrier's ethos of "Fly Better."

The most severe consequence of implementing digital windows across an entire cabin is the immediate psychological toll it will take on the passengers. Humans are biologically hardwired to seek natural light and spatial awareness, especially when placed in confined, moving, and vibrating environments like the interior of a car, train, or aircraft. The physical window is the ultimate antidote to claustrophobia.

It proves to our panicked subconscious that there is a vast, open world just on the other side of that glass oval. Replacing that organic connection with a glowing LCD screen turns the aircraft from a flying ship into a flying submarine. Beyond claustrophobia, digital windows will wreak havoc on our circadian rhythms.

Natural sunlight contains a complex, full-spectrum biological signal that tells our brains what time of day it is, helping us adjust to new time zones. A digital screen, conversely, is just a matrix of red, green, and blue pixels heavily biased toward the blue light spectrum. Staring at a high-definition monitor disguised as a window for a 14-hour flight will increase eye strain, induce fatigue, and confuse our internal clocks.

We are already surrounded by screens, such as the in-flight entertainment screens, our phones, and our tablets, so turning the actual architecture of the cabin into yet another screen will become a sensory overload that steps away from the ultimate passenger experience. Then there is the very real issue of motion sickness. The human vestibular system (the inner ear that controls balance) is highly sensitive to mismatched inputs. When an aircraft banks, turns, or hits turbulence, your inner ear feels the motion.

If you are looking out a real window, your eyes see the horizon tilt, and your brain reconciles the two inputs perfectly. With a digital window, any microscopic latency, also known as digital delay, in the camera feed, or any distortion in the screen's refresh rate, means your eyes and your inner ear will fall out of sync. This sensory mismatch is the exact recipe for aggressive, cabin-wide motion sickness, headache, and nausea, which airlines want to avoid at all costs when transporting hundreds of paying passengers.

As an engineer, one of the first questions I always ask myself when evaluating a new cabin product is what happens when it breaks? Because everything on an airplane breaks, eventually. Liquid gets spilled, heavy bags are dropped, and complex electrical systems degrade over time due to the extreme vibrations of flight.

A traditional acrylic window is incredibly robust: even if the inner protective plastic gets scratched by a passenger's ring, the window still functions perfectly. It still lets light in, and you can still see outside. A digital window, however, introduces a massive, unnecessary layer of technological fragility to the core architecture of the cabin.

What happens when the fiber-optic camera on the outside of the fuselage is obscured by ice, insect impacts, or heavy rain? The passenger is left staring at a blurry, pixelated mess. What happens when the screen panel inside the cabin suffers a backlight failure or a dead pixel matrix?

Instead of a view of the Alps, the passenger who paid extra for a window seat is forced to sit next to a glowing blue screen, or worse, a completely black, dead monitor for an ultra-long-haul flight. This creates an absolute nightmare for in-flight service recovery. If a passenger's in-flight entertainment screen breaks today, a good flight attendant can apologize, offer them an iPad, or give them some frequent flyer miles to make up for it.

However, if the actual window breaks, and the passenger is plunged into claustrophobic darkness while their window-mates have working screens, there is no iPad that can fix that anxiety. The maintenance burden on the airlines not only would be astronomical, but would also require specialized technicians to ground the aircraft simply to fix or replace a glitchy screen on the sidewall. This would entirely defeat the operational cost savings that the windowless fuselage was supposed to provide in the first place.

When we take a step back and look at the trajectory of cabin design, it becomes clear that the push for digital windows is a classic case of engineering for the spreadsheet rather than engineering for the human. Yes, a windowless fuselage is lighter. Yes, it burns less fuel. However, an airplane is not a cargo drone: it is a vehicle designed to transport living, breathing, anxious, and sometimes claustrophobic human beings across the globe.

The physical airplane window is the last remaining organic connection we have to the outside world when we fly. It is the only thing that proves we are suspended in the heavens, participating in the greatest technological achievement of the 20th century, rather than just sitting in a loud and vibrating waiting room.

The commercial aviation industry, and especially widebody aircraft manufacturers such as Airbus and Boeing, must remember that passenger comfort is not just about the density of the seat cushion, the width of the armrest, or having some fancy mood lighting. Rather, it is deeply rooted in psychology and spatial awareness.

Stripping away the natural light, the true horizon, and the infinite focal depth of a real window, only to replace it with a simulated, two-dimensional broadcast, is a profound downgrade to the passenger experience, as it trades our mental well-being for a marginal increase in aerodynamic efficiency and profit. As we look toward the next generation of aircraft design, we have to ask ourselves where we draw the line between structural optimization and human dignity.

Furthermore, we should place a red line for decision-makers in the industry to ensure they pursue true innovations that really elevate the passenger experience, rather than degrade it even further. Wood, whose firm manages various funds focused on tech and innovation, told CNBC there was a "wall of worry" pervading financial markets right now. But her decades of experience as an investor give her a somewhat unconventional view of the state of play.

"I experienced the tech and telecom bubble, and you know there really wasn't much worry about it," she said. "It was the 80s and the 90s, and it was a bull market, and people were just assuming this would go to the moon, that is not how this [the AI trade] has evolved."

She explained that "from the beginning," many investors have been concerned about how much firms are spending on AI development, how rapidly the spending is taking place, and how the hyperscalers have moved from extreme levels of positive free cash flow to negative free cash flow.

"So it's been unsettling the markets for quite some time," she said. "I always feel better when the market is worried, because that tends to suggest a more durable bull market, and I don't think we've derailed this bull market."

In 2025, her flagship Ark Innovation ETF gained more than 35%, far outperforming the S&P 500's 16.4% rise and the Nasdaq Composite's 20.5% rally.

Asked on Friday whether markets were anywhere near the end of the current bull run, Wood replied: "I don't think so."

"I think we're in the middle of a technology revolution," she said, pointing to the development of technologies like robotaxis and AI agents.

One thing that did risk becoming a "showstopper," Wood noted, was the rising cost of capital.

Interest rates and bond yields have risen sharply this year as the U.S.-Iran war pushes inflation higher across the globe. Yields on corporate bonds have been rising too, with creditors demanding greater compensation for lending to companies.

"This correction is partly caused by what's going on in the bond markets," Wood said on Friday.

"I had been saying for quite some time that the wall of worry has been built by senior leaders in the financial world who were young during the bubble," she said.

"It's the elder statesmen and women of the industry who are keeping this wall of worry in place, and that's not bad. Now you can add the bond investor to that. Bond investors in the old days weren't necessary for technology. It was software. It was low fixed cost. It was viral apps, very high margins. [AI] is high fixed cost, so bond investors are experiencing or taking a journey into the technology world, really that they haven't very much before."

But she noted that AI hyperscalers were somewhat insulated by opportunities for growth.

"If a company thinks that taking on debt is going to accelerate its momentum, meaning its growth rate, it hardly matters what happens to interest rates for those companies," Wood explained.

Wood said her advice to investors in the current volatile environment was to identify their high-conviction positions and double down on them as the market shifts.

"When you see a rally in a volatile environment, take some profits. When you see a sell-off, you'll have the psychological wherewithal if you have taken profits to move back in," she said.

"That's what we do. So what we do during periods like this is concentrate towards our highest conviction names. So that's what we've been doing in the last week or so. Our SpaceX position has gone up. If anything, the controversy around data centers on Earth is giving data centers in space a bigger opportunity."

In 2027, Wood said she expects the equity rally to continue.

"This is two steps forward, one step back," she said of the post-election turbulence. "The revolution will become clearer and clearer next year, and I think the market will do well."

Italian architect Matteo Thun looked to the original materials and colours of Milan’s iconic Torre Velasca when creating its new interiors, which feature pink hues inspired by the tower’s dusky facade.

Thun has designed 72 flats in the upper floors of the tower, ranging from suites to duplex penthouses. He approached the interior design of the skyscraper, built in the 1950s by the architecture studio BBPR, with a focus on reinterpretation.

“We allowed the masters and the building itself to speak, imagining a way of living within it that would carry the building a little further into the future, adding a new chapter to its history,” Thun told Dezeen.

“We did not seek contrast: our design was informed directly by the materials and colours of Torre Velasca, and we chose not to intervene where we felt the architecture had already achieved a sense of completeness.”

His studio preserved original details, including doors, window frames, loggias and tiles in the bathrooms and kitchens. These were then juxtaposed with modern furniture and evergreen design classics.

“The interiors bring together new furniture designed by the studio and design references spanning the 1950s to the 1980s, including iconic pieces developed in new colourways in collaboration with historic Milanese companies,” Thun said.

“With a deep appreciation for craftsmanship and a strong attention to detail, these designs share an ability to evolve without losing their identity.”

The colour palette, too, was inspired by the original hues of the Torre Velasca.

In the living rooms, walls are covered in custom-made velvet wall coverings by textile company Dedar in a pale pink colour that resembles the pink plaster on Torre Velasca’s facade.

“The building itself was our starting point,” Thun said.

“The pink plaster, developed and patented specifically for the tower, together with the blue and green floor tiles found both inside and in the loggias, formed the basis of our colour palette,” he continued.

“We developed different gradations of these tones, opting for darker shades of green and blue to define the entrance areas and bedrooms. “

“Working with a building as distinctive as Torre Velasca challenged us to think and work first and foremost as architects,” Thun said.

“The challenge was not to reinvent the building, but to find ways to make its original character feel relevant today, while remaining true to BBPR’s vision,” he continued.

“In this sense, the project became a process of discovery, with the building itself serving as our greatest teacher. It was, in many ways, a lesson in architecture for us.”
