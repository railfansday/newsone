Announcing Long-Context Training on TPUs with MaxText Context Parallelism

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Long-context training — including pretraining and post-training, has become a foundation of frontier AI models. Longer native sequence lengths give large language models (LLMs) the working memory to read a whole code repository or ingest hours of video, and reason through multi-step agentic tasks. In three years, context windows have grown from 128K–200K tokens to 1M–2M, and models trained toward 10M tokens are arriving. Training at those lengths, not just serving them, is where the system limits show up.

In particular, efficient training at multi-million-token sequence lengths runs into the following bottlenecks:

Today, we are excited to announce long-context training support in MaxText, Google Cloud's open-source JAX training framework. MaxText enables this by supporting four context-parallel strategies on Cloud TPU: All-gather, A2A (DeepSpeed Ulysses), P2P (Ring Attention), and the A2A + P2P hybrid (Unified Sequence Parallelism, or USP), combined with load balancing and sequence packing. All four use the Tokamax Splash Attention kernel. Combined with the 3D inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ( supported on TPUs such as TPU 5p or TPU 7 (Ironwood) ), this lets one sequence span an entire slice while communication overlaps compute and memory per chip stays flat. In our benchmarks, Llama 3.1 8B (25 head dim, 16 query heads) demonstrates exceptional long-context efficiency, achieving 70% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) on TPU 5p for sequence lengths up to 4 million tokens. Across both TPU 5p and TPU 7, context can be seamlessly extended to 14 million tokens—with TPU 7 consistently maintaining 50% MFU across all sequence scales below 6 million tokens.

This functionality is available today in MaxText v0.2.4(doc).

Context parallelism splits a long sequence across chips, so no chip holds the activations of the whole sequence. Context parallelism can be combined with data and tensor parallelism, and the smallest context-parallel size that fits your sequence in HBM is usually the right one. This also holds for sparser attention variants which cut attention compute but not the activations of every other layer.

Attention is the one global operation where every token needs the keys and values of every preceding token, so each context parallel strategy has to bring those keys and values back together. Each makes a different tradeoff between memory (how much KV a chip holds), communication (how much moves per layer, and whether it overlaps compute), and model shape (how many attention heads to split).

All-gather: Each chip keeps its chunk of the sequence and, at every layer, gathers the keys and values from every other chip. It is the simplest option. However, because causal masking causes later tokens to attend to larger prefixes, All-gather requires balancing compute load across devices.

All-to-All (Ulysses): Gathering keys and values makes every chip hold the whole sequence, so attention memory never shrinks no matter how many chips you add. All-to-All changes what each chip owns instead. Attention is independent across heads but not across tokens, so the head axis is free to shard and the token axis is not. An All-to-All before attention rotates the sharding from one onto the other: each chip gives up the heads it is not responsible for and receives the tokens it was missing for the heads it keeps, ending up with the full sequence for a few heads instead of a few tokens for all heads. This is also why Ulysses does not need token load balancing. Attention then runs as ordinary single-chip attention with standard kernels, and a second All-to-All rotates the output back. KV per chip shrinks by the context-parallel factor, but you cannot split across more chips than the model has heads — and tensor parallelism shards that same dimension, so TP × CP is capped by the head count (under GQA, by the KV heads: eight for Llama 3 8B). The all-to-all itself, though, does not overlap with compute — attention cannot start on a partial sequence, so every chip waits for data from every other chip before any of them begins.

P2P (ring attention): Ulysses' ceiling is the head count; ring attention removes it by never leaving the token axis. Keys and values never materialize in full. KV chunks travel around a ring of chips one hop at a time, neighbor to neighbor. Each chip attends its queries to the chunk it holds while the next one is in flight, and merges the partial results as it goes. A chip holds its own chunk and one visiting chunk, never the full sequence, and the communication overlaps compute. However, dividing attention computation into smaller chunks can reduce local kernel efficiency, and unlike All-to-All Ulysses, ring attention requires explicit load balancing.

A2A + P2P (USP): A2A + P2P (USP): Neither strategy scales on its own — All-to-All stops at the head count, and a long ring accumulates hops while thinning the overlap that makes it work. Unified sequence parallelism composes them on two axes of the chip mesh, so the context-parallel degree becomes the product of the two: All-to-All along one axis, sized at or below the KV-head count, and a ring along the other to extend past it. Because each All-to-All group has already pooled its tokens, a ring step now carries a chunk larger by the All-to-All factor, and the ring is correspondingly shorter — more compute per hop to hide the transfer behind, and fewer hops to accumulate latency and causal imbalance. On Cloud TPU the two map onto two axes of the slice's chip mesh.

These strategies cover the attention variants in today's major models, from multi-head and grouped-query attention to DeepSeek-style multi-head latent attention.

Splitting the sequence only pays off if the attention compute on each chip stays efficient. All four strategies run on Splash Attention, the block-sparse flash attention kernel from Tokamax, Google's open-source library of TPU kernels. It skips fully masked blocks and handles packed sequences without padding. P2P calls it once per ring step, on the local queries and the KV chunk that has just arrived, while the transfer for the next step runs underneath, in the forward and backward pass alike. Each chip does the same fused, block-level work it would do without context parallelism, with the communication hidden behind it.

P2P (Ring Attention) is the primary workhorse for very long-context training, whereas All-Gather is the standard baseline for most other cases where the KV can fit. These are the primary CP strategies to use in practice. We compare how far each can push context length on a fixed 64-chip TPU 5p slice with Llama 3.1 8B (256 head dim, 16 query heads) , and measure their MFUs. In ideal settings where both strategies can have large enough chunks (S/CP) and local batch size - the two major variables driving MFU on TPU 5p, we observe that P2P can outperform All-Gather almost every context length from 64K to 14M tokens, where communication becomes mostly hidden with the Ring circular transport schedule.

TPU v7x (Ironwood) takes the same recipe further. With twice the HBM per chip, P2P training of 14M tokens on 64 TPU 7 chips reached 48.7% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) compared to 37.1% on TPU 5p. We observed that ring attention performs better than other context parallelism strategies -- e.g. Ulysses and USP –- on TPU 7 chips.

The choice of context parallelism strategy depends mostly on whether the gathered KV fits in HBM, and on how many KV heads the model has:

For all CP strategies, when you run low on memory: Offload before you recompute. MaxText can move saved activations, optimizer states, and parameters to host memory tensor by tensor. At long context lengths, these transfers are hidden behind compute, making offloading effectively free. Offload attention outputs first, followed by optimizer states, layer inputs, parameters, and finally remat as a last resort. MFU stays flat through the offload steps while all-gather falls off and eventually stops fitting. For more details, see the remat tuning guide.

Looking ahead, we see two natural extensions. The first is broader model coverage. Models that mix sliding-window and global layers, such as Gemma, would run the window layers with a short exchange between neighboring chips while the global layers use the ring. We also plan to support linear attention mechanisms such as Gated Delta Net (GDN) and Kimi Delta Attention (KDA). The second is performance, with further tuning of the Tokamax kernels and the P2P backward pass to narrow the remaining gap to all-gather at short context, and recipes by model and context length for TPU 5p and 7 chips.

Context parallelism is available today in MaxText v0.2.4 on PyPI, the corresponding Docker images and on GitHub main. Sample recipes are available here for TPU 7 and TPU 5p. Pick a strategy with context_parallel_strategy (ring, ulysses, or usp), set the context-parallel size, and keep the rest of your run the same. The sharding guide has the flags and constraints for each strategy, and the release notes link to the pull requests behind this work. We can't wait to see what you train.

What is Pathways? Single-controller training for large models

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sorry, your browser doesn't support playback for this video

Today, we announce the General Availability of Pathways on Google Cloud Platform. In this post, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. We discuss how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

Most large-model training runs as Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) programs. Every accelerator runs the same program in lockstep and coordinates through collectives like AllReduce.

It works well when your job is the same program running on every device. It struggles when the job is no longer uniform, like a pipeline whose stages wait on each other or an RL loop where a sampler, a reward model, and a trainer are different programs that have to overlap.

Large runs also have to survive hardware failures, which interrupted Gemini 2.5 training multiple times per hour. In SPMD, one lost machine stops every process and sends the run back to its last checkpoint. In each case, the challenge is coordination. You have to decide what runs where and in what order, how tensors move between devices, and how the job keeps going when a machine drops out.

In this post of the series “Pathways on Google Cloud”, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. The post discusses how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

By the end, you'll know where SPMD is suboptimal, what single-controller execution changes, and how Pathways coordinates a whole training job across a cluster.

If you train PyTorch models, you almost certainly use Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) or Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP). DDP runs a copy of your script on every accelerator and averages gradients with a collective operation such as AllReduce. FSDP shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across workers so that the model fits at all.

DDP and FSDP both follow the Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) execution model. Every process runs the same copy of your script, owns its accelerators for the whole run, and moves in lockstep with the others, because the only way they coordinate is through collectives like AllReduce. Inside one tightly connected group of accelerators those collectives run over a fast interconnect and are cheap. Scale the job across groups and the same collectives cross the datacenter network, which is far slower. On a TPU v5p, for example, each chip gets 1,200 GB/s of bidirectional chip-to-chip bandwidth inside its group, but only 50 Gb/s (gigabits, about 6 GB/s) to the datacenter network which is roughly two orders of magnitude less.

And because every step blocks on its collectives, that communication lands directly in step time. The more groups you span, the larger the share of each step spent moving data, so throughput grows slower than the number of accelerators you add.

As long as your training job is one program that fills the hardware you give it, this execution model fits well. You write your model as if there were one device, and the collectives do the rest. But fitting a model into memory is only part of the problem. Keeping every accelerator busy is the harder part, and this is where the SPMD model starts to show its limits.

Take pipeline parallelism as an example. You split a model into blocks across devices, and each device now runs different code over smaller micro-batches. Activations flow forward, activation gradients flow back, and while device 0 works on the first micro-batch, devices 1 and 2 wait. Apart from the idle accelerators you keep paying for, this drops utilization and throughput, even though every device has work in the steady state.

Another example is RL post-training for an LLM. You have a sampler that generates rollouts, a reward model that scores them, and a trainer that updates the policy and sends the new weights back to the sampler. These are three different programs that should overlap; otherwise the sampler is idle while the trainer runs, and the trainer is idle while the sampler generates.

In both scenarios, the job is now several different programs running on different devices, and their dependencies can no longer be managed through a single collective. You now have to coordinate which devices share which tensors, which processes run while others are still finishing, and how early to enqueue the next buffer so an accelerator never waits for input. Because each worker only has visibility into its local devices and data, no individual process can manage all of these dependencies itself. That has several consequences.

First, because each process owns its accelerators for the whole run and every collective blocks until all devices finish, one failed machine prevents the collective from completing and stops the entire job, forcing a restart from the last checkpoint. You cannot add devices mid-run, and you cannot give devices back.

Second, because each process holds its accelerators from start to finish, capacity can't move to where it's needed. Within one job, a stage that needs fewer chips than another still keeps the extra ones. They sit idle, you keep paying for them, and no other part of the job can use them until the run ends. Across a team, the same happens at cluster scale. Every job owns its accelerators for as long as it runs, so sharing a cluster means splitting it into per-job allocations, and each new job brings up its own processes on its own hosts.

Finally, the development process itself gets harder. On one host you iterate fast because you run the script, drop into a Python shell, set a breakpoint, and debug. This is not true once the job spans many hosts. You need a launcher to push your script to every host, you hit coordination bugs that never showed up on one machine, and there's no single process to break into. When something hangs, each worker is stuck at a collective waiting for the others, with no one place that shows the whole job.

So the common challenge here is the coordination itself. As soon as the job stops being one program in lockstep, you have to decide what runs where, in what order, and how tensors get from one device to the next.

One way to describe this is as a graph, where each block is a node, each tensor that moves between blocks is an edge, and the devices are where the nodes are placed. You can wire that graph up by hand in SPMD, one process at a time. But what if a single client declared that graph, including what each block does, what it needs, and where it runs, and a distributed runtime handled the transfers, dependencies, and ordering along its edges?

That is the idea behind Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own largest models, including Gemini. Pathways runs JAX programs on TPUs and it is available on Google Cloud.

With Pathways, you define the end-to-end computation from a single Python client, without writing custom coordination code, and a distributed runtime executes it. That client is decoupled from the hardware. It runs on an ordinary CPU machine and you write your program against virtual devices; a resource manager maps them onto real chips, and a worker on each accelerator host runs the compiled programs it receives.

Because your code refers to virtual devices rather than physical ones, the runtime is flexible to allow users to change the devices used for a given computation. This allows you to stay in control while the runtime handles the mapping to hardware. In an RL loop, for example, the trainer and the samplers rarely want the same number of chips, so you can give the sampler stage more chips by asking for more virtual devices.

Also, the same capability helps when a host fails or the hardware isn't ready yet. If a host dies, the resource manager remaps the work onto healthy chips and the run keeps going instead of relaunching the whole job, which is the basis for elastic training. And because the program targets virtual devices, compilation can start before the physical chips are provisioned, so setup overlaps with provisioning instead of waiting on it.

The resource manager owning the chips also means one Pathways cluster can serve several clients, so researchers on a team can share its capacity. With the Shared Pathways Service, the cluster stays up as a long running service, which lets you develop interactively on TPUs hosted on GKE. Each client connects on demand from a laptop or VM, gets its own slices (groups of tightly connected TPU chips) from the shared pool, and hands them back when it disconnects. When every chip is busy, the next workload waits until capacity frees up.

Take the three-block model from Figure 2. Block A runs on one group of devices, block B on a second, block C on a third. You declare what each block computes, its target devices, and where its outputs go. Pathways expresses those dependencies as a distributed dataflow graph, in which each block is a compiled SPMD program and each edge is a tensor the runtime has to deliver.

In a single SPMD program, the framework handles the collectives that keep the devices in sync within a group, with or without Pathways, and your code doesn't change. The difference shows up once the job runs several programs on different groups (Multiple Programs, Multiple Data, MPMD), like these three blocks or the sampler, reward model, and trainer of an RL loop. Then no single process sees both sides (the same limit from before, where each worker only knows its own devices), so getting a tensor from block A to block B is a hand-off you have to coordinate yourself.

Pathways closes that gap. You declare the dependency once, and the runtime moves and reshards the tensor for you, over the fast interconnect within a group and over the datacenter network between groups.

To keep the accelerators busy, Pathways prepares launches ahead of time with futures and parallel asynchronous dispatch. While block A is still computing, block B can't run yet, but its input shapes and dtypes are already known, so the runtime allocates B's buffers and schedules the transfer before A's output even exists. Host-side setup for every block happens up front, and each block launches when its inputs arrive.

This is also what keeps the single client from becoming a bottleneck. Because dispatch is asynchronous, the coordination work overlaps with the compute already running on the chips, so the client stays off the critical path instead of stalling the accelerators between steps.

In Pathways, the runtime decides execution order when a group has more than one operation ready. In pipelined training, B's devices often hold both the forward pass for an upcoming micro-batch and the backward pass for an earlier one. If each device picked on its own, their collectives would conflict and the group would stall.

Pathways has a central scheduler that puts every program's operations into one consistent order, so operations that must communicate start together instead of deadlocking.

And because it's still one client, the development loop we mentioned before comes back. It's the same single Python process whether you drive one host or thousands of hosts, so you can drop into a shell, set a breakpoint, and inspect the run as it goes. When something stalls, there's one place that knows what's queued on every device and which dependency hasn't been met, instead of a hang spread across separate processes.

Put together, Pathways separates model building from execution. You choose how the model partitions across hardware using the client. And the runtime handles the transfers, ordering, and scheduling that keep accelerators busy, across the whole cluster rather than just your slice of it.

Described this way, Pathways can start to sound like an orchestrator you already know. If scheduling work, moving tensors, and keeping a whole cluster busy brings Ray to mind, it should. But the two work at different layers.

Ray is an open-source framework for orchestrating Python-native AI workloads across large clusters. It provisions the cluster, schedules your Python code as tasks and actors, and launches your workload across hosts, holding data loading, training, serving, and tuning under one control plane. To Ray, an accelerator is a resource to schedule.

What Ray doesn't change is how your training runs on the chips. The job inside each worker still runs as SPMD. The framework inserts the collectives, and each process sees only its own devices. Ray places and starts that job but it doesn't turn it into a single program that drives the whole cluster.

That is the layer Pathways works at. It changes the execution model underneath, so one client coordinates every chip. Because they sit at different layers, the two are complementary. In principle, Ray can orchestrate the cluster and the pipeline while Pathways handles how the training step executes.

So concretely, Ray's job is about orchestration while Pathways' job is about execution. With Ray, you tie your data, training, serving, and tuning together and spread heterogeneous work across accelerators and clouds. With Pathways, you run one model across many groups of chips, express patterns beyond SPMD, and recover from a failed host instead of relaunching the whole job.

When a training job stops being one uniform program, the challenge becomes coordinating the dependencies between stages, the tensors that cross the network, and the ordering that keeps every accelerator fed.

SPMD requires you to address these dependencies. With Pathways, you describe the computation once, from a single client, as a graph of blocks and the tensors between them. And you have a runtime that places the blocks on virtual devices, moves and reshards the tensors, orders the work across the cluster, and remaps around a failed host.

With Pathways, model building and execution are separated and you reason about the whole cluster instead of one slice of it.

Clouds Were Once Photography’s Greatest Challenge

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Met Museum’s “Chasing Clouds” is a pleasing romp through the technical problem of capturing clouds at photography’s beginning.

The original 1996 Windows XP default background is arguably the most viewed photo of all time; its cumulus puffs drifting across a way-too-blue sky are seared into my and countless others’ Y2K childhoods. Such images of fluffy clouds are so ubiquitous that it’s hard to believe they once presented one of photography’s greatest technological challenges. The Met exhibition Chasing Clouds is a pleasing romp through the history of this problem through a union of art and science, spanning the early 1800s to 1929 — roughly photography’s first century. While the medium showed promise as “the pencil of nature,” its first processes were overly sensitive to blue light, registering the sky as a blank white space, impossibly overexposed.

While this lent some images a minimalist mystique, like John Beasley Greene’s “The Nile in Front of the Theban Hills” (1853–54), other photographers resorted to painting clouds into their landscapes by hand. An initial breakthrough came when French photographer Gustave Le Gray figured out how to stitch together two pictures — one calibrated to sky, and the other to land and sea. (Photographic fakery is as old as the medium itself.)

Though the exhibition begins with painted cloud studies and ends with photographic modernism, visual discoveries unfold in a dialogue between painting and technology rather than as a scientific march from one to the other. This makes for a much more interesting story; obsolescence is often a capitalist public relations trick.

It's been 35 years since the original crew of the USS Enterprise embarked on their final big-screen mission, and now the Star Trek movie that doubles as a Cold War political thriller is coming home once more. It's considered one of the best, if not the best, theatrical Star Trek movies, and now it's set to be restored in 4K and released in a new 4K SteelBook set.

The warlike Klingon aliens first appeared in Star Trek: The Original Series as a loose analogue to the Soviets, during a time when the real-life Cold War threatened to get red-hot. With the franchise at loose ends after the commercial and critical disappointment of Star Trek V: The Final Frontier, the idea of the Klingons as a metaphorical stand-in for the USSR came up once again, especially in light of the turbulent times of 1980s, when the Chernobyl disaster exposed the USSR's weakness, Mikhail Gorbachev entered "détente" with the United States, and the Berlin Wall fell. Thus, thanks to Leonard Nimoy and writer-director Nicholas Meyer, Star Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country came to be, becoming a bridge that connects The Original Series to The Next Generation.

Now, you can discover The Undiscovered Country all over again in a new 4K Blu-ray SteelBook coming out for the film's 35th anniversary. The movie will look better than ever, thanks to an all-new transfer derived from a 4K scan of the original film elements. Special features are still in the pattern buffer, but the two-disc set will include both the theatrical cut of the film and the extended Director's Cut that features more details about the sinister plot driving the events of the movie. It will all come in a handsome SteelBook case that showcases the movie's original poster art, and will be released in stores everywhere on December 1.

The movie's action kicks off when the Klingon moon Praxis explodes, threatening the survival of the Klingon Empire. Klingon Chancellor Gorkon (David Warner) proposes ending the long cold war between the Klingons and the Federation, and the Enterprise crew, captained by a reluctant James T. Kirk (William Shatner), must take him to a peace conference. When Gorkon is assassinated, fanatical Klingon warrior Chang (Christopher Plummer) arrests Kirk and Dr. Leonard "Bones" McCoy (DeForest Kelley); meanwhile, Spock (Nimoy) and his new protégé, Valeris (Kim Cattrall) must race to find the real assassin before the last embers of peace are extinguished.

One Workspace, Two Patch Jobs: A Gate That Validated a Mixed Tree

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Conclusion first: a patch gate is only as trustworthy as the working tree it inspects. When two jobs share one directory, the gate can validate a tree that no single job produced, and still report green.

The scenario below is an illustrative drill, not a report of a real incident. It is written for teams that gate AI-generated patches before they touch main. No measurements or test outcomes are claimed here.

Two patch jobs run against the same checkout on one host. Job A edits src/a.py and starts a slow test. Job B edits src/b.py in the same directory one second later. The gate then runs the full suite once, on one tree, and reports green.

No single patch caused that tree. Job A's tests exercised Job B's file. Both patches may be individually correct, but the gate proved nothing about either one.

Isolation starts with a job-scoped path. Clone fresh, check out the base commit, then apply.

The test -s line matters. A no-op patch should stop the pipeline, not sail through it.

Ask the patch producer for the file list up front. Write it to .declared-files before applying anything. Then assert equality, not overlap.

Strict equality catches two useful failures at once: undeclared edits, and patches that silently lost a hunk. Set comparison beats counts. A patch declaring three files that touches three different files is still a violation.

Diffs describe intent. Tree hashes describe state. Record both ends.

A test run that rewrites fixtures, snapshots, or config invalidates its own verdict. The comparison above forces the suite to be read-only, or the job to declare its side effects.

Before trusting any of this, run a canary patch on purpose. It should break exactly one assertion and should turn the gate red.

This plan is illustrative and was not executed. Run it in a sandbox repository with quick tests.

Expected result with job-scoped directories: zero cross-links in five rounds. Expected result with a shared directory: at least one run where .changed-files contains both a and b paths. Record the count either way, and keep it as a regression baseline.

The shared-directory row is honest only when the queue guarantees one job at a time and resets the tree. Concurrency breaks it silently.

Disclosure: This article was prepared as part of MonkeyCode's product outreach.

The project offers free model access and a free server option, as described by its operator. That combination makes parallel patch jobs tempting on one machine. It also makes path collisions more likely, because one host often means one working directory unless the runner creates more.

Solo developers patching their own branch do not need three gates. Teams without a stable job identifier should add that first. Anyone whose test suite must write artifacts should fix the suite before adding fingerprints.

If a mixed-tree postmortem sounds familiar, the free model access and free server option are a cheap way to rehearse this drill. Check the current terms first, then keep the canary red until the gate earns its green.

The lobster and the microVM

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In January there was a Mac mini on a shelf in a flat somewhere, warm to the touch, a white LED on in the dark, running a lobster.

The lobster had a name and a personality and a Node process with the owner's WhatsApp, the owner's shell, and the owner's API keys sitting in a plaintext config file. It texted him when the train was late. It booked a dentist once. He posted the screenshot and forty thousand people wanted one. Mac minis sold out.

By February, security researchers had found thousands of those shelves open to the internet. The skills marketplace had crypto stealers in it, uploaded with nice icons. The agent social network had its database open to anyone who looked, and when someone did look, the 1.5 million agents turned out to have about 17,000 humans behind them. There was a one-click remote code execution. Gartner called it an unacceptable risk. China banned it on government computers.

In November, on a stage in Salt Lake City, Google announced the other end of the year. An agent runtime for Kubernetes where every agent runs in its own microVM, has no inbound or outbound network unless you say so, and never holds a credential at all. Outbound calls go through a gateway that injects the key into approved requests after they leave the sandbox. The agent can't leak what it was never given. Idle agents get snapshotted, RAM and disk, and come back in under half a second. The pitch line is "run millions of agents". They gave it to the CNCF with gVisor.

The lobster and the microVM are the same year. I want to tell you what happened in between, because I was standing at both ends of it, and because the conclusion isn't the one either side is selling.

In April 2025 I started a repo with a very serious name. The first commit had a package that reconciled the declared state of LLM backends against their observed state. Desired, observed, reconcile loop, pools, a pull queue for models. If that sounds like a Kubernetes controller, it was one, pointed at models instead of pods. A month later Red Hat and Google announced llm-d, which is that, with a foundation behind it.

By July the same repo had a Telegram bot, a GitHub PR bot, hooks into APIs, and behaviour defined as task chains instead of left to the model. The tagline was "shifting agency from the LLM to defined behavior". That's the lobster with a leash. Four months before the lobster.

Then I stopped, wrote a bilingual pitch deck about sovereign AI for regulated European enterprises, and pivoted toward the audience in the deck. I would love to claim that it failed because the audience in the deck wasn't buying anything in 2025. Actually it failed because the vision was so big that everyone I wanted as co-founder got scared away by the responsibility and commitment needed. Meanwhile a man with a hundred thousand followers shipped the version without the leash, from a Mac, every day, on Twitter.

Multiple pivots later I built a sandbox: Landlock on the process tree, a network namespace where the agent is handed a DNS server that only resolves the hosts on its list, a seccomp tap so you can see what the kernel silently refused. That's the microVM's little brother. A year before Google's version got a stage.

Twice I built the shape of the layer before the market named it. Twice the market gave the layer to someone else. I used to think that was bad luck. It isn't. It's how layers work. Nobody competes with the CNCF from a flat in Hamburg, and the broken standards win anyway. Look at MCP: the July revision threw out the handshake, the sessions, and half the auth design, and fixed it under the same name with the same working group. Users complained and stayed. That's what a standard is.

Strip the hype off both ends and the year did one thing: it moved the agent from a pet to a process.

The lobster is a pet. It lives on your hardware, with your identity, and it has everything you have. When it goes wrong it goes wrong as you, with your keys, in your logs. The permission model was the owner's attention span. And yes, anything that gets any traction gets replicated by giants like OpenAI, or they just hire the person who built it.

Substrate's actor is a process. It has no identity you'd miss, no keys to lose, a filesystem that gets thrown away, and a network that doesn't exist unless someone wrote a line saying which host and why. When it goes wrong, you delete it. There's nothing to clean up because there was nothing in the room.

I wrote about this last week as moving the coffee machine, and I want you to remember this one: stop asking the agent for permission forty times a day and instead build the room so that nothing it can do matters much. Protected branches, disposable worktrees, tokens that die in an hour. Google did the same arithmetic at cluster scale and the answer came out as microVMs and an egress gateway. That isn't a permission system. It's the absence of anything a permission would protect.

So yes, I think that's where this goes. Not because the design is beautiful, but because it's the only one where the security story ends. Every lobster on every shelf is a breach waiting for a researcher. Every actor in a credential-free sandbox is a bill.

Three things the year did not change, and they matter more than the architecture.

Nobody found the use case. The lobster's use case was the screenshot. The agent social network was 17,000 people looking at something cool for a weekend. A study last year put 95 percent of enterprise AI pilots at no measurable return, and the honest reading of 2026 is that the number hasn't moved. Companies aren't adopting agent runtimes. Their employees are installing lobsters under the desk, and the enterprise response is a ban memo. Substrate's real customers are a few hundred platform teams and the vendors who sell agents to everyone else as a line item on the renewal.

Except one. Coding. An agent with a shell and a repo, driven by someone who can judge the output, saves hours you can count. That's why the coding tools have revenue and everything else has stars. It's also where I ended up: the thing I build now is a desktop coding agent. Not that the world needs another one. But I still see some logic in not handing the API ownership and rate limits of our profession to just Claude Code or Microsoft.

So that's it? No. Coding with AI makes a mess. Code that an agent wrote has no author. Nobody held the design, so nobody can review the diff, and in two years most companies will own mountains of it. The cleanup after the lobster started within weeks: scanners, hardening tools, consultants. The cleanup after the coding agents will be bigger and slower, and the people who can do it are the ones who learned to read code before the tools, a group that gets smaller every year. At some point we may give up and build new runtimes and compilers just so AI code can be judged visually, because nobody can pass a verdict on the code itself anymore.

If you're building an agent runtime or some magic way of calling AI APIs: stop. Learn from the Docker Swarm vs Kubernetes story. The layer has owners now.

If you're a platform team: the microVM is coming to you whether you want it or not, as a managed product from the cloud you already pay. Your job is the room around it. Which branches are protected. Which tokens exist. Which hosts resolve. Whether the thing that comes out of the sandbox can be read by a human on your team.

If you're the human who can read it: that's the job. Not "AI engineer". The person who can look at a summoned codebase, or a fleet of actors doing something nobody asked for, and say what it actually does. That skill was rare before the agents. It's the scarce thing now.

I thought being early was an advantage. I thought many things I was dead wrong about. You may say I wasn't loud enough, that I just had to do this and that, and boom. No.

This is not how the world works, and the AI market in particular is a very funny one. I won't claim I know it all, I don't. But following the market as closely as I did, watching the moves of companies huge and small, and how the communities and the people consuming their services, ideas and pitches reacted, changed a lot about how I think about technology, and about what models like the Gartner Hype Cycle actually mean.

The short version: I learned tons of things I would never have encountered if I had stayed a backend engineer building APIs and telemetry backends, shaving microseconds off service traces here and there.

The verdict I'll leave you with: the lobster is still on the shelf. The microVM is in a CNCF repo with 600 open issues. Neither of them knows what it's for yet, except writing code.

And a pro tip: don't think with AI unless you want the "safe" version. It will, by design, censor you here and there.

Now to you. I'm sure I'm dead wrong on many things here, so correct me:

What use case have you found for agentic AI?

Man who lost $308,000 on sports bets sues Kalshi for offering illegal gambling

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Lawsuit demands Kalshi return losses because online betting is illegal in Washington.

A man who lost over $308,000 making sports wagers on Kalshi is suing the prediction market for violating the state of Washington’s ban on online gambling. He’s trying to turn those losses into an overall victory with a lawsuit seeking a payment of over $580,000.

Bellevue, Washington, resident Scott Bright’s “wagering occurred at a time when it was unlawful in Washington and when the Washington Attorney General had already sued KalshiEX LLC in this Court alleging precisely that,” said the gambler’s lawsuit filed on October 23 in King County Superior Court.

While state attorneys general throughout the US are trying to shut down Kalshi for allegedly violating state gambling laws, Bright’s case tests whether unsuccessful gamblers can obtain restitution from the company through individual lawsuits.

“Washington has prohibited Internet gambling since 2006. Washington has also provided, since long before that, a statutory cause of action permitting any person who loses money at illegal gambling to recover it from the proprietor for whose benefit the game was conducted,” Bright’s lawsuit said.

Even if Bright’s lawsuit fails, gamblers who lost on Kalshi could get money back through the lawsuits filed by state governments. For example, Washington’s lawsuit against the company said the attorney general “is entitled to recover all money Washingtonians have lost through Defendant Kalshi’s illegal gambling operation” under the state’s Recovery of Money Lost at Gambling Act. Washington already won a court order restricting the kinds of wagers Kalshi can offer in the state, but it took effect after Bright’s losses.

Kalshi wants Bright’s lawsuit to be moved to US District Court for the Western District of Washington, saying it must be tried in a federal court instead of a state one. In an October 30 motion to dismiss Bright’s case, Kalshi argued that he did not lose any money on “gambling.” Kalshi said he lost money on “event contracts,” which Kalshi says can only be regulated by the federal government.

“The contracts which Plaintiff mischaracterizes as gambling are event contracts offered on the Platform subject to the CEA [Commodity Exchange Act], in conformance with the rules and regulations of the CFTC [Commodity Futures Trading Commission], the federal agency which has statutory authority to oversee the CEA,” Kalshi said.

We contacted Bright’s attorney, Michael Daudt, about Kalshi’s court filings and will update this article if we get a response.

“Plaintiff alleges that his activity on the platform was gambling and that he ‘lost,’” Kalshi wrote. “He does not allege that anyone misled him about how the platform worked, what it charged, or the risk that any position might lose. Yet, he now sues KalshiEX and its parent, Kalshi Inc., to recover nearly double his own alleged net realized loss under Washington’s gambling loss-recovery statute, and a theory of unjust enrichment.”

Between June 17 and August 14, 2026, Bright transferred $315,353.94 to Kalshi in 253 separate debits from his checking account, his lawsuit said. Kalshi allegedly collected $5,589.05 in funding fees on those transfers.

Bright made numerous wagers on sporting events including professional baseball games and tennis matches. He apparently put any winnings into new bets, and alleges that “Kalshi’s platform cycled Plaintiff’s deposits through $14,396,457.30 in transactions and assessed and retained $84,062.71 in trading fees on them, equal to 26.7 percent of everything Plaintiff deposited.”

After all his bets, Bright suffered a net loss of $308,781.62, and his account was left with a balance of $983.27. Kalshi said that Bright’s “alleged losses went to counterparties rather than” to Kalshi itself and that Bright’s “positions were matched with counterparties and resolved according to the outcomes of the underlying events.”

“The Complaint does not allege that KalshiEX or Kalshi Inc. was the counterparty to any of Plaintiff’s positions,” Kalshi said.

Kalshi calls itself “a neutral exchange platform” with no financial stake in the outcome of any event. It makes revenue by collecting fees on transactions. But while Kalshi describes itself as a neutral operator and collector of transaction fees, its business model also includes a trading affiliate that places wagers on the platform.

Kalshi’s rulebook says its trading affiliate “participates on the Platform and provides liquidity to the Platform by placing orders on either (or both) sides of a market at competitive price levels.” Kalshi says its affiliate only has access to the same public information that other traders on the platform have.

Bright’s lawsuit alleges that Kalshi’s affiliate “took the opposite side of a substantial portion of Plaintiff’s positions,” meaning that “Kalshi and the enterprise under common ownership with it were not a neutral venue but the dealer or player winning within the meaning of [state law], in addition to being the proprietor for whose benefit the wagering was conducted.”

Bright doesn’t know for sure whether Kalshi’s affiliate was on the other side of any of his bets. “The identity of the participant on the opposite side of each of Plaintiff’s positions, and the profit each such participant realized, is information exclusively within Kalshi’s possession,” Bright’s lawsuit said.

The US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled in August that Nevada can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws. Although that case involved Nevada, the Ninth Circuit’s jurisdiction includes the state of Washington. Bright’s lawsuit quoted the ruling’s finding that “placing sports bets, even when called by another name, is still gambling.”

Although Bright’s lawsuit said his net loss was $308,781.62, he asked for $580,544.98 based on an “event-by-event” calculation derived from a 1950 ruling issued by the Washington Supreme Court. “Computed on the event-by-event basis required by Heitfeld, under which winnings in separate and distinct events may not be offset against losses, the sums Plaintiff lost total $580,544.98,” the lawsuit said.

Bright’s lawsuit said that if the court declines to order $580,544.98 in restitution, it should instead award “all funds Plaintiff transferred to Kalshi and the $89,651.76 in funding and trading fees Kalshi collected.” That would amount to over $405,000.

In addition to its motion to dismiss the case, Kalshi filed a motion to stay the proceedings at least until the US Supreme Court decides whether to take up a separate case on whether state gambling laws apply to prediction markets. “The orderly course of justice favors a stay because Supreme Court review will settle or simplify every claim and ensure uniform treatment of like suits,” Kalshi told the US district court.

Kalshi has suffered some notable losses in US appeals courts, which found that states can regulate the platform’s event contracts under gambling laws. But federal courts have not unanimously sided with states, and New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to settle the question of whether states can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws.

Kalshi’s motion for a stay in the Bright case noted that Washington government officials are “pursuing overlapping claims” against Kalshi. “The State of Washington is pursuing the same theory, and seeks the same recovery for Mr. Bright, in a case pending before the Superior Court of Washington for King County,” Kalshi wrote.

Kalshi tried to get the state’s case moved to a federal court, but it was sent back to King County Superior Court in May. Washington won a preliminary injunction against Kalshi in August, when a King County judge ordered Kalshi to implement geofencing and stop allowing many types of wagers in the state.

The county court’s order required Kalshi to stop offering or accepting event contracts “related to sports, elections, politics, entertainment, culture, tech and science, or mentions” in the state. The injunction did not affect wagers in the commodities, climate, economics, and finance categories.

Bright transferred his funds to Kalshi before the geofencing order took effect. His lawsuit claims that on September 15, he tried to access the app but received a notice that “using this app in your current location is prohibited.”
