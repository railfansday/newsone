Forget ‘You,’ Netflix's Twisted Psychological Thriller With Only 6 Episodes Is Even Better

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

You turns obsession into a long, unnerving game of cat and mouse, while Netflix's Behind Her Eyes takes a sharper route: it starts with a familiar affair, pulls you into a marriage full of secrets, then makes you question what kind of story you’ve been watching. The six-episode British thriller is a compact binge with a swing-for-the-fences finale — one that may delight you, infuriate you, or do both.

The comparison isn’t a knock on Joe Goldberg, as You gives its central monster room to scheme across seasons. Behind Her Eyes, however, unsettles you and leaves before its premise wears thin. If you’re after a psychological thriller that keeps shifting the ground beneath you, this short series makes a strong case for pressing play.

Louise (Simona Brown) is a single mother trying to get her footing after a divorce. One night, she sparks with David (Tom Bateman), a charming stranger. The next morning brings an awkward surprise: David is her new boss, a psychiatrist, and he’s married.

His wife, Adele (Eve Hewson), soon befriends Louise. Now Louise is caught between an affair with David and a growing friendship with Adele, each relationship giving her another glimpse into a marriage that feels wrong in ways she can’t quite name. The show feeds viewers fragments of the couple’s past, including Adele’s time in a psychiatric facility and the troubled friend she made there, Rob (Robert Aramayo).

Louise isn’t simply a bystander in David and Adele’s marriage, as she’s drawn to both of them, and her choices have consequences. The series keeps shifting your sympathies, making it hard to settle on a clear villain or victim. It’s about watching ordinary desires — love, security, escape — turn dangerous.

With only six episodes, the show also makes a tidy binge. Its tension comes from accumulating odd details and unanswered questions, and you can watch it over a weekend and reach an ending that’s likely to start a conversation.

Brown gives Louise a convincing mix of curiosity and apprehension. She makes it easy to see why Louise gets pulled deeper into this mess, even when you’re silently urging her to back away. Bateman plays David with a polished charm that leaves room for doubt, while Hewson gives Adele a cool, watchful presence that can turn unnerving in a glance.

Aramayo brings an inviting spark to the flashbacks as Rob, Adele’s friend from rehab. Those scenes widen the story’s mystery and give the past a personality of its own. The performances help the show cross its sharp tonal turns without winking at the audience.

Behind Her Eyes begins in the world of office crushes, uneasy friendships, and marital secrets; later, it heads somewhere much more fantastical. Whether that pivot feels audacious or absurd depends on your appetite for a story that refuses to stay in its lane.

The finale is the series’ biggest gamble. Some viewers will love that it goes all in on its wildest ideas, while others may feel the show values surprise over making every piece fit. Either way, its ending is hard to forget — and even harder to discuss without spoiling it.

Behind Her Eyes isn’t as sustained a character study as You, and its turns won’t work for everyone. Still, it’s a lean thriller with a terrific central performance from Hewson, a shifting web of loyalties, and the nerve to take its premise somewhere unexpected. If you like your mysteries neat and grounded, look elsewhere. If you want a six-episode spiral that leaves you blinking at the screen, this one earns a spot in your queue.

California voters defeat billionaire tax proposal that divided Democrats

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

California voters rejected Proposition 40, the billionaire tax, which had divided Democrats with opponents saying it would drive the wealthy from the state. Jae C. Hong/AP hide caption

California voters have rejected a proposal to charge billionaires a one-time 5% tax on most of their wealth, according to a race call by The Associated Press.

Proponents said the tax could raise $100 billion for healthcare. Led by a union that represents healthcare workers, the SEIU-UHW, advocates of the tax said the Republican-controlled Congress cut healthcare programs to give tax breaks to the wealthy, so it was time for the wealthy in California to fill the gap. The state has more than 200 billionaires, according to estimates, with some putting the number at a few hundred.

Opponents argued that the proposal, known as Prop 40, would just drive the wealthy out of California, costing the state revenue and harming its economy.

But the measure divided Democrats in a state where they heavily outnumber Republicans.

The state's Democratic Party favored Prop 40, as did Rep. Ro Khanna, D-Calif., and Sen. Bernie Sanders, I-Vt., who went to the state to campaign for it. But Democratic Gov. Gavin Newsom opposed the tax, saying it would be better to have a national tax on the wealthy. The Democratic candidate for governor, Xavier Becerra, also opposed it.

A leading teachers union also opposed the proposal, which would have set aside just a small portion of the resulting revenue for schools. And Google co-founder Sergey Brin led other wealthy Californians in a surge of campaign spending opposing the measure. At least five times as much was spent opposing the measure as favoring it.

Brin also drove a campaign that put two other proposals on the ballot to block the billionaire tax from taking effect, had it been approved by voters.

OpenAI builds kill switch to stop rogue agents within 35 seconds

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Control Room cuts off model output for internal research jobs when people or automated monitors flag safety or security concerns.

OpenAI has built a system that can cut off an AI research experiment’s access to model output, following several disclosures of its models acting beyond their instructions this year.

In a blog post Monday, OpenAI called the system “Control Room,” which is a centralized system that tracks model workloads across its research infrastructure and stops them quickly when safety or security concerns arise.

“As our models get more powerful and the associated safety and security risks become bigger, it becomes more and more critical that we can detect potential issues, and quickly stop models in their tracks when needed,” said the company.

The announcement comes after OpenAI disclosed several incidents this year in which research models acted outside their instructions during evaluations. In July, the company said its models had escaped a restricted testing environment and hacked Hugging Face to cheat on a security evaluation.

In October, it disclosed that a research model had used a crypto wallet without authorization to pay for smart contract deployments using testnet Ether, then omitted the spending from its final summary.

Control Room currently covers OpenAI’s internal research workloads, such as training experiments, evaluations and data processing.

It works by assigning each research job an identifier, which the inference engine checks before generating model output. If a person or automated monitoring system orders the job stopped, Control Room revokes its authorization, blocking further output even if the underlying research program keeps running, according to OpenAI.

OpenAI said cached authorizations could delay a shutdown by up to 35 seconds. The system is also designed to reject new requests if Control Room becomes unavailable.

Dutch Bros vs. On: Which Stock Is a Better Buy?

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Both are growth stocks, but one stands out more than the other.

Can high-growth consumer brands maintain their momentum in a shifting economy? Today we compare Dutch Bros (BROS +6.63%) and On (ONON +0.46%) to see which stock offers the best path for long-term investors.

Dutch Bros specializes in high-speed, drive-thru beverage service, focusing on speed and a unique culture. Swiss-based On has disrupted the athletic shoe market with its proprietary cushioning technology. Both companies are scaling aggressively, but they operate in distinct corners of the consumer market with different capital needs and margin profiles.

Dutch Bros operates a rapidly growing network of drive-thru beverage shops across multiple states. The company specializes in customizable drinks and partners with suppliers like Portland Bottling and Lieb Foods to produce its proprietary Rebel energy drink. By focusing on high-velocity service and a unique culture, it has carved out a significant niche among retail stocks.

In FY 2025, revenue reached nearly $1.6 billion, which was a 27.9% increase from the prior year. Net income climbed to approximately $79.8 million during the same period, showing improvement from the roughly $35.3 million reported in the previous fiscal year. The company continues to see strong demand for its cold beverage offerings and energy drinks as it expands its shop count.

On is a Swiss-based brand that has become a major player in the global performance apparel industry. In its latest annual report, filed for the period ending December 31, 2025, the company reported having 67 retail locations across the Americas, EMEA, and Asia-Pacific. It sells products globally across more than 90 countries.

During FY 2025, revenue reached nearly $3.7 billion, representing an increase of roughly 30% year over year. Net income for the period was close to $248.1 million, resulting in a net margin of approximately 6.8%. Net margin shows how much of each dollar in sales is kept as profit after all expenses are subtracted, and the company has grown sales from roughly $2.2 billion just two years prior.

Dutch Bros faces significant risks including intense competition from other coffee chains and quick-service restaurants. The company is heavily reliant on its Rebel energy drink line, which accounted for more than 20% of systemwide sales in 2025. It also deals with price volatility for key ingredients like coffee beans and dairy products, as well as potential delays in its aggressive shop expansion strategy.

On competes in a crowded market against massive brands like Nike and Adidas. Because it operates globally, the company is vulnerable to shifts in international trade policies and supply chain disruptions. Furthermore, staying relevant in the premium footwear market requires constant innovation and effective marketing to maintain brand prestige.

On appears to be the more attractively priced option because it trades at a lower forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, which compares the stock price to future earnings estimates. The price-to-sales (P/S) ratio, which measures market cap against sales over the past twelve months, also shows a difference between the two companies.

Between these two companies, I would lean more toward owning Dutch Bros. It has its challenges, which range from increased ingredient prices to intense competition to execution risk in expansion. Still, I like Dutch Bros more because it can be part of everyday routines, while purchases at On may only happen once or twice a year for some customers. Also, in tighter economic times, people may trade down in apparel, but for something like a coffee, that's often still kept in a weekly budget, as it's viewed as an affordable luxury.

I also like the overall expansion opportunities for Dutch Bros, as it is in fewer than 30 states. It also has a long-term goal of reaching over 7,000 drive-thru locations.

Over the next year, analysts also seem to have a more favorable outlook on Dutch Bros. According to CNN-tracked data, the median one-year price target among the 28 analysts covering the stock is $54, representing a gain of nearly 60% as of this writing. In comparison, among the 33 analysts who cover On, the median one-year price target is $43.40, representing a 16.1% gain. Between that short-term outlook and the longer-term opportunities with more location expansion, Dutch Bros wins this battle for me.

TL;DR: EDX Markets says it has partnered with BitSafe to introduce spot trading in CBTC, a Bitcoin-backed asset built for the Canton Network. EDX Markets says it has partnered with BitSafe to introduce spot trading in CBTC, a Bitcoin-backed asset built for the Canton Network. The November 5 announcement describes the token's first centralized-exchange listing and an institutional marketplace where eligible clients can trade CBTC against US dollars. This is not a new version of native Bitcoin; it is a separate representation intended to make Bitcoin exposure usable inside Canton's financial ecosystem.

According to EDX and BitSafe, each CBTC is backed one-for-one by Bitcoin. The asset is intended to support activities such as trading, lending and transfers in a privacy-enabled institutional environment. The partners point to distributed reserve controls and Chainlink Proof of Reserve as safeguards around backing. Those features are important, but a proof-of-reserve feed is not necessarily a complete audit of liabilities, ownership or redemption processes. Institutions still need to review custody agreements, redemption rights, legal claims and the conditions under which a token can be exchanged for its backing.

An asset created for use on an institutional chain needs reliable ways for participants to enter and exit positions. A trading pair against dollars can provide a price-discovery venue and simplify access for eligible institutions. Yet listing is not the same as deep liquidity. Effective markets require active market makers, transparent execution, operational reliability and practical transfer arrangements between the exchange and the Canton network. The announcement does not establish a certain future daily volume or demonstrate that every Canton participant will automatically have access to the product.

Native BTC settles on Bitcoin's own ledger under its network rules. A Bitcoin-backed token requires additional trust in the issuer, custodian, reserve accounting and any smart contracts or permissions used to control transfers. An institution may accept those conditions to obtain functionality it cannot easily achieve with native BTC. But the distinction must remain visible in both product descriptions and risk controls. A one-for-one target does not guarantee that a token trades at parity in every market condition, especially if redemptions are delayed or access is restricted.

Canton aims to connect financial applications while giving participating organizations control over sensitive information. A Bitcoin-backed asset could make collateral or financing arrangements easier within that environment, but it must integrate with the controls required by institutions. That might mean identity verification, permissioned counterparties and restrictions unfamiliar to users of open DeFi protocols. The commercial question is whether those features make institutional Bitcoin exposure more usable without creating too many layers of counterparty risk and operational cost.

Useful measures will include verified reserves over time, the number of eligible trading participants, actual volumes, the speed of deposits and redemptions, and any institutional collateral use. Readers should avoid treating a new token listing as an increase in Bitcoin's supply. The underlying BTC remains the backing asset; CBTC creates an additional instrument through which that economic exposure can circulate, with its own terms and risks.

‘Here There Are Blueberries’ Broadway Review: Evil As Banal As A Bowl Of Fruit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Here There Are Blueberries, the exceptional Pulitzer finalist docu-drama that opened Off Broadway two years ago to great admiration, finally makes its Broadway debut tonight at the Barrymore Theatre. The play, about a mysterious real-life cache of one-of-a-kind Auschwitz photos submitted to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in 2007, remains as intriguing as ever – and with source material as grotesquely mesmerizing as these candid snapshots, how could it not?

In fact, the sheer emotional heft of that photo album and everything it suggests dominates Blueberries to an extent that the play itself can seem rather thin at times, not entirely unworthy of the material at hand but not on equal footing either. Revelations about the banality of evil carry a force no matter how familiar we are with the concept, but familiar we are, and Blueberries can feel somewhat naive at times in its presumption that no one in the audience has ever heard of Hannah Arendt.

But in the grand scheme of things, and in light of the historical tale being told here, those quibbles with dramaturgy shouldn’t diminish the real achievement that is Here There Are Blueberries. The play is vital and more often than not riveting. Watch on Deadline

Performed by an excellent cast – any production that features the great Kathleen Chalfant is an opportunity not to be missed – Here There Are Blueberries is, at its core, a detective tale. In 2007, a young Holocaust museum archivist named Rebecca Erbelding (Delia Cunningham) receives a letter at her D.C. office from a man who wants to donate a cache of WWII snapshots.

The offer isn’t unusual – the museum serves as a repository for such keepsakes – but the nature of his collection most certainly is. The photo collection long in the man’s possession is more than a rarity, it’s absolutely unique: The images are of Nazi officers – including some of the highest ranking and most notorious, from Mengele to Höss – in their living quarters just over the wall from the camp’s atrocities. Some are of an almost surreal vacation lodge a few miles away, where officers, SS guards and female office workers alike would be treated to days of rest and relaxation for jobs well done. One officer is given five days there for his murder of two Jews attempting to escape the camp.

Such images had never been observed in photographs before, and their discovery is nothing less than historic, generating headlines across the globe, with much of the public fascination falling on the young German female camp office workers enjoying their vacation lodge days and food-and-drink treats. “Here,” says the caption (in German) of one such photo, “there are blueberries.”

The play is structured as an investigation, lead by the young archivist, as the museum staff seeks to uncover the circumstances under which these snapshots were taken, who took them, who is pictured, and, perhaps most intriguingly, how on earth did they survive when all over such evidence was destroyed by the Nazis themselves as the regime fell?

One such question – how did the man who possesses the photos discover them? – is easily answered: A retired U.S. Army Lieutenant Colonel (Michael Cerveris) found the album in the abandoned apartment he briefly inhabited while stationed in Frankfort at the end of the war. Why he kept them in his possession, and did so secretly, is unknowable – he died shortly after making the museum donation.

Other questions posed by the photos are of a more philosophical nature. Did those seemingly carefree young women at the lodge have any idea that the office work they were doing contributed to the genocide next door? And if so, how could they?

Blueberries, for all its demands on facing harsh truths, plays a bit coy in feigning shock at the answers, as if Judgment At Nuremberg never existed, an excusable but still noticeable manipulation. A bit more difficult to overlook are other red herrings seemingly designed to give the play momentum, such as will the museum board accept the donation of the Nazi photos when its mission is to chronicle the victims, not the victimizers? It’s a stick man argument.

Blueberries is on sturdier dramatic ground when new characters – the relatives of the Nazis and camp workers in the photographs – begin arriving mid-show to offer their perspectives. The sister of an office worker sticks with the usual denials, but others are revelatory, not least the grandson of Rudolph Höss, who relates an unnerving tale of his family’s complicity and his own struggles with the violence he fears is an inheritance.

The grandson’s tale is riveting, as is Brandon Flynn’s performance, a stand-out in a uniformly excellent cast that, in addition to those mention above, includes Conrad Ricamora, Scott Barrow, Michelle Beck and Montego Glover, all playing a variety of roles. Derek McLane’s stark set design, dominated by the many tall-legged stand-up desks used by the museum archivists to study the photos, is stellar, as are the crucial projections (designed by David Bengali) that allows us to see what the archivists are seeing. The photos projected onto the walls would be transfixing even without the superb lighting design (David Lander) and sound design (Bobby McElver) that further an entirely earned mood of dread and even despair, never more so than during a late scene that finally gives all those unseen victims their moment in this story, and in a manner so wrenching it won’t be spoiled here except to say that another photo album enters the story, and its story of survival is every bit as astonishing as the first.

In the end, Here There Are Blueberries has sailed past its own here-and-there shortcomings to land on a final thought, artfully told and with clarity of conviction: Just imagine how many untold boxes of forgotten family memorabilia and grim relics are waiting to be discovered, with secrets as efficiently hidden as those sealed away in the human heart?

Macao: Ex-Lawmaker Gets 12.5 Years After Unfair Trial

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Former lawmaker and veteran pro-democracy activist Au Kam San during an interview in Macao, October 7, 2024. © 2024 AP Photo/Bertha Wang

(London) – A court in Macao, a special administrative region of China, sentenced the former lawmaker and democracy activist Au Kam San to 12.5 years in prison on November 6, 2026, on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. 

Macao’s Court of First Instance, in the first publicly known case under the city’s national security law, convicted Au on five charges involving “subversion of state power,” “establishing links with foreign forces to endanger national security,” and “violation of secrecy.” The Macao government should quash the sentence and immediately and unconditionally release Au.

“The Macao authorities’ bogus prosecution of former lawmaker Au Kam San reflects Beijing’s escalating repression across its territories,” said Maya Wang, deputy Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “The sentence, which could keep the democracy advocate in prison until he’d be past 80, is both outrageous and cruel.”

The court did not release the full judgment, but only a summary of its decision. It ruled that Au worked with unspecified overseas “hostile” organizations “over an extended period” to disseminate materials advocating subversion and inciting hatred against the Chinese government, as well as to provoke hostile foreign action against China and disrupt Macao’s 2024 election for chief executive.

It also found that, while serving as a lawmaker in 2021, he disclosed confidential information about casino license renewals from a legislative committee meeting to overseas organizations. The court did not identify the organizations or substantiate the charges in detail, but claimed Au did not dispute most of the facts.

Au’s prosecution involved serious violations of his right to a fair trial, Human Rights Watch said. He had been detained since July 2025. 

In July 2026, his family told reporters that the authorities had held him incommunicado and denied him access to the lawyer his family found for him. Au was instead represented by a court-appointed lawyer, who had undergone the government’s required national security vetting, the court statement said. Article 36 of Macao’s functional constitution, the Basic Law, guarantees residents access to legal counsel to protect their lawful rights and interests. In its November statement, the court said Au’s family had been allowed to visit him “regularly.”

Au was tried in a closed-door hearing over three days, from September 16 to 18. A law that Macao adopted in March permits closing court proceedings to the public when Macao’s national security committee determines that national security is at risk. The committee’s decisions are not subject to appeal or judicial review.

Au, a Portuguese and European Union citizen and former primary school teacher, was one of Macao’s longest-serving pro-democracy lawmakers. Directly elected to the Legislative Assembly from 2001 to 2021, he was one of its few consistent voices for democratic reform, government accountability, and social causes.

He campaigned for 15 years for free public education and more public housing, scrutinized government spending, and raised concerns about corruption and the misuse of public funds. After leaving the legislature, he remained engaged in public affairs, including efforts to protect Macao’s historic Guia Lighthouse.

Au also played a major role in Macao’s small civil society. For 30 years, his group, the Macao Union of Democratic Development (澳門民主發展聯委會), organized annual vigils commemorating the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre in Beijing, even as he and fellow organizers experienced abuse for their efforts, including physical assaults and loss of jobs.

Macao was a Portuguese colony until 1999, when its sovereignty was transferred to the People’s Republic of China. Macao’s government has long suppressed critical voices. It has arbitrarily arrested opposition figures and journalists, severely restricted protests, and barred Hong Kong pro-democracy activists and journalists from entering the territory.

The crackdown on political opposition and civil society has intensified in recent years, Human Rights Watch said. Authorities disqualified 21 people in 2021 and 12 in 2025 from running for legislative seats, citing vague reasons such as “disloyalty” to Macao. In 2021, Macao’s Court of Final Appeal upheld the government’s arbitrary ban on the Tiananmen Massacre vigils, ruling that the organizers’ political slogans violated the Chinese Constitution and Macao’s Basic Law by urging the public to “overthrow the existing political system.”

Macao’s draconian Law on Safeguarding National Security, enacted in 2009 and amended in 2023, undermines the rule of law and human rights guarantees enshrined in the Basic Law, and contravenes the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which applies to Macao through the Basic Law. The 2023 amendments extended the National Security law to nonviolent acts; previously, only violent acts were punishable. 

The European Union has condemned Au’s conviction and sentencing, and Portugal has raised the case directly with the Chinese authorities. 

“Portugal, the European Union, and other concerned governments should publicly and forcefully press for Au Kam San’s immediate and unconditional release,” Wang said. “Countries should take note of the recent harsh sentences handed down in Hong Kong and Macao and consider what that means for their long-term relationship with the Chinese government.”
