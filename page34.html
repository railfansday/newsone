GTA 6 studio head Aaron Garbutt said that the most valuable lesson Rockstar learned ahead of GTA 5 was to kill its darlings, and "let what's best for the game dictate the way forward."

Speaking to Edge magazine back in 2013, Aaron Garbutt, who served as art director on GTA 5, was asked what the most valuable lessons Rockstar had learned from earlier Grand Theft Auto games had been. In response, he said that it was "mostly not to get locked down to anything, whether it's a process, a plan, a goal, an asset, an idea - anything."

Garbutt said the key was "to keep completely open-minded, adapt to the situation and always look for a better way forward, rather than stick with what's been decided previously. I don't mean chop and change for the sake of it, but let what's best for the game dictate the way forward. With a game that's as large and complex as this, the ideas need to be fluid, and you need to be paying as much attention as you can to every aspect to make sure they're working together."

His philosophy isn't exactly a brand-new one. The idea of killing your darlings - abandoning even your very favorite ideas if you find they no longer actually serve the project you're working on - has long been touted across the entire artistic industry. But applied to the complexity of a game like GTA 5, and its then-industry-leading open-world, the idea of knowing when to move on takes on increased importance.

Even back in 2013, Garbutt acknowledged that Rockstar was lucky to have an art team "that is extremely experienced at building open worlds," and was rarely stumped by brand-new problems. Even so, "there's always new things that come along [...] and the nature of how we approach development means that we need to be ready to adapt." For Rockstar, that's a layering approach, where the world is built first, and then missions, story, and game structure are added on top. "Each of these things influences the others massively," Garbutt said, "so each needs to adapt to the others. We are always playing and refining, adjusting each element of the game to play better with the others."

That approach puts Rockstar in a spot where even when it does do away with ideas that aren't working "we're rarely throwing away content." Instead, plans are adapted, and the studio is "constantly looking ahead with a clean slate, rather than sticking stubornly to old decisions."

It sounds basic, but Garbutt's idea here is a pivotal one in a medium as complex as game design. There's a though that few, if any, other forms of art are trying as hard to not get made as video games are, and when some ideas work well with certain features but clash with others, or take ages to actually implement (often at the expense of other systems), reworking them, or even abandoning them entirely, can be an important way to actually get projects finished on time. Sometimes that means that the grand ambitions of the developers don't get entirely delivered on, but if that means getting the game into players hands - or even avoiding having your studio shuttered around you - it's an important discipline to hone.

Perron shapes Quebec kitchen around tea collection

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A dedicated space for preparing tea has been placed at the heart of this Canadian house’s renovated kitchen, designed by local studio Perron.

Perron overhauled the kitchen for a longstanding client, with whom the studio has worked on several professional and personal projects over the years.

Her residence sits along the south shore of the St Lawrence River, overlooking Quebec City on the opposite bank.

The view across the water, and the client’s love of tea, were the primary guiding elements for the renovation project.

“For this house, we were not simply working from a checklist of requirements,” said Perron founder Nathalie Perron.

“We already knew about our client’s lifestyle, energy and the things that are most important to her.”

The kitchen space was divided into three zones: a space for tea, a central cooking area and a counter overlooking the river.

At the entrance, a small counter wrapped in glossy oxblood red tiles and a partition of burgundy steel bars form a niche that the client has nicknamed her “teahouse”.

Custom lacquered MDF wall shelves store and display cups, saucers, mugs and teapots that she has collected during her travels over the years.

“Each one evokes a specific memory and now has a place in her daily life,” said Perron.

An on-demand hot water tap is also installed within this niche, creating a dedicated space for the tea-making ritual.

The main kitchen area revolves around an angular island, which has space for a bar stool and incorporates a built-in electric stove.

Above, a cylindrical burgundy-hued range hood is framed within more steel bars, suspended from the ceiling to support shelves.

Rows of glass blocks are stacked between the upper and lower cabinets, which are both faced in stained white oak.

Ultra-thin steel shelves hang from the upper millwork, adding extra storage without blocking the light through the translucent bricks.

Opposite, a long counter faces the river through a large window, allowing the homeowner to witness passing ships and changing light conditions as she stands working or washing dishes.

Cristallo quartzite from Brazil forms the countertop and a backsplash.

Long, narrow Cotto Manetti terracotta tiles cover the floor, echoing the shapes of the matte and gloss pink Farblok ceramic tiles by Quintessenza that run up the wall in stripes.

A Mousse wall fixture by Quebec lighting brand JETA adds another feminine touch to the kitchen.

“It is a house that reflects a woman who enjoys going on adventures, but who has created a home that she loves returning to,” said the studio.

Perron has offices in Montreal and Quebec, the latter in an early 20th-century building renovated to house a studio, showroom and Nathalie Perron’s home.

Solana is retiring a label that has accompanied the blockchain since its launch: “mainnet beta.” In a November 16 announcement, the Solana Foundation introduced protocol version 1.0 and said the network should now be described simply as mainnet. It is a meaningful coordination change for developers and operators, but the Foundation was also explicit about what it is not. Users do not need to move funds, update token addresses or assume that a new feature has suddenly been enabled.

Solana has operated as a live production network for years while retaining the beta designation. Removing that wording is not a chain migration, hard reset or new issuance event. Accounts, SOL balances, applications and historical transactions remain on the same network, according to the Foundation. This matters for holders who may encounter posts suggesting they must claim a replacement token or connect a wallet to an upgrade portal. There is no such user requirement in the announcement. The change concerns how the live protocol is named and described.

The more technical development is a shared protocol version that is separate from the software version of any one validator client. Solana infrastructure includes Agave and Firedancer implementations, and those releases have their own numbering schemes. A network-level protocol number can make it clearer which clients support a given set of protocol rules, reducing reliance on one implementation as the reference point. That is coordination infrastructure rather than a substitute for testing. Validators still need to follow release notes and compatibility instructions for the specific software they deploy.

For related context, see our earlier report on recent Solana developer and validator updates.

A common protocol designation makes future network changes easier to communicate across independent implementations and tools. It could also help documentation, explorers and service providers describe compatibility consistently. But the Foundation cautioned that announcing a protocol version does not activate every associated improvement in one step. Feature activations and software rollouts retain their own schedules and technical requirements. Claims that Solana 1.0 instantly adds a particular throughput increase, fee mechanism or transaction feature should therefore be checked against separate activation notices.

For applications and RPC providers, the near-term work is keeping technical references current and watching client release guidance. For everyday users, the main practical change may simply be seeing “mainnet” rather than “mainnet beta” in wallets or interfaces. The market may attach symbolic significance to the milestone, but label changes are not a reliable stand-in for uptime, decentralisation, performance or adoption. Those outcomes still need to be measured independently as the network evolves.

Protocol labels are useful only if developers can connect them to concrete compatibility guarantees. Projects that integrate wallets, exchanges and payment systems still need to test RPC behavior, client changes and transaction-handling assumptions against real releases. Validators likewise need to monitor operator guidance instead of interpreting '1.0' as a permanent freeze in features or risk. A shared versioning convention may make upgrades easier to communicate, but sustained performance and reliable incident response remain the evidence users ultimately experience.

Solana 1.0 is best understood as a naming and versioning milestone. It establishes a common language for the protocol while leaving the hard work of shipping, activating and measuring future improvements to the network’s normal processes.

Talking Point: As A Parent, What Worries You Most About Gaming?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nintendo's guide aims to highlight the benefits

Over the summer, I replayed Ocarina of Time (1998) with my kids. I hadn't intended to, but it dawned on me that once the gorgeous remake landed, the original's visible polygons might not hold the same appeal for them. It was selfish, really, but I wanted them to experience the original magic as I did.

I held the pad but let them direct me, staying silent or feigning ignorance about puzzle solutions, letting them speculate and point out chests and switches I'd intentionally run past, involving them in an adventure they don't quite have the dexterity to control themselves.

I worried their attention might wander while watching for 30-60 minutes at a time, and I don't want to be that parent, forcing something I loved on my disinterested kids ("What do you mean 'Thunderbirds is boring'?!"). I was ready to pull the cord if they didn't take to it.

But no! They were pointing and problem-solving alongside me, jumping up and down, utterly engaged and loving the story and our shared experience.

Beyond the screen, Ocarina provoked laughter over dinner, and discussion about why characters reacted as they did, the cause and effect of actions, and the resulting emotions. Car journeys suddenly had a new soundtrack accompaniment, and the game's themes would come up while drawing or playing with Lego or finding a 'Deku' stick out on a walk. It became a rich source of inspiration for their imaginations, and a springboard to conversation and closeness with my kids. A fantastic family adventure we'll remember for years to come.

It's such a shame, then, when you see parents whose kids love games struggle to recognise the benefits and sheer joy they can bring, and potentially miss out on family experiences like mine. For something so ubiquitous and so fun, gaming can create real tension in the home and throw up generational barriers that take time and understanding to break down.

And with all the online concerns and other ills of the modern age, it's easy to see why some parents might perceive games as a dead-end, dangerous time sink that needs to be contained and quarantined, like fast food or social media.

According to a recent Ofcom report, 88% of children aged 3-17 in the UK play games on some form of device, and given Nintendo's evergreen, all-ages remit to deliver surprise and delight, addressing concerns of gaming-cautious guardians is something the company is keen on, beyond the bare minimum of secure account systems and Parental Control tools.

So that'll be why they've partnered up with Andy Robertson, founder of the Family Gaming Database and longtime friend of the site, for the recently published Parent's Guide to Gaming with Confidence.

Longtime Nintendo Life readers may recognise Andy from the various articles and reviews he's penned for us over the years, or from our partnership with Family Gaming to highlight accessibility information in our reviews and game pages across the network.

Produced in association with Nintendo, in this new guide Andy has also enlisted other experts in the fields of gaming, child psychology, and online safety to help parents of all gaming backgrounds digest the key questions and perhaps reframe how some of them view video games.

The prevalence of online gaming and voice chat worries me a lot. I don't want my kid speaking to strangers or picking up bad language or habits. Everything is so much easier (and tempting) to access than it used to be!

Available as a downloadable PDF, Andy explores common misconceptions and offers sound advice from expert contributors, including child psychologist Dr Amanda Gummer and Dr Catherine Knibbs, a child and adult psychotherapist specialising in online harm.

As you'll see from the breakout boxes on this page, uncertainties about other online players and predatory monetisation systems are the main sources of anxiety for NL staff with kids. "I'm very concerned about exploitative mechanics," says Alex, our senior video producer, "things designed to get their hooks into people's brains and make them dependent, or cough up wads of cash."

According to Ofcom's research, these are common worries:

The biggest type of concern among parents and carers of 3-17-year-olds who play games online is their child talking to strangers through games or via the chat functions (64%). This is followed by concerns about bullying by other players (60%), exposure to inappropriate content such as violence, bad language or disturbing material (59%), and pressure to make in-game purchases (51%).

Dr Knibbs highlights how "digital spaces can feel less familiar" than physical play spaces like playgrounds. The dangers can be less obvious, too, although she stresses that understanding the risks is better than avoidance. "When we learn about the content, the people and the spaces our children are engaging with, we are much better placed to help them navigate those experiences safely and positively."

It's not just about risk assessment, but also realising the potential benefits - and that doesn't require extensive gaming experience. "The goal ISN’T to become a gamer," Andy writes. "It is to better understand the people you care about most." You don't need to have conquered Elden Ring or any other game to participate and 'get' games.

Games with random or chance-based outcomes, especially with microtransactions that encourage you to spend money and try again, are what concern me most. As much as I enjoy it, Pokémon GO is a strong example of this (there’s a reason it was recently bumped up to PEGI 16), but thankfully, there are enough single-player adventures out there (Pokémon included!) that can minimise these often frustrating and FOMO-driven elements for younger players.

Another key point to stress to non-gaming parents might seem almost self-evident, but it's easily overlooked if recognising Mario and Pac-Man represents your sum knowledge of the medium.

For some, even now, 'gaming' remains an arcade-style competition for high scores, and that can create a disconnect between parents with preconceived notions and their kids, who are discovering that games can be so much more.

"A child who is creating, collaborating, problem-solving or connecting with friends is having a very different experience from someone passively consuming content," Dr Amanda Gummer writes in a section of the guide highlighting "one of the most common mistakes parents make" - that all games are essentially the same.

We can all recall 'Wow, games can do this???' moments when the scales fell from our eye. It might have been an emotional beat in Shadow of the Colossus, or a surprising Wii Sports session with grandparents over the holidays; whatever the source, getting the uninitiated to that point is a crucial first step. While it's all notionally 'reading', hopping between Wikipedia articles is obviously not the same as reading a poem or sitting down with James Patterson's latest or doomscrolling social media feeds or taking a crack at Ulysses, and once parents understand that the world of video games is equally expansive, they've got a foundation to build upon.

Andy takes time to break down what he terms "the myth of the isolated gamer" and how, in fact, games can bring families together. Living the Nintendo life as we do, multiplayer has been commonplace for decades, but the lone gamer in the basement/attic stereotype persists.

Likewise — and this has long been a personal bugbear when talking with friends and family — rationing 'screen time' feels like an oversimplification for parents guarding against passive media consumption. Lamenting how young people these days don't get enough fresh air is absolutely valid, and getting out in the park, playground, and beyond is crucial to a child's overall development, but it's not as simple as 'outside=good, inside=bad'. The storytelling, learning, collaboration, creativity, and deep experiences games can offer are enormously valuable.

As Andy points out, "understanding the quality of the experience is often more useful than simply counting the minutes." Bowling in Switch Sports, having the national news on in the background, diving into a Mario world, and throwing Bluey on Disney+ are all different types of 'screen time', each with their own benefits and potential drawbacks.

For the last month, I’ve been levelling up my game with the Nacon Revolution 5 Unlimited, a hefty pro controller for PS5. With a myriad of customisable features, including trigger travel length and even its weight, I’m going to break down whether this controller is worth your time.

The NACON Revolution 5 Unlimited is a big old pro controller, compatible with both PS5 and PC. It connects to PS5 via a dongle, can also connect wirelessly, offering 2.4Ghz connection for either, and offers Bluetooth connectivity too.

It features TMR thumbsticks, eradicating the worry of stick drift, along with swappable thumbstick tips and varying weights to fit in the grips of the controller, so you can customise the feel of the controller to your liking.

The big headliner here is a screen display in the centre of the controller, which allows access to simple customising menus without the need for an additional app — but we'll get into that later.

This Nacon Revolution 5 Unlimited will set you back £179.90/$229.90, actually making it one of the cheaper options in the pro controller market.

The NACON Revolution 5 Unlimited, which I’m just going to call the Rev5 from now on, comes in a rugged, hardshell case, with NACON branding embossed on the front.

Inside, the controller, of course, along with a collection of switchable thumbsticks, a D-Pad alternative, and weights which can be placed on the inside of the controller, a braided USB-A to C cable, the dongle, and the charging base.

The form factor is easily one of my favourite things about the Rev5. This is a pretty large, meaty controller, which, personally, I really like. It fills the hands, and immediately, all of the buttons feel like they are in naturally reachable places – something that wasn’t the case with the SCUF Omega, in my opinion.

Your eye will immediately be drawn to this little screen in the middle, and we’ll get to some of its features in a minute, but it definitely gives the Rev5 a premium look. The grips of the controller offer a ribbed rubber coating with a sleek black finish. The thumbsticks, d-pad, and triggers all have textured finishes, giving each a nice firm feel. There’s a satisfying clicky feel to the face buttons and back paddles, which are naturally placed where my fingers wrap around the back of the controller.

I love the triggers on this thing, which remind me of the Xbox controllers, actually. They have a nice smooth travel for the longer setting, but can also be switched to a mouse-like click, with a toggle on the back. I have to say, these are some of my favourite trigger length toggles that I’ve used on a pro controller. They are so easy to access and customise, without the need for longer nails to pick at them.

My only real complaints about the design are that the finish does make the controller a victim to smudges and smears, and the substitute button for the touchpad is a little small, which can be a pain if you want to quickly check leaderboards, for example.

Connecting this thing to the PS5 is a right doddle. You can use it wired with the braided cable, but most of my use was with the dongle.

What I really liked about this dongle is that it could be used whilst plugged into the back of the charging base, with a USB-A to C cable connecting to the PS5, meaning you can connect and charge the Rev5 with the use of just one USB-A port. However, if you're on the go, you can also just plug the dongle straight into the PS5, without the need for the charging base.

Annoyingly, however, you aren’t able to turn the PS5 on with this controller. You have to manually turn it on, then connect the Rev5, regardless of whether you are connecting wirelessly or wired.

Then there is that screen, which is really cool, but it did take me a moment to get my head around. A customise button on the top of the controller halts all inputs to the console and lets you navigate a simple menu system.

Here you can customise things like thumbstick dead zones, audio and mic input (if you’re using a wired headset), and customise each of the six additional buttons around the controller.

You’re then able to make four custom profiles, which you can switch between with a tap of the Profile button on the back of the controller. It’s not quite DualSense Edge levels of ease, but it is great.

If a little screen menu isn’t cutting it for you, there is also still the option to download the Nacon app and customise more intensely from there.

Then there is the physical customisation. You are able to switch out thumbsticks, the D-pad, and apply additional weights in the grips of the controllers. All of this is incredibly accessible, as there are no tools required. You can just pull one thing off and replace it with another. The only thing I had a bit of struggle getting off was the smaller variation of the d-pad.

Honestly, I think the Rev5 feels great. Once you have a profile that suits you, this thing is just moulded to the hand, with each of its additional paddles easily within reach, and its physical customisation options making it feel tailor-made to you.

I love the travel of the trigger if going for long triggers, but it features a really satisfying mouse-click if you customise it to short triggers too. The TMR sticks do have that weightless feel that's typical for thumbstick utilising Hall Effect tech, but I adjusted to it quite quickly.

The easy profile swapping and customisation on the actual controller, and the feel in the hand, meant I was more inclined than ever to just use this thing for regular games, not just those competitive titles like The Finals or Modern Warfare 4. I actually played most of Zero Parades using this controller, just because it was comfortable to use, despite not using a single customisation aspect.

There is a pretty big flaw, however, regarding that screen – the lack of a touchpad. The button initially used to replace it is very small and not as easy to touch as I’d like when opening up maps or menus in-game. Of course, you can just use one of the other shortcuts for this if you’re so inclined.

However, it does mean if there ever is a game that utilises the touchpad, like Ghost of Yotei, for example, you’re kind of out of luck. Admittedly, there isn’t a huge number of games that utilise this feature, or have anything beyond a single-use touch, so most of the time, it’s a bit of a non-issue. However, it is something to consider if this is to be your main controller.

As is the case with these controllers – and the same with the last Nacon controller I reviewed – this thing doesn’t feature rumble on the PS5, meaning it’s severely lacking in the immersive department.

For a lot of people, that’s just not going to matter, but for me it’s an additional feature that always brings me back to the DualSense Edge – a pro controller with full haptics and adaptive triggers. What’s even worse is that there is actually a rumble motor in this controller, but it’s limited to use on PC.

I can’t knock the controller too much for that because outside of the DualSense Edge, most pro controllers won't feature any form of rumble. It’s just a shame because, for me, if it had rumble on PS5, it would be among my favourite controllers.

Despite my musing over a lack of rumble support on PS5, I do think the Rev5 is worth considering if you’re in the market for a pro controller.

It feels fantastic in the hand, with a satisfying click and travel to each of its buttons; the customisation is easy to change from a software perspective, but also incredibly accessible from a hardware perspective.

It features great build quality, the charger/dongle system is handy, and it comes with a pretty cool carry case. And you get all of this for about £20 to £30 less than most other pro controllers.

Is it perfect? No way. But it’s a pretty damn nice controller.

This '80s R-Rated Superhero Movie Is Unlike Anything Marvel Or DC Has Made

We've been living in a world of mass entertainment dominated by superheroes and comic book characters for more than 25 years now, dating back to Marvel's earlier blockbuster hits like "X-Men" and "Spider-Man." Even small screen stuff like "The Walking Dead" has comic book roots. But decades before any of that, an indie film pioneer made a superhero movie unlike any other.

Lloyd Kaufman is independent film, in many ways, as the founder of Troma Entertainment. His media empire was built on the backs of low-budget schlock like "Surf Nazis Must Die" and "Pterodactyl Woman from Beverly Hills," among many others. But Kaufman's defining hit came in 1984 in the form of "The Toxic Avenger," an ultra violent, campy, R-rated superhero affair. It remains a distinct piece of punk rock cinema more than 40 years removed, unmatched in its insanity by anything Marvel or DC has done on screen.

The movie takes place in the fictional town of Tromaville, known as the toxic waste capital of America. A bullied young janitor in a popular gym falls into a vat of toxic waste and turns into a super strong but disfigured man. He begins cleaning up the streets from evildoers in an often funny yet violent fashion.

"The Toxic Avenger" has a long history on screen and is now a cult legend, but that legend began in '84, at a time when superheroes were far from bankable fare in the mainstream. Co-directed by Michael Herz and Kaufman himself, the resulting movie can't be accused of being high art, but it's one heck of a wild swing, and a testament to what the indie filmmaking spirit can accomplish.

At the time, Hollywood had only had the occasional superhero movie hit it big. "Superman: The Movie" in 1978 being the biggest one, while Batman didn't become a box office hero until 1989. Even that movie just led to the "Batman" franchise taking off rather than a broader trend of superheroes becoming big business. The '90s saw many try but few succeed, with stuff like "Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles" more the exception rather than the rule.

All of this to say, when Lloyd Kaufman decided to make a dark superhero comedy — an R-rated and super violent one no less — it wasn't exactly a recipe for success. At least not on paper. But decades before "Deadpool" broke the Marvel mold by going R-rated and injecting comedy into the genre, "The Toxic Avenger" dared to venture into that territory.

What's more, Kaufman and Co. did it on a shoestring budget of well under $1 million. "Deadpool" gets a lot of credit for being low-budget, but it still cost north of $50 million. What Kaufman pulled off with latex, old school special effects, and can-do indie spirit is nothing shy of impressive. Even those who are put off by the nature of this sort of exploitation film should be impressed by what was accomplished here with so little.

"The Toxic Avenger" has a demented sense of humor, and it doesn't give us a typical hero either. Toxie isn't a square-jawed, muscle-bound vigilante. He's a mutant who decides to clean up the streets with straight-up violence. The film has more in common with revenge driven horror/thrillers than it does any mainstream superhero movie. All the same, it became a cult hit and started a wildly unlikely franchise.

"No theater would play it," Lloyd Kaufman said of "The Toxic Avenger" to Parallax View in 2010. "We couldn't get one theater to play it anywhere and then we took it to Cannes and not one deal. Nothing! And then a few months later we got one movie theater in Greenwich Village, New York, and there was a line around the theater."

That single theater paved the way for more to follow. This was also at the dawn of the VHS home video craze, which is where the movie really thrived — so much so that Kaufman eventually produced a series of sequels. That includes 1989's "The Toxic Avenger Part II" and "The Toxic Avenger Part III: The Last Temptation of Toxie," also released in '89. Much later, "Citizen Toxie: The Toxic Avenger IV" would arrive in 2000. A gore-soaked remake of "The Toxic Avenger" starring Peter Dinklage was also released in 2025.

Kaufman's crown jewel is an elbow grease style, gory, raunchy, rubber-suit take on superheroes that doesn't resemble anything we've seen in the 2000s, really. Sure, "Clayface" brought body horror to the DC Universe, but it's completely without a sense of humor. It's also slick and relatively expensive (for a horror movie).

It was cutting edge then and it's impressively distinctive even now. More than that, "The Toxic Avenger" helped make the empire that is Troma. It's easy to write it all off as schlock, but Troma has been a haven for indie film for decades, with filmmakers like James Gunn ("Guardians of the Galaxy") cutting their teeth there. Without this movie, who knows if the empire as we know it even exists?

That said, there's no inherent harm in trying to determine if you've been blocked. It may even be crucial if you have an honest reason to think it was a mistake. While blocking a number by accident is unlikely, it's not impossible.

Since you're using an iPhone, the clues are mostly in Apple's Phone, Messages, and FaceTime apps. But you may want to expand your sleuthing to third-party messaging platforms, depending on how you previously communicated with someone.

Your biggest clue is likely to be a call in the Phone app that goes straight to voicemail after one ring or no ring at all. You can only be sure after multiple calls at varying hours, however. That's because with a single call, or several calls within a short timeframe, there may be alternative explanations. Perhaps the person has Do Not Disturb or another Focus mode on. A faster way of confirming things is by calling from a second number if you have one, such as Google Voice or a work phone. Exercise caution here, since this could be seen as harassment. Android phones won't provide receipts at all unless they're using RCS instead of SMS.

Missing read receipts or unanswered calls could be evidence that you've been blocked across multiple platforms. However, they might mean something else, such as Meta or Telegram experiencing technical difficulties.

Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle didn’t immediately respond to Fortune’s requests for comment.
