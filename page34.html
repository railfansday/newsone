“Heaven’s Gate has been an obsession of mine for nearly thirty years. When I was eight-years-old, 39 people died in my neighborhood. This event had a profound impact on my life. Capline Dental Services Reports 60% Faster Dental Credentialing Through AI-Driven Automated Workflows

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Houston-based dental revenue cycle management company says automated profile checks, payer follow-up, and status tracking are helping providers reach active payer status sooner.

HOUSTON, Nov. 11, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Capline Dental Services (CDS), a U.S.-based dental credentialing company, has reported a 60% acceleration in dental credentialing after introducing AI-driven automated workflows across its provider enrollment process. The workflows cover provider profile validation, application preparation, payer follow-up, and status tracking, the areas where credentialing files commonly lose time.

Credentialing is the key step that decides when a dentist can bill a payer as an in-network provider. Until an application is approved and the provider is active, claims may be held, paid at out-of-network rates, or denied. Timelines vary by payer, but according to CDS, most fall between 60 and 180 days, with 90 to 120 days being common. For a practice adding an associate or opening a new practice, each week of waiting is a week in which that provider's schedule cannot produce fully insured revenue.

"Credentialing rarely stalls because of one large error," said Abhinav Rastogi, Founder of Capline Dental Services. "It stalls when a document is missing, details do not match across forms, or a payer request sits unanswered. Automation helps us catch those gaps early and keep every file moving, while our credentialing specialists remain accountable for each application."

The announcement comes as administrative work continues to weigh on dental practices. The American Dental Association's Health Policy Institute reports that 72.5% of U.S. dentists owned private practices in 2023, down from 84.7% in 2005. For practices that add associates or open new locations, every new provider means another round of payer enrollment, and the provider pool is large: the ADA counts 205,088 professionally active dentists in the United States as of 2025.

The credentialing workload carries a measurable cost of its own. In a 2019 survey of 1,240 physician practices, CAQH found that practices exchanging credentialing information through multiple methods spent an average of $2,068 a month on the work, compared with $1,249.86 for practices using a single platform. The average practice in that survey held 20.2 health plan contracts, each with its own verification standards. Dental offices enrolling providers with several payers face a similar patchwork of requirements.

Delays seldom trace back to a single mistake. They build from smaller gaps: an incomplete document, a detail that differs between the application and the provider's CAQH profile, or a payer request that goes unanswered for days. Because each payer has its own forms and review rules, these gaps multiply when a practice applies to several networks at once. Front desk and office teams often end up chasing status updates between patient calls, making consistent follow-up hard to maintain.

The documentation side is simple but detail-sensitive. Practices typically need license and education details, malpractice coverage, and provider credentials ready before an application can move. CDS sends a checklist at the start so nothing is overlooked, and its automated checks review the file for gaps before it goes to the payer.

AI supports the credentialing file from the first document a provider uploads to the last payer follow-up, with a person reviewing the work before anything reaches a payer. The idea is simple: catch errors before the payer does.

CDS says its AI-enabled systems help its team analyze patterns, flag documentation gaps, and prioritize follow-ups, while human oversight stays in place on every account. In credentialing, that means software handles the auditing, verification, form filling, and reminders, while a team member reviews every application before submission and handles payer communication when a file needs attention.

The same approach applies when practices grow. When an associate or specialist joins, CDS streamlines the enrollment steps to reduce provider downtime and keep schedules productive.

Keeping Providers Credentialed After Approval

Approval is not the end of the work. CAQH asks providers to re-attest their profile data every 120 days, and recredentialing comes around again later. CDS manages CAQH profile creation, document uploads, updates, and attestations, along with recredentialing, EFT enrollment, Medicare enrollment, and, where payers allow it, fee schedule support. The goal is for providers to stay active and for payments to flow correctly once enrollment is complete.

Connecting Credentialing to Dental Revenue Cycle Management

CDS treats credentialing as one part of dental revenue cycle management rather than a stand-alone task. An enrollment error that goes unnoticed can surface months later as a rejected claim, which is why the company connects credentialing with dental billing and collection, and dental eligibility verification. Keeping these functions under one team gives practices a clearer view of where payments are getting delayed.

"Faster credentialing only helps if the enrollment is set up correctly," Abhinav Rastogi added. "Our aim is for a provider to reach active status with a clean setup, so claims process properly from the first insured visit."

CDS has served more than 1.4K+ clients and has over 12 years of experience supporting general dentists, specialists, and multi-location practices. Its team handles dental billing, credentialing, insurance verification, and patient statements through HIPAA-guided workflows. Practices that want to understand how CDS manages provider enrollment can request a free consultation through the company's website. CDS also offers a free dental revenue health check that reviews denial trends, payer performance, A/R days, and eligibility gaps.

New Richard Avedon Documentary Shows That Fashion Isn’t All Style—It’s Substance, Too

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When The Devil Wears Prada 2 hit theaters this past spring, it painted an accurate and depressing picture of how fashion publishing has dwindled, its creative juices squeezed. Miranda Priestly, is no longer calling on her assistants to ring Patrick, a reference to the fashion photographer Patrick Demarchelier, or anyone of that caliber.

As someone who spent a majority of their career in that industry and had a short stint at Vogue, the publication the “Devil Wears Prada” films were based on, it’s fair to say everyone goes in with a dream of what it means to work at a fashion magazine. Often, those fantasies are informed by the photographs of Herb Ritts, Helmut Newton, and Richard Avedon, the latter of which is the subject of a new documentary by filmmaker Ron Howard. Titled Avedon, it releases on Netflix today. As far as fashion documentaries go, it’s full of style and substance, backed up with anecdotes from Larry Gagosian, Tina Brown, Twiggy, Hilton Als, Samira Nasr, Isabella Rossellini, Calvin Klein, Avedon’s son John, and James Martin, the executive director of the Richard Avedon Foundation. Gagosian, who represents the Richard Avedon Foundation and has just opened an Avedon show in Beverly Hills, recalls going to brunch with the photographer, where all that was served was hard-boiled eggs and champagne. “I thought it was super elegant,” says Gagosian.

Using a combination of archival footage and interviews, Howard echoes the glitz and extravagance of fashion publishing’s bygone era of hefty budgets and creative freedom: Avedon’s photoshoots for Harper’s Bazaar and Vogue would last four days, and often featured models such as Lauren Hutton, Beverly Johnson, and Penelope Tree. Avedon managed to capture not just pretty faces, but the souls and secrets behind them.

Avedon grew up reading magazines such as Harper’s Bazaar, Vanity Fair, and Vogue at his father’s women’s retail business on Fifth Avenue. In them, he found the photographs by Martin Munkácsi, Edward Steichen, and Anton Bruehl.

He got his start in the 1940s, during World War II, working as a staff photographer for the United States Maritime Service, for which photographed around 100,000 faces on plain white backgrounds for ID cards with his Rolleiflex camera. “It’s existential white, the only thing you see is the geography of the face,” Avedon says in an old interview used in the film, referring to the way he spotlit his subjects against a white background.

After the war, he took courses in graphic design and photography at the New School, where he met Harper’s Bazaar’s art director, Alexey Brodovitch, who encouraged him to go to Paris in 1947 as part of the magazine’s mandate of “bringing back the glamor of pre-war Paris.”

At that publication, Avedon bore witness to a fashion revolution. In 1947, Christian Dior presented his New Look collection, an antidote to the rationed fabrics of the war, with full pleated skirts falling below mid-calf length. Avedon, who extravagantly captured five girls swirling in their full-length skirts, says that he lit each photograph he took in Paris as though he were reconstructing a Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers film.

He recalls photographing a model crying at a railway station. The picture was met with some acerbic feedback from Carmel Snow, the renowned editor-in-chief of Harper’s Bazaar, who coined the term the New Look: “Nobody cries in a Dior hat, Dick.”

Avedon’s work for Harper’s Bazaar shaped his career, paving the way for him to become a star beyond the fashion world. In 1955, he photographed one of his most famous images, Dovima with elephants; Gagosian reveals he once sold it for $2 million. By 1957, Avedon had shot to fame like Picasso (he photographed the artist in 1958) and had become the inspiration behind Stanley Donen’s film Funny Face, in which an Avedon-like photographer is played by Astaire. In a nod to the film’s inspiration, Avedon was brought on to shoot the stills used for the film’s opening credits.

Fundamentally, Avedon was always on a mission to capture the human condition. He firstly did so with stars and starlets to illustrate that nothing is really so perfect. Howard’s documentary seems to suggest that this was all related to Avedon’s own imperfect upbringing.

In Howard’s documentary, Avedon, who died in 2004, says that his parents always presented an idealized family in their scrapbooks and often borrowed a dog as a prop to exaggerate the image they cultivated. He grew up in a stern household with a father that was emotionally absent for a big chunk of his life until they reconciled later in life.

His sister Louise, was his first muse. Avedon says in the film that her “beauty affected me,” and that all the models he chose resembled his sister with their oval faces, high cheekbones, and brunette hair. Louise was catatonic and spent more than 15 years living in a psychiatric hospital, where she died in 1968 at age 42.

“One of the great themes in his work is alienation and isolation. He wants to know what’s going on inside human beings,” says John, Avedon’s son. In times of heartache, Avedon reveled in his work—such as when his first wife, the actress Doe Avedon, left him for another man that was blonde and tall, or when his second wife, Evelyn Franklin, fell into depression, leading Avedon to start living out of his studio. “Everything diminishes,” Avedon says here. “Work does not. It’s the one thing that doesn’t turn on you.”

Maybe because his life wasn’t so pretty, Avedon’s photographs didn’t always flatter. When he shot Nancy Reagan, for example, he abided by all of her handler’s guidelines. Then, in the final image, he focused on her claw-like hands, a contrast to her perfectly, smooth face.

Sometimes to get the image he wanted, he would manipulate his subjects, as he did when he shot the Duke and Duchess of Windsor (formerly King Edward VIII and Wallis Simpson). Avedon told them that, on the way to the photoshoot, his taxi ran over a dog. This was a lie, but they seemed to believe it, and Avedon captured the droop in their smile. Simpson told Avedon that she would destroy him when the photos were published, but Avedon, who was Jewish, described the couple as “compassionate,” despite the fact that they sympathized with the Nazis during World War II.

It’s evident through Avedon’s line of work that the photographer saw the world beyond the pages of Harper’s Bazaar and Vogue. He waited seven weeks in Vietnam to photograph the American generals of the war; the residents of the American West and the Chicago 7 also counted among his subjects. The film portrays Avedon as a complicated figure, one whose contributions exceed the field of fashion photography.

It also portrays him as a complicated person, but it could’ve gone even further. Howard shies away from claims about Avedon’s sexuality that have been voiced in books such as Avedon: Something Personal, by Norma Stevens and Steven M. L. Aronson, and Michael Gross’s Focus: The Secret, Sexy, Sometimes Sordid World of Fashion Photographers. Gross reports that Avedon had an affair with his high school classmate, the writer James Baldwin, who even worked on a book with Avedon called Nothing Personal. That isn’t addressed here. (Avedon’s foundation vigorously disputed certain episodes described in Something Personal upon the book’s release, but it did not outright deny the descriptions of the photographer’s sexual identity.)

Howard also glosses over the fact that Avedon stopped working for Vogue when Anna Wintour replaced Grace Mirabella as editor-in-chief, moving away from his cropped headshot covers. This is also skipped over entirely.

Howard is instead more focused on the glamorous ghosts of a vanished era. The fashion industry has been consumed by capitalism, and fashion magazines have lost their ability to influence. All we’re left with is tales of the good old days and a graveyard of nostalgic images. You don’t have to look too closely to see that the devil is in the picture.
