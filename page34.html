6 Underrated Mountain Towns to Visit This Winter

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

You know the big names. Jackson, Wyoming. Stowe, Vermont. Telluride, Colorado. These are the mountain towns that dominate our winter daydreams. The famous ski towns that make us consider taking a sabbatical, buying a van, and living the Aspen Extreme dream for three months in the heart of winter. But there are other winter adventure spots that deserve the spotlight.

I cover mountain towns for Outside, and every year I start plotting out my own winter getaways right about now. Sure, I enjoy hitting up established destinations like Park City as much as the next skier, but the towns on this list get me excited because they fly under the radar. They don’t receive a ton of hype, but they have access to amazing powder stashes, lengthy trail systems, and resort lines that are far shorter than their better-known counterparts’.

A couple of these towns are my longtime favorites, while others are still on my “must-go” list. Here are six often overlooked winter mountain towns that you need to visit once the snow starts to gather.

A legitimate winter town in the South? Yes indeed. Boone is a vibrant college town that grows when Appalachian State’s roughly 20,000 students are in session. It has all of the hallmarks of a fun-loving mountain town, with plenty of dive bars and burrito joints to keep you satiated. But it also sits in North Carolina’s High Country, surrounded by 5,000- and 6,000-foot mountains, which get some of the heaviest snowfalls in the region. There are three ski resorts within 30 minutes of downtown and some of the most consistent cross-country skiing in the South on a series of 6,000-foot peaks near the Tennessee border.

Winter Adventures: You have three downhill resorts to choose from, but Beech Mountain Resort is the highest ski resort in eastern North America, with a summit elevation of 5,506 feet. It also has a bar, the 5506′ Skybar, at the top of the mountain and its own brewery in the base village. As for the terrain, the mountain has 95 acres and just over 800 feet of drop, so you’ll need to do laps to get in your vert. But the terrain park gets better every year, and skiers can usually count on about 80 inches of snow every winter. Full-day adult lift tickets start at $57 on weekdays.

But the best winter adventure near Boone is cross-country skiing Roan Mountain, a high ridge that tops out at 6,285 feet, gets heavy snowfall, and has a variety of terrain to ski, from high-elevation grassy balds to singletrack through rhododendron tunnels. The only catch is you’ll need to bring your own skis, as rentals are scarce in the South. Start at Carver’s Gap and head up the gated Forest Service road, which climbs gradually for about two miles to the Rhododendron Gardens. You’ll get long-range views in both directions and the option to hop onto more challenging trails that branch off the road.

Duluth is a longtime shipping and industrial city at the western tip of Lake Superior that has found new life as an adventure hub, with a world-class mountain bike trail system traversing the hills on the edge of downtown. But the city’s location in northern Minnesota means winter hits the city hard. Duluth gets about 86 inches of lake-effect snow each winter, and that snow sticks around because high temperatures stay below freezing on more than 100 days a year. The constant cold temps are ideal for ice climbing, and the consistent snow and cornucopia of mountain bike trails mean Duluth becomes a fat-biking and cross-country ski mecca during winter.

Need more reasons to visit? How about downhill skiing at nearby Spirit Mountain, which has 175 acres of terrain with 700 feet of vertical drop.

Winter Adventures: Northern Minnesota has a legitimate ski culture, and Spirit Mountain has some of the best blue cruisers in the Midwest, many of which have long-range views of downtown Duluth and Lake Superior. The mountain also has two terrain parks, one for beginners looking to progress and one for shredders looking to send it. There’s a welcoming uphill policy for anyone who wants to earn their turns on touring gear, too. Adult tickets start at $83.

But Duluth is a bike town, and when the snow falls that means it’s time to roll out the fat tires. Many sections of the Duluth Traverse, an epic 40-mile-long route that runs from one side of the city to the other, are groomed for fat bikes in the winter. The machine-built trails in Mission Creek, with their berms and rollers, are particularly fun with a layer of packed snow. Local outfitter Day Tripper offers guided fat-bike tours and rentals.

New England got hammered with snow last winter, with the highest elevations in the mountains getting more than 200 inches of snow, and the tiny town of Jackson was poised to make the most of it. This picture-perfect village, which comes complete with a general store and covered bridge, sits on the edge of White Mountain National Forest with fast access to downhill skiing at Black Mountain and roughly 90 miles of cross-country ski trails scattered throughout the valley.

Jackson doesn’t have the cachet of some other ski towns in New England, but American ski culture got an early boost here when Black Mountain opened in 1935, making it one of the first lift operations in the country. And that culture is still thriving. Case in point: check out Friday Night Lights, a weekly uphill party where skiers put on headlamps and ski to the top of the mountain en masse.

Winter Adventures: The Jackson Ski Touring Foundation grooms and maintains about 150 kilometers of trails, all surrounding a touring center with rentals and lessons. The trails traverse the community throughout three consecutive river valleys over 60 square miles. You’ll get views of Mount Washington from open meadows, tight singletrack through woods, and even the chance to hit trailside restaurants in certain spots. Day passes start at $29.

No trip to Jackson is complete without some laps at Black Mountain, a proudly independent ski resort with 140 acres of glades, narrow groomers, and nonexistent lift lines. You’ll get 1,100 feet of vertical drop, with the steepest terrain at the summit, and a lively pub (the Shovel Handle Pub) at the base of the mountain for après-ski activities. Adult lift tickets start at $49.

The San Juan Mountains are home to some of Colorado’s most iconic mountain towns and most well-regarded ski slopes, but most skiers don’t explore the region beyond Telluride and Silverton. Dig deeper into the mountains of southwest Colorado and you’ll find tiny Pagosa Springs, which sits on the banks of the San Juan River. The hamlet is best known for its geothermal springs, but the mountains surrounding this hot-water hub are even more enticing. Pagosa Springs is 23 miles from Wolf Creek Ski Area, which is known as one of the snowiest mountains in Colorado, pulling down an average of 430 inches of snow every winter.

Want more variety? Summer waterfalls turn into ice-climbing playgrounds when the temperature drops, and the local Nordic club grooms more than 50 kilometers of skate and classic ski trails near town.

Winter Adventures: Forget the champagne huts and concierge service you find at many ski resorts these days. Wolf Creek is a no-frills operation that’s all about the snow and the terrain, with 430 inches of annual snowfall that blanket wide groomers and steep trees. OK, a lot of trees. Wolf Creek has a significant amount of expert terrain, which plays out in a series of steep bowls at the top of the resort and seemingly endless glades all over the mountain. And you won’t have to take out a loan to fund this trip; full-day adult lift tickets at Wolf Creek are right at $110.

The 50 kilometers of cross-country ski trails begin in town, with a mile-long loop inside the town park that’s perfect for beginners. But this is a great chance to dip a toe into fat biking. There’s an 8.5-mile loop that’s groomed for fat bikes when snow allows, winding through a ponderosa pine forest. Pagosa Mountain Sport rents cross-country skis and fat bikes.

Park City, Salt Lake City…these are the ski towns that get the press, but Ogden? Ogden doesn’t typically make the list. But it should. This historic city, which was the first permanent settlement by people of European descent in the state of Utah, sits between the Great Salt Lake and the Wasatch Mountains, just 40 minutes north of Salt Lake City, but seemingly a world apart. Downtown Ogden is anchored by 25th Street, a historic main street with upscale Wild West charm, while three ski resorts known for deep powder are less than 30 minutes away. Powder Mountain anchors the trio, with more than 8,000 acres of terrain, but Snowbasin is continually ranked one of the best ski resorts in the country by Ski magazine and the smaller Nordic Valley has a local-hill vibe complete with night skiing. Bonus: You don’t have to fight Salt Lake City’s notorious canyon traffic to make your turns.

Winter Adventures: You’re here to ski, so get after it at Powder Mountain, the largest ski resort in the Lower 48. Powder has 2,800 acres of lift-served terrain with more than 2,000 feet of vertical drop, but the real gem here is Powder’s sidecountry. Head through the Powder Country or Woody’s World entrance gates from the lift-served side of the mountain and you’ll have 1,200 acres of ungroomed, off-piste action at your ski tips. The best part? A shuttle bus picks you up at the bottom of your line and takes you back to the front of the mountain, so there’s no boot-packing involved. Adult lift tickets start at $99.

You might also want to try your hand at sledding AKA snowmobiling while you’re in Ogden. The Monte Cristo area, east of the Ogden Valley, has some of the best snowmobile terrain in the state, including a 63-mile groomed loop that’s perfect for beginners and has long-range views of the Ogden Valley. Club Rec North offers tours and rentals next to the trailhead. Tours with rentals start at $229.

This former gold-mining post in the North Cascades really leans into its historic roots, with a downtown that’s lined with Old West-style false fronts and wooden boardwalks. And that western heritage is still alive and well. A number of outfitters offer horseback adventures and the annual Ride to Rendezvous, a four-day horse-packing trip complete with a wagon train that rolls into town for Winthrop’s ’49er Days parade. But make no mistake, Winthrop has found new life as a ski town. Well, a skinny-ski town. The Methow Valley surrounding Winthrop has one of the most extensive cross-country trail networks in the country with more than 120 miles of groomed, marked and maintained paths to explore.

Looking for a cozy vibe for the whole family? Take a spin around Winthrop Rink, the town’s outdoor ice-skating rink that serves as the social hub for the season.

Winter Adventures: If you’re spending time in Winthrop, then you’re here to ski the Methow Trails. With more than 120 miles of obsessively groomed paths, there is something for everyone in this valley. Want to ski from lodge to lodge? That’s an option. Want a pretty loop around a lake? No problem. Want to spend the day climbing an obscene amount of vertical? They have you covered. The Methow Trails organization runs clinics and camps all season if you want to improve your kick-and-glide form or maybe try your hand at biathlon. Day passes start at $32.

Loup Loup Ski Bowl is a lift-served mountain with 300 acres that’s operated by a nonprofit. There are 10 runs with 1,240 vertical feet of drop. You’ll find beginner trails and expert trails that drop from the top of the mountain and a very welcoming uphill ski culture if you want to earn your turns. And you’ve got to check out the Ski Bowl’s luge course, where you guide an old-school sled down groomed trails. Adult lift tickets start at $80.

Graham Averill is Outside magazine’s national parks columnist. He loves warm-weather adventures, but he gets absolutely giddy when ski season approaches, and writing articles like this helps him plan his ski trips.

‘Madden’ Sparks Backlash From Raiders Owner Mark Davis Over Father Al Davis’ Portrayal: “That Hurts”

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

As John Madden‘s life gets the biopic treatment, his longtime colleague and friend Al Davis‘ family is not pleased with their late patriarch’s portrayal.

Las Vegas Raiders owner Mark Davis, whose father Al recruited Madden to coach for the NFL team when they were located in Oakland in 1967, criticized David O. Russell‘s Madden for its “totally unfair” depiction his father (portrayed by Christian Bale), who died at age 82 in 2011.

“It was full of turducken,” he told USA Today of the movie, referencing the Thanksgiving dish Madden popularized.

Mark continued, “As far as representations of my dad, yeah, that hurts, and I did the best I could to protect him. It’s absolutely a crime that they’re able to treat people that have passed away and characterize them in ways that are unfair, and there’s nobody around to protect their legacies. It’s just unfair. I don’t know what the purpose of the movie was to be real honest.” Watch on Deadline

He doesn’t consider his father “a pimp like Christian Bale played him, but that’s fine,” noting, “And I don’t blame Christian Bale. I don’t blame Nicolas Cage. All they did was what the director and the writers told them to do.”

Having screened the film two weeks before its release, Mark was surprised by a scene depicting the 1978 preseason game that paralyzed New England Patriots wide receiver Darryl Stingley, in which Al discourages Madden (Nicolas Cage) from seeing the player in the hospital.

“One of the main legacies of my father was how much he took care of people, whether it was on the Raiders or other teams,” said Mark. “People that were ill or needed help or whatever. He helped so many players on other teams that it’s incredible. And he did it quietly.

“And for them to put in the Darryl Stingley incident, that my dad was trying to talk John out of going to see him – that was just such a hurt to me personally, because that’s my father’s legacy, is how he treated people. No matter whether they were Raiders or not, he cared about people. That’s one of the redeeming qualities of my father throughout his life. And I just thought it was totally unfair for them to portray it that way,” added Mark.

While he said that scene “hurt” him, Mark added, “All the other crap, I could take all that (stuff). I don’t care if they talk about my haircuts and stuff like that. I don’t know what it had to do with anything, but I don’t mind that.  They can do that to me. But to do that to somebody that’s passed away, that can’t defend himself, is totally unfair. And I’m sure that there’s gonna be thousands of people that could contradict what that clip portrayed my father as.”

Mark added that the portrayal of his mother Carol Davis (played by Sienna Miller) and John’s wife Virginia Madden (Kathryn Hahn) is “all made up stuff too,” conceding, “But there are some, you know, elements of them in that. I will say that, but not much.”

Co-written by Russell and Cambron Clark, Madden charts John Madden’s career from Raiders coach to TV broadcaster to the namesake of the Madden NFL video game franchise.

Movement can change how quickly you learn from the same visual clues

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In a virtual reality study, swinging a lightsaber or throwing a punch helped volunteers learn visual patterns that required combining two features.

In the textbook account, learning happens in the head, and the hands do nothing more than report the answer.

Volunteers learned to sort striped discs more accurately when they answered by swinging a virtual lightsaber or punching a virtual target.

The advantage appeared when they had to judge the angle and thickness of the stripes together.

In the punching experiment, volunteers kept their lead when they switched to button presses immediately after training.

For sorting tasks that could be solved with explicit rules, bigger movements gave volunteers no clear advantage.

Luke Rosedahl is an assistant professor of biomedical engineering in Florida Atlantic University‘s College of Engineering and Computer Science.

Professor Rosedahl conducted the experiments at Brown University with Yuka Sasaki and Takeo Watanabe.

The 160 participants were young adults recruited around Brown, sorting striped discs in a headset, so how far the result reaches outside a lab is still open.

“We found that larger movements substantially improved learning when people had to recognize patterns by integrating multiple features, but provided no meaningful benefit when people learned categories using explicit rules,” Rosedahl said.

“Even more intriguing, the benefit occurred whether participants swung at the target or punched a separate response box. The movement itself appeared to matter, not whether it directly interacted with what they were learning.”

Some categories come with a rule. If it has whiskers and meows, it’s a cat. Psychologists call these rule-based categories, and people learn them by testing hypotheses, which is thought to lean on the front of the brain.

Other categories can’t be put into words. A radiologist’s sense that a lesion looks aggressive comes from weighing several features at once, and so does a driver’s read on whether a road is icy.

People learn these by comparing each new case with ones they’ve seen.

That kind of learning is thought to run through the striatum, a deep brain structure tied to movement and habits.

Both kinds were tested with the same material: gray discs striped at different angles and thicknesses, shown one at a time in a virtual reality hallway.

For the rule-based version, thickness and angle could be judged separately. For the other version, the dividing line ran diagonally through both, so no single feature gave the answer.

In the first experiment, 80 people each sorted 600 discs into two groups over 12 blocks, with the disc flashing green or red after every answer. Half answered by pulling a controller trigger.

The other half swung a virtual lightsaber and hit the disc.

On the hard, no-rule categories, the swingers pulled ahead by the second block and stayed ahead for most of the session.

On the rule-based categories, the swingers did very slightly worse, by an amount that could have been chance.

A swing takes longer than a button press, and it touches the thing being judged. Either could have been the real cause.

In a second experiment with 80 new volunteers, some punched a virtual response box separate from the disc, while others pulled a controller trigger.

Every disc stayed on screen for 2.5 seconds, however quickly they answered.

The rule-based task was also made harder, with two rules to discover instead of one, in case the first version had simply been too easy to show a difference.

Punching still helped on the no-rule categories and still did nothing for the rule-based ones. Slower answers went with worse accuracy, not better, so taking extra time wasn’t the reason.

In the final 100 training trials of the task that required combining both features, volunteers who punched got 90.7% of answers right, compared with 81.0% for those who pressed a button.

Then everyone switched. The punchers moved to buttons and scored 89.5%, almost unchanged. The button group moved to punching and scored 83.1%.

Whatever the big movements had built was still there when the movement stopped, which points to better learning rather than a boost at the moment of answering.

The authors can’t say what the arm is doing for the brain, and they said so. They offered two possibilities.

One possibility is that more effortful movements increase dopamine release in the striatum, which could help reinforce learning.

Effortful actions have also been shown to steer attention toward the features that matter. And movement has helped in other corners of learning, the authors wrote, such as writing by hand to learn to recognize letters.

The selectivity is the clue. If swinging had simply made people more engaged, Rosedahl’s team wrote, both kinds of categories should have improved.

The benefit appeared only when people had to combine visual features, a kind of learning thought to involve motor circuits.

The result also raises a question about decades of earlier work, nearly all of it done with button presses.

Those studies found that no-rule categories are learned slowly and that written instructions on how to perform them don’t help.

The authors suggested that the buttons themselves may have been holding that learning back.

The team didn’t record which hand participants favored, and controllers were handed out in no particular order, so the role of handedness and motor experience is unmeasured.

Other category types, such as rules with exceptions, may respond differently. And everything happened with participants seated and a headset on.

The team wrote that the next studies should test the effect in real training, in fields such as radiology and aviation.

There, the judgments that can’t be put into words are the ones that take years to learn. Rosedahl framed the stakes in one line.

“Incorporating movement could offer a simple way to improve learning in situations where conventional approaches fall short,” he said.

Whether a bigger swing of the arm can speed up a radiologist’s eye, and not only a student’s sorting of striped discs, is the test that comes next.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

It was an erratic half for Allen, who went into the break 15-of-30 for 173 yards, but a two-touchdown lead attests to the difference between the overall quality of the two teams on the field in Buffalo.

Augsburg homegrown talent Tim Schnitzer seizes opportunity in vital Bundesliga win

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Eighteen-year-old FC Augsburg academy prospect Tim Schnitzer enjoyed an unforgettable afternoon on Sunday, making his first Bundesliga start in his hometown club’s 3-1 victory over Mainz 05. A defensive injury crisis forced head coach Manuel Baum to deploy the youngster in an unfamiliar role, but Schnitzer delivered an impressively composed performance that earned special praise from his trainer.

Speaking to Get German Football News and other journalists in the mixed zone after the match, Schnitzer called it a “dream come true to start where I used to be a ball boy”. The Augsburg native admitted to some nervousness ahead of his starting XI debut. Schnitzer revealed that he had slept less than usual the night before and even awoken before his alarm clock.

“I was definitely a little nervous,” Schnitzer conceded.

The Germany youth international nevertheless settled into his role on the left side of Augsburg’s back-three relatively quickly, displaying maturity and confidence in possession. Schnitzer won 70 percent of his ground challenges and connected with 89 percent of his passes, according to Opta data.

GGFN considered Schnitzer and the rest of the makeshift back-three (consisting of Schnitzer’s fellow out-of-position player Yannik Keitel and young Man City loanee Juma Bah) worthy of above average marks in our player ratings piece.

“When you seize your chance, you become more and more integrated into the team,” Schnitzer remarked in the mixed zone.

Augsburg’s defensive personnel shortage left Baum with little choice but to turn to the 18-year-old, who ordinarily operates as a defensive midfielder. Chrislain Matsima, Jeffrey Gouweleeuw, Noahkai Banks, and Calvin Brackelmann were all unavailable, leaving the returning Juma Bah as the only other natural central defender available to the FCA trainer.

“We were missing four central defenders and were still able to field a back-three,” Baum remarked at the post-match press conference. “And I believe we were able to implement something that we always aim for as a club. Namely, to integrate young players in at the right moment. And we witnessed that with Tim Schnitzer, who delivered a fabulous performance in his first game and the team really helped him out with that.”

“A huge compliment to the whole team,” Baum continued near the end of his opening press conference statement. “And let’s single out Tim again. He’s from our own academy and has been with us since the U9s. I think he grew up just a couple of blocks away from the academy. I think we can be very happy about the result, the team, and the next homegrown talent who was able to start today.”

Schnitzer’s first Bundesliga start represents another significant milestone in a development pathway that Get German Football News has followed closely since the summer. The Augsburg-born midfielder made his senior debut against RB Leipzig last March before spending the summer attempting to establish himself as a genuine first-team option. Schnitzer’s versatility emerged as one of his most promising qualities, with the youngster capable of operating as a defensive midfielder, central defender, or No. 8.

Baum had already identified Schnitzer as one of Augsburg’s most promising academy graduates in August, even mentioning the teenager among several FCA players he hoped might eventually attract Germany senior national-team consideration. Our 2026/27 Augsburg tactical preview also highlighted Schnitzer’s encouraging preseason performances, including his goal against Bournemouth, and suggested that the youngster should have opportunities to collect meaningful Bundesliga minutes.

Sunday’s injury-enforced selection certainly provided one such opportunity. Schnitzer responded by completing the entire 90 minutes and helping Augsburg end their seven-match winless streak. Whether the teenager retains his place once Augsburg’s established defenders return remains to be seen. Baum nevertheless has another option at his disposal, and the youngster who once served as a ball boy at the WWK Arena has taken another important step toward becoming a Bundesliga regular.

Amorim: ‘Milan pace did damage against brave Frosinone’

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ruben Amorim explains how he made the most of ‘brave’ Frosinone’s approach to run riot in the spaces and focus on the ‘pace’ in his Milan side for a 5-1 win. ‘The team believe in the plan.’

It was a morale-boosting performance from the Rossoneri against a Frosinone side who changed tactics to 3-4-3 with a False 9 for this trip to San Siro.

It left massive gaps in defence that Milan took full advantage of with a counter-attacking masterclass, as after Gabriele Calvani’s own goal, Goncalo Ramos, Ardon Jashari, Alphadjo Cisse and Christian Pulisic ran riot.

The only negative was the Patrizio Masini goal to temporarily make it 2-1 going into the half-time break, while Goncalo Ramos went off with a muscular twinge.

“The main thing is that we have a plan in the game, and the team believe in the plan,” Amorim told DAZN Italia.

“You know Frosinone as well as I do, they are all about full pressure, I understood that with a lot of points coming to San Siro, they would be really brave. With the pace we have with Rabiot, Moreira, Pulisic and Ramos, we could make transitions.

“We changed a little bit the way we play the game because of our opponents, and it was a good night for us.”

Goncalo Ramos scored his first goal since October 19, while Jashari hadn’t ever scored in the Milan jersey before this evening.

“We are building principles and understanding the game. We knew Frosinone are about marking man to man, that’s why Pulisic looked like he was in every position, because we knew they would follow us, and we could create a lot of danger with changing roles,” continued Amorim.

“It doesn’t matter who plays, every player in training needs to understand every position on the pitch, so that we can change the characteristics. There are so many things I saw in the game that we need to improve, but we stuck to the plan, and that is something I am pleased with.”

When Ramos went off with a calf twinge, why did Amorim turn to Cisse and not Francesco Camarda?

“You saw the game, we did not spend many minutes around the box, the space was behind. If we were controlling the game in a different way, Camarda was the right way to play, but Cisse is really good with pace. You saw the trouble he made for the opponent was not holding the ball, it was the runs behind, the passes, not a typical striker,” explained the coach.

“I didn’t notice it was Ramos who scored the goal, it was more important that it was 2-0, because Milan need lots of goals to control a game, and also Frosinone had to put more men forward at 2-0, so it opened up more spaces for us.”

Milan have won five of the last six Serie A matches, and consolidated fourth place in the hunt for Champions League qualification.

Hyatt Keeps Pepsi For 10 More Years At 700+ U.S. Hotels—Even Though Few Guests Want It

Hyatt has signed up for another 10 years with Pepsi, covering more than 700 U.S. hotels. After Marriott finally decided to switch to Coca-Cola, Hyatt is sticking with the beverage that prompts a question nobody wants to hear: Is Pepsi okay? No, it isn’t. I rarely drink soda. When I do, I don’t want to waste it on Pepsi. The renewal announced November 19, flagged by The Bulkhead Seat, doesn’t cover every Hyatt brand or every country. But the economics of choosing Pepsi make more sense once you realize that the person negotiating the contract isn’t the one that has to drink it. Which Hyatt Brands Are Covered, And Where Can You Get Coke? The agreement covers U.S. hotels in Hyatt’s Classics and Essentials portfolios. Translating Hyatt’s brand groupings: Portfolio Brands Classics Grand Hyatt, Hyatt Regency, Destination by Hyatt, Hyatt Centric, Hyatt Vacation Club, Hyatt Essentials Hyatt Place, Hyatt House, Hyatt Studios, Hyatt Select, Caption by Hyatt, Unscripted by Hyatt UrCove also belongs to Essentials, but its Chinese hotels aren’t covered by a U.S. agreement. The Luxury, Lifestyle and Inclusive Collection portfolios aren’t part of this deal. No one wants to show up at an Andaz or Park Hyatt and get told they can’t have a Coke. Alila, Thompson, The Standard, and Hyatt Ziva and Zilara are also outside the deal’s scope. Here are some hotels that have specifically listed Coke: Hotel Coca-Cola On The Menu Park Hyatt New York Room service lists Coke and Diet Coke Andaz Maui Meeting and event menus list Coke and Diet Coke Thompson Dallas Room service lists Coke and Diet Coke Alila Marea Beach Resort Encinitas Room service lists bottled Coke Pepsi Doesn’t Have To Be Your First Choice To Win The Business When I covered Marriott’s switch in April, Marriott told hotels that more than 70% of its guests favored the Coca-Cola portfolio. Hotels choose suppliers based on the total deal: product pricing, rebates, equipment, servicing, marketing support and sometimes direct payments. We can see how this works in a public contract. Fresno, California’s 2018 Pepsi agreement for its convention center offered a $20,000 annual sponsorship payment, a 25% vending commission, $3 rebates per case or gallon sold, bulk pricing and equipment maintenance in exchange for exclusive beverage rights. A hotel has a modestly captive audience. You’ve already booked the room, your conference is downstairs, or you’re sitting by the pool. You can buy a Coke elsewhere, but that takes effort. Some people accept Pepsi. Others choose something else. The hotel loses some beverage sales and creates some disappointment. But it still thinks it can come out ahead if its supplier prices and rebates are better and they aren’t seeing guests change their hotel bookings. In other words, the worse product can be more profitable to the hotel – or at least to Hyatt corporate. Here, Hyatt brings access to guests whose hotel decision usually turns on something other than soda. Pepsi doesn’t have to persuade all those guests that it tastes better (it doesn’t). Getting its products stocked on property can shift purchases without changing anyone’s preferences. PepsiCo also brings Gatorade, Starbucks ready-to-drink coffee, Quaker, Lay’s, Doritos, Muscle Milk and poppi. “Is Pepsi Okay?” Was Never A Compliment The question assumes you’ve requested something else and asks whether you’ll accept a substitute you didn’t want. Pepsi!” You could order what you wanted. You were getting what the restaurant had decided to sell. The Pepsi Challenge, New Coke, And What A Recipe Can’t Copy I remember the Pepsi Challenge as a kid. I remember how scared Coke seemed, and the debacle of New Coke. The explanation popularized by Malcolm Gladwell in Blink is that Pepsi’s sweetness does better in a sip test than when someone drinks a whole can. Maybe some people really did prefer the first sip of Pepsi and then found the rest too much. That never seemed right to me. The first sip of Coke is much better. Fortunately, there’s no rule you have to finish a soda. Coca-Cola’s history of New Coke says the company’s lead over its chief competitor had been slipping for 15 consecutive years. It introduced the reformulated drink on April 23, 1985, after taste tests involving nearly 200,000 consumers favored the new formula. The original came back just 79 days later. The tests hadn’t captured how people would react to having their Coke taken away. The company could produce a drink that performed better in a test and still make customers furious by replacing the one they loved. That helps explain why Coke’s business is so hard to recreate even if someone could duplicate the liquid. The secret formula gets the attention, but a competitor would also need to reproduce decades of habits, memories, advertising and confidence that the drink will be what you expect. A familiar brand changes the experience of drinking it. Then there’s the distribution system: bottlers, delivery routes, coolers, fountains and relationships with stores and restaurants. Being easy to find reinforces the habit of asking for Coke. You know there’s already a strong likelihood it’ll be there when you ask. I don’t drink enough soda to make defending Coke a daily activity. If I’m going to have soda, Dr. Brown’s cream is my first choice, followed by their black cherry. Hyatt can negotiate all the Pepsi it wants. I’ll just have water, thanks. Hotels choose suppliers based on the total deal: product pricing, rebates, equipment, servicing, marketing support and sometimes direct payments. We can see how this works in a public contract. Fresno, California’s 2018 Pepsi agreement for its convention center offered a $20,000 annual sponsorship payment, a 25% vending commission, $3 rebates per case or gallon sold, bulk pricing and equipment maintenance in exchange for exclusive beverage rights.

A hotel has a modestly captive audience. You’ve already booked the room, your conference is downstairs, or you’re sitting by the pool. You can buy a Coke elsewhere, but that takes effort.
