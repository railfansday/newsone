Bashpole Location Guide For Phantom Blade Zero

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In Phantom Blade Zero, Phantom Edges are among the most useful tools in Soul's repertoire. While they have their uses as weapons, some of them, like the Bashpole, are great for getting around and uncovering hidden secrets throughout the world.

Even though the Bashpole is required to access some spots in the game's early locations, you can't get it until much, much later. Luckily, being able to fast travel around the map makes going back to these spots easier, so it's easy to wonder just how to get this Phantom Edge. Here's where to get the Bashpole in Phantom Blade Zero.

To get the Bashpole, you'll need to get into the Mandate Encampment. This comes later than it might initially seem, since when Soul arrives in the Village of Lu, he mentions that the road splits: one way leads to the village, and the other leads to the Mandate Encampment.

This makes it seem like you can go to the Mandate Encampment first, but this isn't the case. Instead, you have to get through the whole Village of Lu, then go through Pangzhen until you beat Gu Wanyue, The Wailing Wraith. After you beat her, you can enter the Well of Wei Sanchuan, where you'll find the key to the Mandate Encampment.

Travel to the gate, go through and make your way through the Mandate Encampment until you reach the bell titled Beneath the Watchtower. Next to it is a gate sealed with a large chain, which you can break with the Cleave Axe. On the other side, you'll meet Wan Jun, The Coppermaul.

If you've got decision paralysis about which Phantom Edges to use, look no further.

Once you open the gate and face the behemoth that is Wan Jun, you'll have to protect yourself first and foremost. He deals less damage than his size might suggest, but luckily moves just as slowly as you might think. He's also clumsy, which opens the door for strikes that can whittle down his health and guard.

Most of his attacks will involve the Bashpole and can be parried or dodged. Because he's so slow, dodging is easier than with some other bosses, letting you build as much distance as you need. He can't close the gap quickly, so you have time to react.

Don't be afraid to get in a few quick strikes while he collects himself. He's slow and remains open for a while.

Watch out for double strikes with the Bashpole, as those are his fastest attacks. Unlike some bosses with heavy armor or shields like Wu Xuan, The Fallen General, you can break his guard with attacks, so you don't have to parry or dodge to break it. Stay patient as he goes through his attacks, and use the openings to strike when he falls over or stumbles.

Using the Bashpole is pretty simple, given that it's really just a giant metal melon on a stick. When equipped, you can hold your Phantom Edge button (R2 on PS5) to swing the Bashpole around your head three times, then slam it down onto the ground. If you release the button early, you'll slam it down before completing three rotations.

For traversal, you don't need to equip the Bashpole, just like the other traversal Phantom Edges. When you're near a weak floor made out of wooden boards, you can press R2 to slam the Bashpole into the ground, causing Soul to fall through into the level below.

Medicare Part D premiums projected to rise 50% in 2027 without plan switching: KFF" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Medicare beneficiaries who remain in their current stand-alone Part D drug plans could face sharply higher premiums in 2027 if they don’t switch to lower-cost plans during open enrollment, according to a KFF analysis.

The average monthly premium for stand-alone prescription drug plans, or PDPs, is projected to rise 50%, from $34 in 2026 to $51 in 2027, if current enrollees remain in the same plans, according to the analysis from KFF, an independent source for health policy research, polling and news.

The increase would widen the gap between stand-alone drug plans and Medicare Advantage plans that include prescription drug coverage. KFF projects that drug coverage through Medicare Advantage plans that offer Part D coverage, or MA-PDs, will average $9 a month in 2027, compared with $51 for PDPs. The average PDP premium would rise by $17, compared with an increase of less than $2 for MA-PDs.

For Medicare Part D prescription drug coverage, the average beneficiary has 28 Medicare Advantage prescription drug plans (MA-PDs) and 9 stand-alone prescription drug plans (PDPs) to choose from, with varying costs associated with these different options. KFF analysts examined premiums and cost-sharing requirements for Part D coverage in 2027 and how these costs have changed over time, based on Medicare plan landscape and benefits data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).

The $51 figure assumes current PDP enrollees remain in their existing plans. The actual average premium could be lower as beneficiaries switch to less expensive plans during open enrollment and new beneficiaries select coverage.

CMS projected a much smaller increase in the national average Part D premium, from $35 to $36. KFF noted that CMS, which based its estimate on September 2026 enrollment, did not explain its calculation methodology.

Among PDP enrollees who do not receive low-income subsidies, 76%, or 10.4 million people, would pay higher premiums if they keep their current plans. About 2.4 million would see their monthly premiums increase by more than $50.

That's a sharp increase from 2026, when only 38,000 enrollees faced monthly premium increases exceeding $50, according to KFF. The Trump administration’s decision to discontinue the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration after 2026 ended the additional premium subsidies that were provided to PDP sponsors and the cap on the maximum allowable monthly PDP premium increase, which was set at $50 for 2026, according to KFF analysts.

Another 4.1 million beneficiaries without low-income subsidies are enrolled in zero-premium PDPs this year, but no zero-premium PDPs will be available in 2027.

“With some plans increasing premiums substantially, the stakes are higher this year for people with Medicare stand-alone drug coverage if they sit on the sidelines during open enrollment,” Juliette Cubanski, a vice president and director of the Program on Medicare Policy at KFF, said in a statement. “There are relatively low-premium PDP options, but people facing large premium increases need to take action if they want to avoid paying significantly more.”

According to KFF's analysis, if PDP enrollees who are not receiving Part D Low-Income Subsidies (LIS) keep their current plan in 2027, more than half (55%), or 7.5 million people, will pay premiums of less than $20 per month, but nearly 30%, or 3.9 million people, will pay at least $100 per month.

Average monthly premiums for the eight national PDPs are projected to range from around $6 to $204 in 2027. Among national PDPs, the two lowest-premium plans are Centene’s Wellcare Value Script Rx, an enhanced PDP with an average premium of $6.50 for 2027, up from $5.80 in 2026,  and Wellcare Classic, a basic PDP with an average premium of $10.90 for 2027, up from $8.60 in 2026, according to the analysis. The national PDPs with the highest average monthly premiums are United’s AARP Medicare Rx Preferred at $204.50, up from $126.00 in 2026, and Humana Premier Rx Plan at $137.40, up from $119.60 in 2026.

The analysis also finds that most Part D enrollees will face a deductible of several hundred dollars for drug coverage in 2027, including 85% of MA-PD enrollees, up from 80% in 2026, and virtually all PDP enrollees, 99.8%, up from 96% in 2026, based on current enrollment. The average Part D deductible in 2027 will be $643 for PDP enrollees, up from $540 in 2026,  and $489 for MA-PD enrollees, rising from $360 in 2026. The standard Part D deductible in 2027 is $700, up from $615 in 2026.

Most Part D enrollees will also face coinsurance rather than fixed copayments for preferred-brand and non-preferred drugs, potentially making their out-of-pocket costs less predictable. Virtually all PDP enrollees will face median coinsurance of 25% for preferred brands and 35% for non-preferred drugs, according to KFF.

Humana and athenahealth are expanding electronic prior authorizations nationwide in a newly announced collaboration.

In June 2025, insurers nationwide—including Humana—agreed to take steps to reform prior authorization, including supporting electronic prior authorizations.

Low-Calorie Diet and Supervised Exercise May Reverse Early Onset Type 2 Diabetes

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

The RESET for Remission trial, presented at the 2026 European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting and published in The Lancet Regional Health Americas on October 1, found that more than half of adults with early onset type 2 diabetes entered remission after six months on a low calorie diet and supervised exercise program.

Early onset type 2 diabetes is when the condition is diagnosed before age 45.

Those with early onset type 2 diabetes may be at a higher risk of complications like long-term damage to the capillaries (microvascular complications), adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and earlier death.

Typical treatment for early onset type 2 diabetes is often a combination of lifestyle changes, like diet and exercise, and medications.

However, this new study suggests that a supervised low calorie diet and exercise program may help some people with early onset type 2 diabetes enter remission.

The RESET for Remission trial included 96 adults from England and Canada who were between the ages of 18 and 45, had early onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, and were not on insulin treatment.

The participants had an average age of 38 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 35.2 kg/m2. They were randomized into a control group that received usual care alone and the intervention group, which ate a low calorie diet and participated in supervised exercise programs.

During the first 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group followed an 800–900-calorie-per-day diet and completed twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance exercise sessions, along with one independent exercise session per week.

For weeks 13–24, they transitioned to a weight-maintenance diet and independent exercise.

Both groups were supervised by physicians, and the intervention group was also closely supervised by licensed dietitians.

The low calorie diet consisted of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrate, and 20% fat. Participants were also encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids per day and given access to a fiber-based laxative as needed.

In the first two weeks, all meals were replaced with specially formulated nutritional products (Optifast). Then, in weeks 3–12, the participants maintained the same calorie intake but had more flexibility in their food choices.

This second phase of the diet included one conventional meal per day, with daily intake targets that included protein, fruit, and vegetables. Typically, this meant 60 to 90 grams of protein from all sources combined, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

At the end of 24 weeks, 54% of the intervention group entered remission of type 2 diabetes, compared with only 4% in the control group. Researchers defined remission as an HbA1c level below 6.5% without glucose-lowering medication for at least 12 weeks.

“This study proves what most physicians have advocated for quite some time. Clearly, diet and exercise can work to improve or even resolve diabetes,” David Cutler, MD, board certified family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

Cutler also added that this study provides a clear example of how difficult it can be to enter diabetes remission with diet and exercise alone. He stated that the small number of participants in the study also makes him wonder if these findings can be applied to all populations.

Another limitation of this study, as Cutler pointed out, was that the strict dietary limits imposed on study participants would be very difficult for the average adult to adhere to.

The study also followed participants for only six months, so it’s unclear how many would remain in remission over the longer term.

“It is worth trying diet and exercise first because they can work to achieve meaningful weight loss and remission of diabetes,” he said.

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) notes that losing 5%–7% of your body weight can help reduce your risk of type 2 diabetes.

Early onset type 2 diabetes is a growing issue worldwide, coinciding with the rise of obesity.

Research from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) projects that the number of people in the United States under age 20 with type 2 diabetes could reach 220,000 by 2060. This is an increase of 700%.

GLP-1s May Help Lower Diabetes, Heart Risks Better Than Standard Prevention

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

GLP-1 drugs are more effective in helping prevent type 2 diabetes than the commonly prescribed medication metformin, according to a new study.

Researchers said GLP-1s such as Ozempic and Mounjaro were more likely to reduce blood sugar to healthy levels in people with prediabetes and obesity.

They added that people prescribed GLP-1 medications had a lower risk of progressing from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

The researchers noted that GLP-1 users also had a lower risk of death from any cause as well as fewer heart attacks, strokes, and heart failure events compared with metformin users.

“The great news with prediabetes is that it’s still possible to reverse the progression of the disease,” said Vinicius de Sousa Barzon Serra, MD, a study author and research fellow in cardiovascular medicine at the Mayo Clinic in Scottsdale, Arizona, in a press release. “This research may add to the case for considering GLP-1 medications earlier for certain patients, rather than waiting until they develop type 2 diabetes.”

Serra noted that metformin still remains an effective treatment for people with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

“This is less about replacing one medication with another and more about discussing additional options with specific patients,” he said.

The researchers presented their findings at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

David Cutler, MD, a family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the findings were “quite impressive.”

“It elevates the comfort of prescribing GLP-1 drugs as a front-line treatment,” he told Healthline.

Robert Eckel, MD, a former president of the American Heart Association who was likewise not involved in the study, agrees the research provides more options for patients and prescribers.

“GLP-1s provide another option for prevention, perhaps as an add-on therapy. However, costs and access to these medications are important factors,” said Eckel, an endocrinologist at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, in the press release.

For their study, the researchers analyzed the medical records of 256,000 adults in the United States with prediabetes and obesity. About 76% of the subjects were women, and the average age of the participants was 52.

About half of those studied were taking GLP-1 drugs with the active ingredient semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide, or dulaglutide.

The other half had been prescribed metformin, a medication used to help manage blood sugar levels in adults and some children with type 2 diabetes by decreasing glucose production in the liver, reducing glucose absorption from food, and improving the body’s insulin sensitivity.

The researchers reported that the people in the study who were taking GLP-1 drugs had a 1.5 times higher chance of reversing high blood sugar to healthy levels compared to people who were using metformin.

The researchers also noted that GLP-1 medication users had a 31% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes and a 41% lower risk of death from any cause.

They added that GLP-1 medications were associated with 15% fewer heart attacks, 20% fewer strokes, 25% fewer cases of heart failure, and 8% fewer cases of atrial fibrillation (AFib), a common type of irregular heartbeat.

The researchers cautioned that their study is observational and, therefore, cannot prove a cause-and-effect relationship. They also noted they relied on prescription records, so the frequency and dosage at which patients took their medication was not reflected.

In addition, the GLP-1 drugs were grouped together, so individual differences between medications were not considered.

Eckel said in the news release that the benefits for people taking GLP-1 drugs were probably mainly due to weight loss. Eckel noted that the research results open up a wide range of possibilities.

“This is an important finding because the GLP-1 receptor agonists are commonly used today and this is a population that in the transition from prediabetes to diabetes are at increasing risk for cardiovascular disease,” Eckel said in a taped interview alongside the study findings.

Cutler pointed out that several GLP-1 drugs also target blood sugar levels and insulin production. They could be effective in people with prediabetes who don’t have weight management issues.

Cutler noted that these people, however, might be reluctant to take GLP-1 drugs because they may not be experiencing symptoms of their prediabetes but could develop side effects with the medications.

“They would not be getting any immediate benefits,” Cutler said. He added that metformin, which was first used clinically in 1958, remains an effective alternative. “It’s been proven safe, effective, and it’s very cheap,” Cutler said.

It’s estimated that 40 million people in the United States have diabetes, with more than 90% of those cases being type 2 diabetes, a chronic condition characterized by high blood glucose levels that results from the body’s ineffective response to insulin or insufficient insulin production.

It’s also estimated that 115 million adults in the United States have prediabetes, a condition in which a person has higher-than-normal blood sugar levels but not high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes.

Around 5–10% of people with prediabetes develop diabetes every year. More broadly, up to 70% of people with prediabetes eventually develop diabetes.

Medication is sometimes prescribed for people with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes. Lifestyle changes can help reverse type 2 diabetes and prediabetes, as well as prevent the progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

Cutler said the lifestyle changes needed to prevent type 2 diabetes are pretty clear.

“The most important thing for people with prediabetes to know is they should lose weight, reduce their carbs, exercise daily and, if that doesn’t work, then consider medication,” he said.

Losing Weight in Your 50s May Protect Your Heart Health Later in Life

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Losing weight in your 50s, even a modest amount, may help protect your heart health later in life, according to new research.Adults who lost weight in their early 50s saw their blood pressure, “good” cholesterol and inflammation change at about the same rate as adults who stayed at a healthy weight, according to a study from researchers at University College London and the University of Glasgow.Adults who gained weight in their early 50s saw those same measures worsen faster by their early 60s, the researchers reported. The authors posted the study on a research-sharing website on September 4. Other scientists have not yet peer-reviewed the work.

Charis Bridger-Staatz, PhD, a senior research fellow at UCL, led the study. She told Healthline the main takeaway for adults over 50 was that “it’s not too late to try and do things that are going to improve your health.”Nearly half of U.S. adults ages 40 to 59 had obesity from 2021 to 2023, the highest rate of any adult age group, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Among all U.S. adults, around 31.7% were overweight, according to the CDC.

The findings can apply to Americans because British and U.S. adults share similar weight patterns, said Solveig Cunningham, PhD, a professor of global health at Emory University’s Rollins School of Public Health. Cunningham, who was not involved in the research, told Healthline that Americans tend to gain weight across adulthood.

“By the time you get into your 50s, close to the majority of Americans already have obesity. Many others have overweight,” she said.The World Health Organization (WHO) calls obesity a chronic disease. Healthcare professionals diagnose overweight and obesity using a person’s weight and height, or body mass index (BMI).

Bridger-Staatz said the team set out to learn who lost weight and kept it off before GLP-1 weight loss medications became available, and whether that weight loss improved health over time.

She said researchers had long assumed that “once people had become overweight or developed obesity, people didn’t really lose weight and certainly not sustained it.”The team also asked, she said, “can we try and say something positive about health that isn’t just this kind of like doom and gloom?”The researchers used the British 1958 National Child Development Study, which has followed about 17,500 people born during one week in 1958. Of those, 6,309 took part in a health check in their early 60s, according to the report.The team compared each person’s highest weight between ages 23 and 42 with their weight between ages 50 and 55. Researchers sorted people by whether they gained or lost more than 5% of their body weight and kept the change. For a 200-pound person, 5% equals 10 pounds.To track heart health, the team compared blood tests and blood pressure readings at ages 44 and 62 with those of people who maintained a healthy weight.“We know everyone’s heart health is kind of going to get a little bit worse over that time because you are aging and that’s expected,” Bridger-Staatz said. The question, she said, was, “Does it slow the rate at which people’s heart health is getting worse?”

Weight loss slowed the decline in heart health for people who were overweight and for people with obesity, according to the report.People who were overweight and lost weight had the strongest positive results. By age 62, their body fat-to-lean tissue ratio matched that of the healthy-weight group. The authors wrote that differences in body fat may partly explain why weight changes affected heart health.People with obesity who lost weight had less body fat at 62 than people in the other obesity groups, according to the report.Sudhakara Chennareddy, MD, prevention cardiologist in the Center for Cardiovascular Prevention, Metabolism and Lipids at Georgia Heart Institute, told Healthline those results are what he’d expect. He was not involved in the research.“Even losing 5% has beneficial effects on the cardiovascular side effects,” he said. Long-term blood sugar was the exception. For people with obesity who lost weight, it rose more than in the healthy-weight group, though the authors reported that the result was not statistically significant.

A 2025 study of more than 15,000 U.S. adults, published in Endocrinology, Diabetes & Metabolism, found that adults who had previously had obesity tended to have higher blood sugar than those who had never had it, even after their BMI dropped.

“It’s not the patient’s fault… We need to treat this one like any other chronic disease, like we treat hypertension or we treat diabetes,” Chennareddy said.Compared with the healthy-weight group, people who gained weight in their early 50s had faster increases in blood pressure, blood fats, and inflammation, and declines in “good” cholesterol. Obesity is a chronic metabolic disease that leads to inflammation in the body, which can raise the risk of heart attack and stroke and worsen cholesterol levels, Chennareddy said. “The more weight they gain, they develop prediabetes and diabetes and fatty liver disease as well,” he said.These changes were largest for people who developed obesity or already had obesity and gained more weight, the researchers reported. This group also saw faster increases in long-term blood sugar levels.About 37% of participants gained more than 5% of their body weight in their early 50s. Only about 6% lost weight and kept it off, according to the study.“To me, that’s sort of the stronger message,” Cunningham said of the findings. Her advice for adults in their 50s is to avoid gaining weight.

People with obesity who lost and then gained the weight back saw faster drops in “good” cholesterol and faster rises in inflammation and long-term blood sugar.

By age 62, they weighed slightly less, on average, than people whose obesity remained steady, according to the report’s data. They also carried more body fat and had more inflammation.Weight cycling can do more harm than steady weight, Chennareddy said. He gave the example of someone who gains 20 pounds, loses 30 and then gains 20 again. “That is even more detrimental,” he said.When people lose weight, the body slows its resting metabolism, he said. When the weight comes back, “most of the weight they gain is fat, not muscle,” he said. Even someone who loses and regains 5% to 7% of their body weight may end up with slightly less muscle and more fat, he said.Some research has shown that weight fluctuation is associated with a higher risk of high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease-related death.

The study’s weight changes took place between about 2008 and 2013, according to the study’s timeline.

Wegovy, a GLP-1 medication for weight management, launched in the United Kingdom in September 2023, according to the UK Department of Health and Social Care.Cunningham said the weight-cycling findings raised questions for people who take GLP-1 medications. She said drugmakers’ own data show that “once you get off of those, your weight is going to go right back to where it was before, possibly even worse.”That could create the same up-and-down pattern, Cunningham said. She added that researchers don’t yet know “what happens in the really long term” for people who take these medications.

The study showed links between weight changes and heart health and cannot prove that weight changes caused the results, according to the authors. They wrote that the small number of people who lost weight made those results less precise.Bridger-Staatz also said the team did not look at why or how people lost weight.

Cunningham said adults lose muscle and bone mass with age, and that the loss is especially pronounced for women in their 50s and older. “Maintaining muscles and maintaining bones is so, so important,” she said.Staying active helps people keep muscle, even without weight loss, Cunningham said. “Even a little bit of activity…just keep moving,” she said.GLP-1 medications work best as part of a “comprehensive plan,” Chennareddy said. Leading Medical Groups Team Up to Redefine Female Heart Health

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A quartet of medical organizations has published a comprehensive set of reports calling for a coordinated plan of action to improve heart healthcare for women throughout their lifetimes.

The reports detail how cardiovascular health issues can be prevented, monitored, and treated from the early years of menstruation to peak fertility age to pregnancy to perimenopause to postmenopause stages.

The report authors call for healthcare professionals to identify heart health risks early in girls and women and to coordinate care across medical specialties.

They stress that females have specific biological factors that increase their risks of cardiovascular disease. Those components include hormonal and emotional factors.

The authors note that heart disease is the number one cause of death for females in the United States, as well as the number one cause of death during pregnancy. Yet, only 44% of females are fully aware of the dangers they face from heart disease.

The organizations issuing the reports are the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

The groups presented their findings in a trio of reports published in the AHA’s flagship journal, Circulation on October 26.

“Women experience their healthcare across clinical specialties, but it often is fragmented or disconnected,” said Sadiya Khan, MD, the chair of the collaborative writing group for the reports and a professor of cardiovascular epidemiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, in a news release.

“Specifically, these reports connect the dots for clinicians, for patients, and across health domains so that care is holistic and begins in adolescence, recognizing that women’s heart health is uniquely shaped by biological transitions not experienced by men — periods, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause.”

Experts not involved in the reports told Healthline the updates were a long time coming.

Nicole Weinberg, MD, a cardiologist at Pacific Heart Institute’s Women’s Heart Center in Santa Monica, California, said the reports present a positive framework for women’s health.

“Women have unique cardiovascular risk factors related to pregnancy and menopause, and certain traditional risk factors, including diabetes, may increase their risk of heart disease more significantly than they do in men,” noted Nissi Suppogu, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Women’s Heart Center at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“They are a call to action for increasing education, research, and coordinated clinical care for women’s heart health,” Shah said.

The organizations spelled out their recommendations in three reports.

The first report is a presidential advisory that lays out a “female-specific life course approach to the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that “girls and women uniquely experience fragmented care” across medical fields, including pediatrics, obstetrics, and gynecology.

The authors call for a “unified life course approach that addresses the female-specific interconnectedness of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health.”

This report breaks down the life cycle for girls and women into five stages.

“Emerging data suggest that unique biologic transitions or life stages experienced by girls and women are linked with changes in [cardiovascular disease] risk, which may be due in part to physiologic changes in sex hormones,” the authors wrote.

Weinberg said this life-stage breakdown is important, especially for risk reduction.

She said issues with menstruation during the teen years may be a harbinger of heart health problems later in life. The same is true for health issues during pregnancy, especially high blood pressure or high blood sugar levels.

“We need to be aware of these heart disease risks so we’re not caught by surprise 10 to 20 years down the road,” Weinberg said.

The second report is a science advisory that recommends a comprehensive plan for women’s cardiovascular care.

In it, the authors call for a system that “integrates assessment of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across life stages.”

They propose a “national infrastructure” of centers that would coordinate clinical care, education, training, research, and community engagement around women’s heart health.

“These centers can reduce fragmentation, enhance earlier risk identification, and expand opportunities to increase representation of women in clinical research,” the authors wrote. “In doing so, they offer a bold opportunity for a scalable and equitable model to improve cardiovascular health for all women.”

To illustrate their point, the authors noted that only about 60% of women with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy in the United States have a follow-up visit with a healthcare professional about their condition within the first 6 months after giving birth.

“Given that pregnancy is a cardiovascular stress test and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are risk-enhancing factors for long-term [cardiovascular disease] risk, this represents a major missed opportunity to address cardiovascular risk,” the authors wrote.

“Pregnancy and the postpartum period are important windows into cardiovascular health throughout the life course,” said William Grobman, MD, the vice-chair of the writing group for these reports and a professor of obstetrics and gynecology at The Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island, in the press release.
