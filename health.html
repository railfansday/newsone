An Ancient Virus May Hold the Secret to What Makes the Human Brain Different

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Could a virus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago help explain how the human brain became different?

Researchers at Lund University have discovered that ancient viral DNA embedded in the chimpanzee genome silences a gene involved in early human brain development. Their study, published in Science Advances, found that the gene, LINC00662, remains active in human brain organoids, where it helps support the development of nerve cells and the connections between them.

The finding offers a way to explore how humans and chimpanzees developed different biological traits despite sharing much of their genetic makeup. Evolution can change how existing genes are used, as well as which genes a species carries.

“The virus we investigated specifically affected chimpanzees. Yet humans and chimpanzees have largely the same genetic makeup. That is why it is important to understand the differences in how genes are regulated. Our findings suggest that one gene, LINC00662, plays a significant role in early human brain development,” says Johan Jakobsson, professor of molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The genetic remnant belongs to PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus. Its DNA became embedded in the chimpanzee genome during an ancient infection and has been inherited ever since. The researchers found copies of the viral sequence at numerous locations throughout chimpanzee DNA.

Those sequences are systematically shut down through methylation, a chemical process that helps regulate gene activity. At the LINC00662 location, the inherited viral insertion also keeps the neighboring gene silent.

To examine this difference during brain development, the team grew brain organoids from human and chimpanzee stem cells. These miniature, three-dimensional structures resemble brain tissue and reproduce some features of its early development.

“The gene was strongly expressed in the human organoids, but not at all in the chimpanzee organoids,” says Patricia Gerdes, a postdoctoral researcher in molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The researchers then tested whether the viral sequence caused the shutdown. Using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing tool, they removed it from chimpanzee cells. LINC00662 became active again.

“This shows that it is in fact the viral insertion that is responsible for the gene being switched off in chimpanzees,” says Patricia Gerdes.

A separate experiment explored the gene’s role in humans. When the team used CRISPR to silence LINC00662 in human brain organoids, genes involved in nerve cell development and the formation of connections between those cells became less active.

For Jakobsson, the results show how an infection can leave a lasting genetic change across a population, while helping explain how gene regulation can protect the brain or contribute to damage. The team will next investigate whether other viral remnants influenced brain evolution and whether such sequences are linked to brain diseases.

Is Your Sodium Intake Impacting Your Hiking Performance? A Dietitian Explains Why It Matters.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Take a look at the nutrition label on a freeze-dried backpacking meal, and you might be surprised by how much sodium it contains. Some meals pack more than 1,500 mg into a single pouch, roughly two-thirds of the recommended daily limit of 2,300 mg for most adults. That’s a lot for one dinner, but when you’re spending hours sweating your way up steep climbs, you’re also losing sodium along with water.

Sodium helps regulate fluid balance and supports normal nerve and muscle function. Yet there’s a contradiction in how you might think about it on the trail: you question the sodium in your freeze-dried dinner while adding electrolyte powder to your water bottle and snacking on pretzels, jerky, and cheese.

So, are you consuming too much sodium or not enough? The answer depends on what’s already in your food, how much you’re sweating, and how you’re replacing fluids.

You can pack nearly identical amounts of food and end up with dramatically different sodium intakes. I compared two realistic days of backpacking food, each containing commercial meals, protein bars, snacks, and other familiar trail staples. Both provide roughly 3,150 calories and weigh about 23 ounces dry, yet one contains 5,465 mg of sodium while the other contains just 1,975 mg.

That’s nearly 3,500 mg of difference before you add a single electrolyte packet. Some of the variation comes down to individual products: Mountain House Beef Lasagna contains 1,180 mg of sodium, compared with 340 mg in Good To-Go Pad Thai. Even switching pretzel flavors or protein bar brands can make a noticeable difference. Neither food bag is automatically better, but the comparison shows why it’s worth checking what you’re already carrying before deciding to supplement.

Health experts recommend limiting sodium for a reason. Consistently consuming more than your body needs can raise blood pressure, increasing your risk of heart disease and stroke over time. A particularly salty meal can also leave you thirsty or temporarily bloated as your body regulates its fluid balance. If you’ve been advised to limit sodium because of high blood pressure, kidney disease, or another medical condition, those recommendations still matter when you’re backpacking.

But eating a high-sodium diet day after day isn’t necessarily the same as eating salty meals during a strenuous backpacking trip. When you’re sweating heavily, you lose sodium you wouldn’t lose while sitting at a desk. That doesn’t mean every milligram of sodium in your food is necessary, or that you should add electrolytes without thinking about what you’re already eating. It simply means your activity and conditions matter when interpreting those numbers on the label.

Even if you’re hiking alongside someone on the same trail, you may be losing very different amounts of sodium. Sweat losses depend on two things: how much you sweat and how concentrated that sweat is. Research has found sweat sodium concentrations ranging from roughly 230 to 1,600 mg per liter. Temperature, exertion, clothing, heat acclimatization, and individual physiology all affect your losses, which means mileage alone tells you very little about how much sodium you’ve lost.

Here’s another wrinkle: losing 800 mg of sodium through sweat doesn’t automatically mean you need to consume 800 mg to replace it. Your body regulates sodium and water balance, and research suggests targeted sodium replacement matters more under certain combinations of prolonged exercise, substantial sweat losses, and fluid replacement. That’s why taking an electrolyte packet every hour isn’t necessarily appropriate. A packet doesn’t account for how much you’re sweating, how much you’re drinking, or how much sodium you’re getting from food.

Sodium doesn’t have to come from an electrolyte supplement to count. Salty snacks, meals, and electrolyte drinks all contribute to your intake, although when you consume that sodium can matter during long, sweaty days. Imagine spending ten hours hiking in warm weather, sweating heavily and drinking water but eating very little. At camp, you prepare a freeze-dried dinner containing 1,500 mg of sodium. That sodium helps replace losses and retain fluids during recovery, but it wasn’t available during the preceding hours of hiking.

Eating salty snacks throughout a prolonged, sweaty hike provides some sodium along the way instead of waiting until dinner. That doesn’t mean you need electrolyte powder in every water bottle. If you’re regularly eating pretzels, cheese, jerky, or other salty foods, you’re already consuming sodium during the day. On the other hand, if your trail menu contains relatively little sodium or you struggle to eat while hiking, an electrolyte drink may be a convenient alternative. What matters isn’t whether the sodium comes from a packet or a snack, but whether you’re adding something your food isn’t already providing.

It’s easy to assume that replacing the sodium you lose through sweat will protect you from exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH), a potentially dangerous drop in blood sodium. But according to the Wilderness Medical Society, most cases occur primarily because you drink too much fluid relative to your losses, often combined with your body’s tendency to retain water during prolonged exercise. Sweat sodium losses can contribute under some circumstances, but taking electrolyte supplements won’t prevent hyponatremia if you’re overdrinking.

The guidelines recommend avoiding overdrinking, generally by drinking according to thirst or using fluid needs you’ve estimated from experience in similar conditions. They also recommend having salty foods or drinks available during long, hot activities. Headaches, fatigue, nausea, and muscle cramps aren’t reliable indicators that you need more sodium because they can have several causes. If you or a hiking partner develops worsening confusion, repeated vomiting, seizures, or collapses during or after prolonged exertion, seek urgent medical help rather than assuming more water or electrolytes will solve the problem.

There’s no simple answer. Your sweat losses vary with the conditions and your individual physiology. Even knowing exactly how much sodium you lose wouldn’t tell you how much to replace. For context, 1,500 mg per day is considered an adequate intake for most adults under ordinary conditions, while 2,300 mg is the general recommended daily limit. Neither is a backpacking-specific target. A food bag containing 5,465 mg isn’t automatically excessive for a strenuous hiking day, just as one containing 1,975 mg isn’t necessarily inadequate. And since research hasn’t consistently shown that extra sodium improves endurance performance, there’s little reason to chase a precise replacement target.

The bottom line? Hike your own hike. If you’re curious about your sodium intake, track what you eat and drink, the conditions you’re hiking in, and how you feel during and afterward. You might notice patterns that help you plan future trips, without getting caught up in replacing every milligram of sodium you sweat out.

Every occupation can take a toll on workers’ mental health, but some professions are considered more stressful than others due to the nature of work-related exposures. To date, policing remains one of the most stressful professions. Hidden behind every badge is a law enforcement professional trying their very best to navigate a steady, and at times relentless, stream of psychological and emotional stress that makes resilience a key attribute that is essential for survival where suicide rates exceed those of the average citizen. Most, if not all, law enforcement officers will, at some point in their careers, be faced with stressful, traumatic encounters that quite literally become embedded in their psyche, and if not addressed, will likely worsen.

This topic has been extensively studied, but most studies focus exclusively on male police officers; yet, emerging research suggests that female officers, in particular, often experience these challenges in distinct and deeply personal ways that differ from the experiences of male officers. Exploring these differences can help agencies design wellness initiatives that truly support all who serve by recognizing and adjusting for gender differences. Although the percentage of female officers pursuing a career in law enforcement has been steadily increasing, female officers only comprise 13.8% of all US law enforcement officers; that is likely why their unique circumstances have been largely understudied and, for the most part, overlooked within the research and practitioner literature (Statista, 2024).

Law enforcement officers are repeatedly exposed to traumatic incidents, most of which are attributed to operational stress. Operational stress is commonly tied to the trauma that police officers encounter in the course of their work, such as responding to homicide scenes, domestic and sexual assaults, fatal collisions, suicides, and other acts of violence that can have a profound influence on officers, leading to anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, PTSD, and in some extreme cases, suicide (Bishopp et al., 2019). To compound matters, today’s law enforcement officers are also faced with staff shortages, mandatory overtime, erratic shifts, insufficient pay, public scrutiny, and at times, unsupportive, ineffective leadership. In fact, Violanti et al. (2017) found that female police officers report a roughly 37% higher prevalence of lack of support from supervisors compared to male officers.

The paucity of research to date suggests that female officers are more likely to internalize the emotional impact of stressors that lead to burnout when compared to male officers (Rabichuk, Williamson, & Frankel, 2025). In law enforcement, female officers often place significant pressure on themselves. There is a constant burden to maintain a professional image and avoid appearing vulnerable or weak. In response, female officers tend to suppress emotions rather than seek help because they are persistently compelled to prove their competence in a historically male-dominated field, contributing to the high likelihood of chronic stress and burnout.

Female officers are challenged with unique workplace stressors that extend beyond the basic demands of police work, some of which male officers cannot relate to, and as a result, can adversely affect performance, well-being, retention, and career advancement. Some of these stressors include, but are not limited to, pregnancy, postpartum recovery, menopause, and the demands of shift work. It should come as no surprise that contemporary research suggests that police leaders who understand these realities are better positioned to implement policies that support female officer wellness while strengthening organizational effectiveness.

Pregnancy presents unique challenges for women in law enforcement, particularly when agency policies regarding modified assignments and accommodations vary widely. Research suggests female officers may experience elevated reproductive health risks, including miscarriage, highlighting the need for evidence-based workplace policies, reproductive health education, flexible assignments, and supportive supervision.

Illias, Demou, and Riach (2025) emphasized that returning to work after childbirth presents another set of challenges unique to female law enforcement officers. Recovery from childbirth, sleep disruptions, breastfeeding, and childcare responsibilities often lead to concerns about job security and future career advancement. Female officers who received little to no agency support upon return to work experienced significantly higher rates of depression and anxiety than those who had the support of their agencies (Illias et al., 2025). It is therefore recommended that police leaders create family-friendly policies, comprehensive return-to-work policies and programs, breastfeeding spaces, and other supportive accommodations.

This is undoubtedly one of the least studied issues affecting female police officers. Hofman and Spiegel (2026) conducted a qualitative study in which the study’s participants (female police officers) reported concerns about stigma, perceptions of reduced competence, and a lack of understanding from leadership. For police leadership, menopause should be viewed as a workforce wellness issue as opposed to a personal matter (Hofman & Spiegel, 2026). Educating and training supervisors and fellow officers normalizes conversations about women’s health that are typically shunned.

Understanding the realities that female officers face throughout their policing careers is not simply a gender-equity issue, but rather an organizational issue because agencies that recognize and respond to these challenges are more likely than not to retain female officers, improve wellness outcomes, and strengthen modern-day law enforcement agencies.

Plant-Based Milk Alternatives May Not Give You Enough Iodine. Here’s Why That Matters

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Replacing dairy with plant-based alternatives (PBAs), even partially, could substantially lower iodine intake, especially if those alternatives aren’t fortified, according to a recent study published in the Lancet Planetary Health.

The United Kingdom-based researchers found that only 12% of UK plant-based milk, yogurt, and cheese products analyzed were fortified with iodine. This compares with 61% for calcium and 46% for vitamin B12.

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) provides guidance on the nutritional content of PBAs, but fortification with iodine is voluntary.

The study found that replacing as little as 20% of dairy intake with plant-based alternatives could increase the risk of inadequate iodine intake, particularly among adolescent girls and women of reproductive age.

“People often worry about a decline in calcium intake when they replace some of their dairy with plant-based alternatives. As a result, iodine is frequently overlooked. This study serves as a valuable reminder of iodine’s significance as a nutrient,” said Victoria Finn, MD, board certified endocrinologist at Medical Offices of Manhattan, who wasn’t involved in the study.

The study included analysis of UK plant-based milk, cheese, and yogurt products, as well as dietary data from 3,500 people.

The researchers used statistical modeling to predict how swapping dairy for PBAs might affect iodine and other nutrients.

The people who are most vulnerable to iodine inadequacy are adolescent females and people at reproductive age, especially those who are pregnant or who plan to become pregnant.

The researchers estimated that replacing 20% to 35% of dairy intake with PBAs would increase the proportion of people with inadequate iodine intake:

When the researchers modeled the replacement of all dairy products with PBAs, the proportion of adolescent females with inadequate iodine intake rose to 80%. Among females ages 19 to 49, that proportion remained relatively stable as replacement rates increased.

It is important to keep in mind that the study used dietary modeling rather than direct measurement of iodine status.

Iodine is important for thyroid hormone production, metabolism, and both fetal and child brain development.

“Your thyroid gland requires iodine to produce thyroid hormones, which play a crucial role in regulating metabolism and supporting normal growth and development,” said Finn.

She added that if you consistently consume too little iodine over an extended period, the thyroid gland may enlarge and eventually struggle to produce enough hormones. This condition is known as hypothyroidism, which can lead to various symptoms such as lethargy, sleepiness, weight gain, dry skin, and more.

“Hypothyroidism can be particularly dangerous during pregnancy, as the baby’s brain, nervous system, and organs rely heavily on the mother’s thyroid hormone during the early stages of development,” Finn added.

In the United Kingdom, dairy is a major iodine source due to cattle feed supplementation, not because dairy is naturally high in iodine.

In the United States, dairy is also an important source of iodine for the same reasons.

However, the risk profile for iodine deficiency in the U.S. may differ from the U.K., as iodized salt is also a significant source of iodine and is widely available in the U.S., but not in the U.K.

According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the recommended daily intake of iodine depends on your age.

Plant-based or plant-forward diets have grown in popularity, in part due to the health benefits they offer. For example, consuming less meat can reduce your risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol.

However, as this study suggests, it may be possible to reduce your iodine intake when transitioning to a plant-based diet. There are ways you can protect your iodine intake when choosing a more plant-forward diet, though.

Check the labels on plant-based dairy alternatives to find those fortified with iodine. You can also read the nutrition label. Iodine may be listed as potassium iodide or potassium iodate.

Seafood, eggs, and iodized salt are all good sources of iodine.

You can also discuss iodine supplements with your healthcare professional.

“Iodine supplements should be taken with caution, especially without checking your thyroid levels,” said Finn. “Additionally, be cautious with kelp supplements, as their iodine content can vary, and excessive iodine intake can disrupt thyroid function.”

CHICAGO — Sudden cardiac arrest statistics are chilling. In the United States, 350,000 people’s heartbeats, breathing, pulse, and blood circulation will all halt outside hospitals this year, seemingly out of the blue. These are not heart attacks. Only 1 in 10 people will survive them.

CPR and defibrillation can save their lives (and brains), but that depends on bystanders and emergency response systems being both nearby and prepared to step in — immediately. Survival chances drop by 10% for every minute that goes by without CPR.

Hints from observational studies suggested better, more comprehensive community training can help, which means educating 911 dispatchers, first responders, and potential bystanders. But results from a randomized controlled trial presented Sunday at the American Heart Association’s scientific sessions and published in the New England Journal of Medicine on Sunday show that approach has not worked, or at least not yet. But examples in Denmark may point the way forward.

“We performed an efficient, large, and high-quality trial engaging large segments of the community in emergency response, and in spite of this, survival remained low and unchanged,” Carolina Malta Hansen, the study’s lead author, said at a media briefing. “A multifaceted community and EMS implementation strategy did not improve survival with a favorable neurologic outcome for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. However, in the final year, there appeared to be an improvement in bystanders.”

Starting in 2021, researchers randomly assigned participants in 60 counties in North Carolina to receive intensive education on cardiac arrest intervention or to continue with standard practice. The intervention entailed training 911 dispatchers to improve their rapid recognition of cardiac arrest and better guide bystanders through CPR. First responders were educated on the use of automated external defibrillators before emergency services arrived. Community members in workplaces, religious groups, government offices, and other settings received training in CPR.

About 8.5 million people live in these counties. During the study’s five years, 22,459 adult residents experienced cardiac arrest outside the hospital. Their median age was 64; 37% were women, 33% were Black; and 29% lived in rural areas.

The numbers were split this way: 11,615 people experienced out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in counties that received intensive education, and 10,844 people had out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in communities that did not receive the intervention.

Amrish Deshmukh, a cardiac electrophysiologist who treats patients with heart rhythm disorders at the University of Michigan Health Frankel Cardiovascular Center, called the study results disappointing, but not surprising.

“Many interventions that have been studied in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and in post-arrest care unfortunately have not been shown to improve survival or neurologic outcomes,” he told STAT in an email. He was not involved in the new trial. “Prior studies have shown that mobile phone alerts, volunteer training, and increased automated external defibrillator (AEDs) availability can increase the performance of CPR and use of AEDs, but not all have shown improvements in survival.”

And yet, study author Malta Hansen, a cardiologist and clinical research associate professor at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital and Copenhagen EMS in Denmark, is hopeful. In the trial’s fourth year, an exploratory analysis showed the percentage of patients who underwent CPR performed by a bystander increased, suggesting that this component of the intervention was effective. More time may be needed to see which elements mattered and whether they affect survival.

“In light of the apparent improvement in CPR for the last year, further evaluation in the longer, more intense intervention is important,” she said.

Malta Hansen cites the case of Denmark, where rates of bystander CPR and survival are much higher than in the United States. The North Carolina study trained almost 5% of the population, at which point researchers started seeing a difference in delivery of CPR between the group that received training and the group that did not.

“Obviously, that’s not enough. We have observational evidence from other countries like Denmark, where 80% of the population is trained and 75% of patients receive high-speed care,” she said.

In Denmark, the defibrillation rate is 11%, compared to 2% in the U.S. In Denmark, survival is about 16%, compared to 10% in the U.S.

That CPR-trained rate in Denmark climbed from 20% in the early 2000s to 80% now, Malta Hansen said. “It’s not something that’s done in two or three years.”

Here’s what helps: To get your driver’s license in Denmark, you have to show that you have certification in CPR. “It’s just an incredibly efficient way to reach everybody,” she said.

Brahmajee Nallamothu, an interventional cardiologist and editor in chief of the AHA journal Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes, had another theory on timing. Starting the trial right as Covid-19 was emerging could have affected educational efforts and could explain why effects of the training were delayed. He also asked if the educational intervention was the right approach.

“I don’t think anyone would argue with the importance of early treatment in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. When we think about bystander CPR, early defibrillation with AEDs, the challenge is: How do you translate that?” Nallamathu, who was not involved in the trial, said. “I think we still struggle with what’s the best way to train individuals so that they have sustained ability to understand and then utilize that tool when that situation arises, that’s an area that’s still complicated and still unknown.”

Malta Hansen’s team will follow this population in North Carolina for at least another year.

“We have established a fantastic infrastructure and network and collaboration, and everybody’s very excited to keep working on this,” she said. “What we need are communities who are very engaged and very eager to keep working on this. So we believe that future efforts to guide us will be successful.”

That won’t be easy, Michigan’s Deshmukh said.

“Implementation science is challenging (as evidenced by this and other negative trials), but is what I suspect will move the needle the most,” he wrote.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Many clients who come to see me in therapy are coping with an acute injury or health change. For example, they may have sustained an injury while playing a sport or been given a new medical diagnosis. A sudden injury or health change can feel shocking and is often difficult to cope with. You may be trying to adjust to changes in mobility and functioning and worried about when and if you will return to your “normal” self.

Often, the most pressing concern is: Will I get better? And when? The uncertainty is difficult to tolerate. While some injuries or diagnoses have clear treatment plans and timelines, more often it is not crystal clear what someone’s recovery or medical journey will look like. The anxious brain likes to jump to worst-case scenarios when uncertainty exists. It takes a “better safe than sorry” approach, leading us to think about all the possible horrible outcomes so we can be prepared. However, these worst-case scenarios may never come to pass, and anxiety can start to take up more and more space in your daily life.

An injury or new diagnosis can feel like a never-ending emotional roller coaster ride that you never wanted to take. It can be challenging to acknowledge the reality of the injury or diagnosis, including sudden changes you may be facing, without thinking that your life is doomed. Below are some strategies for coping with an acute injury or health diagnosis.

It is helpful to be informed about your condition, as a general understanding can help you have effective conversations with health care providers. It is useful to use reputable sources for gathering information, and your medical team may be able to offer resources. Keep in mind that not all of your questions may have firm answers, and there may be limits to what you or your medical team may be able to know.

While it is difficult to tolerate uncertainty, be mindful of not going down an information-gathering rabbit hole that consumes too much of your time and life. If you notice that trying to gather information about your condition is taking over your daily life or that your anxiety is increasing, set time limits for gathering information. For example, 30 to 60 minutes a week.

A sudden injury or diagnosis can create a wave of emotions. You may feel scared, apprehensive, sad, angry, frustrated, etc. Trying to suppress your emotions can intensify them. Find an authentic way to express how you are feeling. For example, take five to 10 minutes daily to write out your thoughts or feelings, say them aloud on a voice memo app, or share how you are feeling with a trusted friend or family member. If it feels unfamiliar and anxiety-provoking to talk about your feelings, consider doing so in a less focused or pressured environment. For instance, you may talk about your feelings while traveling in the car with soft music playing.

It can be unfamiliar and challenging for some people to accept help from others. Maybe you are used to doing everything yourself, and accepting help leads you to feel incapable. However, accepting help from others allows people who care about you to be of assistance during a difficult time. This is beneficial for them as well as for you. Also, spend time with people you are comfortable with and enjoy. It is natural to feel down, withdraw from others, and isolate ourselves during stressful periods. Yet, isolation can worsen mood. Even if you don’t initially feel like it, carve out some time with others. Your desire to be with others may increase over time. Also, it may be helpful to seek out connection with others who have the same injury or diagnosis. Talking with others with a shared understanding and hearing about others’ treatments, recovery, and coping strategies can feel relieving and supportive.

It can be difficult to accept limitations or the reality that there are some things you may not be able to do or need to do in a different way. It is important to give yourself space and time to grieve any losses you are experiencing. Expecting too much of yourself or trying to ignore the current circumstances can lead to increased frustration, anger, and sadness. Also, trying to meet unrealistic goals may deplete you and lead to less functioning and ability than you would have if you set more realistic and sustainable goals.

Just because you have limitations or there are certain things you cannot do does not mean you are incapable. Non-judgmentally ask yourself what you can and want to realistically take on, and aim to set small, doable goals.

A new injury or diagnosis can be anxiety-provoking and scary, and can highlight what or who you care most about. Certain activities may be different now, yet there are likely things or people who continue to be meaningful to you. Carve out time for them. Stay connected, or reconnect, to what feels most important and fulfilling to you. You may find that many of these things are simple and joyful, such as spending time with loved ones or time in nature.

The anxious part of our minds equates uncertainty with bad based on self-protection and preservation. While a good outcome is not guaranteed, neither is a bad one. The future could be incredibly difficult, and it also could be better than you are thinking or neutral. When your mind wants to leap ahead to worst-case scenarios, pause, take a slow breath, and remind yourself, “Uncertain does not mean bad; it simply means unknown.” Then refocus on the present moment and a values-based action you can take today.

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

What Is Rhabdo? What Every Woman Should Know About the Rare Condition

A woman’s death during the New York City Marathon has put the condition back in the spotlight.

At the New York City Marathon on Sunday, 24-year-old Rachel Apa of Lewisberry, Pennsylvania, collapsed two miles from the finish line. She later died at the hospital.

The medical examiner’s office is still investigating her cause of death. Her father, Steve Apa, told the New York Times that the cause was rhabdomyolysis, commonly known as rhabdo. The rare condition occurs when skeletal muscle breaks down, releasing its contents into the bloodstream, says Robert Glatter, MD, an emergency room doctor and an assistant professor of emergency medicine at Lenox Hill Hospital.

Apa’s death comes amid growing conversations about rhabdo on social media. People have shared their experiences with the condition after intense exercise, particularly at cycling classes. https://www.tiktok.com/@bryybell_/video/7693630991839153421?_r=1&_t=ZT-9Axae4rjSXu

But while these stories are alarming, rhabdo is still relatively rare. Yet they raise questions about the risks of intense exercise, how to recognize when something is wrong, and just how concerned the everyday exerciser should be. Here’s what experts want you to know.

Rhabdomyolysis happens when skeletal muscle breaks down and releases its contents into the bloodstream. The resulting spill can produce dangerous electrolyte imbalances and kidney injury, particularly when someone is dehydrated, Dr. Glatter says.

Although rhabdo is often associated with intense exercise, physical exertion is just one possible cause. “Rhabdomyolysis can also follow crush injuries, seizures, prolonged immobilization, heat stroke, severe infections, certain drugs or toxins, metabolic abnormalities and, more rarely, inherited muscle disorders,” Dr. Glatter says.

Exercise-related rhabdo typically happens when an individual suddenly performs an unusually difficult, repetitive workout involving large muscle groups—particularly when doing eccentric exercise, or when the muscle contracts while lengthening. The risk may be higher when the activity is unfamiliar or far more intense than the person is accustomed to. Some examples include high-repetition squats, lunges, downhill running, and intense cycling, Dr. Glatter says.

“The key issue is often not simply that the workout was ‘hard,’ but that its intensity or volume was far beyond what that person’s muscles were conditioned to tolerate,” he says.

Symptoms include muscle pain "dramatically out of proportion to ordinary post-workout ache,” Dr. Glatter describes, as well as dark-colored urine, swelling, weakness, and difficulty moving the affected muscles. Other possible symptoms include dizziness, fainting, confusion, persistent vomiting or feeling generally ill, he says. “Someone who develops severe or rapidly worsening muscle pain or swelling after a strenuous workout—especially together with marked weakness, dark urine or substantially decreased urination—should be evaluated urgently.”

It’s important to note that symptoms can start while someone is still exercising. An athlete may notice muscle pain that feels disproportionate to effort, unusual heaviness or weakness, severe fatigue, or a drop in pace or intensity that would normally be manageable, Dr. Glatter says. In severe cases, athletes can collapse, appear confused, or develop nausea, dizziness, and swelling.

Other risk factors also include dehydration, exercising in extreme heat or humidity, a recent viral illness, certain medications or drugs, and underlying genetic or metabolic disorders, such as sickle cell.

Although many recent social media accounts of exercise-related rhabdo have come from women, it’s important to know that an unaccustomed high-intensity workout can overwhelm muscles regardless of sex. Those stories don’t necessarily reflect who is most likely to develop the condition, and studies have shown generally higher rates in men.

It’s complicated. “It’s probably a combination of greater awareness and a genuine increase in some types of exertional rhabdomyolysis, but the evidence does not support describing rhabdo as a new epidemic,” Dr. Glatter says.

Rhabdo has been around for decades, and it’s difficult to determine overall incidence rates because mild cases may never be diagnosed. Still, some research suggests that exercise-related cases have increased in certain settings. A 2022 analysis published in the Physician and Sportsmedicine looked at emergency department data from 2000 through 2019. It estimated more than 40,000 cases of exertional rhabdomyolysis and found an increase over that period.

Dr. Glatter suggests this might be, in part, due to shifts in exercise culture. An increase in the availability of fitness classes such as high-intensity interval training, cycling, and CrossFit-style workouts can expose people to unfamiliar movement patterns at high intensities.

“These workouts are not inherently dangerous, and millions of people perform them safely. The risk arises when workload increases too quickly relative to conditioning, particularly when heat, dehydration, illness, medications or inadequate recovery are added to the equation,” Dr. Glatter says. “A highly motivated newcomer trying to keep pace with a class may continue well past the point at which her muscles are accustomed to working.”

It’s also important to note: Being well-trained does lower the risk of rhabdo, but it does not eliminate it. “In a trained athlete, rhabdomyolysis is often best understood as a ‘multiple-hit’ phenomenon, where several physiologic stresses occur at the same time,” Dr. Glatter says. Someone may be proportionally prepared for the activity, but factors such as unusually high intensity or duration, heat and humidity, dehydration, recent illness, inadequate recovery, electrolyte abnormalities, and certain medications or supplements can lower the threshold for injury. Rare underlying conditions, such as sickle cell trait or certain metabolic conditions, can also predispose a healthy athlete to rhabdo.

That doesn’t mean you should fear strenuous exercise. Starting at a manageable level, building up gradually, and staying hydrated can help reduce the risk.

“The body adapts extraordinarily well to exercise when intensity and volume are increased gradually,” Dr. Glatter says. “Going from relatively little training to an all-out, high-repetition workout involving large muscle groups is a very different physiological challenge.”

Exercise-related rhabdo is uncommon, but can happen when a workout places demands on muscles that are far beyond what they’re accustomed to. High-intensity exercise can be part of a healthy routine; the key is to build toward it gradually and pay attention to how your body responds. If you develop severe muscle pain or swelling, weakness, dark urine, or substantially decreased urination, seek urgent medical care.
