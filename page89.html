A real estate note lets you lend against someone else’s property and collect the interest. Before you put money in, confirm your loan is no more than 70% of the property’s appraised value and gets repaid first if the property sells. If you can’t check that yourself, spread small amounts across many loans.

The interest is the appeal. The property is your protection, but only if you can claim it and sell it for at least what you’re owed.

You can buy a performing note, one whose borrower is paying, or pay less than the loan’s balance for a non-performing note, one whose borrower has stopped paying. You can lend straight to a house flipper. Or you can buy slices of many loans through a real estate crowdfunding platform. The real estate bonds some apps sell are loans to the company itself and give you no claim on a particular property.

A default can end in foreclosure, and foreclosures are slow. Homes foreclosed in the second quarter of 2026 had spent an average of 563 days in the process, according to property data firm ATTOM. Texas averaged 155 days and New York 2,007. You can’t cash out early, either. You get your money back as the borrower repays, or by finding someone to buy the note.

The $60,000 between the loan and the value has to cover those costs and any drop in price.

Unless you can run all five checks on every loan, spread the same $100,000 across 50 loans of $2,000 each, so one default puts a fiftieth of your money at risk instead of all of it.

Ubisoft ‘Celebrates’ GTA 6 Day with Canada Layoffs

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The release of Grand Theft Auto 6 is finally here, bringing with it an avalanche of rave reviews and jokes about creative excuses to miss work. Unfortunately, for many Ubisoft workers, the drive home today will also be their last one.

According to Jon Hicks of GamesIndustry.biz, French gaming giant Ubisoft has dropped the guillotine on 166 roles in Canada, concentrated in the company’s Québec and Montreal studios. Ubisoft attributes the move to matching its resources with “current production priorities and business needs.”

Ubisoft says workers affected “are being provided with severance, extended health care benefits, career transition services and other resources” to help smooth out the ride.

Since the announcing of a joint venture between Ubisoft and Tencent, and a major restructuring plan that comes with it, the company has gone through a turbulent period to try and align its workforce with the newly-set goals. As has become the norm, this has come at the expense of workers.

Ubisoft moves all-in with new bosses, a new operating model, and a billion-dollar investment.

The terminated roles appear to represent approximately 4% of the affected studios’ workforce, primarily in support and production positions, a testament to just how massive Ubisoft’s Canadian operation has gotten over the years. Earlier this year, Ubisoft closed down its Halifax studio in Nova Scotia.

Ubisoft has not commented on specific projects affected, but Jon Hicks claims some of the workers laid off were part of Vantage Studios. Previously known as Creative House 1, Vantage Studios focuses on the company’s big money titles, namely the Assassin’s Creed, Rainbow Six, and Far Cry franchises.

Layoffs have left a dark stain in the gaming industry for a while now, especially as companies have begun overcorrecting for the post-lockdown hiring spree.

According to Ubisoft, the company is finally seeing the light as it closes in on the targeted €200 million in savings (approximately $220 million as of today). Unfortunately, just as the French company is about to leave the meat grinder, its Microsoft peers across the Atlantic are just getting started.

However, the road to a long-term vision for a sustainable, successful XBOX brand also seems to go through the rough parts of town, namely divestments and layoffs that have so far cost us iconic studios like Hellblade’s Ninja Theory.

Elsewhere, California-based Electronic Arts brought the hammer down on BioWare last month, in a move that was equal parts shocking and predictable. The studio was known to be struggling even before its parent company’s acquisition by a Saudi public investment fund became official earlier this year, and it appears EA felt we don’t need a new Mass Effect when there’s Star Wars in the making.

The next release from Ubisoft’s Vantage Studios is set to be a new spin on Rainbow Six, called Tom Clancy’s Rainbow Six Tactics. The game will be a single-player tactics game that brings the franchise back to its more grounded roots as a counter-terrorism simulator, with the 2027 release window set to ride the wave of Star Wars: Zero Company.

Rainbow Six Tactics looks to tick all the boxes of what franchise oldheads have been waiting for, but according to an insider, there is an even bigger nostalgia bomb in the works. As reported by Insider Gaming, Ubisoft is quietly working on a sequel to Rainbow Six: Vegas under the codename Spades.

Christopher Nolan Explains Why Fans Will Never See an Odyssey Director’s Cut

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Don’t expect to see any deleted scenes in the epic’s home release

With a runtime of just under three hours, Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey doesn't necessarily need to be any longer, even as an adaptation of Homer's epic poem. Although it isn't Nolan's longest film, fans are always clamoring for more from the prolific director. With The Odyssey's release on home media​​​​​​​, there was some hope that a director's cut or deleted scenes would surface. Nolan has shut that idea down, going so far as to say people will likely never see director's cuts of his films.

Nolan has been pretty excited about the home release of The Odyssey. While the director is known for shooting in IMAX and encouraging audiences to see his movies on the biggest screens possible, he also knows home viewing has a place in movie culture. Speaking to Polygon, Nolan said that at home the viewer is able to control the timeline and look at details, specifically saying that "it's really about the performances." He also supports talking over the movie​​​​​​​ in the comfort of your home.

Still, home releases often come with bonuses such as deleted scenes. Nolan told The Independent, however, that isn't his style. “I don’t really believe in director’s cuts. I want to stand by the film that goes out,”​​​​​​​ the director said. He added that, in the case of The Odyssey, "there are a lot of deleted scenes up in the wastebasket that no one will ever see." Nolan stresses​​​​​​d that there aren't actually too many scenes that didn't make the final cut because shooting on film requires planning. "I tend to try and weed things out on paper because it’s crazy expensive to shoot things that aren’t going to be in the film." He admitted that this has led to a lack of bonuses on home releases, "which always disappoints the DVD crowd."

For those watching the movie for the first time or the tenth time​​​​​​​, Nolan does have some opinions on how to best emulate the movie theater experience. Specifically, he suggests people get a projector. "You can get a relatively low-cost, pretty high-quality projector, darken your living room, maybe introduce some popcorn into the equation or whatever," he told Polygon. "Have a mini movie theater and do it on your own timeline. That's tremendous fun."

Over the course of his career, Nolan said that he's been impressed by how much the home-viewing experience has improved thanks to technology like better projection technology​​​​​​​. Because of that, he told Polygon that a film like The Odyssey carries its cinematic excitement and spectacle to people's living rooms. "Even if people are seeing it for the first time, I think the film sort of carries that with it."

How to switch camera views in GTA 6

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The game only offers a first-person perspective in very limited circumstances.

Grand Theft Auto 6 gives players lots of ways to customize the experience, from the clothes the protagonists wear to granular user interface controls. Being able to shift your perspective of the action is part of that. Rockstar has long offered GTA players the ability to reposition the camera, and you can do that this time around as well.

In the camera tab of the settings menu, you can adjust the height of the camera while you're on foot, as well as the horizontal and vertical follow speeds. You have similar options for land and water vehicles, along with specific follow speeds based on whether you're the driver or a passenger. When it comes to aircraft, you can adjust the follow speed and determine whether the camera follows your plane when you do a barrel roll.

When you're out there exploring the map in a land or water vehicle, you can change the height of the camera on the fly. To switch between the three options, hold the down button on the D-pad, pick the height you want and confirm. You'll also be able to switch to the cinematic camera mode here. This makes things like high-speed chases look cool, but you might find it harder to steer your vehicle.

In addition, there are some very limited ways in which you can switch to a first-person perspective. While you're on foot, you can hold the left trigger on your controller and click the right thumbstick. This lets you take a closer look at your surroundings.

Going back to the camera section of the settings menu, you can enable a first-person perspective when your character is looking at their phone. If you look at your phone while you're driving (in GTA 6, never in real life), you'll see your device and the road from a first-person view as well. Perhaps Rockstar will add a proper first-person mode to GTA 6 down the line, as it did with the previous game.

The unsecured bonds mature in October 2031, and the company is seeking at least $4 billion through an investment-grade loan

CoreWeave co-founder Brian Venturo spent $30 million on debt issued by the AI cloud firm and intends to keep it until it comes due, according to Bloomberg.

Venturo holds the title of chief strategy officer at CoreWeave, and he picked up the unsecured debt last week, according to Bloomberg. The bonds mature in October 2031. He disclosed the purchase in a LinkedIn post.

"When we started CoreWeave, I couldn't have imagined the scale of what our customers are building with us today," Venturo wrote, according to Bloomberg. "I believe in their work, and I'm proud of the team we've built to support it. I've never been more confident in CoreWeave."

A CoreWeave representative declined to comment, according to Bloomberg. Venturo could not immediately be reached.

CoreWeave has been landing additional clients and building out its infrastructure footprint, according to Bloomberg. Its spending pace and its dependence on speculative-grade borrowing have made investors uneasy about the price tag of the AI boom. Several of its bonds dropped into territory associated with distress, then recovered modestly last week once quarterly results topped forecasts.

Bloomberg described such a move as unusual, since executives seldom put money into their employer's debt and then disclose it to the public. Regulators generally require disclosure only for equity stakes.

Another CoreWeave co-founder, Brannin McBee, shook the confidence of bondholders and shareholders last month by laying out how the firm intends to borrow, according to Bloomberg. Speaking again on Wednesday at a technology conference in Austin, McBee said he expects stronger returns from investing cash in computing capacity than from repurchasing some of the company's high-yield debt.

CoreWeave is also looking to borrow no less than $4 billion in an investment-grade loan, according to Bloomberg.

Lightroom Alternative ACDSee Photo Studio Is Leaning Heavier Into AI

Lightroom Alternative ACDSee Photo Studio Is Leaning Heavier Into AI

ACDSee Photo Studio has announced version 27, which brings a bunch of new features, including several powered by AI, perhaps elevating the tool to the level of Adobe’s offerings.

Version 27 of the photo management and editing software for macOS introduces generative AI tools for creating and editing images, along with AI-powered organization that the company says can find and group photos together by how they look. These new options don’t compromise non-destructive editing, either.

In short, ACDSee has embraced AI editing.

“AI is shaping the future of photography and it’s our goal to lead the way in that space,” Frank Lin, CTO of ACD Systems, says. “With ACDSee Photo Studio for Mac 27, we’re bringing generative AI directly into the creative process, making it easier to find, organize and transform images while giving photographers the flexibility and control they need to bring their vision to life.”

The bulk of the updates to version 27 are AI-focused, with the top-billed new feature having the highest chance of being the most controversial: image generation.

“Turn a text prompt into an image, choose a preset visual effect, or bring in reference images to guide character, style, and composition, right inside the photo workflow,” ACDSee says. AI generation isn’t new for photography tools, as Photoshop has had it for years now, but it’s not necessarily something that photographers are specifically asking for in a Lightroom competitor. Still, it’s here now.

ACDSee appears to be following Adobe’s lead with the next feature, as it now supports editing via prompting. Photographers can describe a change in plain language or “use reference images to guide the look, and reshape a photo without a single brush stroke.”

The AI search tool looks to be an alternative to Excire Photo and will find photos visually related to any primary target, with no keywords or tagging required. Images can also be grouped by similarity.

ACDSee Photo Studio for Mac 27 is available for $119.99, including 10,000 AI Credits for generative AI tools and 100GB of cloud storage.

‘Aurora’ Short Film Team Angad Aulakh And Guy Kent On Tapping Into The Emotional Weight Of Self & Outer Space

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Angad Aulakh’s lifelong fascination with space inspired Aurora, a sci-fi short about an astronaut left stranded after a mission goes catastrophically wrong. Freddie Ballard (Guy Kent) is sent to investigate a mysterious signal, but an accident makes him the sole survivor, with no way home and growing doubts about what his superiors have told him.

Shot in black and white, the film draws on German Expressionism and retro-futurism to create its unique visual world. Aulakh and Kent talk to Deadline about shaping Freddie’s journey, building the film’s cosmic scale on a short-film budget and their plans for what lies beyond the vast reaches of the galaxy they created. Related Stories News Actor Russell Tovey Dismantles The Manosphere In His Award-Winning Performance In 'I Am The Prize' News 'Vultures' Swoops To Top Award At Oscar-Qualifying HollyShorts Film Festival: Winners List

DEADLINE: How did you come up with this concept? Watch on Deadline

ANGAD AULAKH: It really started with a lifelong obsession with space and the idea that we are not alone in the universe. It’s one of the most compelling ideas for storytellers and people in general. When we look at how short our lives are, how small Earth is and how vast the universe is, the possibilities for what’s out there are so exciting. It’s the idea that we might not even be asking the right questions yet. We don’t know which questions to ask because there are concepts so grand and profound. Exploring them through storytelling felt like a wonderful opportunity.

With short films, you have more leeway to be a little avant-garde. I often liken short films to haikus, while features are full-fledged novels. So I wanted to focus on the emotion, the moment, the visuals and the wonder of space. Technology has evolved so much that it’s why we can make a film like this on our budget today.

I’m happy to say we didn’t use any AI on the film. I’m not throwing shade at AI, by any means, but I know it’s a big part of the conversation right now. It started with looking up at the stars and wondering what’s out there and what stories I could explore in the vastness of space.

DEADLINE: Guy, I read that the two of you have partnered on several projects. Is this one the most ethereal? When you heard about the idea, what went through your mind?

GUY KENT: I was thrilled because I also have a lifelong fascination with space and our place in [the cosmos]. As Angad said, we have so many questions and no answers. Finding some faith that there’s meaning despite not having the answers is profound to me. When this idea came up, it was an exciting adventure to embark on.

Angad and I have been deliberate about budget and scope, especially with short films. We prioritize quality and want to understand what we can and can’t achieve. With this film, the next question was, “How are we going to pull this off? How do we make it feel like the work we want to do, while making sure it works, resonates and lets the emotional moments land?”

Our pre-production work and the team we’ve worked with in the past gave us a built-in shorthand. That was especially true with our cinematographer, but finding a stage where we could create a set and build on it was also a huge part of making this feasible.

DEADLINE: The short film rests on you, Guy. You have a lot of scenes on your own. Your character is not only lost in space, but lost to himself, or somewhere in between. How did the two of you work on that story? What would you like audiences to think about through that juxtaposition?

KENT: Angad and I had a lot of discussions that began with isolation. Isolation isn’t something you show; it’s something you sit in. It’s revealing to see what happens when all distractions are removed and there’s no barrier between you and your own thoughts.

For Freddie, there’s a chance to find himself again. He’s an astronaut trained for crisis, someone who understands how to solve most of the problems he faces. Here, he’s confronted with something very personal and existential that he has no answers for.

It would be interesting if audiences were left wondering not only how they would react to meeting something beyond Earth, but also considering the hope that there’s something greater. As Angad said, even if we don’t have answers to these questions, there can be meaning in an unknowable answer. That makes you feel found in a way.

DEADLINE: Angad, what was the hardest thing about putting this story together?

AULAKH: The biggest challenge was looking at short films as a whole and thinking about how much time we had and how long the film would be. There are practical considerations, too. A lot of festivals won’t accept a short film longer than 15 minutes.

AULAKH: There are practical considerations around the film’s life after it’s released, but audiences also expect a short film to be short. Ideally, you’re not making a 30- or 40-minute short, because then you might as well make a feature. We had to think about the real estate available to us and the most effective way to make audiences leave having felt something and gone on an emotional experience. That’s what I’m interested in as a filmmaker: moving people. A big part of that is considering how the music, sound design, performance and production design work together to convey something cinematic and effective.

You also want to tell a compelling story. Weaving Freddie’s journey into a cohesive narrative in 15 minutes was always going to be a challenge, but I’m pleased with how it turned out. We focused on the emotion and on the fact that a mission has gone wrong. This astronaut is either going to be killed aboard the spacecraft or find his way home. I hoped the quieter moments would sustain the audience because they’d want to stick around to see whether Freddie makes it.

DEADLINE: Let’s talk about the scope of the film. The cinematography is great. Why black and white as your color scheme?

AULAKH: German Expressionism was on my mind as I wrote the screenplay and thought about the aesthetic I wanted. The other reason is that we don’t see many black-and-white films set in space or black-and-white science-fiction films. More than anything, I think it looks gorgeous on screen. The light on the set, shining through the top grates, was beautiful.

We had scouted locations beforehand, so we knew what the set would look like. But the first image that came to mind was Freddie Ballard lying on the floor, screaming and tumbling. I saw it in black and white. I hadn’t shot a black-and-white film since film school, and I thought this was the perfect opportunity. The stars look beautiful in black and white. There was also a practical reason. Black and white is more forgiving when you don’t have a huge budget for production design. You can hide things in the shadows, and it worked well with our special effects and the 3D modeling. We also made sequences in Unreal Engine with Stuart Lippincott, who’s an absolute genius. His work was a huge inspiration to me. I thought the lighting, images, and compositions would be tremendous in black and white.

KENT: On an emotional level, I think black and white subconsciously creates a sense of melancholy. That’s the basis of German Expressionism, at least in my experience. There’s an emotional texture it brings.

AULAKH: Yeah, it’s very melancholic. Black and white also has inherent cinematic associations. Audiences think of films from the 1930s and ’40s, most of which were made in black and white. I love the idea that Aurora can feel like a product of the past, something you might have seen in those bygone eras. That was exciting because retro-futurism, which is used extensively in science-fiction films and video games, is powerful and interesting. I gravitate toward it, so I loved being able to use that aesthetic in Aurora.

DEADLINE: What were the biggest technical challenges, and where did you have to get especially inventive? You’ve talked about using black and white, but is there another example? How did you get inventive with the production design?

AULAKH: A lot of the credit goes to our visual effects artist, Ian Mills Miller. The entity you see in the film was originally going to be an actor, Ethan [Daniel Corbett], wearing a latex bodysuit. We were going to use his movements and digitally map them onto a being, the way they did with Gollum in The Lord of the Rings. Instead, we used Ethan as a reference, and Ian created the magnificent 3D being you see in the film.

That’s a hard shot to pull off because it’s expensive and time-consuming, and you have to be careful about how much you show. Black and white allowed us to hide the entity in the shadows. He’s a being of light at the end of the film, but throughout it he looks dark and mechanical. He’s influenced more by machinery than by alien life.

It was tricky. Having the ability to hide him in the shadows and take a less-is-more approach was effective. If you hold an effects shot too long, the audience may start to look at it differently. Even in major motion pictures, it can be disconcerting and create an uncanny valley effect. We wanted to avoid that with our digital effects.

DEADLINE: Talk a bit about your experience on the film festival circuit.

AULAKH: I’ll take the first part, and Guy can talk about where we’ve been and what’s coming up. We premiered at HollyShorts this August, which was a lovely experience. I’m a small-town kid from Mountaintop, Pennsylvania. We didn’t have a movie theater in town, and we still don’t. Getting to screen a film at the historic Grauman’s Chinese Theatre was an honor.

The festival was so well put together, and the audience response was wonderful. There were events throughout the week, so you had a chance to meet other filmmakers and talk with people watching your film. As a filmmaker, you get real-time feedback from the room. Unfortunately, we can’t attend many festivals because they’re all over the world. The few opportunities we get to sit with an audience and see the film on the big screen are phenomenal. It’s been wonderful so far. Our last few shorts had great festival runs, and we hope Aurora will top both of them. We’re just getting started. Guy can tell you more about where we’re headed next.

KENT: We’re going to FilmQuest. We are nominated for Best Sci-Fi Short and Best Visual Effects, which is fantastic. We’re thrilled to be part of that festival and what it represents. They’ve been wonderful. We’ve also done Anomaly Film Festival and Toronto Arthouse, and we’re going to Festival of Fantastic Films in the U.K. We have some other festivals to be announced toward the end of the year and early next year.

The greatest thing, as Angad said, is connecting with audiences and getting the film’s message out there. I think it’s a special piece, and there’s more to this world that we hope to build beyond the short. The more audiences see it, the more we’ll be able to go down that road.

AULAKH: We’re developing a television series based on Aurora and its world. If we can build some excitement throughout the festival circuit, it’ll make getting the series off the ground that much easier. We hope people respond to the film. After the festival circuit, we’ll release it online; we’re working on distribution for that, too.

These festival runs can last upwards of a year and a half, if you’re lucky. We’re excited to get the film out there around the world, and it has officially gone international.
