Ben White believes England supporters would not boo him if they knew the complete story behind his departure from the 2022 World Cup squad.

However, the Arsenal defender maintains that he has no intention to "throw anyone under the bus" by speaking out about the incident.

White left the national camp in Qatar citing "personal reasons", following a reported fallout with assistant manager Steve Holland, and subsequently ruled himself out of selection for the remainder of Sir Gareth Southgate’s time in charge.

Though he returned under Thomas Tuchel in March, White was booed by supporters before injury ruled him out of this summer’s World Cup.

His comeback was eventful during a friendly against Uruguay, where he scored and later gave away a penalty.

“I think before the game, I was thinking, just don’t…not stand out, but just nice and easy, you know,” he added.

“Then I came on and obviously get booed. Then I score. Then I give away a pen. So it was obviously full of emotions that night, and it was a good night.

“Everyone can do whatever they want. You know, it’s fine with me. Whatever they want to do, and I think just think about maybe not knowing the full story.

The 29-year-old says he stands by his decision of four years ago, but is looking forward to his “story continuing”.

“It’s something that I’ve not spoken about, and I won’t go into detail about it,” White told talkSPORT. “Yes, it’s a decision that I don’t want to be talking about anyone negatively or throwing anyone under the bus.

“It was obviously not an ideal situation, and (a decision) that I probably still would make.

“There’s loads of things that happened and communication wasn’t great.

“It’s something that I don’t want to throw anyone under the bus. I think there’s a lot of people who tried to do that to me. So it’s something that I wouldn’t want to do to someone else.”

He added on BBC Radio 5 Live: “I don’t think I would (change decision).

“Obviously, I was a bit younger. I’m a bit older now. I think, as a person, I’m quite real, and I think I made the decision that I would probably continue to make.

“It’s one that I’m not happy about, but obviously it happened, it’s time to move on.

“It’s such a privilege to ever get close to playing for your country, something I probably didn’t even dream of because I didn’t think it would be anywhere near my reality.

“There’s been a few bumps, I’m obviously here now, and looking forward to this story continuing.”

The basis of White’s alleged falling out with Holland is thought to be over an interview where the Arsenal defender admitted to not watching football on television.

He said: “To be fair, I don’t. I don’t know if I actually said I don’t like it because people say I don’t like football, which is obviously not true.

“It’s because I don’t watch it, which I think is quite normal for someone who does a job and then doesn’t go home and then continue watching that job.

“But I think it’s my way of release from the game because it’s so intense, obviously.”

Scholar Dishes About Finding a Long-Lost Caravaggio Painting in Paris

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On a gray winter day in Paris three years ago, art historian Maria Cristina Terzaghi walked into the back room of a Parisian gallery and came face-to-face with a painting that had been missing for nearly two centuries. The picture was covered in grime, but she recognized it right away: Young Man with a Carafe of Roses, a work believed to have been painted by Caravaggio in the 1590s.

Terzaghi, a professor at Roma Tre University and one of the world’s leading Caravaggio scholars, has now offered her most detailed account yet of the discovery. Writing in La Repubblica on Sunday, she explained why she believes the painting is an original by the Italian Baroque master, rather than one of the copies that have intrigued art historians for decades. The painting shows a curly-haired young man in a white shirt leaning against a table covered with fruit, an artichoke, and a glass carafe filled with roses. His mouth hangs just slightly open. One eyebrow is raised, like he’s about to say something. Look closely at the carafe, and there’s another surprise: reflected in the glass are a window and the figure of another young man, possibly Caravaggio himself.

The composition has fascinated scholars for nearly a century. Four old copies are known to exist, including one at the High Museum of Art in Atlanta, but the original had apparently vanished. According to Terzaghi, the newly identified canvas is that missing painting, made around 1596 or 1597, when Caravaggio was in his twenties and beginning to establish his reputation in Rome.

Her research found that the painting appeared in a 1607 inventory of works belonging to Giuseppe Cesari, known as Cavalier d’Arpino, whose workshop employed the young Caravaggio. Cesari eventually ran into trouble with papal authorities after being accused of illegally possessing weapons and orchestrating a street brawl that “disfigured a painter” named Pomarancio. He surrendered his art collection to escape prosecution, and the works eventually passed to Cardinal Scipione Borghese, nephew of Pope Paul V.

The painting was recorded in the Borghese collection in 1693, and Terzaghi believes it remained with the family until at least 1831, when restrictions that had previously prevented its sale had passed.

By the early twentieth century, the painting had made its way into the holdings a Paris bookseller. Following his death, his widow put the work up for auction in 1909―cataloged simply as a work by an anonymous Italian painter. An ancestor of the current owners bought it, and it has remained in the family ever since.

Terzaghi believes the painting is closely connected to Caravaggio’s Boy Bitten by a Lizard, another early work showing a young man alongside an elaborate still life. Scholars have previously suggested that the two compositions might belong to a series representing the five senses, with the lizard representing touch and the roses representing smell.

For Terzaghi, the painting’s quality is the best evidence of its authenticity. She points to the young man’s expressive face, the shimmering white fabric of his shirt, and the remarkable handling of light passing through the water-filled carafe. The floral arrangement also bears a striking resemblance to a description of Caravaggio’s work by his contemporary and rival Giovanni Baglione, who recalled the artist’s extraordinary treatment of water, glass, and flowers covered in dew.

The discovery isn’t entirely new to the art historical world. In January 2025, Spain’s Ars Magazine reported that a French family believed it owned the missing Caravaggio. Terzaghi also alluded to the painting in the catalog for the Caravaggio 2025 exhibition in Rome. Her account in La Repubblica, however, offers a much fuller explanation of the attribution and the provenance research behind it.

There are still questions, particularly about how the painting traveled from Italy to France. And as with almost any newly attributed Caravaggio, other specialists will need to assess the evidence before the work can gain wider acceptance.

The news comes during an unusually busy few days for Caravaggio scholars. On Saturday, another Italian art historian, Gianni Papi, attributed a Portrait of a Young Man to the artist during a conference in Montepulciano.That painting had previously been sold at Dorotheum as a work from the 16th-century Tuscan school. Papi has yet to publish the evidence supporting his claim.

Supreme Court allows Trump administration to continue border wall construction

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Supreme Court on Monday cleared the way for the Trump administration to resume construction of a border wall in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas. In a four-page, unsigned opinion, the justices granted the government’s request to pause an order by a federal district judge that had temporarily halted the project. Only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson indicated, without explanation, that she would have denied the government’s request.

The dispute began earlier this year, when then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and her successor, Markwayne Mullin, issued three waivers of various environmental and conservation laws to authorize construction of the wall in the Big Bend region, which includes parts of Big Bend National Park. Noem and Mullin relied on a provision of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 that gives the DHS secretary the power to “waive all legal requirements” necessary “to ensure expeditious construction” of border walls and roads. When they announced the waivers, Noem and Mullin indicated that the Big Bend area “is an area of high illegal entry” requiring additional walls and barriers.

A group of plaintiffs opposed to the construction – an environmental group, a local landowner who works as a river guide, and a group formed to preserve a church – went to federal court to challenge the waivers’ legality. On Oct. 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone temporarily barred the Department of Homeland Security from continuing construction on the border wall in the Big Bend region “until and unless” the government complies with environmental and other applicable laws.

In a written opinion that she released on Oct. 14, Cardone reasoned that the challengers were likely to prevail on their claim that the DHS secretary did not have the authority to issue the waivers. She relied on the major questions doctrine – the idea that if Congress intends to give a federal agency the power to make decisions of vast economic or political significance, it must say so very clearly. In particular, she emphasized, the Big Bend region is not an area with a high number of illegal entries. To the contrary, she stated, although it “comprises over 25% of the U.S.-Mexico border,” it makes up only a tiny percentage (“between 0.3% and 2.2%”) of the migrants apprehended while crossing the border. Therefore, she wrote, “if the Big Bend Sector is an area of high illegal entry, so is every other sector, and the waiver authority would apply to the entire U.S.-Mexico border. If that were the case, Congress could have simply authorized construction ‘to deter illegal crossings.’” The major questions doctrine instructs courts to avoid “interpretations that would render limiting language meaningless,” Cardone concluded.

After Cardone declined to pause her ruling to give the government time to seek Supreme Court review, the Trump administration came to the Supreme Court, where it asked the justices not only to temporarily freeze Cardone’s order but also to grant review and summarily (that is, without additional briefing or oral argument) reverse it. U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer argued that Cardone lacked the power to consider the challengers’ claim based on the major questions doctrine because their complaint specifically alleged that the DHS secretary “lacked ‘clear congressional authorization’ … to issue the waivers” under the IIRIRA – which, he said, was a statutory claim that “Congress denied district courts” the authority “to entertain.” But in any event, Sauer continued, even if the DHS secretary’s power to issue waivers under IIRIRA “implicates a question of ‘vast economic and political significance,’ Congress did clearly authorize the Secretary both to build border barriers and to waive legal requirements in order to facilitate their expeditious construction.”

The challengers urged the justices to leave Cardone’s order in place. Eric Glitzenstein, a lawyer representing the plaintiffs, told the justices in a 40-page filing that if her order were paused or lifted, it would permit the Trump administration to “immediately resume blasting, bulldozing, road construction, and other border barrier-related construction across one of the most remote and ecologically and culturally significant landscapes in the United States.”

The challengers argued that the major questions doctrine applied to their claim. They emphasized that the DHS secretary’s “decision to waive myriad federal, state, local, and tribal laws across vast geography for the purpose of building border infrastructure, even through national and state parks where border crossings are miniscule, is a truly ‘extraordinary’ assertion of power.” Because Congress “did not clearly authorize the sweeping exercise of power asserted here,” they wrote, the government’s “actions therefore amount to a constitutional violation of the major questions doctrine.”

In Monday’s opinion, the justices denied the government’s request to summarily reverse Cardone’s ruling but granted its request to put it on hold. “The Government,” the court explained, “is exceedingly likely to prevail on its argument” that Cardone did not have the power to consider the challengers’ “so-called major-questions-doctrine claim.” That claim, the court emphasized, contends only “that the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority,” which is not a constitutional claim. This is true, the court continued, even if the challengers rely on the major-questions doctrine, because that doctrine is a rule used to interpret laws. “There is no such thing as a ‘constitutional’ major-questions claim.”

The other factors that courts normally consider in deciding whether to pause a lower court’s decision also point in the government’s favor, the justices said. For example, Cardone’s order “delays the Government’s construction of border barriers and roads in the Big Bend Sector” and, as a result, “impedes the Government from advancing its ‘compelling interests’ in border security and integrity.” Moreover, the government is suffering permanent harm from the costs – “nearly half a million dollars … per day” – that it is incurring while the construction is on hold, but which cannot be recovered. Finally, the court wrote, “[t]he Government’s interest … outweighs any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm alleged by” the challengers.

IBM plans to bring Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to enterprise customers

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mythos would hunt for unknown flaws in customers' code, and developers would approve every fix.

IBM’s office in Dubai Internet City, Dubai, 3 November 2026

IBM plans to add Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to Concert Protect so customers can scan their own code for unknown flaws, test which are exploitable and get fixes for developers to approve. It is aiming for early 2027. Mythos itself remains limited to vetted organisations.

IBM plans to build Anthropic’s Claude Mythos into its Concert Protect security software, the company said on Monday. Mythos is so good at finding security flaws that only vetted groups can use it. IBM wants to offer the feature in early 2027.

Mythos would scan code that customers pick for flaws no one knew about, including ones that normal scanners miss. Concert Protect would then check whether hackers could really use each flaw in that customer’s systems.

IBM and Red Hat tools would draft fixes, which developers must review and approve before they go in. IBM cautioned that its plans could change. It is not betting on one lab: in June it joined OpenAI’s cyber programme for a similar service.

“The bottleneck is no longer finding vulnerabilities. It is determining which can actually be exploited and fixing them before attackers do,” IBM wrote.

That gap is real. Project Glasswing, Anthropic’s restricted Mythos programme, flagged more than 10,000 possible serious flaws in its first month, but only 97 had been patched by then.

IBM says its past Mythos work, through Glasswing, was internal research. This is its first step toward selling it to customers. That fits Anthropic’s plan to put Mythos inside partners’ products, where users get findings and fixes rather than the model itself.

Access to Mythos is tightly controlled. Anthropic briefly switched it off in June to comply with US export controls, and the White House now reportedly decides who joins its partner list. Anthropic released Mythos 5.2 on Monday, still only for vetted organisations.

Italian food is inherently celebratory. The cuisine embodies togetherness, featuring giant loaves of semolina bread, big salads, and overflowing family-style platters that snake their way around the table until there's a little bit of everything on everyone's plate. And while we didn't all necessarily grow up eating meatballs, gnocchi, and lasagna, these meals have slowly become a fundamental part of the American cooking canon. Italian food isn't necessarily easy to prepare, but its familiarity and simple elegance make the dishes feel accessible, even if they're difficult to master.

Italian food is also so well suited for the holiday table because of its adaptability. Regardless of your culinary background or self-identified food heritage, most classic Italian dishes can be reconfigured to fit any cuisine or occasion, including Thanksgiving. When you think about it, the two are a natural fit. Italian food is hearty, cozy, and comforting, with plenty of creamy carbohydrates and hearty main courses. The same goes for your typical Thanksgiving fare, allowing this list to feature recipe ideas that blend the two. These interesting mashups will undoubtedly bring some extra excitement (and cheese) to your upcoming holiday menu.

A whole roast turkey is one of the most divisive holiday dishes. ‘One of the highest-stress occupations’: Why $148,000 median pay can’t fix the air traffic controller shortage

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. has about 6% fewer air traffic controllers than it did a decade ago, even as the number of flights relying on the air traffic control system has grown 10%, according to a January analysis from the U.S. Government Accountability Office. The shortage isn’t confined to American airspace.

Countries from Australia to Qatar now compete for the same small pool of certified controllers, recruiting across borders with work visas and pay pegged to experience. Aviation training company CAE projects that 71,000 new air traffic controllers will be needed worldwide by 2034, or roughly 7,000 a year.

The U.S. can’t join that bidding war. Every FAA controller must be an American citizen, leaving the agency dependent on a domestic pipeline that turns away the vast majority of applicants.

For airlines, that pipeline sets the pace of the whole system. Delta CEO Ed Bastian told Fortune in April that the biggest opportunity for AI in aviation is in air traffic control, not inside the plane, because it would “do more in terms of helping our customers have quicker travel, more efficient travel.”

The GAO traces much of the decline to disruptions in training. Government shutdowns in 2013 and in 2018 and 2019 froze hiring and training, and the COVID-19 pandemic halted training for four months, then held it at reduced capacity for nearly two years. High attrition from 2019 through 2024 made it harder to catch up.

The 2025 shutdown made things worse: by November of that year, the FAA said nearly half of major air traffic control facilities were short-staffed as controllers worked without pay.

The deeper problem may be the job itself. “It’s one of the highest stress occupations out there,” says Tiffany Bisbey, an assistant professor of industrial/organizational psychology at George Washington University. When controllers show up in academic research, she says, it’s usually as the subjects of studies on stress.

The federal government says as much. The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes the work environment this way: “Their work can be stressful because maximum concentration is required at all times. Night, weekend, and rotating shifts are common.”

Money is not what keeps people away. The median American air traffic controller earned $148,080 a year, or $71.19 an hour, as of May 2025, according to the BLS, and the top 10% earned more than $215,610.

The bar to apply is also lower than many people assume. The FAA requires a bachelor’s degree, one year of progressively responsible work experience, or a combination of the two, and new hires then spend more than a year training on the job. When the FAA pitched the job to Gen Z video gamers this spring, it advertised pay of at least $155,000 after three years.

Other countries are short too, and some are filling the gap by hiring controllers trained elsewhere. In September, Airservices Australia posted a call for experienced international controllers, offering a work visa with eligibility to transition to permanent residency and promising “the perfect balance between work and leisure.”

Australia needs the help. That same month, unplanned absences left eight of Sydney Airport’s 49 controllers unavailable and delayed roughly a third of inbound flights one morning.

The competition cuts both ways. Qatar has been recruiting Airservices Australia controllers, Canada’s NAV CANADA used an outside firm to recruit eight foreign controllers, and South Africa lost 28 controllers in eight months, Reuters reported this month. The country that trains a controller usually bears the full cost of that training, even if the controller later leaves, a spokesperson for the industry group CANSO told the news service.

The FAA’s qualification rules narrow the pool well before training begins. Applicants must be U.S. citizens under 31 and pass pre-employment tests, a medical exam, and a security investigation. “Less than 10% of all applicants meet these requirements and are accepted into the training program,” the FAA says.

Even fewer finish. About 2% of applicants qualify for and complete the full training process, the GAO found.

The agency is hiring faster than it has in years. The FAA hired 2,235 controller trainees in fiscal 2026, the most in its history, and its 2026-2028 workforce plan puts the certified workforce at about 11,000 as of April, against a target of 12,563.

That target is new. In May, the FAA lowered its staffing goal by about 2,000 controllers, citing forecast demand and changes to scheduling and training. The National Air Traffic Controllers Association said it was not involved in drafting the plan.

Bisbey sees no safe shortcut. Fast-tracking applicants or compressing training, she says, is not a viable fix, and she suspects the people who designed controller training “probably thought they needed to do even more, not less.” As she puts it: “It’s such a high-risk organization, and the training is so arduous for a reason.”

The FAA has experimented at the margins, including a 2024 pilot program that let graduates of two Oklahoma schools skip additional FAA Academy training and go straight to facility training. But certifying a controller still takes years, and until the pipeline catches up, travelers should expect more delays.

Bisbey thinks most will take them in stride, since few would trade a delay for a less safe flight. “As annoying as it is, it’s not going to make people stop traveling.”

Scientists Discover a Hidden Weakness in Prostate Cancer That Could Transform Treatment

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A newly identified vulnerability in a cancer-driving protein could pave the way for a different approach to treating prostate cancer.

Researchers at the University of Michigan have identified a potential way to target a protein that helps drive prostate cancer but has long been considered beyond the reach of drugs. Their experimental compound, PBITE-1, interfered with the protein’s cancer-promoting activity in laboratory models, offering a possible starting point for more targeted treatments.

The protein, known as ERG, becomes abnormally active when two genes, TMPRSS2 and ERG, fuse together. This genetic rearrangement occurs in roughly half of prostate cancer cases among patients of European ancestry and promotes tumor cell growth and migration.

For years, ERG has been considered “undruggable” because it appeared to lack the molecular pockets where medications typically attach. In the new study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, researchers discovered an accessible pocket in a region called the PNT domain, which helps regulate ERG’s interactions with other proteins.

After screening more than 1,600 compounds, the team identified a promising molecule and refined it into PBITE-1. In experiments involving prostate cancer cell lines and laboratory models of human and mouse prostate tissue, the compound inhibited ERG activity, triggered cancer cell death, and reduced the ability of cancer cells to invade surrounding tissue.

“Although we were the first to identify the gene fusion as a driver of prostate cancer, it was challenging to find a drug target for ERG,” said Arul Chinnaiyan, M.D., Ph.D., S.P. Hicks Professor of Pathology and member of the Rogel Cancer Center.

Most existing prostate cancer therapies focus on androgen receptors, proteins that respond to hormones such as testosterone. These receptors can activate the TMPRSS2-ERG fusion, helping fuel tumor growth. Drugs that block androgen signaling can slow the disease, but tumors often develop resistance, and treatment can cause side effects that affect quality of life.

Targeting ERG directly could provide an alternative approach for patients whose tumors carry the gene fusion, rather than relying exclusively on treatments that interfere with hormone signaling.

“This work shows that instead of treating all prostate cancer as one disease, we have the potential to personalize therapy for subtypes of prostate cancer.”

PBITE-1 remains an experimental research tool, and its effects have not been established in patients. Further research will be needed to determine whether compounds targeting ERG can be developed into safe and effective medicines.

“Although it is unlikely that PBITE-1 is ready to become a drug any time soon, this study shows that ERG is a druggable target,” Chinnaiyan said.

“It is exciting that we now have a path forward to target ERG in prostate cancer.”

9 All-Inclusive Resorts in Cancún That Go Way Beyond an Open Bar

Since then, my priorities have shifted, and thankfully, Cancún’s all-inclusive resorts have grown up alongside me.

I’ve also discovered that Cancún’s enduring spring break reputation tells an incomplete story about what's on offer. Choose the right resort, and you can have a butler draw your evening bath, a private pool overlooking the Caribbean, and à la carte restaurants you’ll look forward to all afternoon, whether you prefer the Hotel Zone’s long sweep of white sand, the relative quiet of Playa Mujeres, or a boat ride out to Isla Mujeres.

The frozen margaritas are still here. But now, there’s much more worth checking in for. Read on for the best all-inclusive resorts in Cancún worth planning a vacation around.

Mexican art gives this Playa Mujeres resort its personality. Rubén Soto’s concrete mural depicts native wildlife amid geometric patterns, while suites carry the aesthetic through warm woods and marble rather than tropical clichés. At Atelier, which welcomes ages 16+, you might have lunch overlooking the water at El Suspiro, then enjoy a pre-dinner mezcal cocktail at Bar de la Calle. Back in your suite, a rolling minibar holds wine and gourmet snacks, and room service answers around the clock. Most dining and premium spirits are included, though María Dolores by Edgar Núñez warrants a separate reservation—and budget—for its contemporary Mexican menu. Book an Inspira suite, and a butler can arrange your dinner reservations while you spend the afternoon at the rooftop infinity pool.

At Le Blanc, the pampering begins before you’ve unpacked—and your butler can handle that, too. Once you’re settled, the day’s agenda might amount to a poolside cocktail, a soak in the spa’s hydrotherapy pools, and deciding what to wear to dinner. This adults-only Hotel Zone retreat pairs pale interiors and Caribbean frontage with an unusually generous all-inclusive package: À la carte dining, premium drinks, 24-hour room service, and access to the spa’s hydrotherapy circuit are all included. Dinner might mean a French tasting menu at the esteemed Lumière or Italian cooking at Blanc Italia. Massages cost extra, but the daily chance to steam, soak, and unwind in the spa does not.

Two beaches are better than one, especially when they come with a lighthouse and Caribbean views in nearly every direction. Perched on the tip of Punta Cancún, Hyatt Ziva makes the most of its peninsula setting, with room for both family playtime and grown-up downtime. Little ones can race down waterslides and splash beneath fountains, while teenagers claim their own turf over billiards and foosball in the teens' lounge.
