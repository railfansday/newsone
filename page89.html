Why Wayfair Stock Is Up Today

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shares of Wayfair (W +10.99%) leaped 11% on Wednesday after the home goods-focused e-commerce leader delivered stronger-than-expected profits despite a tepid overall economic environment.

Wayfair's net revenue rose 9.7% year over year to $3.4 billion in the third quarter.

The gains were fueled by an 11.4% jump in U.S. net revenue to $3 billion, marking the online shopping platform's fastest pace of growth since the early stages of the pandemic.

"At the start of this year, we told you we expected to take share in the U.S. at a pace we had not seen in several years," CEO Niraj Shah said. "With U.S. revenue growing double digits against a roughly flat category here in Q3, we've done exactly that."

Wayfair's active customers grew 4.7% to 22.2 million as of Sept. 30.

Although average order value declined to $311 from $317 the prior-year quarter, customers are ordering more often and spending more overall.

Orders delivered in the third quarter jumped 12.2% to 11 million, while last 12-month net revenue per active customer increased 3.1% to $596.

All told, Wayfair's adjusted earnings per share surged 33% to $0.93. That was well above Wall Street's estimates, which had called for per-share profits of $0.79.

Looking ahead, Wayfair sees "high single-digit growth" in revenue in the fourth quarter.

"The momentum we have on orders and customers gives us confidence that our share gains are structural, and we are still in the early innings," Shah said.

The hits keep coming for wild-card hopefuls chasing the Packers

Heading into Week 9, the Green Bay Packers—and the rest of the NFC North—would make the NFL playoffs if the season ended now. Per the league's website, the NFL projects the Packers make the playoffs about half the time in simulations, with the Minnesota Vikings (81 percent), Chicago Bears (82 percent), and Detroit Lions (80 percent) well ahead.

Here's the good news: The Packers have a full-game lead over the next two teams breathing down their neck for the final wild-card spot, the Seattle Seahawks and Los Angeles Rams. On paper, it's a tight race between these teams, as the Seahawks are given a 47 percent chance of making the postseason, and the Rams are at 46 percent.

With how the landscape is playing out right now, the three wildcard spots in the NFC will likely all come down to North or West teams, and the South and East will just get one playoff team apiece. That means that two of Minnesota, Chicago, Detroit, Green Bay, Seattle or Los Angeles will be on the outside looking in at the end of the year.

Here's another bit of fortunate (I'll hesitate to call it good) news for the Packers, though: The Seahawks and Rams are banged up.

Already, it's been announced that Seattle quarterback Sam Darnold will miss next week's game against the Arizona Cardinals with a quad injury and is considered week-to-week. Darnold's backup, Drew Lock, is also dealing with an elbow injury. Per the NFL's projections, the Seahawks' chances of making the playoffs drop from 47 percent to 29 percent if they lose this weekend.

On the Rams' end of things, cornerback Jaylen Watson, their big $51 million free agent signing, will have shoulder surgery, as announced by head coach Sean McVay on Wednesday, and will not return this season. If, for whatever reason, they lose to the Washington Commanders, the Rams' playoff chances will drop from 46 percent to 31 percent.

Packers fans might not like it, but the team is firmly in the "already caring about the results of other teams' games" portion of the season — and honestly has been for a couple of weeks now. The Seahawks and Rams are the only legit threats to push Green Bay out of the postseason, at least for now, and there hasn't been a lot of positive news to come out about those clubs early on in Week 9.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

This Train Offers a Front-Row Seat to One of Colorado’s Greatest Natural Wonders

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“Locomotives all have their own attitudes and personalities,” says Adam Clawson, the 24-year-old engineer at the helm of Royal Gorge Route Railroad, as our train winds along the Arkansas River. “They are their own living, breathing machines, and you have to learn their personalities.”

It’s a warm Saturday afternoon in October, and Clawson is at the controls of a locomotive he knows well: “Black Betty,” a jet-black, 3,000-horsepower diesel-electric unit that runs along a 12-mile stretch of tracks in Southern Colorado. Clawson is a third-generation railroader, following in the footsteps of his grandfather and great-grandfather, who both worked along this same route.

The Royal Gorge Route Railroad winds along the Arkansas River in Colorado.

As he eases the rumbling locomotive through the gorge, he offers a running commentary on the geology and history unfolding outside the window. Millions of years ago, he says, as the Rockies rose above the plains, the Arkansas River began carving its ancient path through the emerging peaks. Over time, the water wore away the hard granite, cutting a narrow, V-shaped gorge that plunges as deep as 1,250 feet in places.

The result is the Royal Gorge, a geological wonder nicknamed the “Grand Canyon of the Arkansas River.” Visitors can experience the dramatic 10-mile-long chasm from several perspectives: From above, while gazing down from the Royal Gorge Bridge, the tallest suspension bridge in the nation (or for more adventurous travelers, while rock climbing a 5-hour via ferrata); and from below, while paddling the Arkansas’s roaring rapids on a whitewater raft. But for travelers looking to sit back and savor the scenery passing by, there's no better viewpoint than the Royal Gorge Route Railroad's open-air car, standing face-to-face with the staggering canyon walls.

The writer's view aboard the Royal Gorge Route Railroad

This train line is as historic as it is scenic. Though the tourist train didn't launch until 1998, the route itself has been here for nearly 150 years—but it was close to never being built in the first place. In the late 1870s, a pair of competing railroad companies—the Denver & Rio Grande and the Santa Fe—duked it out for the chance to build tracks through this narrow canyon. Their two-year battle, which involved everything from court injunctions to sabotage to gun-slinging hired guards, became known as the “Royal Gorge War.” Eventually, the two companies signed a treaty and the Denver & Rio Grande prevailed, building tracks that eventually reached the lucrative mining hub of Leadville.

Decades later, the train still threads through the same Old West landscape, but the ride is decidedly more comfortable. Today passengers can choose from three classes: Vista Dome, which offers nearly unobstructed views of the gorge from glass-enclosed dome cars; Deluxe Class in the vintage dining car, where four-top tables with cushioned booth-style seats and a bar are nestled against the large windows; and Coach Class, with four-person table seating and the option to order food and drink from the casual grill menu and bar. On select dates, there are also First Class dining tickets available for lunch and dinner, with elevated menus and wine pairings. Regardless of where you sit, all ticket holders have access to the open-air car, which is standing-room only. With Clawson or one of his fellow engineers at the controls, all travelers have to do is sit back and let the scenery unfold.

The journey begins roughly an hour south of Colorado Springs in Cañon City at a historic depot originally completed in 1914. A few miles later, a dilapidated pipeline comes into view, running along the canyon wall just above the river. Completed in 1910, this eight-mile-long, mostly wooden structure provided water to Cañon City until 1973. As the train passes under the Royal Gorge Bridge, Clawson encourages me to open the cab window, lean back, and look up. The wind whips my hair as I stare at the bridge some 1,000 feet overhead. It’s 18 feet wide, according to Clawson, but from this vantage point, it looks like a thin wire that’s been strung across a gaping expanse.

As the train works its way through the river gorge, water rushes past and red rock walls tower on both sides.

Next, the train squeezes through the gorge’s narrowest and deepest section, the towering red granite walls growing taller and more imposing as they tighten around the tracks. Then we chug over the famous “Hanging Bridge,” which was an engineering marvel when it was completed in 1879—and remains one even today. At this point, the chasm narrows to just 30 feet wide, creating a bottleneck that required a little ingenuity to get a train through. Engineer C. Shallor Smith came up with a brilliant solution: He designed a 175-foot-long bridge that’s suspended above the rushing water by A-shaped supports anchored to the canyon wall.

Soon after, Clawson spots a pair of plastic kayaks hauled out on a small beach on the other side of the waterway. “We might get mooned,” he warns, referencing how paddlers traditionally greet the train when it passes. But when we spot the kayakers standing on the bank a few minutes later, they simply smile and wave. Clawson blares the train’s horn in response.

Eventually, the train reaches its western terminus, the town of Parkdale. Clawson hits the brakes and we jump down onto the tracks. Standing in front of the train up close, I’m struck by just how imposing she is. Thinking back to Clawson’s personality comment, I imagine her as fiercely independent—a self-assured, no-nonsense type of gal who’s not easily pushed around.

We clamber back into the cab and the train heads in reverse, retracing the route toward Cañon City. I relax back into my seat, letting the engine’s vibrations and the rhythmic drone of the wheels lull me into a pleasant daze. Outside, the rocky surfaces of the gorge’s walls alternate between light and shadow while the river glints in the midday sun. High overhead, I spot a turkey vulture circling on a thermal, its wings arching in a shallow V as it glides without flapping.

Clawson follows my gaze. “You can’t make this up,” he says. “Somebody might type into AI, ‘Take this picture of me and make it look like I’m in paradise.’ [But] all I gotta do is step outside."

NYC Votes to Cut Construction Red Tape in Bid to Spur More Homebuilding

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New York City voters overwhelmingly approved a plan to overhaul the city's notoriously complicated construction permitting process Tuesday, handing Mayor Zohran Mamdani a victory in his effort to accelerate housing development.

Question 4, one of five ballot measures approved in the Nov. 3 election, received roughly 68% support, according to preliminary election results.

The measure promises to simplify construction by bringing 40 types of permits and approvals currently handled by 18 separate government offices and agencies into one centralized digital system.

"New Yorkers voted for a government that moves as fast as they do," Mamdani said in a statement Tuesday night, adding that the reforms would "directly address the affordability crisis and help New Yorkers stay in the city they love."

However, the new system isn't required to launch until 2030. Even when it does, faster permitting will address only one of the many obstacles making housing so difficult and expensive to build in New York.

"No single reform will be a silver bullet for housing supply and affordability, especially in a city like New York," explains Jake Krimmel, senior economist at Realtor.com®. "Instead, each pro-supply policy change is a baby step in the right direction. And these marginal gains will add up."

The Big Apple needs approximately 700,000 additional homes over the next decade to meet existing and future demand, according to an August 2026 draft assessment from the city's housing and planning departments.

Yet developers filed plans for just 8,064 multifamily units in the second quarter of 2026, in a stunning 52% decline from the previous quarter, according to the Real Estate Board of New York.

That amounts to less than half the 17,500 new homes the city needs to add each quarter—and they're only proposed units, not completed homes.

Centralizing permits could help close that gap by cutting down the time and cost builders are forced to spend navigating city bureaucracy.

"Streamlining approvals can make a huge difference," Krimmel says. "It might not be the flashiest reform, but when it comes to new construction, time is money."

And permitting takes a very, very long time in New York City.

Of the nearly five years it takes to build a typical 20-unit apartment building in the Big Apple, roughly 1.5 years are spent securing permits alone, according to a September 2026 study examining construction timelines across 60 U.S. cities.

Now compare that to similar projects in Raleigh, NC, or Phoenix, AZ, where it takes roughly two years from application to completion.

"In a city like New York City with high land acquisition costs and a complex regulatory environment, predictability in timelines and financing can be critical for ensuring new construction can pencil," Krimmel says.

And research suggests those time savings could translate into more homes.

Cutting approval times by 25% could have increased multifamily housing production in Los Angeles, CA by 12.7% between 2010 and 2022, according to a 2026 study published in the Journal of Urban Economics.

When researchers accounted for additional development that faster approvals might encourage, the estimated increase reached 23.7%.

The question for New Yorkers, though, is when those reforms might translate into more housing options and lower rents. The median asking rent hit $3,785 in October—up 4.5% from a year ago.

Under the new rules, applicants would have one digital portal to submit documents, track applications, and obtain approvals, rather than navigating separate systems across multiple agencies.

But streamlining applications doesn't necessarily mean they'll be reviewed any faster.

In fact, the average time required to approve applications through the Department of Buildings' existing digital portal climbed from 8.3 days in 2020 to 20.3 days in 2025, according to a July report from New York State Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli.

And its hardly the only challenge. Krimmel says the overhaul won't address other barriers to construction, including zoning restrictions and the complexity of financing multifamily developments.

Still, he sees the reform as one step toward untangling a much larger problem.

"In the same way that a complex web of red tape and veto points is extremely good at creating barriers to new construction, real solutions will require the city untangles that web bit by bit," he says.

With the new system potentially four years away, the first test will be whether it actually shortens permitting timelines. Any corresponding increase in housing construction—and relief for renters—could take considerably longer.

As Mamdani acknowledged Tuesday night, "Now comes the work of turning these votes into results."

The Chicks Recall ‘the Incident’ and Compare Bush to Trump in ‘Taking the Long Way’ 20th Anniversary Shows: Concert Review

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

Natalie Maines does not normally talk a great deal in concert. This may sound counterintuitive to say, since objectively there is no person in music history more famous for talking in concert than Natalie Maines. But she has always been more of a quality than quantity gal when it comes to that, so her legendary 2003 comment about being ashamed to share a home state with then-president George W. Bush was more of an anomaly than an indicator that she or her bandmates are going to be prone to lengthy speeches at any given concert by the Chicks. Related Stories 'The Mosquito Bowl' Review: Football Heroes Take to the Battlefield in Peter Berg's Emptily Macho WWII Epic 'A Few Good Men' Review: Broadway Revival Breathes New Life Into Aaron Sorkin's Courtroom Classic

Fortunately, the short current tour the group is wrapping up was custom-built to feature a steady stream of personal asides, which are welcome, if you happen to be a fan of the trio as personalities and not solely as musical talents. The main reason for that is that the outing is officially a celebration of the 20th anniversary of “Taking the Long Way,” the 2006 record that won five Grammys in total, including a rare Grammys sweep of the top three categories, album, record and song of the year. Not much was revealed in advance about the exact format of the tour. As it turned out, the Chicks (nee: Dixie Chicks) were not only down for spending the first two-thirds of the concert performing “Taking the Long Way” in its 14-song entirety, but for providing between-song commentary on most of the tracks, along with background on the preceding events that ultimately led to this left turn of an album — including “the incident,” as the Bush flap has been euphemistically known in band circles for 23 years.

This made for a rare treat, as Maines, Martie Maguire and Emily Strayer sat on stools on a riser for an hour and a half and went deep into “Storytellers” mode, engaging in a mixture of somewhat pre-scripted and fairly spontaneous dialogue before nearly all of the songs, also sometimes putting up documentary film clips on the big screen to further illustrate what was going down in the studio in the mid-2000s. (Some of the clips were from their “Shut Up and Sing” documentary, but many were unreleased.) The show fell into that rare sub-genre many of us love from a veteran performer: the docu-concert.

But the other reason for some added chatter during the tour was the timing, which was coincidental (seemingly?), leading up to — and, in Los Angeles, overlapping — the 2026 midterm elections. Their three-night stand at the Dolby Theatre in Hollywood began Sunday, two nights before the nation went to the polls, followed by a show on election night Tuesday, with a wrap-up of the stint and the tour scheduled for Wednesday. (Wednesday’s show will be a make-up for the one that was supposed to happen Monday, but got canceled in the hours beforehand due to an unspecified band illness.)

Naturally, the topical events of this week were bound to inspire a few thoughts comparing the current climate with the heated temperature that resulted in a lot of attempted Chick-broiling in 2003-4.

“It was 20 years ago, and a lot has changed since then,” Maines told the Dolby crowd near the start of Sunday’s show. “Now I would give George Bush a big kiss. Now we’re ashamed the President of the United States from the United States.” (Maines had made similar remarks earlier in the tour, and noted that these had been publicized enough that the White House issued an official statement “calling me a peanut head… or peanut brain, I can’t remember.” For the record, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle said on Oct. 15, “Natalie Maines is a despicable nobody who clearly suffers from a severe case of Trump Derangement Syndrome that has completely rotted her peanut-sized brain.”)

The crowd of 3,400, skewed disproportionately toward progressive cowgirls, went nuts. As they did later in the first act, when, before singing “I Hope,” Maines shared that they had recently visited the Obama museum, which gave them hope of again seeing a country that is not “run by a deranged nutjob.”

The only other overt reference to current events came during the second act — the greatest-hits set — when Maines sang “White Trash Wedding” and followed the time-honored opening line “You can’t afford no ring” with the contemporary addendum: “Because gas prices are too high! And electricity. And medical!” They expounded upon this a little more during Tuesday night’s show, when all three members fanned themselves with large “Fuck Trump” fans.

There’s one word to describe the trio’s nearly quarter-century history of not backing down: Nice.

Not every artist who does a full-album throwback show plays the original record in order; some rearrange the sequence to put a big single at the end. But the Chicks adhered to original running order, which meant that “Not Ready to Make Nice” provided a natural climax to the show only three songs in. And anyway, sometimes gratification that isn’t delayed is the best kind. So when Maines broke from the visage of chagrin and anger that characterizes most of “Not Ready…” to bust into a defiant smile after admitting “I kinda like it,” we’d gotten the most cathartic moment of the show out of the way less than 15 minutes into a two-and-a-half-hour gig. And we kinda liked it, because after all, act-2 favorites like “Sin Wagon” and “Goodbye Earl” don’t exactly count as anticlimactic.

Any serious fan of the group might have heard those latter barnburners in concert enough for one lifetime… not that retributive murder and taunting fornication ever get too old. But the main draw of this show for a more hardcore Chick-aholic was as it usually is for veteran acts’ full-album gigs: the promise of long-ignored deep tracks as inherently part of the concept. And “Taking the Long Way” certainly has its recently unplumbed depths. Four of the songs from the 2006 album have remained staples of the group’s sets: the quasi-title track “The Long Way Around,” the balladic “Easy Silence,” the rocking “Lubbock or Leave it” and, of course, “Not Ready.” That left 10 the group has not gone back to in a show for at least 10 years and, in several cases, 20.

Or never. “Baby Hold On” is apparently the one track the group never performed live before this tour (at least according to setlists.fm). Four had not been played since the arena tour of 2016: “Everybody Knows,” “Silent House,” “Favorite Year” and “I Like It.” Another, “Lullaby,” had not mollified an audience since 2014. And four hadn’t been performed since the year the album came out, two decades ago: “So Hard,” “I Hope,” “Bitter End” and “Voice Inside My Head.” In fact, the last two of those had apparently only ever been performed live once. All to say: the Chicks were taking the long way back around to a huge part of this setlist.

How’s it holding up, anyway? Probably everyone can think of Grammy album of the year winners that seemed like a good idea at the time, to somebody, but haven’t dated well. And it would be wrong to imagine that “Taking the Long Way” made it into the winners’ circle solely on musical merits, when there was clearly an appetite to give the Chicks a medal of valor for all that they’d put up with on top of a slew of Grammys. Even I, not having listened to the full album in quite a while, approached this concert playback with a touch of trepidation: What if it didn’t live up to memories and it felt like the Recording Academy had given out all that gold as a consolation prize for their crucifixion?

So there was good news Sunday — not quite as good as Tuesday night’s national news, but still pretty good — in just how solidly “Taking the Long Way” stands up as not just some sort of political milestone but a superior collection of music. (I’d still give the nod to “Home” as their best, but this remains a strong second.) The dance the trio did in collaboration with producer Rick Rubin and their songwriting collaborators was a slightly tricky one, with enough themes of personal iconoclasm running through several key songs that it almost came to feel like a concept album, even if they were only being allusive to the controversy that continued to follow them… with the exception of “Not Ready,” which was pretty darned specific, given how only a minority of us can relate to receiving death threats.

That anthem was preceded by a film reel of right-wing commentators having their way with the Chicks in the immediate wake of the Bush controversy, including Fox host Bill O’Reilly famously opining that “these are callow, foolish women who deserve to be slapped around.” Maines recounted their first writing session with Dan Wilson, who co-wrote six of the album’s songs, where “he said, ‘So you probably wanna write about the incident… You wanna write like an apology song?’ We said, ‘Fuck that.’ And he was like, ‘OK, fine. How about “Not Ready to Make Nice”?'” The result gave songwriting blind dates a good name. “Even though we went through all the stages of grief, we landed on anger for most of it,” said Strayer, the trio’s banjo player, “and we thought it was so specific to our story that it wouldn’t necessarily relate to everybody else, but little did we know or anticipate that it would become such an anthem for everybody else’s story as well.” Now, maybe even more than then, there’s something about their righteous Troll Derangement Syndrome that rings a bell.

The members were able to point out instances in other songs that referred in some way to the controversy. While each verse in “The Bitter End” was about a different kind of parting, and in some cases a sweeter one, they confirmed that the final verse did indeed describe their immediate estrangement in 2003 from the country music establishment they’d spent years kissing up to, be it label execs or radio programmers or fellow country stars. The last verse rang afresh with a mixture of resignation and the triumphalism that is inherent in any of their three-part harmonies: “You had a good time / Drinking all of our wine / After the show / We all rode the wave / Of that crazy parade / Oh, where’d you go / What happened to / The ones we knew / As long as I’m the shiniest star / Oh, there you are… / We’ll still be here when you come round again.” That’s a family reunion that never quite came true, as the group came to be more accepted in rock and Americana circles than in mainstream country… though the number of hats and boots in attendance at the Dolby made it clear there was a huge part of the former audience that never got lost back when sides were taken.

There were some more amusing memories during this first section, as “Lubbock or Leave It” led to a discussion of whose parents were most supportive at the time. Maines’ father, famed steel player Lloyd Maines, “a father figure to us musically,” was seen in a film clip describing how he’d tossed off an angry letter to the hometown Lubbock country station that banned the Chicks — whereas Strayer and Maguire, who are sisters, had “our mom was saying things like, ‘I’m gonna put her (Maines) over my lap and spank her’ — stuff like that. So we had a little different dynamic going on at home.”

Some other family fighting came up. “I bet that’s the only time you’ve ever heard sisters fight about in vitro,” said Maines, following a film clip in which Maguire and Strayer got into it about the difficulties of scheduling dates on their European tour around her IVF procedures. “I had to go see a different gynecologist in a different country every other day,” explained Maguire, while her sister had already had success using the procedure. The resulting song, being performed on this tour for the first time in two decades, was “So Hard,” surely one of the first songs on a bestselling album devoted to in vitro fertilization. (Ironically, Sara Bareilles just performed a contemporary one on the same subject at the Dolby just a couple of weeks ago.)

And speaking of songs that very much capture a moment in time, Strayer pointed out that “Baby Hold On” referred to the three children under the age of 3 1/2 she and then-husband Charlie Robison had at the time, and the need to find marital intimacy amid that, when they sang, “Let’s kick the babies out of bed / How ’bout you and me instead.” Strayer and Robison would be divorced within a couple of years, which may account for why the song was never played live until this year. “I think I thought it was about hope, but it was probably more about desperation at that point,” Strayer admitted to the Dolby audience. “We’ve all been there,” responded Maines.

The album also had its share of less overtly autobiographical but still deeply personal-sounding songs. “Favorite Year,” co-written with Sheryl Crow, was one of these, a spirited but still haunting recollection of a past love affair that left a lot of “what if” questions behind, even though the singer has a husband and child. If this one had a personal meaning for one or more of the members, we didn’t learn, because there were a few numbers from “Taking the Long Way” when they did just shut up and sing. But “when we were together, I was at my best” is both a warm thought and a chilling one. And the song provided yet another example of how, on this album, the trio sometimes sounded a lot closer to the Bangles than anything out of Nashville or even Texas.

Come the end of the album playback, Maines said they would be “taking a brief intermission, and come back and play this whole album backwards.” Instead, the second act brought nine songs that are a part of any of their contemporary sets — including the mashup of “Long Time Gone” and “Daddy Issues” they’ve been including ever since they premiered it in collaboration with Beyonce on the 2016 CMA Awards. (Is it really the 10th anniversary of that controversy?) The only original song performed from the last 20 years came from their only album released in the last 20 years, the title track of 2020’s “Gaslighter” … a divorce song, not a Trump song, though it being played during the week that America effectively voted to divorce the president gave it a little extra resonance.

There was more rock and less talk in this final greatest-hits segment. Of course fans would hope that another album might be in the hopper from the music world’s least studio-prolific superstar act shy of Lauryn Hill. Perhaps collecting memories and overseeing the documentary footage for the “Long Way” retrospective has reminded them they like the recording studio? Or perhaps they will continue to lean on repertoire. They’ve got one of the greatest catalogs in contemporary music, so it’s hard to complain if they find a way to milk it every few years. They took the short way around to becoming instantaneous pariahs 23 years ago, but when it came to going from that panic-button moment to being transformed into heroines for the ages, it’s been a hell of a long tail.

The 10 Best Disney Dances on 'Dancing With the Stars,' Ranked

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Dancing with the Stars has been entertaining audiences on ABC for more than 20 years. It turns celebrities into dancers while becoming one of the network's top-rated programs. The television show eventually began hosting theme nights, one of them being Disney.

The show is currently airing its 35th season, and this year, contestants danced to songs inspired by Disney movies that were developed into Broadway musicals. While it's different from previous Disney nights, it does not take away the importance of other dances from previous seasons. With that in mind, here is a list of the best Disney dances on Dancing with the Stars.

Due to Season 31 only airing on Disney+, the show changed things, and created a Disney+ night. Charli D'Amelio and her partner Mark Ballas were selected to perform a jazz number to the theme song from The Simpsons. The duo put on their best Marge and Homer Simpson outfits, and performed a showstopping number that made the Simpsons look like the perfect couple.

There were times in the routine where Charli appeared to be nervous, which is normal for all contestants who make it halfway through the season. Those nerves began to disappear when she and Mark performed simple moves, and the rise in confidence gave her the edge she needed to show audiences that she is more than just a social media personality. Her confidence boost helped her earn a 36 out of 40 from the judges, and find herself winning the mirror ball trophy without ever landing in the bottom two.

James Maslow and his partner Peta Murgatroyd celebrated the show's first-ever Disney night while competing in Season 18 by dancing a contemporary piece to "Let It Go" from Frozen. The routine brought out a gentle side of James that Big Time Rush fans had never seen before, and his relaxing facial expressions brought a calmness to a dance that wasn't over the top.

Peta was just as calm, and it helped her and James have incredible chemistry throughout the entire routine. Their loving embrace at the end of their performance was the cherry on top of a fantastic contemporary piece. The judges also loved the beauty of the performance, and awarded James a perfect score once he finished dancing.

Heather Morris already had dance experience before competing with her partner, Maksim Chmerkovskiy, in Season 24. After Maksim got injured, she danced with Alan Bersten, and the two performed an enthusiastic jazz routine to "For the First Time in Forever" from Frozen. It was unfortunate to see her performance lack jazz moves, but the pure joy she had throughout the entire routine is too much to overlook.

The happiness she felt while dancing was contagious to a point where late judge Len Goodman gave spirit fingers in his seat. Even though her performance lacked the kind of jazz the judges wanted to see, Heather was still able to earn 34 out of 40 points just for bringing her passion to the dance floor. Sadly, Heather's love of dance was not enough to spare her from elimination, and she was booted off the show a week later in one of the most shocking eliminations in the show's history.

Juan Pablo Di Pace gave off the most arrogance imaginable during the show's 27th season when portraying Gaston in his Viennese waltz to the song "Gaston" from Beauty and the Beast. He and his partner, Cheryl Burke, reminded audiences through dance that Gaston would never be husband material. The choreography was brilliant, and the build up to Juan hanging from a chandelier was priceless.

The actor's success in filling Gaston's egotistical shoes brought the performance to life. His portrayal outshined the other pros, and the fierceness he brought to the Viennese waltz was refreshing to see on a fairy tale evening. The judges were just as impressed with Juan's performance, and awarded him a 29 out of 30.

Rumer Willis and her partner Val Chmerkovskiy crushed their Samba to "Poor Unfortunate Souls" from The Little Mermaid. The actress went from top contestant to graceful sea witch in the blink of an eye. Everything from the shimmying to the overdramatic arm movements made audiences get to know Ursula as a creature, and not as a witch planning to conquer Atlantica.

Rumer also never came close to breaking character, which is extremely hard to do when having fun in a dance routine. Ursula is the villain fans love to hate, but the star's dancing made viewers forget why Ursula is a manipulative monster. The judges also saw past the evil in Ursula, and gave the soon-to-be Season 20 champion a 39 out of 40.

Kylie Kelce Reveals the One Thing That Makes Someone the Perfect Houseguest

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Kylie Kelce has revealed what a visitor needs to do to be the perfect guest in their home—and it has to do with impressing her and her NFL-star husband Jason Kelce's daughters.

On a recent episode of her podcast, "Not Gonna Lie," the mom of four revealed that their daughters— Finnley, 18 months, Bennett, 3, Elliotte, 5, and Wyatt, 7—love having guests read to them.

“I know one way to my girls’ hearts, and really kids, I always tell people you don’t need to bring gifts when you come to our house. If you come in, you sit down, you offer to read our girls a book, they will be like forever," she revealed.

Kelce added, “Like genuinely, we have obviously family [that] will come and they’ll bring this and they’ll bring that, but if when you come and you sit down, you read a book, I think it’s one of the best ways to connect with kids.”

The glimpse of her houseguest request came just days after she defended her and her spouse's decision to snap up multiple Philadelphia properties right next to each other.

In an episode of her podcast, which featured guest Nikki Glaser, Kelce, 34, and the comedian chatted about hate comments.

"Do you ever get in the comments? Do you ever get feisty?” Glaser asked.

In her reply, Kelce detailed an Instagram account that frequently posts information about their real estate.

“One of the ones that I did it—which was not to a person so it doesn’t really feel as aggressive—was like ‘Celeb Homes.’ They put a bird’s-eye view of where we live with our children, and they were trying to give details of our property," she said.

The account then revealed that the Kelce family had also purchased the home "next door."

“[They were like], ‘Why would you need this many houses?’ And I’m like, ‘for f---ing security and privacy, you pieces of s--t.' They left the post up, so it’s still there. You can still find it," Kelce explained.

“‘Why would anyone need three neighboring properties?’ For safety, privacy, and security. Get the f outta here," reads the comment Kelce left.

She then told the comedian, "I think I typed ‘get f---ed.’ And then when I tried to post it, it wouldn’t let me. Why? I want to tell people to get f---ed."

Her admission came after she offered a glimpse of the family's childcare arrangements during an appearance on an episode of "IMO with Michelle Obama and Craig Robinson." She explained that she and the former Philadelphia Eagles star prefer to keep their relatives closely involved in raising their children.

The couple have built a support system that allows them to balance their professional commitments with family life.

A major part of that arrangement involves Kelce's parents, Lisa and Ed McDevitt, who live just 15 minutes from the family's Philadelphia-area home.

Kelce explained that her parents regularly help care for the girls, making it possible for her and Jason to navigate their busy schedules without relying on round-the-clock childcare.

Although the couple do employ a nanny, Kelce revealed that the arrangement is limited to traditional working hours rather than having someone live in their home.

“My husband and I have very busy schedules. We don’t necessarily want a live-in nanny or someone who’s going to be raising our kids void of us. That feels weird. So we do a lot with my parents every chance we get. We do things with Jason’s parents. We find that those are the values that we were raised with that we want our kids to have, not just because we tell them that, but because we live it," she explained.

The decision, she explained, reflects their desire to remain actively involved in their daughters' everyday lives.

Rather than bringing in someone to provide full-time care, the Kelces turn to their extended family for additional support.

Kelce said maintaining those close family relationships is particularly important because she wants her daughters to grow up with the same values that shaped her and Jason's childhoods.

She also emphasized how grateful she is for the nanny who helps the family during the workday, acknowledging that the additional support is essential to managing their responsibilities.

However, the couple's approach to childcare goes beyond convenience.

For Kelce, living close to her parents has been a priority, and she admitted that the thought of settling somewhere far from her family has never appealed to her.

“We couldn’t do it without my parents. My parents live 15 minutes from us, and they have helped us in every way imaginable," she said.

The Pennsylvania native explained that remaining in the area where she grew up has allowed her to preserve those relationships while raising her own family.

That connection to her hometown has also influenced the kind of upbringing she hopes to provide her daughters.

Kelce wants the girls to become familiar with the places that shaped her childhood, from local businesses to the community organizations that continue to play a role in her life.

She believes that growing up with those personal connections can help children develop a stronger sense of responsibility toward the communities around them.

Her own involvement with the Eagles Autism Foundation is one example of how she has maintained those local ties.

For Kelce, the opportunity to contribute to the community where she was raised is another benefit of remaining close to home.

“Now, granted, we do have a nanny. I always give a shoutout because we couldn’t do it without her, but we have her during regular work hours and really nothing outside of that," she added.

Andrea Bowers Salutes the Climate Change Activists of the 1980s at Fondazione Sandretto Re Rebaudengo

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andrea Bowers’ solo exhibition “La Ragnatela” at the Fondazione Sandretto Re Rebaudengo in Turin, Italy, is unintentionally, aptly timed, given its focus on the politics of climate change: Last year, Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni hit pause on solar panels on farmland, calling it a “threat to our food sovereignty.”

Bowers’ show spotlights the Italian anti-nuclear feminist movement and women’s camp La Ragnatela—“the spiderweb”, in Italian—that launched in 1983 in Comiso, Sicily, to protest the installation of US nuclear cruise missiles at the NATO base in Comiso. The show is a mini-retrospective composed of Bowers’ political activist work which was in dialogue with the movement. There are works by Bowers that she created in 2003, straight out of graduate school, as well as pieces produced in 2013, 2022, 2023 and four newly created artworks for the exhibition. Related Articles Melting Ron DeSantis Statue Makes a Point About Climate Change in Florida Collective Climate Action Implemented by Los Angeles Arts Institutions

Much of La Ragnatela’s history has been forgotten, so Bowers worked with curator Bernardo Follini to find and rebuild the network of Italian ecofeminists. Follini found three women, who are now in their 80s and 90s, who contributed documents and clippings to the exhibition.

“The social engagement part of my practice is the least known about or documented because the art world is more focused on objects, but this is what I’m most proud of,” said Bowers during a preview in Turin, dressed in head-to-toe black with her Botticelli-esque hair flowing freely.

The show opens with Defense of Necessity (2003), a quilt that nods to women’s traditional crafts, and it’s one of the first pieces that Bowers created as a political artist. On close inspection of the quilt, there are sowings of spiderwebs, a symbol for the Women’s Pentagon Action, an American grassroots ecofeminist and anti-nuclear movement that was simultaneously taking place in Italy by way of La Ragnatela and in England with the Greenham Common Women’s Peace Encampment. Defense of Necessity is held up as a fence in the show. “The fence is the site often where nonviolence is questioned whether it’s becoming violence. Activists who are involved in nonviolence are always saying, if we cut that fence, does it become violence? Or is this called an offense of necessity, which is an argument that smaller injustices, or you break small laws to protect the world from larger injustices,” explained Bowers. “It’s a legal term and because it has the word ‘fence’ in it, it’s a play on words and it’s poetic.”

On the other side of the fence are four vitrine cases, two of which feature a timeline of the La Ragnatela movement. The others display letter exchanges between the women in Comiso and their comrades in England, who eventually traveled to Comiso to help them protest. There are photographs of some of the women who were arrested for protesting, along with a letter from one of the women, who was detained for a long time and put on trial, during which she wore a T-shirt that says “question authority.” Bowers took the tagline and added it to Neon Spiderweb #3 (2026), a neon spiderweb with the words sitting on the outer string of a web—the artist also made three other neon works with such slogans: “A better end of the world is possible,” “Return our future” and “To be free from fear.” The political slogans of hope pour over into Political Ribbons (2026), brightly colored sheets of silky ribbons that repeat the mottos on the neon signs or feature new sayings: “People over profits,” “Outlaw women are the only hope for earth’s survival” and “Widen the web to break the nuclear chain.” Visitors are urged to take ribbons from the wall with them when they visit the show. Bowers explains that it’s a “simple way for people to take a position.” It’s a methodology that she’s used before, adding that to this day, she still gets a thrill out of seeing them in the wild or online.

Bowers’ pieces in the show command attention no matter their size. Her graphite on paper works: Diabloblockade, Diablo Nuclear Power Plant, Abalone Alliance, 198 (2003) and Women’s Pentagon Action, 1981, Detail of Woven Web Around Pentagon (2003) are hyperrealistic. the artist has recreated photographs that were given to her by her mother’s friends who were part of the Californian eco-group Mother for Peace. In Cardboard (2026), a larger-than-life cardboard with an “empowered Italian woman” facing her back to the audience holding a flag that says “Sun and wind,” the artist taught herself how to use an iPad to draw the image that’s been projected onto a thick cardboard.

The show at the Fondazione Sandretto Re Rebaudengo is sort of a homecoming for Bowers, as she last showed at the foundation in 1998 as part of a group show titled “Guarene 98.” Some 28 years later, her pieces from the Chandeliers of Interconnectedness franchise grace the halls of the space as visitors make their way to other exhibitions by Nan Goldin or Joseph Yaeger. The show, however, feels cut short. Perhaps Bowers has more to say than can fit the space she was given. (It would have been a better idea to give her two galleries to really demonstrate two decades of hard passion for the environment.)

Still, she managed to make a mark with Neon Opland (2026), another neon sign that imitates a doodle from a La Ragnatela poster of a woman kicking a missile with the words “No ai missile” written beneath. It’s somewhat phallic, a comical touch made more apparent by teenage couple who were flirting on a bench opposite its neon lights.
