Humanity braces for an alien invasion in 3 Body Problem S2 teaser

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Wake up! They haven’t come 24 trillion miles to negotiate. They are coming to exterminate us.”

You might be excused for forgetting about the existence of 3 Body Problem, Netflix’s heady sci-fi series adapted from the award-winning novel The Three-Body Problem by Liu Cixin, the first book in his Remembrance of Earth’s Past trilogy. After all, it debuted in March 2024. But it was an exceptionally well-done, thematically rich series, and we’re finally getting the second of a planned three seasons in January. Netflix just dropped the first teaser.

Created by David Benioff, D.B. Weiss (Game of Thrones), and Alexander Woo (True Blood), the first season followed two primary timelines. In the earlier one, a young Chinese astrophysicist, Ye Wenjie (Rosalind Chao), witnessed her father being beaten to death by Red Guards during the Cultural Revolution. Ye figured out a means of sending an interstellar message to possible extraterrestrial civilizations, receiving a response from a planet called Trisolaris.

The planet had three suns, which wreaked havoc on Trisolaris via unpredictable “chaotic periods”—hence the novel’s title, which refers to a classic problem in celestial mechanics. Despite being warned that the aliens intended to invade and conquer Earth, Ye responded to the message and invited them to do so, bitter and disillusioned by the state of the world. She eventually became an established professor (Zine Tseng played the older version).

In the present day, the consequences of Ye’s fateful decision began to play out, beginning with a series of mysterious suicides by scientists, many of whom had possessed a highly advanced VR gaming headset. Among the dead: a particle physicist named Vera Ye (daughter of Ye Wenjie). Five of her former students (known as the Oxford Five) joined forces to figure out what was going on, eventually finding themselves caught in a global fight to save Earth from the invading Trisolarians (or San-Ti). Another human cult faction was just as intent on enabling the San-Ti invasion, believing they would be spared as a reward, while Ye Wenjie still believed the entire human race deserved to be annihilated.

The second season will adapt the second novel in the series, The Dark Forest, with the third book, Death’s End, serving as source material for the third season. Per the official S2 synopsis: “As the alien invasion nears, humanity prepares to meet them—on Earth and beyond. While a space fleet embarks on a mission that spans centuries, a secret locked inside of one man’s mind might be humanity’s only hope for survival.”

We lost two members of the Oxford Five in S1: Jack Rooney (John Bradley), killed by cult member Tatiana (Marlo Kelly); and physics teacher Will Downing (Alex Sharp), who was dying of cancer and chose to euthanize himself and donate his cryogenically frozen brain to a bold effort to stave off the invasion. (While the probe carrying Will’s brain veered off-course in the season finale, the original plan was for the San-Ti to use the brain to reconstruct his body, so some version of Will could show up again.)

Kelly’s Tatiana is returning for S2, along with surviving Oxford Five members Jin Cheng (Jess Hong), Saul Durand (Jovan Adepo), and Auggie Salazar (Eiza Gonzalez), whose pioneering nanofibers were memorably used in S1 to slice through a ship in the Panama Canal, killing all the pro-alien cult members on board in gruesome fashion. Also returning are Liam Cunningham as Thomas Wade; Benedict Wong as MI6 agent Clarence “Da” Shi; Sea Shimooka as Sophon, an avatar in the show’s mysterious VR game; and Saamer Usmani as Jin’s Royal Navy officer boyfriend Prithviraj Varma.

New cast members include Claudia Doumit as Captain Van Rijn and Ellie de Lange as Ayla. In addition, Alfie Allen, David Yip, and Jordan Sunshine will appear in as-yet-undisclosed guest roles.

The second season of 3 Body Problem drops on Netflix on January 14, 2027.

‘Both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed’: Fed advisor Marc Andreessen on AI going from boom to bust

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Marc Andreessen got the last word at the Federal Reserve’s big AI conference in San Francisco on Monday, and he used it to tell the central bank that nearly everyone in the room was going to be let down.

“My line has been both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed,” the venture capitalist said at the San Francisco Fed, “specifically disappointed by the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact, and specifically the economic impact.”

Andreessen co-leads the Productivity and Jobs Task Force, which Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh set up to tell the central bank what super intelligence—“SI,” in the White House’s preferred term—will do to growth, prices and jobs. On Monday, Andreessen previewed what he’ll contribute to the group’s report. Without using the name, he described one of the oldest problems in economics: William Baumol’s “cost disease,” the reason flat-screen TVs get cheaper every year while hospital bills, tuition and rent go the other way. His case is that AI won’t cure it, and could even make it worse.

The Fed hasn’t quite laid out that outcome.

The task force is advisory, and Andreessen has no vote on interest rates. He co-leads it with Stanford economist Charles Jones and Microsoft executive Asha Sharma, one of five outside panels the Fed named in June to rethink how the central bank works. Its charge, per the Fed, is to assess the economic impact of “new general-purpose technologies, including super intelligence, to inform the Federal Reserve’s policy judgments.” The panels report to the rate-setting committee with recommendations due by year-end, though Warsh said in October that “some, but not all” would turn their homework in on time. The Fed’s task force page lists no ethics or recusal terms for the panels. Andreessen’s firm, Andreessen Horowitz, raised more than $15 billion in new funds in January, as venture firms raced to keep up with the AI boom.

Andreessen is also Silicon Valley’s best-known accelerationist. His 2023 “Techno-Optimist Manifesto” declared, “We believe in accelerationism,” which he defined as “the conscious and deliberate propulsion of technological development.” It listed @BasedBeffJezos, the pseudonymous account behind the “effective accelerationism,” or e/acc, movement, first among its “patron saints,” and it named the enemy: “Our enemy is stagnation.” When OpenAI’s board fired Sam Altman a month later, Andreessen posted simply “E/acc!” This is the man who told the Fed that stagnation might win out over an AI-powered industrial revolution on Monday.

Warsh opened the day by laying out three “illustrative scenarios” Fed board staff have begun to evaluate. In one, AI lifts productivity, the gains are broadly shared and the economy stays close to full employment. In another, growth still runs above its long-term average, but automation pushes unemployment higher. In the third, AI “could disappoint after significant capital investment and stock market gains,” stocks fall, the economy slows and it eventually recovers “without the productivity gains.”

All three turn on whether AI arrives in force. Andreessen’s version is duller: most of the economy just doesn’t let it in.

He splits the economy in two. The “fast sectors,” like consumer electronics, media and commodity goods, take up new technology quickly, and “what those sectors all have in common is they have rapidly falling prices.” The “slow sectors”—health care, education, housing, and law and government—“are not legally allowed to have rapid change, like literally not.”

The machine “cannot get an MD, cannot prescribe medication,” he said. “The machine cannot get admitted to the bar.” The slow sectors, in his telling, pair restricted supply with subsidized demand: you can’t easily build new housing or open new hospitals, and when voters revolt over the cost, Washington steps in with student loans and housing subsidies. “So, of course, prices can only go up.”

Then he followed it to its end. As prices crash in the fast sectors, they shrink as a share of GDP, and the slow sectors take over. Health care, he said, “is already a fifth of US GDP and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%.” (The government’s count is 18% in 2024, or $5.3 trillion, per CMS actuaries.)

“You might interpret this as good news because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is productivity growth just simply may not grow very much. Economic growth just simply may not grow very much. We may just continue to stagnate.”

Andreessen called himself “extremely optimistic,” saying that AI gives the country “a magic formula” and comparing it to being “handed this elixir to boost productivity growth.” But policy is a problem, he suggested. The decisions about what to do with AI, he said, “are going to matter actually way more than what the technology is capable of,” because without them “the technology will simply not get rolled out.”

That landed in a room where, three hours earlier, Warsh had brought up “secular stagnation,” the 1930s worry that economists returned to after the financial crisis, and asked whether superintelligence “dramatically” changes that picture.

Baumol, the late New York University and Princeton economist, worked this out in the 1960s, starting with the economics of the performing arts. A string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same playing time it did in Mozart’s day, but those musicians have to be paid wages set by an economy where factory workers keep getting more productive. So live music, and later education and medicine, keep getting more expensive relative to everything else, and keep taking a bigger share of spending.

In his 2012 book, The Cost Disease, Baumol projected that if health costs kept growing at their recent pace, they would rise from 15% of the average person’s income in 2005 to 62% by 2105. He was an optimist about it. “No matter how painful rising medical and educational bills may be, society can afford them,” he wrote, because productivity elsewhere makes everyone richer. The real threat, he added, was “foolish public policy.”

For Baumol, the disease was built into the work: you can’t speed up a string quartet. For Andreessen, it’s built into the law. That matters for the Fed, because an agency that sets interest rates can’t fix zoning boards or medical licensing.

The economist who has done the most to bring Baumol into the AI debate was in the audience. Jones, who is on leave at Anthropic, stayed quiet because the company is preparing to go public, as Andreessen remarked to the crowd. In a 2017 paper for the National Bureau of Economic Research, Jones, Philippe Aghion and Benjamin Jones argued that “growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve,” crediting the idea to Baumol.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly asked a panel of executives where their “weak link” was, and Erik Wexler, CEO of the nonprofit hospital system Providence, gave the slow-sector answer. “We’re part of the weak link,” he said. Providence operates in seven states, and as each writes its own AI rules, “it’s almost like sometimes being in seven different countries.”

Earlier on the same panel, though, Wexler complicated Andreessen’s picture. AI tools that listen in on appointments and write up the notes are saving some physicians “two to four hours of time a day,” he said, and doctors have called it “life-changing.” In 35 years in the industry, he said, he’d never heard them say that about anything else. So the hours are being saved inside a slow sector. The open question is whether they ever show up in the price of a hospital visit.

Andreessen’s forecast cuts against the case Warsh made before he got the job. In a Wall Street Journal op-ed late last year, Warsh wrote that AI “will be a significant disinflationary force, increasing productivity and bolstering American competitiveness,” as Axios reported. In speeches and writing, he argued AI-driven productivity could justify lower interest rates, an echo of Alan Greenspan’s Fed holding off on hikes in the late 1990s.

As chair, he has hedged. “My judgment is we have seen an improvement in productivity; we can’t measure it precisely,” he told reporters on Oct. 28, adding that “most of that change in productivity precedes everything we’re talking about—super intelligence.” The Fed held rates at 3.75% to 4% that day, and a reporter told him most of his colleagues “seem to be on board with raising rates in December.” Warsh declined to prejudge the meeting.

On Monday, Warsh didn’t push back on Andreessen. He restated the argument in Fed terms, as product prices versus services prices, and moved on. He closed the day “cautiously optimistic,” calling it “a hinge point for the US economy,” and said, “I’d rather have our problems than anyone else’s in the world.”

Andreessen has been making this case for years. In 2017 he warned that the slow sectors were “basically just going to eat the economy,” and in March 2023 he wrote that “AI is simply already illegal across most of the economy.” Back then the argument was a rebuttal to the job-loss doomers. On Monday, in front of the Fed chair, it came with the other half of the bargain: if the jobs are safe, the productivity boom may be stuck too.

For companies, the slow sectors are a line item. Workers now pay an average of $7,471 a year toward employer family health coverage, up 9% from 2025, according to KFF’s annual survey. That’s the part of the economy that, by Andreessen’s account, AI isn’t legally allowed to touch.

For the Fed, the question is what kind of disinflation AI brings, if any. Earlier in the day, Warsh asked a panel of economists whether AI might do to services what the China shock did to goods—“productizing services”—and push prices down. Stanford’s Nick Bloom said falling token costs made him “kind of bullish.” Stanford President Jonathan Levin gave Baumol’s answer without the name: health care and education are “the parts of the economy where the disinflationary pressures from automation have not hit,” where “the costs have continued to rise with wages.” Whether that changes depends on what people want, he said, “and there’s also regulation.”

If Andreessen is right, AI’s price cuts land mostly where prices were already falling, and services, where inflation has been hardest to bring down, stay out of reach.

Stripe CEO Patrick Collison sketched the range for the room. At one end sits the old complaint that new technology shows up “everywhere but in the productivity statistics,” a line that goes back to economist Robert Solow in 1987. At the other is a bet between two people Collison described as having “considerable influence” over AI, on whether global energy use doubles in a single month sometime in the next decade. Andreessen is betting on a third outcome: a decade where nothing that dramatic happens, and the prices Americans complain about most keep climbing.

Warsh has asked his task forces for recommendations by year-end, and the Fed’s next rate decision comes Dec. 9. Andreessen put the choice to the central bank as “a political economy” question: “what kind of pace of change do we want, how much productivity growth do we actually want?”

New 'Pokémon' TCG Lucario Set Leaks In Stores Months Before Official Release

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Dollar General customers claim they've purchased the unreleased Pokémon set from shelves

The Pokémon Trading Card Game's latest set, Delta Reign, just came out a couple of weeks ago in early November. The next set, Flowing Fists, isn't scheduled to drop for another few months. Most people haven't seen Delta Reign boxes in stores yet, never mind the recent Pokémon 30th Anniversary set. Despite all of this, real people have apparently already acquired packs of Flowing Fist. Heck, some people have already pulled the next set's chase card — which hasn't even been formally revealed yet!

Pokémon leaks happen all the time, but in many instances, they seem to be the result of a potential theft or some kind of warehouse shenanigans. Maybe a factory worker sneaked a picture, or the set was close enough that distribution had already begun behind the scenes. This case may not be the same, judging from reports on social media.

Pokémon fans have shared evidence of going into stores like Dollar General and buying several blister packs with a Tynamo promo card straight off the shelves. One alleged DG customer produced a receipt with the Flowing Fists purchase. While a receipt could ostensibly be faked, multiple other people have apparently also purchased packs at Dollar General locations.

At least one early set recipient attempted to sell a special rare illustration card from Flowing Fists featuring Mega Lucario Z-EX, a Pokémon that debuted in the Mega Dimension DLC for Pokémon Legends: Z-A. The Flowing Fists set is the fighting type's first appearance in the card game.

The listing has since been pulled from eBay, but it does give us a good look at what the next set has to offer. The card, which is illustrated by prolific TCG artist Rend, depicts Lucario in the middle of a jump. The collectible appears to have a new type of engraving detail on the left side of Lucario that emphasizes his Aura Seeker attack. The set as a whole is themed around the fan-favorite monster, and is known to include new cards for Riolu as well as double-rare EX cards for Lucario.

In one video where a TCG fan opens a Flowing Fists pack early, he pulls a Kubfu by Hyogonosuke, a Hattrem, a Wartortle, and a Weepinbell by Ikeda Saki.

Notably, none of these cards have been officially revealed yet by The Pokémon Company. So far, we've only seen reveals for monsters like Oricorio, Pawmi and Mega Absol Z-EX among others. While most cards from the Japanese version of the set are put in their Western counterparts, The Pokémon Company sometimes alters the set list regionally.

The early sales snafu does not appear to be widespread. Many speculate it may have been a distribution error — and one that will be fixed quickly. So don't expect to find a Flowing Fists pack in the wild, especially now that the Meowth is out of the bag!

Yet another round of layoffs has apparently gone down at Tomb Raider studio Crystal Dynamics, as developers report that they've been cut from the studio. This is at least the sixth round of layoffs since the studio last shipped an original game.

Multiple developers have posted on LinkedIn about the layoffs, as reported by Vertical Slice. Those include UI software engineer Garin R. K. Richards and senior game designer Joshua Mitchell. "After several rounds of layoffs at Crystal Dynamics, today it was my turn," Mitchell writes. "My first layoff in 14 years making games."

Also cut is senior environment technical artist Kevin Baker, who led the development of "procedural tools used extensively in the creation of Tomb Raider: Catalyst's game world," and "integrated Claude Code into procedural tool development." We already knew that Legacy of Atlantis is being made with AI, and it seems the studio is keen to use similar generative tech in Catalyst – though not keen enough to keep the people responsible for it employed, I guess.

Crystal Dynamics has not shipped a new game since the release of Marvel's Avengers in 2020. Since then, it was acquired by Embracer Group, a parent company that's undergone no shortage of financial upheaval in recent years. In 2023, Embracer insisted that Crystal Dynamics would be unaffected by the ongoing layoffs at the broader company, a few months before laying off people at the Tomb Raider studio.

Counting today's cuts, there have been five more rounds of layoffs since then. "The Social Reckoning" is finally hitting the home market. It will become available to buy or rent on digital starting November 24, 2026, prior to its physical media release on January 12, 2027.

Almost two decades after he won an Academy Award for writing the screenplay for "The Social Network," a 2010 dramatization of the founding of Facebook directed by David Fincher, Aaron Sorkin decided to make a sequel that focuses on what Facebook has become in the 2020s. Jesse Eisenberg, who earned an Oscar nod for playing Facebook founder Mark Zuckerberg in "The Social Network," declined to return to the role and was replaced by "Succession" veteran, Emmy and Tony winner, and Sorkin's "The Trial of the Chicago 7" collaborator Jeremy Strong. With Mikey Madison, Jeremy Allen White, Bill Burr, Wunmi Mosaku, Betty Gilpin, and Billy Magnussen starring alongside Strong, the project seemed almost too big to fail before it premiered in theaters on October 9, 2026.

Fail it did, though. Though it reportedly only lost a couple million for Sony (per Deadline), the film absolutely underperformed at the box office, and its rating on Rotten Tomatoes is a middling-to-bleak 54%. So what happened here? Well, from my perspective, "The Social Reckoning" is just a bad movie ... and /Film's chief film critic Chris Evangelista agrees with me. What went wrong as Sorkin tried to make a legacy sequel to one of the best films of the 21st century? Quite a lot actually!

I'll circle back to the "Social Network" of it all shortly, but the bottom line here is that "The Social Reckoning" just ... stinks to high heaven. From the embarrassing prologue featuring Portia Doubleday as a caricature named Kayleigh (I'm not kidding) to the fact that Aaron Sorkin keeps forcing Oscar winner Mikey Madison into outfits that would make her title character from "Anora" blush (in the name of corporate espionage), I found "The Social Network" to be a uniformly dreadful watch from start to finish. Not only is the script clunky as all hell — unfortunately, an ongoing problem with many of Sorkin's recent screenplays — but the story is attempting to make some sort of larger point that doesn't just fall flat but thuds directly to the floor. We already know social media makes everyone angry and insane, and we also know that social media has contributed to right-wing radicalization. Sorkin making a movie that beats us over the head with these two ideas and also ends in a full recreation of the January 6, 2021 insurrection is just as bad as it sounds in theory.

It's also incredible to me that Sorkin managed to cast a lot of great actors and coach them into giving some of the worst performances of their careers. Madison is an enigmatic and compelling performer, and Sorkin transforms her into the robotic Facebook whistleblower Frances Haugen, a deeply unlikable person who doesn't seem to understand human behavior. Jeremy Allen White's journalist Jeff Horwitz is nothing but an exposition machine, and Jeremy Strong's Mark Zuckerberg has no interiority and sounds like Kermit the Frog. The only person who's good in this movie is Bill Burr. Think about that for a second.

"The Social Network" might be one of my very favorite movies of all time, but I can tell you, from a completely unbiased perspective, that "The Social Reckoning" is a wholly undeserving sequel to that 2010 masterpiece. It's clear now, with plenty of hindsight and movies like this, that "The Social Network" was good because of director David Fincher and despite Sorkin. To be fair to Sorkin, he basically admitted that to The Hollywood Reporter after "The Social Reckoning" got soundly panned.

"I would have rather David Fincher directed it — I would rather David Fincher direct everything — but David has an exclusive deal at Netflix, and this was owned by Sony," Sorkin told the outlet on their "Awards Chatter" podcast. Not only did Sorkin also admit that he's "very worried that the parts of directing that [he's] not good at are those parts that you can't learn," he also said, "I was surprised by how little of the science of filmmaking I'd managed to absorb [from other directors] ... I have no visual sensibility, it turns out."

I have to agree with him. Despite the same cinematographer — Jeff Cronenweth — working on both "Network" and "Reckoning," the sequel is visually bland, unimaginatively shot, and even recycles a couple reaction and establishing shots throughout its run time. Basically, "The Social Reckoning" is a poorly made film that I cannot in good conscience recommend; in fact, I'd recommend you just watch "The Social Network" instead. But if you're still interested, you can check out "The Social Reckoning" at home once it's available.

Wales will be without Neco Williams after the right-back picked up an injury in the defeat in Norway, while goalkeeper Karl Darlow is a doubt. Key midfielder Ethan Ampadu is suspended after picking up a second booking against Norway. Centre-back Ben Cabango and winger Sorba Thomas will be available after missing out in Oslo through suspension.

There is pride on the line for Wales in Cardiff this evening as they prepare to host Portugal to conclude their Nations League campaign.

Craig Bellamy’s side know they will be finishing bottom of Group A4, following Saturday’s 3-1 defeat to Norway in Oslo.

But they can begin to prepare for a Nations League relegation play-off in March as they host one of the world’s best sides in Portugal.

The visitors started life without Cristiano Ronaldo in the worst possible way on Saturday, losing 4-1 to Denmark.
