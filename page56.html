Gun Rights and Guilt by Association

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Some excerpts from the longish opinion today in Shilling v. County of San Diego, decided today by Ninth Circuit Judge Patrick Bumatay, joined by Judges Kenneth Lee and Jennifer Sung:

The San Diego County Sheriff revoked Kenneth Shilling's concealed-carry weapon ("CCW") license over his affiliation with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club…. [We conclude, among other things, that] the district court improperly dismissed his First Amendment [freedom of association] claim….

Kenneth Charles Shilling resides in San Diego, California. He's a member of the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which, according to Shilling, was founded in 1946 by World War II veterans. In his view, it's not like some other motorcycle clubs, which are known for criminal and gang activity. Instead, the Boozefighters focus on charitable work and the "core values of family, work, and community." Shilling's record is clean; he has no criminal history.

In June 2022, when the "good moral character" requirement [for getting a concealed caryr license] still existed [under California law], Shilling applied for and received a CCW license from then-San Diego County Sheriff Anthony Ray. This was short lived. Less than a year and a half later, in December 2023, a different San Diego County Sheriff, Kelly Martinez, used the "good moral character" requirement to revoke Shilling's CCW license. In a letter notifying Shilling of the revocation, Sheriff Martinez explained that the "San Diego Sheriff's Department['s] good moral character definition" excludes a person who:

Has a pattern of or propensity for criminal conduct, as demonstrated by anything in the application or investigation for a license. This includes, but is not limited to, evidence of criminal behavior or a close nexus to a criminal gang. In making this determination, the Sheriff's Department shall use an objective inquiry, considering the facts and circumstances known at the time of the application or through the investigation.

The letter said that Shilling's license was revoked due to his association with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which the Sheriff described as a "criminal street gang." Shilling was not given the opportunity to contest the allegations before his license was revoked, but he could request reconsideration of the decision. The new CCW regime with the "dangerousness" requirement took effect on January 1, 2024, shortly after Shilling's license was revoked….

Here's the court's analysis allowing Shilling's First Amendment freedom of association claim to go forward:

The First Amendment guarantees all Americans the "right to associate with others." … [T]he right protects groups of individuals "in pursuit of a wide variety of political, social, economic, educational, religious and cultural ends." …

The government may infringe the freedom of association in "varied ways," including by "punish[ing] individuals for their affiliations." The government cannot impose "criminal sanctions or deny[ ] rights and privileges solely because of a citizen's association with an unpopular organization." For example, a group of students could not be denied the "benefits" of official campus recognition at a state college merely because of its affiliation with a national organization associated with disruptive and violent campus activity…. [Likewise,] "Civil liability may not be imposed merely because an individual belonged to a group, some members of which committed acts of violence." … If the government seeks to deprive an individual of a benefit, privilege, or right based on association with an alleged criminal organization, "it is necessary to establish that the group itself possessed unlawful goals and that the individual held a specific intent to further those illegal aims." …

Given this understanding of the First Amendment, Shilling easily pleaded a violation of his freedom of association…. [His] allegations sufficiently raise an infringement of Shilling's free-association right because the Sheriff deprived him of a benefit, privilege, or right—the ability to carry a concealed weapon in public—"solely because of [his] association with an unpopular organization." Unless the Sheriff adequately meets her burden of showing the Boozefighters "possessed unlawful goals" and Shilling's "specific intent to further those illegal aims," she cannot "punish [Shilling] for [his] affiliations." This kind of "guilt by association alone, without establishing that an individual's association poses the threat feared by the Government, is an impermissible basis upon which to deny First Amendment rights."

In dismissing this claim, the district court misunderstood the nature of the free-association right. It asserted that Shilling had to show the revocation of his CCW license somehow prevented him from associating with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club or participating in the Club's activities. While preventing an individual from participating in a group's lawful activities may be one way to violate the First Amendment right, it's infringed in "varied ways." And "denying" an individual benefits, rights, or privileges "solely because of [his] association"—as Shilling alleges—is certainly another way….

For the court's analysis of sovereign immunity and of whether Schilling's Second Amendment claim was moot, see the full opinion.

Gary W. Gorski (Veterans Law Center) represents plaintiff.

Things look bleak for Warriors, but they can survive this

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Warriors are in free fall, losing five games in a row, including their last four by 20 or more points.

Golden State Warriors guard Stephen Curry, right, confers with head coach Steve Kerr during an NBA game in Denver, Monday, Oct. 26, 2026. AP Photo/David Zalubowski

Of the 15 Western Conference teams, only 11 are going to have a real shot at securing the 10 playoff spots unless something drastically changes.

In other words, the West cellar is so weak that even if the Warriors can't arrest their precipitous slide without Steph Curry, they'll have more than enough time to recover when he returns.

So, while what's happening to the Warriors is unfortunate, it's far from catastrophic.

Curry is expected to be sidelined close to a month after suffering a right oblique abdominal tear on Nov. 11.

The 38-year-old had carried the Warriors to eight straight wins while playing some of the best basketball of his career, averaging 27.8 points (50% shooting from the field, 45.7% from beyond the arc), 3.7 rebounds and 3.9 assists per game.

Before Curry sustained the injury, he and Yaxel Lendeborg were looking like one of the NBA's best duos. Lendeborg, whom the Warriors drafted with the No. 11 overall pick in June, was having a Rookie of the Year campaign, averaging 21.6 points on 52.2% shooting.

But with defenses collapsing on him without Curry, the rookie has wilted, averaging 13.7 points on 26.8% shooting over the last four games.

Not only is Curry going to be back in the lineup soon, but Jimmy Butler (torn right ACL) is expected to return midseason.

The Warriors could still be really good. They could still have a deep playoff run. They could still be a contender.

This has been a roller coaster for Warriors fans.

Entering the season, it was questionable whether the Warriors would make the playoffs after they pretty much stood pat during the offseason. And after dropping their first three games, it seemed all but guaranteed that this was going to be a wasted season for Curry, a two-time MVP who carried the Warriors to four championships in eight years from 2015-2022.

Lendeborg emerged as his sidekick. He sprinted past his expectations. He became a force.

Yaxel Lendeborg, left, and Golden State Warriors head coach Steve Kerr smile during a game against the Houston Rockets on Wednesday, Nov. 11, 2026, in Houston. NBAE via Getty Images

All of a sudden, Curry and Lendeborg were stomping on their competition, beating contenders such as the Lakers, Timberwolves and Nuggets over a stunning stretch that had pundits questioning whether the Warriors were championship contenders.

But the short-handed Warriors were always one Curry injury away from disaster.

And when their nightmare was realized, the Warriors went from being one of the league's most fun teams to one of its biggest duds.

Curry and Butler will return, and the Warriors will have more than enough runway to correct course.

So the excitement the team was generating a little over a week ago?

Steelers-Eagles preview: What to expect from Philadelphia’s offense, defense Week 11

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Steelers will travel to Philadelphia — a city where they haven't won since 1965 — on Sunday in a game that many fans have had circled as an Eagles victory since before the season.

However, the 2026 Eagles aren't the same team that made the playoffs every year from 2021-25. They enter Week 11 with a dismal 2-7 record, with all those losses coming in a row against the Bears, Rams, Jaguars, Panthers, Cowboys, Commanders and Giants. Philadelphia hasn't been in the win column since a Sept. 20 victory over the Titans.

Is this Eagles team finally bad enough for the Steelers to break such a lengthy losing streak in The City of Brotherly Love? Or will Philadelphia, fresh off a Week 10 bye, be able to hold off the Steelers at home for yet another season? We'll find out in Week 11.

RB: It's been a rough year for the Eagles offense, now on its third quarterback of the season ahead of Week 11's matchup versus the Steelers. The numbers above speak for themselves: Philadelphia has struggled on both the ground and in the air in 2026, dealing with injuries, the sudden retirement of right tackle Lane Johnson, and poor execution from players on the field. The Eagles have only scored over 20 points four times this season, and have yet to break 30 once.

Before a Week 10 bye, the Eagles faced the New York Giants in Week 9, falling 19-11 in an ugly game that's been all too familiar for the Philly faithful. But one thing did change, with the Eagles trying to spark their Jalen Hurts-less offense by replacing backup Tanner McKee with Joe "Bazooka" Milton at quarterback. He's expected to start again against Pittsburgh on Sunday.

The results were not great against the Giants, with Milton finishing with 169 passing yards and 18/34 completion in Week 9. He didn't throw a single touchdown or interception, and was sacked a whopping seven times. However, the guy with the "Bazooka" nickname does indeed have a powerful arm, with Milton logging an 11.8% deep throw percentage along with eight air yards per attempt (both team highs for the Eagles this season). He's also a much better runner than McKee, leading the Eagles with 64 yards on the ground and a 7.1 average against New York.

Frankly, Milton plays like a still-developing quarterback. He holds onto the ball too long and doesn't seem to go through his reads at NFL speed. However, his arm talent does allow him to rifle balls into open zones when he sees them, and there's always a little bit of worry when playing a passer with his physical abilities — even if everything only clicks a few times a game, he can still make defenses pay. It's worth noting that Pittsburgh hasn't played many highly-mobile quarterbacks this year, and has had some issues with deep coverage this season.

With Milton under center, the Eagles have mixed up their playbook from McKee a bit with a few more rollouts and designed quarterback runs. What hasn't changed, however, is that Philadelphia remains an 11-personnel heavy team that likes short throws outside the numbers to move the ball in the passing game. Making matters worse is that despite the short passing game, Philadelphia ranks dead-last in the league in yards after catch in 2026, with just 738 on the year.

First-round receiver Makai Lemon is a name to watch as a twitchy underneath target, and Milton will get to throw to the now-healthy Devonta Smith for the first time this season after the latter missed the Giants game with injury. That's a big boost to what was a decimated Philly receiving core in Week 9. However, Dontayvion Wicks will still be out for the Eagles against Pittsburgh.

Capping the Eagles' offensive ceiling has been a rough year from the line, which lost All-World right tackle Lane Johnson to retirement in October and has struggled with some regression. Jordan Mailata remains a very good left tackle and Philadelphia now has Landon Dickerson back from injured reserve, but the Eagles still rank seventh-highest in sack rate allowed (9%), third-highest in pressure rate allowed (41.9%), and second-highest in runs stuffed percentage (23.5%) despite facing the seventh-most light boxes in the league.

Having inexperienced signal-callers since Hurts landed on injured reserve certainly hasn't helped the Eagles in pass protection. Philadelphia struggled mightily with interior pressure against the Giants, and gave up a number of unblocked rushers on stunts and twists.

One name to watch is rookie right tackle Markell Bell, a massive human being who has struggled some in pass protection this year, but is a promising run blocker.

Still, on the ground, the Eagles' inside zone-heavy attack is tied for 28th in the NFL in yards per rush (3.7), with just 28 non-Milton yards on the ground against New York. However, star running back Saquon Barkley, who played through injury against the Giants, should be rested up after the Eagles' bye and looking a bit more explosive. In two of Barkley's first three games this season, he averaged 5.5 yards per carry — when healthy, the 29-year-old is still a legitimate offensive weapon.

After a nightmare game against the Bengals last week, the Pittsburgh defense matches up much better against Philadelphia. The key will be stifling the Eagles' run game and pressuring Milton, which the Steelers' front seven is certainly capable of, especially with Derrick Harmon back in play on the interior. Milton averaged 3.12 seconds to throw last week — that should give TJ Watt more time to get home against a rookie right tackle.

Still, with Devonta Smith back in the lineup for the Eagles, along with solid ancillary weapons in Lemon and tight end Dallas Goedert available, a Pittsburgh secondary that got torched last week will still need to bring a better performance to the table. That said, there should be a lot more optimism heading into this week than the last for the Steelers' defense.

RP: Much like the Eagles' offense this year, the defensive side of the ball still possesses a lot of talent, but has performed well below expectations. A unit that was the driving force behind a Super Bowl victory just two seasons ago, the Eagles seemed poised to field a young and imposing defense that would keep them contenders for the rest of the 2020s. Instead, they were sellers at the deadline, shipping away defensive tackle Jordan Davis, whom they'd signed to a three-year, $78 million extension this March.

I spoke with Brandon Gowton of Bleeding Green Nation this week, to try to get a sense of why the defense has underperformed, and he said it's been one of the more baffling developments this season. He cited a lingering injury to superstar defensive tackle Jalen Carter and a general lack of pass rush as the biggest factor. Whether or not Carter is living up to the extension he signed this past summer (four years, $152 million) is seemingly a hot topic among Eagles fans and media, but a Steelers offensive line that just lost Mason McCormick should still be plenty concerned about his presence. Even in a "down" year, Carter's five sacks are only two off the NFL lead for defensive tackles.

As a defense, the Eagles' 16 sacks on the season rank 28th, and their 109 pressures rank 26th. According to Next Gen Stats, the Eagles are below league average in blitz rate (26% vs 32%), and in generating pressure, whether that's with the blitz (41% vs 43%) or without (30% vs 33%).

Carter's 20 pressures this season are tied for second on the Eagles behind only Moro Ojomo (22), an upcoming defensive tackle who allowed the Eagles the flexibility to trade away Davis. Jalyx Hunt, a rotational pass rusher who hasn't played more than 43% of the defensive snaps in a game since Week 4, is tied with Carter. Nolan Smith (17 pressures, 5 sacks), Jonathan Greenard (11 pressures, 1 sack), and Zack Baun (9 pressures, 2 sacks) round out the Eagles' underperforming pass rush.

Baun, the Eagles' top off-ball linebacker, is out for the game, the team announced Friday.

With the lack of pressure, the Eagles' secondary has predictably struggled. Third-year nickel corner Cooper DeJean has taken a step back, allowing four touchdowns this season after allowing just one during his first two seasons, and he has nearly as many missed tackles this season (13) as he had in his career (19) before this season. Meanwhile, Riq Woolen remains a volatile corner who has looked like a shutdown player some weeks and like a weak link in others, including surrendering four touchdowns against zero interceptions so far this season.

If there is one bright spot in Philadelphia's secondary, it's cornerback Quinyon Mitchell, who is still performing at a high level. He's allowed just a 55.6% catch rate and a QB passer rating of 76.5 when targeted. Philadelphia has typically let Mitchell stick to one side of the field, but in their most recent game, the Eagles finally tasked Mitchell with shadowing an opponent's number one receiver. Against the Giants, Mitchell followed Malik Nabers and shut down New York's most explosive playmaker.

As for what coverages we can expect from Philadelphia, the Eagles are a team that has lived in both nickel (68% of defensive snaps) packages and single-high safety looks (54% of snaps) for multiple seasons now. That has most typically shown up as Cover-3 looks in zone coverages (36.5%) and Cover-1 in man looks (15.3%).

On the ground, the Eagles aren't quite as bad as the numbers might indicate. While the Eagles' raw run defense totals look poor, they're mostly in the middle of the pack in most metrics, and are surrendering a high yardage per game total mostly because they have been in negative game scripts for much of the season. Philadelphia allows the seventh-fewest average yards before contact (0.81); however, they also allow the eighth-highest average yards after contact (3.33). As a unit, they've missed 43 tackles on running plays, per PFF, but only DeJean (8) and safety Andrew Mukuba (6) are particularly heinous in this regard. No other defender has more than three missed tackles against the run. Philadelphia ranks 15th in stuffed run percentage (17%), but are tied with the Vikings and Patriots for the 13th-most runs of 10+ yards allowed (27).

In theory, the Steelers' strength — running the ball — should fare respectably against this Eagles front. However, the loss of Mason McCormick cannot be overstated. Spencer Anderson takes his spot and has shown a troubling deficiency in play strength and power thus far in his career. He's excelled on the edges as a sixth offensive lineman, where he can take on lighter and less powerful defenders. Having him line up against Carter, though, gives me pause. Meanwhile, the Eagles' secondary should be gettable, but Rodgers has not played well this season. The Eagles haven't gotten pressure consistently, but Rodgers has often found his way into pressure even when the line is holding well. Now, without McCormick, there is potentially an easier path for the Philadelphia defensive line to get into the face of Pittsburgh's mobility-challenged passer.

This could be another frustrating day for the Black and Gold offense.

It maybe doesn’t work as well when written down. Hogh – pronounced who - is clearly a good sport. However, as Martin O’Neill has said on a number of occasions, and repeated on Friday at Lennoxtown, at some point strikers need to start scoring goals.

Of course, Hogh thought he had finally bagged his first home goal in the hoops two weekends ago. Nygren set him up with a neat pass into the Dane’s path and seconds later the net billowed. Hogh celebrated with understandable gusto before the dismaying news that it was being ruled out for an innocuous foul in the build-up. “For him, that was a killer,” admitted O’Neill on the eve of the latest home game against St Mirren.

All eyes will be on the team sheet. Will Hogh - yet to score after eight Parkhead appearances - play? Having been dropped from the Danish squad for their most recent games against Portugal and Norway, the striker was given some time off in the recent international window.

“He has been here, we have given him some time….he has had no break at all because where he came from (Norway) they were playing matches throughout (the summer),” explained O’Neill.

The manager made an extraordinary – to use one his favourite words - admission following the recent game against Kilmarnock, when Nygren wasted a spot kick. He claimed he would not have let Hogh take the penalty, which visiting keeper Nico Mantl saved, if the Dane had been on the park at the time.

Despite the ever more urgent need for Hogh to break his duck at home, O’Neill clearly felt the potential mental harm from failing to convert from 12 yards would have been too great a risk. The game, with Celtic winning 1-0 at the time, was also on a knife edge. A pressure penalty might not have been what Hogh needed.

It will be interesting to see what happens against St Mirren if Celtic are awarded another penalty and Hogh is available to take it. One might expect the Dane to demand to be given the ball and put this ridiculous issue to bed. Adding to the intrigue is that Nygren, who has an Achilles knock picked up on international duty with Sweden, might not play. Not that O’Neill trusts him from the spot now in any case.

The manager was not a fan of Nygren’s penalty technique against Kilmarnock. “All I will say is that if Nygren plays, which he may or may not, he won’t take it!” he said. “All this (stuttered) walking up….why not just do an Alan Shearer and blast it into the net!? If you hit the goalkeeper, you’ll take his head off!”

Non-scoring strikers is a theme just now given Oli McBurnie’s continued struggles at international level for Scotland. After just being subbed on, one wondered whether Sebastien Pocognoli might have ordered him to take the penalty against North Macedonia at Hampden Park last week. Again, with the manager’s future prospects possibly resting on the kick, maybe not. John McGinn did the necessary and Pocognoli survives, for the time being.

Hogh won’t want any handouts. He’s a centre forward who thrives on goals. He will feel capable of working it out for himself. No charity, please.

As shown against Kilmarnock, he’s not far away. He missed one very good chance shortly after his chalked-off goal when one-on-one with Mantl. “If he had the first goal he would have taken that on another yard,” insisted O’Neill. “But that looked like a man in a real hurry to try and get his first (Celtic Park) goal.”

Easy going and charming, there are still limits to what Hogh can endure. He wouldn’t be human if it wasn’t having an effect.

“He seems philosophical about it at this minute,” O’Neill reported. “He thinks if he is working really hard for the side then he can get in….he should have taken a tip or two from Duran!”

Camilo Duran is the opposite. With 13 goals in 21 appearances, he has made a flying start to his Celtic career.

Or maybe the aforementioned Larsson can make a visit. Like Hogh, the Swede was not an immediate hit at Parkhead.

O’Neill himself suggested Larsson could have some input - “The best thing for me to do is bring Henrik Larsson over and show him how to hit the ball into the net!” - when the discussion turned to players who initially laboured under great expectations.

Perhaps the late Trevor Francis, his old teammate, was one too? O’Neill was at Nottingham Forest when the striker arrived at the City Ground to become Britain’s first £1 million player. Talk about pressure.

“He took a couple of weeks,” remembered O’Neill. “It didn’t help that he had just been signed for a million pounds and Brian Clough told him to make the tea for everyone, which he did!

“First match, he made the tea for us in the dressing room! That wasn’t exactly what Trevor was expecting.” Hogh, who cost eleven times what Clough paid for Francis, has not been made to suffer such indignities - yet.

What can AI learn from a thousand days of hearing what a baby hears?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Princeton recorded eight babies for their first 1,000 days, and a model trained on one child's audio learned language on the same schedule.

A toddler can pick up hundreds of words just by hearing people talk. Could a computer start from scratch and recognize many of those same words on roughly the same timetable?

Researchers at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute wired up 15 homes with cameras and microphones and recorded babies for most of their waking hours across the first 1,000 days of life.

The researchers turned speech recorded around eight children into clean audio using synthetic voices.

They trained a separate model on each child’s audio in the order the speech was originally recorded.

The models identified recurring sound patterns that corresponded to words.

On average, each model reached the halfway point on a vocabulary checklist within 22 days of the child it was paired with.

“We wanted to turn the usual logic of developmental research around,” said Uri Hasson, a professor of psychology and neuroscience at Princeton who led the study.

“Instead of observing a child for an hour in the laboratory, we asked whether we could build a model capable of learning and immerse it in the child’s actual experience, day after day.”

The families were in New Jersey and spanned a range of incomes and backgrounds. Each home got four to 14 recording devices spread across two to five rooms.

The devices ran 12 to 14 hours a day, from February 2022 to May 2025.

The project gathered roughly 1.2 million hours of audio and video, of which the eight homes analyzed in this paper supplied about 600,000. An automated pipeline worked out where the child was in the house and pulled out the speech happening nearby.

Each child ended up with between 1,900 and 6,600 hours of usable speech, three to seven and a half hours a day. That adds up to 6 to 27 million words per child, over a median of 960 recording days.

Every month from eight months on, caregivers filled out a standard checklist of which words their child understood and said. Those forms are how the researchers knew what each child knew, and when.

Children reached half of the 649-word checklist anywhere from about 12 to 22 months old. That ten-month spread between children is the thing most studies average away.

The model started from random numbers. It had no dictionary, no phonetics, no notion of where one word ends and the next begins, and no text.

The program’s first part chops the incoming sound into 20-millisecond pieces and sorts them into categories it invents for itself. Its second part tries to guess the next piece from what came before.

Whenever a run of pieces becomes reliably predictable, the program files it away as a unit, which is the closest thing it has to a word.

Nothing about that design is specific to English or even to speech. What makes it unusual is the schedule.

The audio arrives in the order it was recorded, one day at a time, rather than shuffled into batches the way an AI language model is normally trained.

At the end of each simulated day, the program gets a sample of speech from earlier days, weighted toward recent ones. The researchers call it replay.

English has 39 phonemes, the sound units that distinguish bat from pat. Children spend their first year tuning in to the ones their own language uses.

Within roughly 12 to 15 months of simulated time, the program had carved out dedicated categories for nearly all of them.

Across the eight children, the models averaged 37.5 phonemes each. The lowest count came from the household with the least recording.

The order it happened in, sounds before words, is the order infants follow.

Words came after. For the child the paper calls Coral, the checklist said she understood 97 words at 12 months, and her model had 91.

Across all eight children, the models and the children hit the same vocabulary milestones within an average of 22 days of each other, with the worst case 56 days.

The models also reproduced the differences between children, landing close to each child’s own halfway age.

They did not match perfectly. The models tended to start learning words a little late and then catch up faster, so the shape of the curve was only partly right.

The surprise in the paper is what happened when the researchers took replay away.

Coral’s household produced something like 10 million words a year, which is a rich environment by any measure. Hearing all of it once, in order, wasn’t enough. With a single pass the program barely learned words at all.

Around 25 to 30 passes over each day’s material was enough, and more than that added very little.

The researchers link this to what sleep is thought to do for memory. A program replaying audio files is not a sleeping infant, and the paper is careful about that.

The amount of recording mattered just as much. Fed only a tenth of Coral’s daily speech, under an hour a day, the program learned none of the checklist words by 24 months.

At 30% of the day, about two and a half hours, it learned 80 of the 649 checklist words.

At 70% it got about nine-tenths of the way to the full result. The rarer words, it seems, only turn up for a listener who is there most of the day.

One limitation shapes everything else. Household recordings are full of clattering dishes, television, and two people talking at once, and the program was not asked to cope with any of that.

Instead the researchers transcribed all the speech around each child, then had a text-to-speech system read the transcripts back as clean audio.

Each household got a mix of eight adult female voices, with 30% of segments read in a voice matched to the child’s mother.

So the word order, the timing and the vocabulary are real, and the sound isn’t. The acoustic problem a baby actually solves, picking one voice out of a noisy room, was left for later work.

Three of the eight children also had shorter recordings, 14 to 20 months of usable input, so their later trajectories are extrapolated rather than modeled.

The program also sees nothing: no faces, no pointing, no object being handed over, which is a large part of how toddlers work out what a word refers to.

The program learned sounds and words. It did not learn grammar, and whether the same machinery would arrive at sentence structure is the open question the authors name.

The team wants to use the setup for questions nobody can put to a real baby. What would less sleep do, or screen speech instead of a parent’s?

Future studies could also add eye contact and the rest of the social world the cameras captured but the model never saw.

Hadas Raviv, a computational cognitive scientist in Princeton’s psychology department who built the analysis pipeline, framed the goal as the opposite of the usual one in artificial intelligence.

“Unlike much of modern AI, which aims for superhuman performance, we’re trying to understand how humans learn and how that learning gives rise to intelligence,” Raviv said.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

The Couple Created a Giant Sunflower Walkway For Their Multicultural Sun Valley Wedding

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Parker Selman and Karan Rai’s love story was born from a sunset. “Our paths crossed a few times before, but the first time we remember speaking was on a roof as we watched the sun go down our senior year at Tufts University,” Parker recalls. A few weeks later, they found themselves alone on a dance floor. “I looked down at my phone to see it was midnight and, therefore, officially my birthday,” Parker says. “I told him he should give me a kiss because it was my birthday, and he did. We were both smitten, and constantly in one another's orbit.” Despite the two lining up jobs on opposite coasts, they quickly realized being practical was not an option, and Karan managed to convince his company to transfer him to New York.

The couple got engaged on a crisp autumn walk around Governors Island—Parker’s favorite spot in the city. She owns and operates a wedding photography business, and as they strolled along, she asked to take a picture of Karan on her medium-format camera with their apartment building and skyline in the background. “While I was snapping the photo, he got down on one knee,” she says, “and I had the privilege of capturing my own proposal.” They rode the ferry back to Manhattan and went to Della’s, a local wine bar, where their families and friends were waiting to celebrate.

Two months later, she proposed back. “While we were walking in Sun Valley, I requested he take a picture of me sitting in the snow with our dog Grumpy,” she says. "I proposed the same way he proposed to me, the moment captured on my Rolleiflex.” Parker used diamonds and emeralds from her late great-grandmother’s engagement ring and gold from Karan’s late grandmother’s earrings to create his signet ring.

Their multi-day wedding unfolded in early September 2026 near Sun Valley, Idaho (where Parker’s family lives). They chose Central Idaho 4-H Camp, located 30 minutes outside of town, for its series of cabins and a large field up in the mountains. “The space was a blank canvas with no sound ordinance,” she says. “Studio Sully transformed the campsite into something magical.”

Going into the planning process, the couple knew they wanted the entire event to feel like a fusion of their cultures. In the days leading up to the Sun Valley celebration, Karan’s parents threw a series of traditional Indian wedding ceremonies and celebrations in his hometown of Pleasanton, California. Once guests arrived in Idaho, they all checked in at The Sun Valley Lodge, where each day the couple had organized group activities.

Festivities kicked off on Friday with a Western-themed welcome party at their favorite après-ski bar, Grumpy’s. Parker wore a tweed corset and skirt by Wiederhoeft accessorized with brown leather Manolo Blahniks and a wooden horse purse by Vin. When the weather cooled down, she tossed on a vintage suede fringe RRL jacket. Karan followed a similar script and wore a Bode suede fringe jacket and One of These Days pants with a painted horse and cowboy detail on the knees. His bandana was made by his mother. “She created a piece inspired by Marie Lichtenberg’s jeweled bandana, utilizing earrings and jewelry of Karan’s grandmothers, mother, and sister,” Parker says.

Guests were encouraged to wear textiles, paisley, and eclectic patterns to evoke a Wild West meets India atmosphere. In one corner, Boise Thread Studio set up shop to embroider initials onto trucker hats, shirts, and bandanas. Meanwhile, guests drank schooners (the name for Grumpy’s beer chalices) on the patio and ate burgers and hot dogs. “Karan and I named our beloved miniature dachshund Grumpy after the bar’s corn dogs, so it is only fitting that we ordered them until they ran out,” Parker says. Her mother welcomed the group with a sunset toast before moving inside. “We took over the electronic jukebox and closed down the restaurant until Karan had to stand up on the booth to usher everyone out the door.”

The next afternoon they hosted a sangeet by the Big Wood River. “The estate has a series of winding paths that lead to the riverside, and we wanted the afternoon portion to feel like a mountain garden party with activities and new discoveries tucked into each corner,” Parker says. The flowing river and live music by Boise-based Ashley Rose Band served as the soundtrack as guests played croquet, horseshoes, and badminton. Near the band, Henna by Sanjana created patterns on guests' hands.

Parker wore Danielle Frankel’s cream Mariela dress featuring two layers of hand-painted wildflowers. “I wanted a long braid with flowers woven in, and Jasmine Burnside helped bring the vision to life,” she says. Karan wore a light blue Jatin Malik sherwani with juttis from his personal collection.

As the sun set, guests were escorted to a clearing where a buffet was set up for dinner, and the dance floor was built around a large willow tree. The vignette was inspired by a 1905 Impressionist painting called Nighttime Festivities that depicts a turn-of-the-century party beneath the trees of a French palace. “I told Melissa [Sully] I wanted the sangeet to be based on this and we became obsessed with bringing the painting to life,” Parker explains. Lights and flowers were strung into the branches that draped overhead. Karan’s parents and Parker’s best friend, Ana, gave speeches before a series of traditional dances were performed by various family members and friends, followed by a lively dance floor courtesy of DJ Anupya.

The morning of the wedding, Parker and Karan got ready with their families and friends. “We are terrible at surprises, and he had seen my dress many times before, so I saw no purpose in a first look,” she says. The couple set off in a convertible (Parker’s hair protected in a vintage scarf from the year she was born), stopping at a vista for photos before arriving at the venue.

For her wedding look, Parker worked with Patricia Voto of One/Of to create a custom gown inspired by Christian Dior’s 1955 Soirée de Lahore. Early on, she knew she wanted something that felt like a combination of Karan and herself. “The original gown was made by the designer two years before his death and was based on a sari with the one-shoulder design and gold embroidery,” she explains. All of the embroidery was done in India, and Parker paired the gown with a custom One/Of veil featuring gold embroidery along the edge, as well as light green shoes by Paula Nadal.

Karan wore a custom Bode tuxedo with a golden sunflower detail, and as a boutonniere, he wore his own emerald kalgi (a traditional Indian headpiece for turbans). “His cufflinks and studs were my late grandfather’s, his cummerbund was unbranded vintage ’70s made in India with embellished sunflowers, and his shoes were suede Hermès.”

Guests arrived to the ceremony dressed in jewel tones and metallics, walking down a pathway with giant sunflowers on either side. Parker and her mother, an amateur florist, had collaborated with Madison of Hart Floral to create the dramatic entrance. “When I spoke of our love for sunflowers and our desire to have them serve as a recurring visual throughout the day, Madison and Melissa joined forces,” Parker explains.

For the ceremony, they stood within a chuppah structure with draped gold chainmail and burgundy sunflower motifs. Karan’s best friend Drew officiated the ceremony, and the fathers of her late friends Alex and Josh read an excerpt by Ovid of the Greek myth of Baucis and Philemon. “Karan’s vows and my own were mirrors of each other. Unknowingly, we made many of the same references, and we each ended by quoting the story of Baucis and Philemon, who, when they died, received the honor of becoming intertwined as trees so they could live on forever.”

Coming from mixed religious backgrounds (Karan’s parents are Sikh and Hindu and Parker’s are Episcopalian and Jewish), the couple chose to honor both. “Karan arrived on horseback as a nod to traditional Punjabi weddings. St. Christopher medallions were placed on each program, and my sister, Alison, shared a short speech about the patron saint of travelers, the namesake of the church we attended as children, and spoke about the new journey Karan and I were embarking upon,” Parker says. “My sister-in-law, Kareena, shared a speech about the jaimala ceremony as a nod to the Hindu wedding tradition, and spoke about the importance of seeing each other as equals.” Karan’s maternal uncle led the group in a Sikh Ardas prayer, and the ceremony ended with the Jewish tradition of the breaking of the glass. As the couple exited, guests threw dried bougainvillea from Karan’s family garden in Chandigarh.

Cocktail hour kicked off with oysters shipped from Island Creek Oyster Co. (the site of the couple’s first two dates). “We really wanted textiles to be an important decor motif throughout the celebration,” Parker says, noting that there was leveled seating overlaid with rugs, a rug-covered bar, and a rug-covered makeshift bandstand. Later on, their sisters shared speeches, and the couple danced to “Can’t Take My Eyes Off You,” then launching into “Dancing in the Moonlight” as tambourines were passed to guests. “Guests followed Karan and I through the field to reveal the tent, set among the cabins.”

The band continued to play as guests entered the tent and danced. At each seat, Parker and Karan placed a letter explaining what their friendship meant to the couple. After the buzz died down, Parker’s father opened the evening with a speech. “We then had a family-style dinner of our favorite bites: kale salad, focaccia, Idaho trout, pesto gnocchi, and vodka meatballs,” she says.

After dinner wrapped up, Parker and her father danced to “Wide Open Spaces” (a song they listened to as she was growing up) before everyone joined in. The couple snuck away to cut the cake—a 30-inch round flourless chocolate confection covered in raspberries—which was passed out on the outskirts of the dance floor. Indian sweets called ladoos were also passed around.

After a full week of wedding events between California for the traditional Indian celebration and four days in Sun Valley, the two were understandably exhausted. “It took weeks to recover, but the whole time all we could talk about was how deeply happy we were.”

Brandon Ingram is set to make his season debut with the Los Angeles Clippers on Monday, Nov. 23, according to ESPN's Shams Charania.

The Clippers will play the Houston Rockets at the Toyota Center in Houston on Monday.

Ingram had been working his way back from right heel surgery he had in May. The two-time All-Star also had a partially torn Achilles tendon.

Ingram was initially supposed to have a bone removed from his heel, but the doctors found the partially torn Achilles during that procedure.

Earlier this month, Ingram told reporters that he was "pretty close" to returning and had been on the court a bit more.

He also stated that he had not played any 5-on-5 during the summer but has since been "gaining that confidence."

The Clippers acquired Ingram as one of the primary trade assets in the package sent over by the Toronto Raptors for Kawhi Leonard during the offseason.

Ingram has remain close with the team since his arrival.

He took some time to praise his teammates despite the Clippers' slow start to the season. L.A. has a 4-10 record through the first 14 games.

"I actually want to commend those guys because everybody is in new positions and asked to do more," Ingram told reporters earlier this month. "So the guys are trying. They're playing really, really good defense."

The Clippers fell short in a 124-112 loss to the San Antonio Spurs, in what was a competitive game considering both teams are on opposite sides of the standings.

Ingram return would be welcomed, especially on the offensive end of the floor. The All-Star led Toronto with 21.5 points and was third in rebounds (5.6) during the 2025-26 season. He started 77 games for the Raptors.

"They're trying to figure it out on the offensive end," Ingram told reporters. "When I come in, I want to play make, make it a little bit easier for guys to catch and shoot, and not have to make so many decisions. And be able to, when it gets down to it, be able to score."

Ingram already had a level of familiarity with head coach Tyronn Lue and assistant coach Jeff Van Gundy from playing for Team USA during the 2023 FIBA World Cup.

The Clippers had several players injured to start the month, including Ingram, center Yanic Konan Niederhäuser and Max Strus.

The trio was recalled on Nov. 2 and participated in a weekend assignment with the team's G-League affiliate.

Strus returned to the team after recovering from a partial tear to his right plantar fascia. Konan Niederhäuser made his season debut on Nov. 19 against the Clippers, after recovering from right Lisfranc surgery.

Bradley Beal (right knee injury management) and Jordan Miller (left rotator cuff surgery) will remain the only Clippers without a timetable to return.
