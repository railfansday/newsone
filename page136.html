Goldman, Citi and Global Banks Commit $270 Million to Challenge Crypto’s Stablecoin Giants

Goldman, Citi and Global Banks Commit $270 Million to Challenge Crypto’s Stablecoin Giants

The group of global banks building a joint stablecoin has put real money on the table, committing around $270 million to fund the venture as it moves from blueprint to buildout.

Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, Banco Santander, and Deutsche Bank are among the institutions backing the effort, Bloomberg reported Tuesday, citing people familiar with the matter.

The capital is meant to cover five years of operations for a planned U.S.-incorporated company that will issue a dollar-denominated stablecoin. Known among participants as Project Faro, the venture is still searching for a chief executive, and its final name remains under discussion.

A spokesperson for the project declined to comment, as did Santander and Deutsche Bank, while Goldman and Citi referred questions to the project.

The funding is the concrete next step for an initiative that first went public late last year. As Decrypt previously reported, 21 of the world’s largest financial institutions committed in September to form a company to issue a U.S. dollar stablecoin, targeting a launch in the first half of 2027, with a euro version and other currencies to follow.

That consortium had more than doubled from an initial group of 10 banks that began exploring the idea in October 2025, and now spans North America, Europe, East Asia, the Middle East and Africa.

Beyond the four named by Bloomberg, its participants include Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Fidelity Investments, PNC, Capital One, TD Bank, UBS, BBVA, Deutsche Bank, Lloyds Banking Group and MUFG, among others. JPMorgan, notably, is not on the list.

The venture is one of several collaborative pushes by established finance into a business long dominated by crypto-native players. Visa, Mastercard and Stripe are among more than 100 companies backing the issuer Open Standard, while European banks have formed Qivalis to issue a euro-denominated coin.

At the moment, Tether’s USDT and Circle’s USDC together account for about 85% of a market worth roughly $290 billion, nearly all of it dollar-pegged. When the bank consortium first went public in September, Circle’s stock fell about 6% as investors priced in the prospect of bank-backed competition for USDC.

The banks are betting that institutional trust, regulatory alignment and existing client networks can win them a share of a settlement business that promises round-the-clock, cross-border payments at lower cost than traditional rails.

The token is being designed to comply with the U.S. GENIUS Act, which created the first federal framework for dollar-pegged tokens in 2025, and, where applicable, the EU’s MiCA regime. The group has said it wants the stablecoin used across wholesale, institutional and retail markets.

For now, Project Faro remains pre-launch, with no issued token, no named CEO, not even a final name, and a 2027 target that leaves ample room for delay.

But the $270 million commitment marks a shift, and a concrete signal that this bank consortium intends to compete for the onchain dollar rather than leave it to crypto.

Scott McTominay has revealed he had to receive treatment for an irregular heartbeat before Scotland's opening game at the World Cup in June.

McTominay recently returned to action with Napoli, after being diagnosed with a "mild benign arrhythmia" and undergoing a minor procedure to correct the condition.

The former Manchester United midfielder subsequently missed the first four games of Scotland's Nations League Group B1 campaign, from which they took four points.

He could feature under the Tartan Army's new head coach Sebastien Pocognoli for the first time this month, having returned to the squad for their games against North Macedonia and Switzerland.

And ahead of Friday's meeting with North Macedonia at Hampden Park, McTominay has revealed his heart condition began affecting him five months ago.

"I had to go and get the shock on my heart. It was two days before the Haiti game," McTominay – who then played the full 90 minutes as Scotland started their World Cup campaign with a 1-0 win – said.

"You've prepared so well. I was in such a good condition and then something like that happened. You're thinking, 'how is this happening to me?'

"You have to get on with it; those are the cards that you're dealt in football. Sometimes, things don't go your way.

"I was 100%, and obviously, when you're sat in a hospital bed two days before the first game of the World Cup, about to get put to sleep, it's not ideal, is it?"

Tod’s creative director Matteo Tamburini has stepped down, the company announced on Tuesday, leaving yet another designer vacancy at the helm of a luxury house. A successor has not yet been named.

“I would like to thank Matteo for his creative and professional contribution throughout his tenure, for his commitment and for the work carried out in close collaboration with the company’s teams. I wish Matteo every success in his future professional endeavours,” group chairman and CEO Diego Della Valle said in a statement.

Tamburini joined Tod’s in December 2023, and showed his first collection for the brand in February for Fall/Winter 2024, which was recognized for its attention to house codes. His Spring/Summer 2027 collection, shown in Milan in September, was his last for the brand.

Tod’s Group has been having a tough go in recent years. In a release shared by Tod’s Group, which owns Tod’s, Hogan and Roger Vivier, the company said that the decision in line with their decision to “embark on a strategic direction aimed at more decisively communicating the brand’s values, the distinctive character of its codes, and the artisanal quality of its products, as well as the Italian lifestyle”.

Vanderbilt University was hit by a cyberattack. Heres what we know.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Vanderbilt University professor was in the middle of class on Monday, Nov. 9, when about 40 files labeled "Read Me" popped up on her screen and a spoken message started playing. That would be creepy enough on its own. But the professor's account was one glimpse of an ongoing cyberattack that prompted Vanderbilt to tell students and staff to shut down their school-issued devices and disconnect from campus WiFi.

While Vanderbilt has restored access to some of its systems, an update posted to the university's X account on Tuesday morning stated that "Vanderbilt Information Technology continues to work with government agencies and other partners to manage the situation,” promising further updates.

Vanderbilt hasn’t said how its IT team first spotted the attack, but there are clues, namely an alleged ransom note on a Vanderbilt computer published by BleepingComputer.

"If you could not tell already, your databases are locked," a ransomware group calling itself BYOD wrote in a post claiming responsibility for the Vanderbilt cyberattack. "We have your entire estate and research data," the group added, claiming it had stolen more than 1 million Social Security numbers along with student and employee records. In a separate ransom note obtained by BleepingComputer, the group threatened to release the data unless Vanderbilt negotiated.

Whether BYOD actually has that data is still an open question. A Vanderbilt spokesperson told The Washington Post that the university hadn't verified the group's claims. Just two weeks before the Vanderbilt attack, on Oct. 26, employees at the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center heard a similar message.

Several UNM Health employees told the Associated Press that a voice played over their work computers, claiming that student, employee, and patient information had been stolen. BYOD also claimed responsibility for that attack. As UNM Health responded, it diverted ambulances to other hospitals and temporarily suspended outpatient surgeries and appointments. (UNM later said its investigation found that the attack affected some staff and student devices but didn't reach its core systems or servers.)

Vanderbilt's WiFi was back by Monday evening, but getting online didn't mean students could get to everything they needed. By Monday night, Vanderbilt's IT team had cleared Brightspace, where students access course materials, and the YES student portal for use, but File storage services OneDrive and Box were still affected. Vanderbilt said classes and student services would follow their usual Tuesday schedule and told students who were having trouble finishing assignments to ask their instructors about extensions.

The disruption went beyond coursework. One person told the Nashville Scene they'd lost access to research stored on Vanderbilt's systems and were relying on personal backups.

The next question is whether Vanderbilt can determine whose information was exposed, if anyone's.

14-year-old creates tool for AI agents to band together like a team

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Autark can act without constant prompting, but leaves users to enforce their own security boundaries

Built by a 14-year-old developer, a new tool called Autark treats AI agents as a virtual team that can work without constant prompting.

Users put tasks in an inbox, where a manager agent delegates them to other agents that carry out the work and report their results. Agents can also ask clarifying questions if tasks are too vague.

“Agents do not wait for a prompt,” Autark’s website explains. “A schedule, a timer, a new task, a message or an event wakes them up. They decide what to do, do it, and can plan their own next step.”

As one would hope from a product like this, Autark is model agnostic and can be set up to connect to OpenAI Codex, Claude Code, Gemini CLI, GitHub Copilot CLI, and Kimi Code through those services’ CLI tools. Local models are also supported. Your mileage may vary if you choose to use anything besides Codex or Claude Code, though, as Autark’s website mentions support for other models is built in but hasn’t been tested in a live run yet, as Autark is still under active development as an open-source tool.

Autark is the brainchild of teenage Austrian developer Stanislaus Hees. He’s still in school, and tells The Register he’s deeply interested in AI and came up with Autark after being unhappy with existing AI tools.

“I could get help with individual tasks, but I still had to organise the work, remember what was unfinished and keep pushing it forward,” Hees said of his time using Claude Code and Codex. “I wanted a system that could take responsibility for ongoing work, not just respond to my next message.”

He developed Autark - or, more accurately, prompted AI coding tools to do it for him - to come up with a tool that could fulfill that want. You know what that means: It’s capable, but that capability also makes it dangerous.

Autark’s page on access and data makes clear what’s at risk if you give it unfettered access to a PC, and why you don’t want to let the thing run roughshod over a system.

“It can do everything,” the page explains, because “Autark does not put walls around its agents.”

There’s no sandboxing in Autark, no approval prompts, and no permission gates. It doesn’t stop agents from spending all the money they can get their hands on, deleting files, or sending unsolicited emails, either. Agents run with the privileges of the machine's user, with approval checks and permission restrictions disabled or bypassed. Even the safety limit parley that users have with Autark establishes guidelines, not rules.

“The first thing the AI asks is how far it may go,” the access page points out. “That is a question, not a technical barrier.”

That means agents given a task that conflicts with their stated limits may still attempt to carry it out. As we’ve seen in recent months, some AI agents have found ways around restrictions, instructions be damned.

“In our own live test runs, agents given a vague request reached real repositories, backups, SSH keys and .env files within minutes, and one changed the permissions of a file it had found,” the access page notes.

In other words, it’s probably best to stick Autark on a machine that doesn’t contain anything you’d want to avoid losing - even Hees admits as much.

“I would not claim that … the app is suitable for unattended access to sensitive systems,” the teen dev told us.

That’s not to say Autark lacks protections for secrets, though. Autark’s site explains that stored passwords and keys are encrypted and referred to using IDs that keep their values out of prompts. It also says its servers don't store user data, with a meeting server used to relay encrypted traffic between devices.

If you're willing to try Autark to see whether it can improve your workflows, you don't need to rely on its servers. While prepackaged apps are available from Autark’s website, it’s also fully open source and available on GitHub. Everything, even the meeting server code, is available for self-hosting and modification under an Apache open source license.

The bottom line is that this is a tool built by an ambitious young person, and it’s a tool with a lot of potential if used correctly. That correct usage is the major challenge, though, so be sure to build your own guardrails.  ®

The Belgian is reportedly happy at Chelsea and is not actively pushing for an exit. Nevertheless, the prospect of securing regular first-team football could potentially persuade him to consider a new challenge.

Chelsea are under no immediate pressure to sell Lavia, especially since his current contract runs until the summer of 2030.

The Blues are not actively looking to offload the midfielder, although an attractive proposal could encourage them to reconsider.

According to the report, Chelsea could demand between €35m and €40m to sanction his departure, putting Villa’s proposed budget under considerable pressure.

Meanwhile, Emery must also consider the possibility of losing Boubacar Kamara, who has attracted interest from Inter Milan, Arsenal and Liverpool.

Losing the Frenchman would represent a significant setback for Villa, making the pursuit of Lavia potentially even more important.

The clubs have not agreed at this stage, but Villa’s interest appears serious. Much will depend on Chelsea’s willingness to negotiate and whether Lavia is open to leaving Stamford Bridge mid-campaign.
