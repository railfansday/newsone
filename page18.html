Dr. Edith Eva Eger, sometimes called the female Viktor Frankl, died this year at age 98. At age 16, Edith and her family were deported to Auschwitz. She and her mother stood in line before the Angel of Death, Dr. Josef Mengele. Mengele directed the mother to go toward the gas chamber, and pointed Edith in a different direction to live. As Edith watched the smoke rise from the gas chamber, she knew her separation from her mother was permanent. That night, Mengele forced Edith, a gymnast and ballerina, to dance for him. In the depths of her numbness and grief, she willed herself to dance, then kindly shared the bread that Mengele disdainfully tossed her with other prisoners.

In facing this experience and other childhood adversities, Edith showed remarkable courage and resilience. Her mother was critical, being stuck in grief and anger for losing her mother as a child. Edith felt like the invisible sister, only gaining a sense of worth when she was able to rescue her parents from their loneliness and pain. She was cruelly shamed for being Jewish and teased for her awkwardness and crossed eyes. She coped with her sense of shame by becoming a perfectionist in gymnastics and dance to gain approval.

In overcoming her pain, she learned wisdom that she shared well into her 90s as a practicing psychologist. For example, she taught of the peace of having a clear conscience, relating that Mengele would always have to live with what he’d done; he was more of a prisoner than she was.

Edith taught that one survives adversity by committing to a cause outside of self and focusing on what one still has. In the concentration camp, she participated in mock beauty pageants and fantasized about a delightful future and a loving marriage. Although the circumstances were depressing, she realized that she had a purpose and that only she could do certain things in the way that she could.

Rather than becoming embittered, she clung to her faith, reasoning that it wasn’t God who ran the death camps, but hateful people. Even among the Germans, she found people who were secretly kind. She experienced, and chose to believe, that imperfect people are redeemable. She even forgave her German captors, whom she believed were themselves prisoners.

Understandably, Edith suffered long after her liberation. Yet she came to believe that suffering can reveal inner strengths and grow the confidence that one can endure intense adversity. She chose to believe in hope, love, and her ability to find joy in nature and little children.

She also encouraged people to tell their story in safe and appropriate ways, to release pain’s grip. She courageously accepted an invitation to return to Germany, where Hitler had once stood, and returned to Auschwitz to come to peace with her haunting memories.

Edith showed us how to overcome crushing adversity by reclaiming one’s sense of worth and purpose. In her healing journey, she gained hard-earned wisdom that she generously shared with others, still high-kicking into her 90s.

Her life reminds us that suffering crosses everyone’s path. That suffering can be a stumbling block or a stepping stone to greater resilience and life satisfaction (Schiraldi, 2021; 2017). We thank her for her inspiring example. Rest in peace, Edith Eva Eger.

Condo vs. Townhouse: Which Investment Will Turn Around More Equity After a Decade?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Between 2014 and 2024, the difference in price appreciation between condos and single-family homes was less than 5 percentage points nationally, according to an analysis from Realtor.com®. Townhouses came even closer to matching the performance of detached homes—trailing by just 0.8 percentage points.

It's a remarkable outcome for the buyers opted out of the which picket fence dream, and instead invested in properties that often offer a more affordable entry point into homeownership. But that outcome may be harder to replicate in today's rapidly changing housing market.

Condo values fell 0.8% year over year in August 2026, even as single-family home values rose 1.8%, according to data from Intercontinental Exchange. And as home price growth cools, expenses that were easier to stomach during years of rapid appreciation stand to take a bigger bite out of homeowners' returns.

Even so, those national numbers tell only part of the story. In some regions, condos substantially outperformed townhouses over the decade examined by Realtor.com, challenging the notion that owning a piece of land necessarily leads to stronger appreciation.

Understanding what drove those differences—and how the economics of owning each property type are changing—offers some clues as to where buyers might find the greatest opportunity to build equity over the next decade.

Single-family homes appreciated 87.3% from 2014 to 2024, according to Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones. Townhomes nearly matched that pace at 86.5%, while condos trailed only slightly at 82.7%.

That modest gap are striking given the disparity in purchase prices. In October 2026, the median existing condo or co-op sold for $365,100—nearly $57,000 less than the median single-family home, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR).

“Single-family homes are still the most popular type of homes purchased in the U.S. However, condos do appeal to buyers who want a smaller square footage and less upkeep,” explains Jessica Lautz, NAR deputy chief economist and vice president of research. “Condos are also significantly more affordable than single-family homes, which allows buyers who are downsizing or those just starting into homeownership to embrace condos.”

Still, the numbers show townhomes have been edging closer to single-family performance.

“Over the last decade, condos and townhomes appreciated almost in lockstep, but townhomes have pulled slightly ahead recently as buyers chase ‘house-like’ living without the single-family price tag,” says Jones.

While both property types have delivered strikingly similar returns from a national lens, as in all things real estate, the real gains come down to location, location, location.

In the Midwest, condo prices rose 78.3% over the last decade, outpacing the 70.7% growth of townhomes. The South followed a similar pattern, with condos climbing 66.7% compared with just 53.1% for townhomes.

The dynamic flips in higher-cost regions. In the Northeast, townhomes surged 80.5%, far ahead of the 58.6% gain for condos. And in the West, townhomes appreciated 83.8%, edging past condos at 78.9%.

Affordability explains much of the split, says Jones.

“In the more affordable Midwest and South, condos have gained ground faster than townhomes. In higher-cost regions like the Northeast and West, townhomes have appreciated more quickly,” she explains.

In lower-cost markets, condos benefit from their clustering in downtown areas and their appeal to buyers chasing city living. But in expensive, supply-constrained metros, the premium shifts toward townhomes.

“Townhomes are commanding a growing premium as buyers seek a house-like property when single-family homes are out of reach. In more affordable markets, condos—often concentrated in dense city centers—have captured stronger growth as buyers compete for limited housing options,” she adds.

“Townhomes look set for steadier growth,” says Jones. Part of that is because of what you get when you buy one.

When you purchase a townhouse, you own both the structure and the parcel of land it sits on. That land ownership gives you more control, typically fewer restrictions, and it can be a stronger driver of long-term appreciation.

“Generally townhouses have strong appreciation potential because the land itself appreciates in a more reliable way,” explains Aman Sharma, a real estate agent at SERHANT.

Eric Hughes, a real estate investor and coach with Rental Income Advisors, agrees. “The price growth of townhomes will track extremely closely with the price growth of single-family homes in general,” he says.

That connection to single-family homes is key. The “single-family feel” broadens their appeal to a wider range of buyers—families, downsizers, and even first-timers looking to get their foot in the door.

They also tend to come with lower HOA fees than condos. That not only frees up more buying power for the mortgage itself, but it also makes resale easier since future buyers are less likely to be put off by steep monthly dues or looming assessments.

Builders have taken notice, too. Townhouses accounted for a record 18.7% of single-family housing starts on a one-year moving average basis in the third quarter of 2025, according to the National Association of Home Builders.

But Jones warns that this investment isn’t free of potential headwinds. In markets where single-family inventory is expanding and prices are softening, townhome demand may take a hit as more buyers opt for detached homes instead.

“In tight urban markets, condos can still shine as buyers get creative,” says Jones.

Even though the appreciation rate of condos has lagged behind townhomes and single-family homes nationally over the last decade, they can still deliver strong returns in the right conditions.

“In big urban markets, condos are often the most desirable type of urban residence, and can appreciate strongly,” says Hughes. That means that in places like Miami Beach, New York, and San Francisco—where land is scarce and demand for city living is strong—condos dominate.

“There are certain parts of our city where highly amenitized condo high-rises perform better than townhouses from an investment standpoint,” says Miltiadis Kastanis, executive director of sales at Compass, based in Miami.

Lifestyle amenities are another draw. Concierge service, pools, gyms, and other perks make condos especially attractive to luxury buyers or second-home seekers who prioritize convenience. For full-time residents, those amenities can also translate into higher perceived value when it’s time to sell.

But again, Jones stresses that there is greater potential for some bumps in the road for condos, ranging from rising ownership costs to competition from single-family homes and townhouses.

The experts we spoke to stress that your return on investment isn’t just about appreciation and loan paydown. The hidden costs of homeownership can chip away at returns. However, it’s worth noting that even single-family homes can fall prey to these same traps.

“While single-family homes may not have an HOA fee, some communities do even for single-family homes,” says Lautz. “Maintenance can be more expensive when a homebuyer must account for costly items such as roofs, which a condo owner is unlikely to be responsible for.”

While it’s true that owners of single-family homes must shoulder all their own maintenance costs, condo owners risk getting hit with special assessments that may cover repairs or upgrades to facilities they don’t use, or work that should have been done properly the first time around.

Rick Bannon, global real estate adviser at One Sotheby’s International Realty, points to a condo in Miami where residents were hit with a $1 million special assessment just to bring the building up to safety standards.

That’s why Joe Luciano, broker at Re/Max Bentley’s, takes special care when helping his clients through a condo purchase.

“When advising clients on a condo purchase, we always want to get the meeting minutes to see if there’s any chatter around potential projects coming up and then also the financials to see if they have the appropriate amount in reserve funds to cover any surprises,” he says.

Those reserves are set to become an even more important consideration for buyers. Beginning Jan. 4, 2027, Fannie Mae will raise its standard minimum replacement reserve requirement from 10% to 15% of annual budgeted assessment income for condo projects subject to its Full Review process.

While the change could help protect condo owners against the financial fallout of deferred maintenance, it could also put upward pressure on monthly dues in underfunded communities. And buildings that fail to meet mortgage eligibility requirements could face a smaller pool of buyers.

Outside of special assessments, Luciano says monthly HOA fees can take a big bite out of a homeowner's return on investment.

Nearly 3.4 million American households paid at least $500 a month in condo or HOA fees in 2025, according to data from the U.S. Census Bureau. Roughly 1 million paid at least $1,000 a month.

“When looking at the two as investments side by side over 10 years, the appreciation could be similar, but paying an extra $300 to $500 a month is a sunk cost of $36,000 to $60,000 that didn’t go toward your mortgage principal,” explains Luciano.

At the extreme end, he points to luxury Boston high-rises with $2,000-plus monthly HOA fees that have seen stagnant appreciation in recent years.

If the last 10 years prove anything, it’s that condos and townhouses will both continue to deliver strong returns—just not always for the same reasons, or in the same places.

Townhomes have shown they can reliably shadow the trajectory of single-family homes, powered by land value and a “house-like” appeal that buyers continue to prize. Condos, by contrast, thrive where affordability and density drive demand, making them especially potent in urban cores and lower-cost regions where buyers are squeezed out of detached homes.

The next decade may sharpen these divides. As affordability pressures deepen, first-time buyers could lean more heavily into condos, reviving demand in downtowns that have seen uneven recoveries since the pandemic. At the same time, zoning reforms and the ongoing shortage of single-family homes could keep pushing buyers toward townhouses as the closest substitute for a detached home. Policy choices—from property tax incentives to how cities handle condo reserve requirements after recent safety crises—will also influence which type of property captures the bigger share of appreciation.

For investors, the lesson isn’t to pick one winner and walk away. It’s to understand how local affordability, supply constraints, and regulation interact to tilt the playing field.

Fifteen chains from Walmart to Whole Foods, sorted by whether they close, open briefly or leave the pharmacy shut on Thanksgiving 2026.

Many shoppers think of Target as the backup for a holiday emergency, one stop for groceries, cold medicine and batteries. That backup won't exist on Thanksgiving: Totally The Bomb reports that Target is closed for the holiday, and the closure covers every store, according to Savannah Morning News. Target is making the same call at every location, so a call to the nearest store won't turn up a different answer. That certainty helps anyone planning the week. Target is a fixed closure to work around, not a possibility worth testing on Thursday afternoon.

The closure also reaches the coffee counter that many shoppers treat as a separate stop. The Starbucks counters inside Target stores open only when the store does. Every Target is closed on the holiday, according to LiveNOW from FOX, which means no Starbucks inside a Target will serve coffee on Thursday. A holiday coffee has to come from a freestanding Starbucks, where hours vary from store to store. A shopper who wants one should look up that location's hours the night before instead of driving to the familiar counter inside Target on Thursday morning.

Most holiday-hours questions come with a catch, such as a shortened day, a closed counter or a store that sets its own schedule. An Aldi shopper gets a plain answer: every store in the chain is closed on Thanksgiving, according to Totally The Bomb. LiveNOW from FOX reports the same closure for all Aldi stores and lists no location as an exception. A drive to an Aldi in the next town over won't turn up an open store. That spares an Aldi regular the usual holiday chores of searching a store locator or calling a local manager, because the answer is the same at every store.

Shoppers can treat this closure as settled. Holiday schedules often reach shoppers secondhand, pieced together from what a store did in past years. The closure applies to Thanksgiving 2026, so a shopper has no reason to wait for a last-minute update before making plans. Publix also stays closed on Christmas and Easter, according to WTSP, so a Publix regular will need the same early grocery trip before each of those holidays.

A host who finds a broken fixture or a missing supply on Thanksgiving morning won't be able to fix it with a trip to Home Depot. The chain's holiday closure is as settled as any decision on this year's calendar. Totally The Bomb lists Home Depot as closed on Thanksgiving, Thursday, Nov. 26, and Voz reports that its stores will not open that day. A shopper can cross Home Depot off the holiday plan without calling the local store. No Home Depot will be open on the holiday to sell a replacement part or a box of light bulbs. Until Home Depot posts them, any repair pushed to Friday morning depends on a schedule nobody can plan around. A host who puts off the fix shouldn't count on the store opening at any particular time. Shoppers can count on Kohl's being closed on Thanksgiving, and that certainty lets them drop it from the holiday route without calling the local store. Totally The Bomb reports that Kohl's stores won't open for the 2026 holiday, and Patch lists Kohl's as shut that day as well. With the answer resting on more than one report, a shopper has no reason to drive by and hope for an open door. Anything a shopper planned to pick up at Kohl's for the holiday has to be bought before Thursday.

The closure covers a single day, and shoppers get their first chance to return while most households are still asleep. A Kohl's shopper has no Thanksgiving trip to plan, only a Friday-morning one, and the main decision is whether to set an early alarm rather than whether to make a holiday detour.

Uncommon Thinkers: Saf Rabah wants to make it easier to build housing, one permit at a time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Grace Kaste on Nov 24, 2026 at 9:50 amNovember 24, 2026 at 9:53 am

Editor’s note: This series profiles five Seattle-area “Uncommon Thinkers,” who will be recognized Dec. 10 at the GeekWire Gala in partnership with Greater Seattle Partners.

When startup founder Safouen (Saf) Rabah decided to get in on the AI boom, he didn’t start by coding or by training any models. He got in touch with urban planners at Bellevue City Hall, and together they combed through nearly 300 land use permit applications to find common mistakes and bottlenecks.

Bellevue began using Rabah’s platform, Govstream.AI, in the summer of 2025, starting with an AI assistant for city staff. In a test using past applications, about three times as many were judged complete with Govstream’s tool. Rabah says that change alone has the potential to alter the housing stock of the city.

“Why can’t we build more housing? All the macro economic reasons, inflation, material costs, labor, land scarcity, are all exacerbated by the slow permitting process,” Rabah said. “That’s what we want to change.”

So, how did Rabah, a B2B software engineer by training, find his way to land use permitting?

Rabah hadn’t heard of the government technology sector, aka GovTech, until mid-career, but looking back, the signs were all there. He was born in Sfax, Tunisia, and grew up in Tunis. Throughout his childhood, he dreamed of running for office.

“I come from a small developing country, so there’s always this idea of, what could a small developing country become? How can it prosper and grow?” Rabah said. “Building has always been a big part of what I imagined myself doing.”

As a teenager in the 1980s, he attended a USAID-funded boarding school in Tunis. The school was part of Cold War efforts to expand American soft power around the Middle East; classes were conducted in English and many of the students went on to university in North America.

“The school had the first Macintosh lab in all of Africa,” Rabah said. “I can still picture the USAID logo on the back of each computer.”

Rabah got a scholarship to University of British Columbia, where he studied electrical engineering while running what he calls his “first entrepreneurial venture”: a service that built and hosted websites for small businesses.

That led to a job at a startup called Pivotal, a customer relationship management platform where Rabah led a team responsible for e-business applications. After Pivotal went public, Rabah left for a job at TELUS, one of Canada’s “big three” telecommunications corporations, but quickly felt frustrated by the dynamics at such a large company.

“Even though I worked on nascent products, it was very, very bureaucratic,” Rabah said. “There were committees, a hundred thousand employees. I missed building things.”

Rabah began to look for a new job that would not only allow him to build but also to make a difference.

“Being an immigrant, it’s always tempting to stay content with having a good job, good status,” Rabah said. “But I’ve always enjoyed the ‘what if?’ mindset, the clean slate. It’s this way today, but could we imagine it to be something different?”

That vision led him to Socrata, which in 2010 was just a nascent government technology startup.

“I really connected with the founder,” Rabah said. “It had a really cool mission, it combined the ‘do-well’ with ‘do-good.’”

Socrata was one of the first platforms to host data portals for local governments and federal agencies, allowing citizens to access and map public data sets. Many U.S. cities, including Seattle, still use Socrata’s software today to publish information including crime logs, permits, and budgets.

“We were a big part of pioneering the open data movement,” Rabah said. “At one point almost every major city was our customer, and half the states.”

Socrata was acquired by Tyler Technologies in 2018, and Rabah stayed on until 2024.

“The funny part is today we use that data quite a lot in our work because one of the most popular data sets that any city publishes is its permit data,” Rabah said. “We’ve even built a connector between Socrata and Govstream.”

But once Socrata was acquired, Rabah began to feel the same way he had at TELUS: boxed in by bureaucracy.

“Tyler was a really good company, but it is a big company,” Rabah said. “Tech was getting repetitive, and I was thinking about leaving it altogether.”

“The thing that pushed me over the edge is that AI happened,” Rabah said. “It was one of those moments where once you start working with it, you really can’t help but think, ‘I can revisit virtually every assumption I’ve made over the last 20 years, every constraint.’”

Immediately, he thought of his passion for building—building a startup, building a better country, and literally building a building.

“I’ve been fascinated by the built environment since my Lego days: What makes some cities livable and others not?” Rabah said. “Then, living in Seattle, you see the lack of housing, affordability, and the homelessness problem we have, and you’re like, why can’t we build more housing? I started believing that these problems are in fact solvable if we simply speed things up and simplify things with AI.”

In July of 2024, Rabah began working with his “design partner,” Bellevue’s Development Services Department, to learn more about common permitting problems.

“We started with something visionary and high-level, but you can only get there if you understand the granular problems of the day-to-day,” Rabah said. “So we talked with developers and builders and we talked with the people on the other side of the counter who are processing these permits and guiding these builders.”

He learned that most permitting mistakes are avoidable, like applying for the wrong zone or forgetting to include a north arrow on an architectural plan. Those oversights can send an application back to square one while clogging up the permitting process for all other projects.

“The wait time disproportionately affects lower margin projects,” Rabah said. “It really constrains the supply of housing, especially the housing we need more of, which is the more affordable kind.”

“I’ve always enjoyed the ‘what if?’ mindset, the clean slate. It’s this way today, but could we imagine it to be something different?”

Govstream’s AI assistants are designed to help first-time builders avoid those errors and make permitting faster for everyone. Rabah and his team began by building what they call an “intelligent intake” platform for a city permitting portal, which guided users through preparing to submit their applications.

Bellevue tested Govstream’s intake tool on 276 past residential building permit applications. The city reported a completeness rate of 67% in the test, compared with 22% when those applications were originally filed.

More recently, they’ve begun to build out an AI assistant for the backend, which assists city permit techs with the initial review and reduces what Rabah calls “toil,” like answering unnecessary pre-application emails and tracking down missing documents.

Bellevue staff estimated that Govstream’s tools saved them 152 hours over one month this summer by helping them research and respond to pre-application inquiries.

“For a developer, time is money, so the type of housing that can be built in Bellevue will become more affordable,” said Jake Hesselgesser, Bellevue’s development services director. “This technology has a direct and material impact on the cost of housing.”

Eventually, Govstream.AI will be an end-to-end service, assisting in later stages of the permitting process, too. But Rabah is emphatic that Govstream will not give AI the final say.

“We always put our AI systems in the role of assistants that suggest stuff, but never decide stuff,” Rabah said.

Rabah says Govstream uses preset rules, outside the AI model, to check whether a portion of the application is complete, whether a site needs a geotechnical report, or whether an architect is properly licensed. In Bellevue, city staff retain final authority.

Hesselgesser said that Rabah’s conservative approach to AI is what made the city of Bellevue want to work with his startup.

“Our core principle around Bellevue’s use of AI is to retain human decision making, and we wanted to ensure that we were aligned with a company with the same core value,” Hesselgesser said. “Saf took that holistic approach.”

Around the office, Rabah has become known for his eagerness to build. According to Mariia Derevianko, head of marketing, he spends his weekends building out a product or an idea in order to workshop it with the team.

“He’s very hands-on with the product, with design, marketing, all of the aspects,” Derevianko said. “He never waits for the engineers to build something.”

Most recently, he built a lead tracker to research which cities Govstream should partner with, and previously, he vibe-coded one of the research assistants in the Govstream platform as a template for his software engineers.

Derevianko, who joined the team last year after Rabah poached her from AWS, said he’s also known for his kindness.

“I originally didn’t want to join a start-up,” Derevianko said. “I only said yes because I saw he is a genuinely good person who really cares about this project.”

Govstream.AI has already won at least 12 contracts around the Puget Sound, including King County and the City of Everett, as well as cities across the country, such as Louisville, Kentucky.

Ultimately, Rabah has his eyes set on Seattle, where construction and development has been on the decline. Last December, the city council passed permanent “middle housing” rules to upzone residential neighborhoods and encourage the construction of ADUs, all of which will need a permit.

Seattle tested a competing AI tool from CivCheck in 2025. In June, city staff recommended a pilot that would let applicants check their submissions for completeness before filing.

“We need affordable housing in Seattle,” Rabah said. “We would really, really love to help our home city streamline permitting and remove it as a barrier.”

In founding Govstream.AI, Rabah has found the job he has been looking for: one in which he can build things without the bureaucracy of a large company, all while “doing good.”

“This has become my obsession,” Rabah said. “I can point to things in a city and say, maybe Govstream was a part of what allowed this project to get built.”

Does Working Harder Make Learning Fun?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new psychology study challenges the classic educational advice that simply putting in more effort makes learning enjoyable. Three experiments reveal that while cognitive effort improves comprehension, it does not spark curiosity or enjoyment, instead, preexisting interest motivates students to engage in the first place.

Parents and educators frequently repeat a familiar piece of advice when children groan over difficult homework: if you just try harder, you’ll start to enjoy it. Behind this common wisdom lies an intuitive assumption that sustained cognitive effort naturally blossoms into curiosity and intrinsic motivation.

Yet a new psychological investigation from Kyushu University suggests that this relationship works in the exact opposite direction.

Published in Acta Psychologica, the study demonstrates that forcing effort improves basic comprehension, but it does not make dry or unappealing material more interesting. Instead, genuine interest and liking come first—serving as the psychological spark that motivates learners to invest cognitive effort voluntarily.

“I’m curious if a student is made to work harder, does that bring the same benefits as effort they choose to give,” asked Yimeng Jin, a doctoral student at Kyushu University’s Graduate School of Systems Life Sciences and first author of the study. “It matters because many learning environments rely on required effort, such as assignments, on the assumption that more effort is always better.”

To disentangle voluntary motivation from mandatory mental exertion, Jin and senior author Professor Johan Lauwereyns at Kyushu University’s Faculty of Arts and Science conducted three behavioral experiments involving 110 university students.

Participants read pairs of seemingly unrelated sentences and decided whether they wanted to construct an explanation connecting them. By tracking participants’ initial choices, task completion, and time spent, the team isolated two distinct components of learning: willingness to engage and cognitive effort invested.

Across the three experimental phases, a clear cognitive pattern emerged:

“Interestingly, participants who were required to explain spent more time on the task than those who chose to do so,” Jin noted. “Perhaps they initially skipped it because the sentences were harder to understand, so they needed more time to make sense of them. It’s a bit like struggling through homework in a subject you don’t enjoy. Together, the experiments suggest that effort can help people make sense of information, while interest is more closely linked to their willingness to engage.”

While the researchers caution that short laboratory reading tasks among university students differ from long-term classroom learning in younger children, the findings carry broad implications for how educators, parents, and communicators design learning environments. Rather than relying solely on mandatory drill work to manufacture enthusiasm, sparking authentic curiosity first may unlock deeper, faster comprehension.

“The findings have implications far outside education. The connection between engagement and effort is key whenever our understanding depends on reading texts,” concluded Lauwereyns.

Does working harder on a subject automatically make it more enjoyable?

No. Researchers at Kyushu University found that while putting in cognitive effort helps students better understand difficult material, it does not increase their interest in or enjoyment of the subject.

What actually motivates students to put effort into learning?

The experiments showed that preexisting interest and liking come first. When students already find a topic intriguing, they are far more willing to engage voluntarily, and they comprehend the material more efficiently than when forced to work through topics they want to skip.

What happens when students are forced to work on material they don’t find interesting?

When participants were required to explain material they initially chose to skip, they spent significantly more time struggling with the task, gained less comprehension compared to voluntary effort, and showed no increase in enjoyment.

Effort enhances understanding, while appreciation drives engagement in Cross-sentence semantic processing

Comprehensive reading requires active semantic processing, going beyond explicitly stated information to construct connections and meaning. Building on discourse coherence theory, Reader-Response Theory and insights from Language Poetry, the present research investigates how active engagement and cognitive effort shape semantic processing.

We propose that evaluative dimensions central to aesthetic appreciation, namely understanding, interest, and liking, also play important roles in semantic processing.

To examine these processes, we developed a novel cross-sentence elaboration paradigm using unconnected, randomly paired sentences that create semantic gaps and encourage reader-driven meaning construction.

In Experiment 1, participants could voluntarily invest effort by thinking aloud during elaboration. Results showed greater volitional engagement was associated with higher levels of understanding, interest, and appreciation.

Experiment 2 dissociated willingness to engage from actual effort, revealing that appreciation predicted participants’ willingness to invest further effort, whereas additional completed effort did not consistently increase appreciation.

Experiment 3 examined forced effort, showing that externally imposed effort enhanced semantic understanding but did not consistently increase appreciation, highlighting the distinct roles of volitional and imposed effort.

An integrated 2 × 2 analysis of Experiments 2 and 3 showed that active engagement and cognitive effort made distinct, interactive contributions to evaluation and processing time, with completed cognitive effort more reliably predicting accurate recognition memory and active engagement more closely tied to memory for the processing context.

Together, these findings clarify the interplay between engagement, effort, understanding, and appreciation, offering new insights into the cognitive dynamics of active meaning construction in semantic processing.

Sebastian Stan has been playing Bucky Barnes/The Winter Soldier since 2011's Captain America: The First Avenger. His character has gone on quite a journey, from being a World War II sergeant to a brainwashed assassin to a Congressman. Stan says he is "always happy" to keep suiting up as the hero, as he said on the Next Best Picture podcast recently, adding, "I don't know if the decision's up to me, ultimately."

While Stan said "we've done our best so far" in exploring Bucky's character, he does think there are more layers to peel back. "I don't think that that character totally got to fully live out his arc in the way that he has in the comics, for instance, or that I've wanted," Stan said.
