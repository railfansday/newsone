When I think about the best sports movies, often the big match or game at the center of it comes to mind immediately. For instance, with Rocky, the titular character’s fight with Apollo Creed is something I instantly picture when the film gets brought up. So, I was surprised to see the fight wasn’t really featured within the story of I Play Rocky, the project on the 2026 movie schedule about the making of Rocky. Instead, it was used during the credits.

It turns out there is a reason why that big match was cut from the film and moved into the credits.

Now, even though they took a “week” to film the fight between Rocky and Creed for this movie, it was eventually cut…kind of. Ultimately, the scene was moved to the credits. So, why did that happen? Well, director Peter Farrelly told me the match was actually supposed to be the end of I Play Rocky. However, a big realization about the stairs scene changed his mind, as he recalled during an interview with CinemaBlend:

And by the way, it wasn't originally supposed to be over the end credits. It was supposed to be the end of the movie. But when you get in an editing room, as good as the script is, you have to let the movie find its way. And when he goes up the top of those stairs [and raises his arms up], there was such a high watching it that you couldn't get there again.

While the director was very open about this being the right call with me, he also admitted that it took him a while to fully accept that. It took a long time to film the fight, and it’s such an iconic moment from Rocky. However, he eventually came to the conclusion that they had to move it to the credits:

Like even though we took a lot of time making that fight and doing it, you will never get that high again. You're not going to feel it. And so I showed it to a few people in the editing room. They go, ‘You should end on the top of the stairs.’ I was like, ‘No, I took a week. I took a week to shoot the fight. I'm not cutting the fight.’ And it took me a couple weeks to finally realize I have to cut the fight. It's got to end on the stairs.

I know I was a bit shocked not to see the fight prominently featured in the movie, when so many of the other scenes are. However, I did think it was a cool surprise to cut it into the credits, and it served as a full-circle moment to the scene where we watch Anthony Ippolito’s Sylvester Stallone and Stephan James’ Carl Weathers train for the fight.

Speaking of Ippolito and James, they, like Stallone and Weathers, trained and learned how to box for this movie. That means they took a few punches too. When I asked the Sly actor about filming the fight between Rocky and Creed, he said:

We took some real we took some real shots during that. Me and Stephan were just trying to figure it out, and you know, sometimes we messed up here and there, and we tag each other, but it was all right.

James also confirmed that they took some hits while preparing for and filming this movie. He revealed that he and Ippolito trained together as well, and “got close in a very short period of time,” noting that they were “in the trenches with each other.” That became very clear too, as he told me:

I may have taken a punch or two that I wasn't looking forward to. But you know, had to do what I had to do to pull it off. And I think it was all worth it that we were able to, you know, not only step into these guys psychologically, but physically as well.

Well, considering the amount of time, effort and punches that went into this, I’m happy that Rocky and Creed’s fight did find its way into the movie…even if it wasn’t in the way it was originally intended.

Daniel Jones is officially on the waiver wire.

The NFL transaction report has gone out this afternoon, and Jones appears as a waiver from the Colts. After the trade deadline, every player who gets released by his team has to go on waivers before he becomes a free agent.

That means any team that wants Jones on his current contract can claim him, but that is extraordinarily unlikely to happen. As the waiver transaction notes, Jones is a vested veteran with a fully guaranteed salary for the rest of this season. No team is going to want to claim that salary.

Once he clears waivers, however, he’ll be an unrestricted free agent, free to sign with any team, and it won’t be surprising if some team wants to add him for veteran depth. Jones has been terrible this season, but he’s been a competent starter at times in his career, and a team that wants an experienced backup may be interested in signing him for a minimal salary.

Jones was also waived almost exactly two years ago, on November 22, 2024. He cleared waivers and signed with the Vikings’ practice squad before signing with the Colts after that season.

Sam Woods is TheGamer’s Senior Reporter. He’s been in games journalism since 2020 and has undertaken a variety of roles in that time. These have included roles such as SEO Editor, Managing Editor at DualShockers, and Global Head of Gaming, and he's also been seen on the BBC. He was promoted to the Senior Reporter position in 2025 following a series of high-quality reports and exclusives for TheGamer.

Since 2020, Sam has written over 3,000 articles and specializes in telling the stories of the people behind the games. He’s interviewed the biggest game directors, some of the industry’s top talents, told the stories of how indie games came to be, and spoken to players about what really makes them tick. He’s also interviewed some of the world’s biggest musical artists to find out how the medium has inspired their work, and TV and Film actors about their roles in video game adaptations. He even hosted a PAX panel in 2021 and made a documentary in 2024 surrounding the creation of a Disney game.

Alongside this, Sam focuses on breaking news. He got the scoop on Perfect Dark’s cancellation, provided context on some AI drama, and revealed that Lady Dimitrescu’s actress would love to step back into the character’s giant shoes. These articles have been picked up by the biggest gaming sites, including IGN, GameSpot, and PC Gamer. He also loves to dive into the numbers behind the games.

Sam’s journey in games journalism has taken him all over the world. He’s covered Gamescom in Germany, Summer Game Fest in Los Angeles, and Tokyo Game Show in Japan, each on three occasions.

He’s been learning Japanese since 2024 with the intention of identifying and translating Japanese-language news and bringing it to English-language audiences first, something he’s done countless times.

Sam’s first review was 2020’s Battletoads reboot, and he’s since reviewed games in The Legend of Zelda, Super Mario, and Pokemon franchises.

Chances are, if you’ve played a video game, you’ve heard the voice of Roger Craig Smith. He’s the video game voice of Sonic the Hedgehog, plays Chris Redfield in the Resident Evil series, and portrays Ezio Auditore da Firenze in Assassin’s Creed.

His 20-year career has seen him appear in almost 200 video games, playing original characters, dubbing international ones, and voicing many of the medium’s greatest stories.

His latest role comes as the English dub of Phantom Blade Zero’s protagonist, Soul. Following the overnight success of the Game of the Year contender, we sat down with Craig Smith to discuss it, including how it came to be last minute, as well as, of course, some chat about Sonic.

Jia Xiongkai is the voice of Phantom Blade Zero’s Soul in its native Chinese. For the longest time, Xiongkai’s English counterpart was Jay Makensuk; however, at the last minute, Craig Smith stepped into the character’s shoes. I had to find out why.

“It was, I guess, late into the game. But that's also fairly common for what this effectively was, which was a localization,” he tells me. “The game would have essentially been done before this happened. It's funny, I guess it's maybe a little unorthodox for a video game, but not so much for a dubbing project.”

How much did he know about replacing Makensuk, though?

“The elephant in the room is the fact that the early dev examples released featured another actor. I don't know the circumstances, but obviously someone else was in the role before that,” he says. “I honestly don’t know why it was, this stuff just happens in the business. I’ve had it go against me in the same way. You’re doing something, and all of a sudden, somebody decides they want to change course.”

“I’ve done vertical slices where I’ve thought that I was going to be the final choice, and it was like ‘maybe not, maybe this was just to help obtain the financing they wanted for the game’. I’ve had things taken away from me at the last minute; I’ve had things fall into my lap, but I never celebrate until the check clears the bank and the project is released.”

Despite playing the English version of Soul, Craig Smith feels it’s equally as important, if not more, to play through the game in its native language, even if that means not hearing his voice.

“If you want to understand what the tone was intended to be and to understand and even broaden your horizons culturally, to kind of hear cadences and delivery styles and the way that protagonists are approached in different cultures, you would want to experience it in that way first,” he says. “I say that as somebody who's been lucky enough to be hired to be the English dubbing voice for tons of games.”

Phantom Blade Zero is rooted in Chinese culture and folklore; does this make voicing a character like Soul more difficult?

“It’s really like threading a needle,” he tells me. “You need to understand the source material and the performance style while trying to blend in more of an English-speaking style of dialogue. You have to try and make sure it works a little bit better for the English-speaking ear.”

“It’s a fascinating part of what we do, trying to make sure all markets are covered, and that people get to experience things in the best way possible for them”

Dubbing is a big part of what Craig Smith does. He’s the English voice of Daigo Dojima in the Yakuza series, and has appeared in Naruto, Bleach, and Aggretsuko. How different is dubbing a character from being the original voice?

“It’s very different. You’re not creating the character, it’s more of an homage,” he says. “You're trying to make sure that you're not stepping on the toes of anything. I don't know that you necessarily get to make it your own. You're trying to give reverence to an original performer and yet also do something in a style that makes sense for maybe localizing”.

I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.

Craig Smith mentioned earlier that projects often come about last minute. It reminded me of a conversation I had 18 months ago with Alix Wilton Regan, Aliona Baranova, Rasmus Hardiker, and Pepe Balderrama about Lies of P. Two of the actors preferred to get their scripts in advance to prepare their lines, while the other two preferred to dive straight into them in the booth—which did Craig Smith prefer, the ones with more time to prepare or the last-minute projects?

“Both have their pluses and minuses, I suppose. There can be an energetic element to the latter approach of just jumping in and going for it. And certain projects really lend themselves to that,” he says. “Now that I'm 20-plus years in the business, I very often ask for stuff in advance. I love to get stuff because I want to come in with some choices. I want to understand some things. It depends on the project. It depends on the character. But I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.”

With Craig Smith’s role in Phantom Blade Zero very much falling into the “dive straight into the booth” camp, was there anything he saw that made him think that the game would be as much of a runaway success that it is?

“I couldn’t believe it. It surprises me that anything gets an audience these days with every device vying for our time, so it’s amazing, but with this game, it wasn’t necessarily a surprise,” he tells me. “It’s right in line with how much work the development team put in. It’s aesthetically beautiful, the fight mechanics are incredible, and there is so much to chew on with the story. Maybe I’m a little surprised, but I’m just so thrilled, I really am.”

Perhaps Craig Smith’s most notable role is the video game version of Sonic the Hedgehog. He’s played the character for 16 years, first appearing in Sonic Colors and Sonic Free Riders back in 2010. For many, he is the Blue Blur, at least until Ben Schwartz stepped into his big red shoes for the live-action silver-screen adaptation.

Smith has always been super complimentary about Schwartz’s version of the character, but he’s never explicitly said whether he was sad that he didn’t get the role, so I had to ask.

“What do you think? I mean, what do you think? I mean, like, no, I don't feel like being a part of some of the biggest movies on Earth,” he says chuckling. “It’s like asking me if I’d come back as Ezio in Assassin’s Creed. What do you think?”

“I mean, as actors we're inherently envious and jealous and, you know, we want to do it all. We want so bad for you to like us, you know. So, of course, I would have. That's always been my biggest hurt, that I never even got a chance, that it was not even an option to get in and have a meeting or anything. It just never happened. The industry's weird, but I get it. It is what it is. But yes, of course, I would have killed for a chance to get in there. I get I might not be right for you, but having the opportunity is the first and foremost thing for me.”

He couldn’t talk about the Sonic Movie version of the character without heaping praise on Schwartz, though.

“I've stated so many times, I'm like, you couldn't ask for a more wonderful human being and a more talented, creative genius in Ben Schwartz to be in the role, doing such a great job with it and instantly understanding the fan base. There’s so much attention on this franchise again, and a big part of that is the success of the films. It’s really reinvigorated the entire brand.”

There is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role.

With my time with Craig Smith running short, I wanted to ask more about his illustrious career. He’s touched many of gaming’s biggest franchises, but there are still a lot that remain free of his voice. Is there any he’s desperate to appear in, or sad that he hasn’t?

“No, not really. And what an unbelievably fortunate position to be in to say that. I look at it like lightning has struck many times in my life. And I'm very, very lucky,” he says. “Maybe deep down there's some stuff in there. But the reality is, I would not be doing myself any service by dwelling on that. And instead just pinching myself repeatedly and going, ‘can you believe that you woke up on your 30th birthday with essentially no career?’”

“I've reached a certain phase and an age where I just go. I cannot believe this. There is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role, and my attitude always remains the same: I just want to make everybody on the other side of the glass happy.”

This Egg Wash Secret Makes the Prettiest Pie Crusts Every Time

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The secret to a beautifully golden Thanksgiving pie might be the simplest ingredient in your kitchen.

Thanksgiving pies have a way of bringing out the perfectionist in even the most relaxed bakers. Yet you might spend half an hour creating the perfect pie dough design, carefully crimping the edges or cutting little leaves from scraps of pastry, only to pull the finished pie from the oven and discover that your beautiful handiwork has browned unevenly. It’s a small disappointment in the grand scheme of things, but after all that effort, a little indignation is understandable.

An egg wash is supposed to give pastry a lovely golden color and delicate sheen, but getting it to cooperate isn’t always as simple as beating an egg and picking up a pastry brush. Fortunately, there’s an easy trick that makes a noticeable difference, and it comes courtesy of a familiar face in the baking world: Great British Baking Show judge Paul Hollywood.

Paul’s secret is simply to add a pinch of salt to the beaten egg. He lets it sit for about five minutes before brushing it onto pastry dough. In a BBC Food demonstration, Paul uses the technique to glaze scones, but it works just as well for giving pie crusts a beautiful, even finish.

Beat an egg with a small pinch of salt until they’re well combined, then set the mixture aside for a few minutes. Give it another quick stir before brushing a thin, even layer over the pastry. The amount of salt won’t affect the flavor, and there’s no reason to make any other adjustments to your favorite pie recipe.

The difference is subtle but useful. Instead of clinging stubbornly to your pastry brush or collecting in the little crevices of a crimped edge, the egg wash spreads more easily.

If you’ve ever tried brushing a plain beaten egg onto pastry, you’ve probably noticed that it can be surprisingly thick and stringy. That’s because the proteins in the egg give it structure—a nuisance if you need to spread it evenly. Adding salt loosens those proteins, making the egg wash thinner and more fluid after a few minutes. It glides over the pastry more smoothly, leaving a light, even coating rather than stringy puddles.

The egg itself is what gives pastry its golden color and glossy sheen. Salt doesn’t make the crust magically browner; it simply helps the egg wash do its job better. The result is a more consistent finish, particularly around the edges and decorative details where egg wash tends to collect.

A pinch of salt won’t turn a lopsided pie into a masterpiece, but it can help a well-made crust look its best. And after we’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time weaving strips of pastry into a lattice for a Thanksgiving pie, I think we’re entitled to a little help with the finishing touches.

The 16 Most Expensive Artworks Ever Sold at Auction

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Daniel Cassady, Alex Greenberger November 20, 2026 5:00pm

Auction records make for great headlines, like the 2026 banner sale of Gustav Klimt’s Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer (1914–16), which topped $236.4 million at Sotheby’s. There’s a thrill in watching numbers climb into the stratosphere, each sale another reminder that, in the art market, money has a way of bending reality. But the price of a painting has never been the most interesting thing about it.

Look at the list of the most expensive artworks ever sold at auction, and you’ll see more than just a parade of billionaires with good taste (or at least expensive taste). You’ll see a history of ideas—what mattered to the artists who made them, what obsessed the collectors who chased them, and how we decide, decades or centuries later, which works still hold power.

Yes, Salvator Mundi sold for $450 million, but is it really a Leonardo? Picasso’s Women of Algiers, Pollock’s Number 7A, 1948, and Warhol’s Shot Sage Blue Marilyn all shattered records, with the Pollock work shooting far beyond its $100 million estimate when it came to auction at Christie’s in May 2026 in a sale of works from the S. I. Newhouse collection. But these works’ real value has nothing to do with what someone was willing to pay on a given evening at Christie’s. The best art—the kind that shifts culture, lingers in your mind, and changes how you see the world—exists in a space money can’t touch.

But of course, once a work crosses a threshold, there’s something almost inevitable about the price spiral. With each record-breaking sale, the bar gets set higher, the stakes get sharper, and the frenzy deepens. The game has changed, but in a way, the auction houses are just as much a stage for the spectacle as for the art itself. Every price tag opens the door to another possibility. If one work can sell for $450 million, what’s to stop the next from reaching half a billion—or more? With so many players in the game, all vying for the ultimate cultural trophy, the numbers have a way of multiplying faster than the logic behind them.

And yet, the more those numbers rise, the further they drift from the essential thing that makes art so valuable: the art itself. High prices can make us think we’re in the presence of something transcendent, but it’s the work that holds the power, not the price. So, here’s the list. The numbers are staggering, but don’t get too caught up in them. The real story is in the art itself.

A version of this article was first published on November 19, 2025. It was last updated on November 20, 2026.

Sold for $119.9 million at Sotheby’s New York in May 2012

When it sold for $107 million at Sotheby’s New York, Munch’s pastel The Scream (1895), one of the four versions—two painted and two in pastel—that he made of the same subject, became the most expensive work ever to be sold at a public auction, stealing the trophy from Picasso’s 1932 Nude, Green Leaves and Bust, which sold at Christie’s in 2010 for $106.5 million.

At the time, it was the only version of The Scream still in private hands; its seller was Petter Olsen, a Norwegian businessman whose father, Thomas, was Munch’s friend and patron. The picture, which opened at $40 million, sparked a 12-minute bidding war, with wide-eyed potential buyers both in the salesroom and on the phones. According to the Wall Street Journal, its buyer was investor Leon Black, a trustee at the Museum of Modern Art, where The Scream later went on view.

After the sale Olsen, described by Art in America as “the wild-haired and bespectacled seller,” read a statement that tied the work to climate change and global warming: “The Scream for me shows the horrifying moment when man realizes his impact on nature and the irreversible changes that he has initiated, making the planet increasingly uninhabitable. . . . More death, less life. And very few lifeboats left as we go down.”

Sold for $121 million at Christie’s New York in November 2024

The “crown jewel” of the auction market in 2024 (as Christie’s so humbly put it) was perhaps the finest example of a subject that Magritte revisited more than 25 times throughout his career: an otherworldly scene showing a house on tree-lined street at night. But instead being enveloped in darkness, the sky above the trees is impossibly blue, with big, cottony clouds floating by. This 1954 version of Empire of Light, which sold for $121 million and came from the collection of designer and philanthropist Mica Ertegun, set a new record for Magritte’s work at auction.

After the sale, Christie’s chairman of 20th and 21st century art Alex Rotter said the house that evening had adopted a “masterpiece approach” to this sale. “In a market that is not so easy to maneuver, we thought that if we present the greatest works we can get … these are the best examples,” Rotter said, admitting that the auction had its share of lots that didn’t catch collectors’ attention. But “the works that we put all the emphasis behind really proved us right. They had multiple bidders and showed that a market based on individual taste is on the rise.”

Sold for $137.7 million at Christie’s New York in November 2022

Cézanne’s deliciously rich landscape of the French countryside and his beloved Mont Sainte-Victoire is the second of five works from the Paul Allen sale at Christie’s to make this list. When the hammer fell, Mont Sainte-Victoire (1888–90) doubled Cézanne’s previous auction high of $60.5 million, for a still life from the collection of John Hay Whitney.

Like Cézanne’s best works, Mont Sainte-Victoire breaks aspects of the world—in this case, a French mountain—into shapes shaded with color, as though they were three-dimensional geometrical forms. In that way, Cézanne paved the way for Cubist artists such as Pablo Picasso and George Braque, who would push his innovations even further toward abstraction.

Sold for $139.3 million at Sotheby’s New York in November 2023

Woman with a Watch (1932), one of Picasso’s many paintings of his mistress Marie-Thérèse Walter, became the second most expensive work by the artist ever sold at auction when it fetched $139 million at Sotheby’s in 2023. The canvas, from the collection of late philanthropist Emily Fisher Landau, drew intense bidding before selling to a phone bidder represented by Sotheby’s head of global fine art, Brooke Lampley.

Woman with a Watch had a rarefied provenance, changing hands just a few times since Picasso painted it in 1932. It was acquired by Landau in 1968 from Pace Gallery, which had purchased it from Switzerland’s Galerie Beyeler two years earlier.

Pictures of Walter typically go for top dollar. In 2010, Nude, Green Leaves and Bust—also painted in 1932—broke records at $106.5 million. Still, the most expensive Picasso of all time is a work that does not depict Walter. But more on that in a bit.

Sold for $140.8 million at Poly International Auction Co., Ltd., Beijing in December 2017

Qi Baishi’s Twelve Screens of Landscapes (1925) shattered records in 2017, selling for RMB 931.5 million ($140.8 million) at Beijing Poly Auction and making it the most expensive Chinese artwork ever sold at auction. The bidding war lasted over 20 minutes, with more than 60 bid increments before a Chinese collector secured the 12-panel piece.

Each 70-inch panel captures a sweeping vista from Qi’s travels across China. Originally displayed at his solo exhibition in 1954 and later featured in a memorial show in 1958, the set of panels was kept by Qi’s pupil, Xiuyi Guo, before resurfacing at auction.

A similar version of Twelve Screens of Landscapes, painted around 1932 as a gift to Kuomintang general Wang Zuanxu, resides in the Chongqing Museum. The set sold in 2017 was the only one in private hands at the time, making it a rarity.

Sold for $141.3 million at Christie’s New York in May 2015

Alberto Giacometti’s Pointing Man (1947) became the most expensive sculpture ever sold at auction when it fetched $141.3 million at Christie’s New York in May 2015. The life-size bronze, standing at more than five and a half feet tall, was sold by real estate magnate Sheldon Solow, surpassing Giacometti’s previous auction record of $104.3 million, which was set at Sotheby’s London five years earlier by Walking Man I (1960).

One of six casts made by the artist (not counting an additional artist’s proof), this particular Pointing Man had been held privately for 45 years before the sale. Four of those casts reside in major museums, including the Museum of Modern Art in New York and the Tate in London, which made this auction a prime opportunity for collectors. Similar sculptures by Giacometti depicting skeletal, elongated figures have likewise sold for big sums.

At the time, Christie’s head Pylkkänen called the piece “one of the finest works of art I have had the honor to handle.” Whoever bought the piece clearly agreed about its quality.

Sold for $142.4 million at Christie’s New York in November 2013

Francis Bacon’s Three Studies of Lucian Freud (1969) briefly became the most expensive artwork ever sold at auction when it sold for $142.4 million at Christie’s New York in November 2013. The triptych surpassed the previous record, held by Edvard Munch’s pastel The Scream, which sold for $119.9 million at Sotheby’s in 2012 (and which also appears on this list, above).

Bacon’s three-panel portrait—each part stands six and a half feet tall—depicts Bacon’s longtime friend and artistic rival, Lucian Freud, in a series of contorted poses. The piece had an unusual history: its panels were separated by a dealer in the 1970s and remained apart for 15 years before being reunited by a collector in 1989.

The work was expected to sell for over $85 million, but after a five-minute bidding war, it was secured by New York’s Acquavella Galleries. In a strategic move, Christie’s shifted the Bacon painting to the ninth lot of the evening to allow unsuccessful bidders a chance at other works—an approach that proved successful, contributing to the auction house’s record-breaking $691.6 million total for the night.

Shortly after the sale, the anonymous buyer lent the triptych to the Portland Art Museum for public viewing from December 2013 to March 2014.

Sold for $149 million at Christie’s New York in November 2022

In November 2022, an early 20th-century masterpiece by Georges Seurat, Models (Small Version) (1888), sold for a staggering $149 million at Christie’s New York. The painting, depicting three nude figures in Seurat’s signature pointillist style, was one of the rarest privately-owned works by the artist. It came from the collection of Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen and was expected to surpass $100 million at auction.

The bidding opened at $75 million, drawing competition from four bidders, including specialists representing clients in New York and Asia. After several minutes of heated back-and-forth, the work hammered at $130 million, with Christie’s Asia chairman securing the piece for a final total of $149 million, including fees. Why The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping Is The Darkest Movie In The Series

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The following post contains spoilers for "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping."

It's rare for a franchise to get better after 4 or 5 movies, especially when they are prequels that explore questions no one needed answered. It didn't work for "Star Wars," it didn't work for "Harry Potter," and it shouldn't work for "Hunger Games." At best, these prequels just introduced characters that don't matter in the large scheme of things because everything they do or accomplish gets erased in the original story, at worst they try so hard to answer questions no one cares about that it ends up tarnishing the original work.

And yet, when it comes to "Hunger Games," not only has the franchise improved with time, but the two spin-off prequels are arguably better than even the original series. The latest film, "Sunrise on the Reaping" deepens what came before, while introducing new characters that are as engaging as those we know and add to the larger worldbuilding of Panem. Or, as /Film's review puts it, "Beyond the shock value, this story remains human, emotional, and gripping from beginning to end."

Even if this movie doesn't have the compelling romance that was central to "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes," it delivers a harrowing tale of rebellion. What's more, this is somehow the darkest movie in the "Hunger Games" series, and it's not even close. Not just in terms of deaths — though there are some truly brutal deaths in the movie (which are somehow tamer than the book!) — but also in the overall tone the movie explores. This is the "Hunger Games" equivalent of "Andor" or "Rogue One," a bleak tragedy about one man who dared stand up against a tyrannical government and paid for it with everything he had.

The "Hunger Games" movies are full of death and suffering; it's part of the game. But "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes it to another level. Though it takes a long while before the nearly 3-hour runtime gets to the actual games, there's a feeling of hopelessness from the first moment. Early on, a kid gets shot for trying to escape the Reaping. Later, on the day of the opening ceremony, the youngest tribute from District 12, Loella, gets brutally killed in a chariot accident.

But it's not just the fact that there are plenty of deaths, or that they're horrible to watch. Sure, seeing poor Ampert get completely mauled by a swarm of cute forest animals and Maysilee Donner getting gored to death by birds is harrowing. The Capitol doesn't just kill children; it tortures them and pulls them apart and makes their parents watch.

Indeed, more than the deaths themselves, it's the meanness around them that makes this movie stand out. When Loella dies, it isn't a murder done by another tribute trying to get an advantage. Instead, it's an accident caused by Capitol audiences throwing flares at the parade circuit knowing full well it will cause the chariot horses to go berserk, because they want to see the tributes suffer. Compared to every other "Hunger Games" movie, the tributes are not only livestock sent to slaughter, or pawns in a political game. Here, they are subjects of absolute torture, playthings to be slowly toyed with and brutalized before getting discarded for the pleasure of others.

We especially see this in the presence of Ampert, whom we learn is only in the games as a punishment for his father Beetee's rebellious activities. Beetee is forced to mentor his own son and then watch him die.

Throughout "Sunrise on the Reaping," there is a sense of hopelessness and inevitability. We know that, eventually, the Hunger Games end and the Capitol falls — but the characters in the movie don't know that. At every turn, Haymitch and his allies see their every effort get squashed and mocked. Their attempts at leveling the playfield just a little bit are met with overwhelming force. Even before the games begin, Haymitch knows he'll never get to go back home because President Snow has personally seen to it that Haymitch dies for his insubordination during the opening ceremony.

Then we get to the third act, where the movie just becomes a cruel feel-bad experience, as Plutarch Heavensbee convinces Snow to let Haymitch survive to prolong his suffering. Haymitch is forced to watch Maysilee die brutally at the hands of waterbird mutts while he is virtually unscathed. Then, even after he wins the games, Haymitch discovers that the televised games were heavily edited to make him look selfish and cowardly, a traitor to every one of his allies, who won only because he was lucky. As if that wasn't enough, Haymitch has to see his family and his girlfriend die horrible deaths after he goes back home from the death games, in what's without a doubt the most nihilistic ending in the franchise.

It makes sense, however. "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes place at a pivotal moment in the "Hunger Games" timeline, at the height of President Snow's power. We are far removed from the games being almost canceled due to lack of interest like in the previous movie, but also far removed from the simmering rebellion of the original movies. Here, Snow gets away with everything, and he relishes in the cruelty of his games.

‘War’ Creator George Kay on Writing the Celebrity Divorce Drama, That Shocking Season Finale and the Real Couple Who Inspired a Key Scene: ‘It’s Probably an Awful Moment in Someone’s Life’

Over eight episodes of “War,” actress Carla Duval (Sienna Miller) and tech bro Morgan Henderson (Dominic West) have been squabbling over the couple’s multibillion-dollar fortune as they prepare to part ways for good. The winners, of course, have been the lawyers.

The series centers around two law firms: the modern and fast-paced Taylor & Byrne, founded by Nicholas Taylor (James McArdle) and Serena Byrne (Phoebe Fox), and the stuffy, old-school Cathcarts & Sons, led by St. John Smallwood (Pip Torrens) and Beatrice Ubosi (Nina Sosanya).

If there’s bad blood between Duval and Henderson, relations between the lawyers are worse and bloodier still. Which is perhaps why, in a season finale twist, the divorcing couple decide to negotiate their own settlement amicably, and away from their scheming lawyers.

With Season 1 wrapped up, creator and showrunner George Kay sat down with Variety to talk about some of the real-life inspirations for the show, why he decided on that surprising ending and what we can expect from Season 2, which will see Taylor & Byrne and Cathcarts return with a new case and new clients.

A little bit. Twenty years ago, I had a company that made a documentary series about divorce, and it was quite grim viewing, unsurprisingly. Especially because it was real people, and there was no charisma going on — [“War” director] Ben Taylor wasn’t directing it, basically, and it wasn’t Sienna and Dominic. So I remember having that at the back of my mind, thinking the thing we don’t want here is to make this grim or bleak or anything. And in fact, one of the phrases that we came up with in the writers’ room was that this divorce would be the world’s most expensive couples’ therapy. Even though you don’t physically stay married, you end up closer together.

You want to leave people feeling: “That was good. I liked hanging out with those people.” They were awful a lot of the time, but everyone’s redeemable. And Carla and Morgan don’t despise each other. They just drifted and drifted and drifted.

I wanted to end that story in Season 1, because I wanted him to find his home. And so that was always intended as a season-long story, and really wrapped around that is that thing of “Is it possible to be a good lawyer and a good person?” And I firmly think it is.

We’re not going to extend the espionage thing. But with Johnny, it’s a question of can you ever trust someone who’s done that? Which I think is a relatable thing, whether that’s your relationship or your workplace. If someone’s done something really untrustworthy, can you ever trust them again? So that will linger, and become a big part of Johnny’s story. But the other thing is, we wanted to find a similar character who’s not at the upper echelons of a law firm to really get behind in Season 2. That is Emma Laird, who plays Molly [a junior lawyer at Taylor & Byrne]. We cast Emma, and I remember saying to her, “You’re amazing. Just so you know, please come and do Molly in Season 1, because there’s loads more coming in Season 2.”

Every season, I think it’s worth having someone slightly more junior through which to see the whole thing through a prism of them.

We don’t see her in Season 2. We have guest stars for each season. But we do have a world. So people [in “War”] don’t use X; they use Zephyr. The whole thing keeps kind of bleeding into itself.

That was written with a writer called Toby Finlay, who’s brilliant, and we talked about that a lot in the writers’ room. If you can’t arbitrate on a divorce, you’ll go to litigation, and that’s when it will go public, because it’s not protected anymore. And what’s fun about that is when you’ve got a kind of Morgan Henderson and a Carla Duval, and they are very much in the public eye, that scene triggers the going public of the whole thing. So we were always thinking we want this whole thing to blow up in public on chat shows around “The Milliner,” which is Sienna’s film.

We wanted to put the camera behind the chat show host and the guest, because it’s only when you go on stage, you suddenly get the on-stage eye-view of what it’s like to be on a chat show. We’re so familiar with Graham Norton or Fallon or whatever, seeing it from the front. We felt it was such a private thing to see it from the other side, and then you can hear the lights kind of cracking in the heat, you see the light obfuscating the audience and the pressure of that. She’s such a talented actress, Sienna. She can do this thing of having a public smile, but with private worry in the eyes.

She was really great in terms of dealing with the script and talking to me about, “I don’t think she’d quite say it like that,” and we would sit there together on set and tweak stuff. But never did we talk about the meta thing of Sienna Miller in the public glare. She was treating it as a proper part.

“Notting Hill” plays that much more for laughs with Rhys Ifans in his pants. But actually, I felt that [showed] the press physically bearing down on you.

I really wanted to use it as a way to pressurize Sienna, like that door might physically collapse on her. It was a quicker way into just seeing that the weight of the press attention was right there, so it’s a little nod to “Notting Hill,” just in the sheer kind of [gasp] of opening the door. But it’s not used for laughs.

I think it’s more that sort of stuff happens in hotels, and there’s CCTV in hotels. You’re always looking in thrillers to find ways you could kind of revisit. So it wasn’t trying to plug into any one particular incident.

I wanted to make it a bit different because — full disclosure — I didn’t want Olivia Wilde to think we were just aping her experience. But also, I thought by distancing ourselves from it, we could make it even worse for Carla by putting it right in her biggest night, her biggest moment. This really is the culmination. She’s on a career comeback at that point. She’s been in some less good movies than “The Milliner,” if you can imagine, and so this was a really special night for her. So using that for dramatic effect felt good.

But the thing that I was really inspired by the Olivia Wilde thing was just how professional and upset she was at the same time. Like we’re talking about Sienna’s ability to [show] the pain it’s caused, and then you see how she’s painting on a smile quickly to get through it. And I really spotted that in Olivia Wilde’s way of dealing with that in that moment, which I thought that just reflected very well on Olivia Wilde. So I was happy to be inspired by that because it’s probably an awful moment in someone’s life, and you’ve got to be choose the right way to kind of be inspired.

There were lots of fun names. The script editor came up with a brilliant list of films that are sort of mid-level Euro, kind of awards worthy, like “The Red Violinist of Marseille” and things like that, or you know, “The Harpist of Madrid.” But in the end, it was just “The Milliner.” We had this story that she was undercover in occupied Europe with a hat.

The thing we look for in the divorce [case] is to analyze the relationships of all the lawyers. And with the libel case, it’s all about reputation. So it’s about playing those two things off against each other, and bringing in a few new characters and a few new bits of casting to make it believable that what we see as predominantly family lawyers can also have other people in their departments who have libel experience.

Tonally, it’s adjacent but it’s not exactly the same. And we want to have different aspects. It’s a different album.

I’m actually looking the other way. I’m looking for the thing that affects people and then looking for the legal equivalent. It’s the relationship stories that we’ve seen in movies or in our own lives that we really relate to on an emotional level, and then quickly scouring law to say, “Well, where is that felt?” So it’s less with Walliams or Russell Brand. Like, I’m interested in how the justice system will treat those people, but I’m not using that for inspiration for drama because I don’t know how it feels for anyone involved because I don’t know it. So we’re trying to start with the feeling.
