How to Do the Walking Lunge for Lower Body Gains

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Build stronger quads, hamstrings, and glutes one step at a time.

YOUR LEGS ARE built for movement, whether you’re running, walking, or jumping. So why do most go-to leg exercises have you planting your feet in one spot?

Enter the walking lunge, a truly dynamic lower-body exercise that’s a perfect complement to exercises like heavy squats and Romanian deadlifts. (A bonus: It’s a unilateral exercise that isn’t the dreaded Bulgarian split squat.) The walking lunge is one of the few strength moves that truly gets you moving. And in doing so, it exposes muscle imbalances, trains balance and coordination, and challenges your core and upper body more than you might expect—all while attacking every muscle in your legs.

Walking lunges might appear deceptively simple at first, especially if you have done standard reverse lunges before. And in truth, they can be simple. Here, Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. walks you through the steps to take so that you’re not dragging your feet but making gains toward powerful legs.

FORM IS EVERYTHING with this move. You’ll want to stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, squeeze your glutes and shoulder blades while keeping your gaze at a neutral spot straight ahead of you.

Once you’re comfortable with this slow and controlled movement, you can add weights to build extra muscle and strength. You can hold them at your sides, hold them by your shoulders in what’s called a front rack position, or grab one heavy dumbbell and hold it to your chest in a goblet position. “Regardless of how you hold the load, make sure to keep your upper-body tight,” says Samuel. “Grip the dumbbells like you mean it, squeeze your shoulder blades, and tighten your abs, too.”

Don’t obsess over keeping your torso too upright, though, says Samuel. “Especially as you work with heavier loads, don’t be afraid to lean forward slightly,” he says. “That can help you capitalize on your glutes, one of your strongest muscle groups, with each stride.”

When you’re starting out, says Samuel, aim to take a step that keeps your front shin perpendicular to the ground when you lower your torso into the lunge. But as you grow more experienced, you can play with the length of your stride to chase different goals. “If you take very small steps forward,” says Samuel, “you’ll attack your quads more. Larger steps can help you build hip mobility and knee health. If you take larger steps, go lighter with the load.”

“The greatest benefits of the walking lunge come from how it challenges us while in motion,” says Samuel. “In addition to building strength and muscle, every step forward challenges your front leg (primarily tibailis and quad muscles) to decelerate your body in space, and then quickly work to accelerate into the next step. That’s building coordination and balance, in addition to training almost every lower-body muscle and building serious strength.”

You’ll work a number of major leg muscles when you do walking lunges, including your calves, hamstrings, quads, and glutes.

SMALL MISTAKES IN your walking lunge technique can turn the lower body exercise into a potential knee-wrecking disaster.

You want to land on the heel of your foot first to provide a strong base for the rest of your lunge position. When you get started with walking lunges, aim for a vertical shin as you lower down to the ground to stay at a 90-degree angle in your front and back legs.

You also don’t want to think of taking a step forward, but stepping out a couple inches by slightly turning your glute. Another adjustment to protect your knee is to shift your knee slightly outward instead of toward the midline. (If you still feel any knee pain during lunges, try a reverse lunge instead).

Keep your torso straight and avoid dipping forward, which you may be more inclined to do if you’re carrying heavy weights for this exercise. Your chest should be aligned with the back of your thigh the entire time. As you get up from the lunge, you want to avoid using momentum. That means no shoulder or torso rocking.

THE WALKING LUNGE is a great way to sneak in some movement on leg day if most of your lower-body exercises have both feet anchored to the ground. Load up some weights, and try to aim for 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps per leg, 1 to 2 times a week. You can alternate each leg or finish up all your reps on one leg before switching to the other. Samuel says that will keep more tension and gives you an opportunity to really focus on the muscle.

An advantage of this move is its versatility, allowing you to do it anytime, anywhere. If you’re crunched for time or in a hotel room, try bodyweight walking lunges. Do it for 45 seconds with a 15 second rest for 5 minutes to break a sweat and build muscle.

Recognizing the variety of dystopian science-fiction novels and movies, from Brave New World to Gattaca and, more recently, Star Trek, on the future of humanity in which eugenic policies are implemented, genetic engineering has been getting a bad reputation for valid but, arguably, mostly historical reasons. The same objections are used against technological enhancement of our brains (e.g., computer chips or via drugs). This is why I've published an academic article on the topic.

In this post, I critically examine the claim from Maxwell J. Mehlman and Jeffrey R. Botkin that human enhancement will inevitably accentuate existing inequality in a free market and analyze whether prohibition is the optimal public policy for this objection, as egalitarians might advise. Of course, various moral theories will evaluate this claim, which I shall call the inequality-objection, differently. Yet, for the purposes of the present post, I shall only consider whether the inequality-objection is a problem to the dominant moral framework in policy-making (i.e., utilitarianism).

Though perhaps having raised the largest concerns in the public perception of human enhancement, the inequality-objection is neither a very sophisticated argument nor a very new one, against the introduction of new technologies that could enhance humans beyond what might be considered "normal." In fact, the whole argument is old wine in new bottles. Concerning inequality, Bostrom and Roache claim that one could hold the justified worry that:

People with radically enhanced cognitive capacities might gain vast advantages in terms of income, strategic planning, and the ability to influence others; in other words, an enhanced cognitive elite may gain socially significant amounts of power.

Of course, it seems undesirable if an enhanced elite accumulates a lot of power, but whether this is even a plausible scenario is an open question. From a utilitarian perspective, the inequality-objection can be fleshed out as follows:

Let me begin with the premise that human enhancement will increase inequality. This premise is empirical in nature and, as such, requires supporting evidence. More specifically, there are reasons to believe that human enhancement will not affect inequality at all, or even decrease it, as Bostrom and Roache suggest, by making people “more equal,” as is the case with modafinil. Bostrom suggests that we know far more about genes responsible for inheritable diseases than genes responsible for talents, intelligence, and longevity; hence, human enhancement might especially help the genetically worst-off in society.

This means that some enhancements, like an increase in cognitive capacities, might enable agents to become better competitors. Considering this, it seems unlikely that people wouldn't consider taking loans for enhancing themselves, in order to increase their expected wage. Banks might even give loans for human enhancement in a similar fashion to education loans. Instead of paying for advanced training courses, employers might consider paying for cognitive enhancements of their employees.

According to egalitarians, everyone should be equal. Utilitarians, of course, are not concerned with these kinds of egalitarian theories, but rather the maximization of well-being. Several reasons could come to mind: potential oppression, discrimination, unfairness, exploitation, envy, or simply a preference for equality. Even if we grant that these are worrisome consequences of human enhancement, in the utilitarian framework, we would still have to weigh the benefits against these costs. While we may grant that human enhancement might decrease well-being by introducing inequality, there are other areas where human enhancement can potentially increase well-being enormously.

I argue that it is wrong to think that we have the choice between status quo and a world where human enhancement takes place. Applied to human enhancement, this means that a world where it is illegal does not equal a world where this technology is not available. Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom claim that a prohibition will be an incentive for the creation of black markets, which, as with drugs, may tend to make these technologies even more expensive, while legal enhancements would become less risky and less expensive as time goes on. Mehlman and Botkin raise the question of how those breaking the law should be punished—for instance, will someone with genetic enhancements be forced to alter his DNA back to the point it was before or have to be sterilized? Will people who would have died without enhancements have to be sentenced to death by revoking their enhancements?

In short, there are several reasons speaking against prohibition and some that speak in favour of other solutions. For instance, Bostrom proposes subsidies or free access for children of poor families.

There seem to be at least three major problems with the inequality-objection. First, there is no empirical evidence that human enhancement will lead to inequality. Second, even if we accept that human enhancement will lead to inequality, it is not clear whether this will outweigh the potential benefits of human enhancement. Third, even if it does, prohibition is not necessarily the best policy for maximizing well-being. In light of these concerns, I conclude that even if human enhancement leads to inequality, this is not sufficient to conclude that human enhancement should be prohibited by policy-makers.

Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya minority, top UN court rules

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Watch: Genocide judgement will 'mean a great deal' for Rohingya community

Myanmar has been found guilty of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority by the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

The verdict marks the first time a state has ever been found to have committed genocide by an international court.

Yuji Iwasawa, president of the UN's top court, said Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population and engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" to hide its crimes.

A military crackdown in 2017 killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar, formerly known as Burma. Myanmar denied the claims against it, saying its forces had responded to attacks by armed groups.

Rohingyas at the Cox's Bazar camp in Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now reside, watched the verdict live

The court found on Thursday that "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence" it had seen.

The verdict was reached by a majority of nine votes to six among a panel of judges from around the world. The ICJ also found in favour by majority that Myanmar had violated several other parts of the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The case brought by The Gambia centred around so-called "clearance operations" initiated in 2017 in Rakhine State, where many of the Rohingya Muslim minority resided.

After fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (Arsa) launched deadly attacks on more than 30 police posts there, Myanmar troops, backed by local Buddhist mobs, launched a wave of violence, prompting a mass exodus of Rohingya.

Refugees recounted entire villages being burned down and the mass killing of civilians, while numerous survivors and eyewitnesses provided testimony that the Myanmar military had also raped and abused Rohingya women and girls.

The ICJ determined that around 10,000 Rohingya were "violently killed" during the clearance operation, while more than 700,000 are thought to have fled to Bangladesh by foot or by sea. The court found that approximately 815,000 had been targeted in some way.

Rohingya genocide: What was said in court and what happens now?

Iwasawa said Myanmar's military engaged in "indiscriminate" killings, the execution of men, "widespread" sexual violence, "extreme violence" against children and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes.

He said the military had also attacked Rohingya as they were fleeing to Bangladesh.

Myanmar, he added, had engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" by burning or disposing of bodies, burned and bulldozed sites that were cleared and failed to prosecute those who had perpetrated the acts of violence.

While the ICJ recognised the role Arsa played, it found there was "overlap" between an "anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign" - which portrayed the Rohingya as "sub-human" and "a violent threat" - and the clearance operations that made violence against the population more likely.

Myanmar consistently denied genocide against the Rohingya, saying its operations had targeted militant or insurgent threats in response to the Arsa attacks.

In January, government representative Ko Ko Hlaing told the ICJ that the country "was not obliged to remain idle and allow terrorists to have free reign". He said he had "no comment" following Thursday's verdict.

The ICJ also looked at the wider treatment of Rohingya leading up to the clearance operation and found that policies like limiting married couples to two children were discriminatory and aimed to erode their rights.

It said Myanmar had an obligation to pay proportionate reparations, but said an exact figure could be determined at a later date.

The court's decision was welcomed by Rohingya communities and human rights groups.

"This is like formal recognition of our suffering," says Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya who was driven out of Rakhine in 2017 and now lives in Canada. "The Rohingya have been waiting for justice for decades."

Human Rights Watch said the ruling was "long-awaited recognition of the suffering the Rohingya have endured".

To implicate the Myanmar government in genocide, the court had to determine not just that terrible acts had been committed, but that there was an intent on the part of officials or arms of the state to partially or completely destroy the group.

Thursday's ruling is without precedent. Myanmar now carries the stain of being the first state to have been found to have violated the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The ICJ has only twice before ruled on claims of state-backed genocide in the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in the aftermath of the Holocaust, in both cases finding there was insufficient evidence to attribute the actions to the state.

Dozens of individuals have been found guilty of genocide, but finding their actions were at the direct behest of the state requires a higher evidential bar to prove genocide, the gravest of crimes under international law.

Philippe Sands KC, a top human rights lawyer who helped bring the case, described it as an "historic judgment".

He told BBC Radio 4's World at One programme that it was "right up there with the great moments of international justice: Nuremberg, Pinochet, and others", referring to the trials of Nazi leaders following World War Two and that of the Chilean dictator, respectively.

However, the ICJ is not a criminal court and its powers of enforcement are limited. It cannot send military officials to prison, while it relies on the UN Security Council and political pressure to impose its rulings.

The ruling will, though, have implications for other genocide cases pending with the ICJ, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over its war in Gaza.

Sands said that pending case had been the "elephant in the room" during the Myanmar hearings.

He added that, while ruling against Myanmar did not mean the court would find similarly with Gaza, if it had not found Myanmar responsible, the case against Israel "would, in effect, be over". Israel says the case against it is "wholly unfounded".

Antonia Mulvey, executive director of Legal Action Worldwide, told the BBC that the court had not accepted Myanmar's counter-terrorism defence and had found for the first time that rape and sexual violence was an act of genocide, positions it would bear in mind for future cases.

Perplexity Publishes Guide to Managed RAG for Enterprise AI

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Perplexity published a guide on November 19, 2026, explaining how companies can outsource retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), the process that gives enterprise AI systems access to information beyond a model’s training parameters, through a managed service.

The guide calls this approach RAG as a service, or RaaS, and applies the term to arrangements where outside providers handle most or all RAG components and processes. Responsibility for vector databases, embedding models and retrievers moves to the provider, which can also manage ingestion, storage, security and continuing optimization. Depending on the package, the vendor supplies the language model and user interface or connects its retrieval service to the customer’s existing AI system.

Three deployment models frame the choice. Local RAG puts ingestion, retrieval and storage on the user’s own servers, potentially alongside locally run embedding and language models. A self-built cloud system uses APIs to reach hosted databases and third-party models, but internal administrators manage the connections and orchestration. With RaaS, the provider manages all or most of the system, and the enterprise supplies access to its database or cloud storage.

In the guide’s knowledge-management example, the provider receives knowledge-base access through an API. After cleaning the text, it turns passages into vector embeddings and places them in a vector database on its own servers. The provider also adds newly available documentation and connects its retriever API to the customer’s orchestrator, the software that coordinates user inputs and sends augmented prompts to the language model.

When a user queries the company chatbot, the orchestrator passes the query text to the vendor, which embeds it and searches the database. The orchestrator adds the returned information to the input context and sends it to the language model, whose answer is then delivered through the chatbot. The guide says supplying information absent from training parameters improves the accuracy and completeness of generated outputs.

The principal applications are internal knowledge management, customer support, sales assistance, customer-data access and customer-facing product chatbots. Internal assistants can search documentation stored in services such as Google Drive, SharePoint and Confluence. Sales systems can draw on support tickets, competitor research and call recordings, while customer-data applications can support subscription management, returns and changes to personal details. Product chatbots can also connect to backend databases to answer discovery and technical questions.

Perplexity lists faster deployment, reduced maintenance, scalability, automatic content synchronization and enterprise-platform integration as advantages of managed RAG, while noting that service and package-tier limits still apply. Its build-versus-buy framework centers on cost, scale and complexity. Self-hosting can suit clearly bounded usage and may avoid token and storage charges when every component runs on internal servers, although hardware and maintenance can offset those savings.

Advanced architectures can add multimodal and hybrid retrieval, along with reranking, and the guide says specialized vendors typically offer the most economical access to that expertise. Because most RAG systems already use some vendor services, the decision centers on how much of the overall system a provider will build and manage.

The guide divides providers into general AI platforms with built-in RAG and managed backend infrastructure vendors, presenting the former as ready-to-use products with integrations and the latter as options for tailored setups. Its seven-provider editorial selection comprises Perplexity, AWS, OpenAI, Microsoft Azure, Pinecone, LlamaIndex and Vectara.

Perplexity Enterprise appears in that selection as a suite of agentic tools with internal and project-level knowledge bases, web search, access to multiple generative models, a proprietary embedding model, external-data integrations, security and employee permissions.

On its separate Enterprise page, Perplexity says more than 50,000 organizations trust Perplexity Enterprise and describes Computer as drawing on 20 advanced models, with the broader platform connecting company files and tools for internal knowledge, research and complex projects.

The same page states that Perplexity does not train its large language models on enterprise customer data. Listed controls include SOC 2 Type II certification, configurable file retention, user management, SSO and SCIM, and audit logs for tracking user activity.

Companies can also split the approach, building straightforward systems themselves and using a provider for advanced applications. The practical consequence is that outsourcing need not be enterprise-wide: each deployment can place a different share of retrieval complexity, expense and maintenance with the provider, while the enterprise retains responsibility for whatever it keeps internal.

Genie 4 requires a paid-tier Gemini API key and is not offered on the free tier. Its standard pricing is $1.50 per million tokens for text or reference-image input, $9 per million for text output, and $17.50 per million for video output with stereo audio included. At 240 video output tokens per frame, or 5,760 tokens per second, the listed effective rates are approximately $0.10 per second and $6.05 per minute. Batch API, Flex inference and Priority inference are unsupported.

To build a session, developers create an interaction, connect over WebRTC or WebSocket, and send one world_setup event before steering with user_action or text input events. Browser applications can use an ephemeral token scoped to one interaction, keeping the long-lived API key on the backend. WebRTC is recommended for interactive browser apps, while WebSocket supports Python and Node.js workflows, frame capture and custom pipelines.

Closing a browser tab or detaching the local transport does not immediately stop server generation. Calling complete() ends generation and billing, while cancel() aborts server-side generation, making explicit shutdown part of cost control.

Genie 4 does not accept live microphone or webcam streams. It also lacks function calling, Google Search grounding, structured outputs and the ability to resume a completed interaction.

Annotations are stored as object contexts, a Cloud Storage metadata construct containing a key, string value and timestamps, with an optional JSON payload of up to 128 KiB for detailed results. Applications can filter object listings by context keys and values, while Identity and Access Management permissions control who can add, change or remove them. Contexts transfer automatically during copy, rewrite, compose, move and restore operations.

Storage Insights datasets can export the metadata across millions or billions of objects to BigQuery. Data teams can then query the object_contexts_catalog_view with standard SQL and join labels or document classifications with structured business tables, supporting asset discovery, training-data curation and multimodal analytics without writing a separate ETL pipeline.

Google says its image annotations cost 75% less than similar services from other cloud providers. The published Cloud Storage prices provide a more concrete baseline: $0.25 per 1,000 objects for each image model’s first 1 million monthly objects, then $0.05 per 1,000 above that threshold.
