Mortgage rates jump to 7.58% as inflation lifts bond yields

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mortgage rates rose sharply last week, with 30-year conforming loans at 7.58% and jumbo loans at 7.87%. Hot inflation data and higher yields pushed Fed hike expectations above 93%, while builders continued offering rate incentives for new homes.  AI Summary

Mortgage rates skyrocketed last week on the back of rising inflation and bond yields, erasing some of the recent affordability gains for potential homebuyers.

At HousingWire‘s Mortgage Rates Center on Tuesday, rates for 30-year conforming loans averaged 7.58%, up 23 basis points from one week ago. Rates for 30-year jumbo loans jumped 31 bps to average 7.87%, while rates for 30-year loans through the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) rose 15 bps to 7.37%.

HousingWire Lead Analyst Logan Mohtashami wrote over the weekend that “last week wasn’t good for mortgage rates or the bond market” due to hawkish statements from Federal Reserve officials and hot inflation data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Additionally, the U.S. conflict with Iran and potential tariff increases against Mexico propelled long-term Treasury rates and mortgage rates upward.

Interest rate traders took notice. According to the CME Group‘s FedWatch tool, more than 93% expect benchmark rates to increase by 25 bps at next month’s Fed meeting. That’s up from 76% one month ago. It would be the second hike of the year by the Fed and put the federal funds rate at a range of 4% to 4.25%.

Kyle Bass, business production manager at Refi.com, pointed to Mortgage Bankers Association application data showing that recent downward moves in rates had positive impacts on loan demand. But with visible volatility in rates on a day-to-day basis, he said, preparation between loan officers and borrowers is needed to lock in the lowest possible rate and get lower monthly payments.

“Borrowers who benefit are the ones who prepared before the number moved. A shadow application, with income documents, statements and credit in order, lets a homeowner ask what their current mortgage could do for them the day rates cooperate. Waiting for a better number only works if you are ready when it arrives,” Bass said.

A report released Tuesday by Realtor.com shows that the new-home segment is giving the typical buyer lower rates and better affordability than existing homes. Homebuilders, who are also under financial pressure, continue to offer rate buydowns and other incentives as they seek to offload their inventory.

Realtor.com reported that the average mortgage rate for a new-home buyer in the third quarter was 5.01%, more than a full percentage point lower than the 6.15% rate for an existing-home buyer. That translates to a difference of $258 in monthly payments for someone buying a $400,000 home with a 10% down payment.

“Builders have adjusted to a market where affordability is still a major hurdle, but that adjustment isn’t always visible in the listing price,” Joel Berner, senior economist at Realtor.com, said in a statement. “Reduced mortgage rates can help make a new home more affordable for buyers while allowing builders to hold asking prices relatively steady.”

The report noted that reduced rates are often temporary and only last for the first few years after origination. But even when comparing 30-year fixed rates, those who bought a new home in the third quarter saved, with average rates of 6.19% compared to 6.68% for existing home-buyers. On the same $400,000 purchase with 10% down, the new-home buyer saves about $117 per month in interest.

“Mortgage-rate incentives can make a meaningful difference in what a buyer pays each month, but it’s important to understand how long the lower rate lasts,” Berner said. “A temporary buydown can provide relief early on, while a lower rate fixed for the life of the loan offers a different kind of savings.”

Selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality, said in a statement that tariff-driven increases to building materials are putting pressure on the new-home segment. In turn, this winds up being felt by consumers through higher home purchase prices. Regardless of the type of home being purchased, rising insurance premiums and property taxes are also straining budgets.

“For housing, the key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power and bring more buyers and sellers back to the market,” Hepp said.” Until then, I expect activity to remain relatively subdued, making the spring homebuying season an increasingly important test of whether the market can finally turn a corner.”

While housing market professionals continue to grapple with fewer home sales and lower mortgage origination volumes, the U.S. economy as a whole continues to show resilience to inflation, tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty.

Melissa Cohn, regional vice president at William Raveis Mortgage, pointed to last week’s Producer Price Index (PPI), which showed 6.5% inflation on an annualized basis in October. It was the largest year-over-year increase since November 2022. That came only days after the Consumer Price Index showed monthly inflation of 0.4%.

“Unfortunately, the CPI number that we got earlier this week has set off bond yields, and bond yields were probably up almost 25 basis points from where they were at the end of the previous week,” Cohn said Friday in a statement. “It’s not just because of the CPI number, but because of expectations about the Fed, where members are saying, ‘Well, we’re definitely going to have to hike rates.’ We know that the market’s expecting it.

“All of the benefit that we got in mortgage rates over the course of the past three weeks was basically just thrown out the window again, because mortgage rates are really tied to the rate of inflation. We’re back on the inflation wagon.”

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh offered some forward-looking remarks on Monday during a jobs and productivity conference in San Francisco, saying that the central bank continues to look closely at the development of “super intelligence” and its impacts on the global and national economies.

“At the Fed, our role in all this is neither advocacy nor opposition. Our responsibility is to recognize that the widespread adoption of this technology will have significant impact on the economy, with important implications for monetary policy down the road,” Warsh said.

Senate Poised to Vote on Affordability Package that Hunters, Anglers Worry Is a Trojan Horse for Public-Land Rollbacks

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 17, 2026 1:09 PM EST

The U.S. Senate plans to vote this week on a bill aimed at easing America’s affordability crisis and fighting climate change that hunting and fishing groups worry will also erode many of the nation’s bedrock conservation laws.

The complex and winding 400-page Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act focuses on lowering energy costs, something both Republicans and Democrats have championed and and that helped drive Democratic gains in the midterm elections. BAAJA also, however, ties jobs and household bills with curtailing public-lands protections. And in a twist that’s perplexing many conservation groups, the bill is co-sponsored by Sens. Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) and Mike Lee (R-Utah), two lawmakers known for being on nearly opposite ends of the public-lands spectrum.

BAAJA is largely focused on permitting reform, something the country hasn’t tackled in decades, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center. In practice, the legislation would raise the standard for whether projects approved by one administration can be revoked by a future administration.

That means an industrial solar project permitted by one president, for example, could not be stymied by a future president intent on preventing renewables. It also speeds up permitting processes and cuts red tape for everything from energy projects to landscape-scale changes like prescribed fire.

But sportsmen and conservationists say the bill would not just expedite climate or environmentally friendly projects. BAAJA would also make it much harder — or potentially impossible — for an administration to overturn leases or approvals for projects as controversial as hyperscale data centers on public lands or mines in the same watersheds as sensitive wilderness areas. It’s a bill that prioritizes development, many observers say, at the cost of the very “red tape” that has been responsible for conserving many of the country’s most priceless fish, wildlife, and wild places.

“I understand this is a big, bipartisan bill — it has some things in it I would rather it not have. But I negotiated the deal based on what was actually possible to get signed into law,” Heinrich wrote in an email to Outdoor Life. “Because when I say climate change is an existential threat, I mean it. And I will not let an opportunity to do something about it pass by.”

Conservationists like Ingrid Lyons, executive director of Save the Boundary Waters, understand the need for stability around permitting development. The whiplash between issuing and revoking permits for public land development projects every time an administration changes has likely hurt the U.S.’s ability to move forward with important energy projects, including in renewables like wind and solar.

But she fears this bill could instead cement the creation of controversial projects like a copper-sulfide mine at the headwaters of the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness or a copper-molybdenum-gold mine in the salmon-rich waters of Alaska’s Bristol Bay. Permits for projects like these have been denied, among other issues, because of environmental risk. If permits are reissued in similar scenarios, legislation like BAAJA could prevent future administrations from stopping development in places that truly need protection.

“What about landscape certainty?” Lyons says. “We’re overlooking in some ways the impact to communities that are relying on protection and not just projects.”

The conservation community has become increasingly vocal in its opposition to the bill since Heinrich and Lee partnered with Sens. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), and Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.), to introduce the legislation in late September. Lee has a clear track record of supporting public land sales and undermining existing public-lands conservation, including 2025’s attempt to include federal land sales in the One Big Beautiful Bill.

A lengthy version of BAAJA is before the Senate as of Tuesday. The current bill includes a variety of red flags for hunters, anglers, tribes, endangered-species advocates, and other conservationists.

The Theodore Roosevelt Conservation Partnership acknowledged the need for permit reform but said in a statement that “progress should not come at the expense of the conservation safeguards and public review that give hunters and anglers a voice in how our lands and waters are managed.”

For Lyons, it comes down to the potential unintended consequences of sweeping legislation like BAAJA.

“We need to be exceptionally aware of the implications of this bill, which could be that a lot of these really beloved places could have projects many communities have worked really hard to oppose cemented into place.”

Many in the conservation community were surprised by the bill’s sponsors, given Heinrich’s history as a sportsman and conservationist who regularly defends public lands and sensitive places, and Lee’s general position as a proponent of development and public-lands sell-off.

But in Heinrich’s statement to Outdoor Life, he emphasizes the impact of climate change on our planet, including the places where people hunt, hike, bike and camp. Independent modeling, he said, predicts the bill will lead to a 20 percent reduction in power sector emissions, or the “equivalent to taking 39 coal plants offline for 10 years.”

“You cannot protect a trout stream without paying attention to the warming temperatures and declining flows that threaten it,” he wrote. “You cannot protect wildlife habitat while ignoring the impacts of increasingly severe drought and wildfire. We need to reduce emissions, and that means getting more clean energy onto the grid.”

In an Oct. 30 op-ed in the Albuquerque Journal, Heinrich explained how New Mexico’s SunZia Wind and Transmission project took 17 years to permit and build.

He also pushed back on what he called misinformation about the bill, stating he doesn’t believe data centers should be on public land. The bill also requires large scale data center operators to pay the full cost of electricity consumed by the facility.

The Izaak Walton League’s conservation director, Kate Miller, thinks each of the bill’s sponsors are acting in good faith and trying to do what is right for their constituents. Many projects, including those aimed at restoring forests and wildlife habitat, have been held up by litigation. Something like BAAJA could move those forward.

But Miller would have liked to see these issues tackled individually instead of in a massive bill. And while she hopes that, if the bill passes, the administration will respect existing laws, the past two years has brought an onslaught of changes to most of the nation’s bedrock environmental laws including the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the National Environmental Policy Act.

“We want to make sure what comes out is not something that sacrifices those core protections that allow us to have healthy waters and habitats,” Miller says. “That we don’t sacrifice one for the other.”

You’ll soon be able to turn off Gemini model training but still retain access to your previous chats with Google’s AI.

Google likes to use your conversations with Gemini for model training to help improve the AI. But for people with privacy concerns, the only way to opt out of this was to delete your entire chat history. Now, Google is overhauling the process to eliminate that drawback.

In a change set to roll out in the coming weeks, you’ll be able to put the brakes on Gemini model training without losing access to your previous conversations with the AI. Here’s how this works now — and how it will work down the road.

Currently, Gemini offers a setting called Keep Activity that controls not only whether and for how long your chats are saved but also whether they’re used to train the AI. You can’t separate the two.

To correct this problem, Google will replace Keep Activity with two new settings. With the first setting, you’ll be able to keep or delete your past chats and choose how long your history will be saved. With the second setting, you’ll be able to decide whether your chats, including all text and attachments, will be used for model training.

Also: I asked Gemini Notebook to ‘make me a site’ from my notes

“With this change, you will be able to keep your history while independently controlling generative AI model training,” Google said in an announcement shared to Gemini users via email.

Here’s how this will play out. If your Keep Activity setting is off when this change is made, both new settings will be off. If your Keep Activity setting is on, both new settings will be on. That means you’ll need to head to Gemini’s Settings if you wish to opt out of model training but still be able to view and access your previous chats.

Almost all AIs include a setting for model training that you typically have to manually disable if you’re concerned about your privacy. But most let you opt out without turning off your chat history.

ChatGPT offers a setting called “Improve the model for everyone,” which is double-speak for model training. But this is easy enough to turn off. Microsoft Copilot provides settings for “Training on conversation activity” and “Training on voice conversations,” both of which you can disable to opt out of model training. Claude’s AI training option is turned off by default. But you can always control this by checking the setting for “Help improve our AI models.”

In contrast, Google has been the only AI player among the majors to impose a restriction for those who want to opt out of AI training. The Trump administration finalized a major update to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule designed to give patients easier, faster access to their health records and streamline care coordination.

The final rule, issued by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights (OCR), strengthens and clarifies the right of an individual to access their protected health information and reduces regulatory and administrative burdens, the agency said.

The proposed rule was issued more than five years ago, in January 2021. An unpublished version of the final rule is available here.

Under the final rule, healthcare organizations must respond faster to patient requests for copies of their health records. HHS said covered entities will have no more than 15 calendar days to respond to a request, reduced from the current 30-day period. Organizations are allowed one extension of no more than 30 calendar days, HHS said in the final rule.

The rule also establishes that patients can access their protected health information through personal health apps when the covered entity has the technical capacity to deliver the records in that form and format.

The rule also expands circumstances when patients must receive copies of their health records for free, requires advance notice of fees and modifies identity verification requirements.

To make it easier for healthcare organizations to share data for care coordination, the final rule clarifies that health plans may use and disclose protected health information for care coordination and case management for individuals. The rule also expressly permits covered entities to disclose PHI to social services agencies, community-based organizations and similar third parties for care coordination and case management for individuals.

To reduce regulatory burden, OCR eliminated the requirement for providers with direct treatment relationships with individuals to make a good faith effort to obtain an individual’s written acknowledgment that they have received a copy of the health care provider’s Notice of Privacy Practices. The final rule also permits disclosures to Telecommunications Relay Services (TRS) communications assistants for persons who are deaf, hard of hearing, or deaf-blind, or who have a speech disability, and excludes TRS providers from the definition of business associate.

Provisions in the final rule will become effective 60 days after publication in the Federal Register, and compliance will be required 180 days after that date, according to HHS in a fact sheet.

TPU Model Design Considerations and Optimization Guide

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google designed TPUs from the ground up to be exceptionally efficient for machine learning workloads, delivering top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Because TPUs trade general-purpose hardware scheduling for wide systolic arrays, dedicated SparseCore engines, and custom interconnect fabrics, adhering to specific architectural best practices is essential to achieve maximum performance. To help developers get the most out of TPUs, we are launching the open-source AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign guide. This post highlights its key principles—spanning shape alignment, architectural choices like LatentMoE, and parallelism strategies—to accelerate LLMs, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, and other hardware-intensive workloads on TPUs.

Performance tuning starts with model definition. Architectural choices made before the first training step set permanent ceilings on MFU (Model FLOP Utilization), cost, and serving latency. This guide gives concrete, hardware-grounded design rules for teams building or adapting models for TPUs, organized into three groups:

How to use this guide. The shape rules apply to anyone designing a new model. If you are adapting an existing model rather than designing one, the Expressing Parallelism section applies without touching the checkpoint. The general rules hold for any large-matmul architecture (dense LLMs, multimodal, and world models), and the companion AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign repository grows model- and workload-specific sections over time.

Eighth-generation TPUs. These principles carry forward to TPU 8t and TPU 8i and to the newly announced TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max. The reasoning stays the same and the thresholds move with the hardware. TPU 8t and TPU 8i both add native FP4. For training, TPU 8t also brings more balanced Vector Processing Unit (VPU) scaling. For serving, TPU 8i adds the Boardfly topology, a Collectives Acceleration Engine (CAE), and 3x more on-chip SRAM. We note the effects inline in Principles 5–8, and follow-up posts will cover eighth-generation designs in depth.

Measured evidence. Alongside the published sources, notes marked Measured carry results from our own Ironwood profiling program: open 20–30B MoEs (a Qwen3-30B-derived LatentMoE variant; GPT-OSS-20B) plus dense controls, single slice (except for the 512-chip TPU v7x and 1,024-chip TPU v4 ablations in Principle 4), BF16, training shapes up to 32K sequence, all against tuned-systems baselines. Treat the trends and rankings as transferable, the exact percentages as config-specific.

TPUs are purpose-built custom ML ASICs designed from the ground up to deliver state-of-the-art performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). While their architectural choices—such as wide 256×256 systolic arrays, software-managed VMEM, dedicated SparseCore engines, and direct multi-dimensional ICI fabrics—enable exceptional efficiency, they inherently create specific structural constraints and programming patterns that must be carefully followed to unlock maximal performance. Reaching peak efficiency requires aligning model shapes, routing, and parallelism directly with these underlying hardware characteristics:

Make d_model, FFN and expert intermediate widths (d_ff), latent widths, attention projections, and padded vocabulary sizes (used as contracting or output dimensions) multiples of 256 to match the 256×256 MXU systolic array. Token/batch axes and counts such as heads, layers, and experts are exempt. The most frequently missed dimension is head_dim: at 64 or 128, flash-attention QK products leave the MXU ≥ 50% idle. Reshape to head_dim = 256 with proportionally fewer Q and KV heads, preserving total projection widths and logical KV cache bytes (iso-quality in our ablations).

Measured: Reshaping Qwen3-30B MoE attention from 32 × 128 to 16 × 256 (identical parameters and FLOPs) sped up end-to-end training, with gains widening at longer context:

These rules apply for non-quadratic attention as well, notably GDN ablations from Yang et al. 2025 also noted at par or better quality for head dimension 256 for their Gated DeltaNet Attention.

XLA compiles each program for exact tensor shapes, so every buffer has a fixed address and every loop a known trip count, and a new shape means a recompilation and a host stall. What about dynamic shapes? Dynamic workloads fit this model: the trick is to separate the shape of a buffer from the extent of the data inside it.

Instead of padding to the largest group or the longest sequence, we keep the buffers at fixed shapes (tokens sorted by expert and padded only at the tail, the KV cache as a fixed pool of pages) and describe the ragged structure with a few small integer arrays (group sizes and offsets; KV lengths, the page table, cumulative query lengths). Pallas copies these into the TensorCore's scalar memory before the grid starts (PrefetchScalarGridSpec), and the kernel reads them to choose the next tile and bound its loops, with a mask covering a tile that straddles a group boundary or a partly filled last page. The data dependence becomes an address and a trip count instead of a shape, so the compiled program is the same for every routing and every batch composition and the kernel runs at the unpadded FLOP count.

Standard EP dispatches every routed token at full d_model width, making EP + FSDP slower than plain FSDP in our Ironwood benchmarks. LatentMoE projects routed tokens from d_model down to latent width ℓ (d/ℓ ≈ 2–4×) before dispatch, executes experts in latent space, and projects back up after combine (introduced by Nemotron 3 and adopted by 962B MAI-Base-1 and 2.8T Kimi K3):

Measured: On a 30B / 128-expert MoE, an ℓ = 768 (2.67×) LatentMoE cut on-device permute time 2.8×, lifted end-to-end training throughput +14% tokens/s (322 vs 270 TFLOP/s/chip, or +19%, since the latent projections add ~5% FLOPs per token; tokens stay ℓ-wide past the down-projection, so on-device permute and combine shrink alongside EP all-to-all), and freed ~25 GB/device of activation HBM when remat checkpoints were anchored on the compressed latent. While current MoE recipes in tpu-recipes run FSDP (+ TP), LatentMoE + FP8 dispatch makes EP roofline-viable when per-device batch is too small to hide FSDP weight all-gathers. Under both FSDP and EP, avoid over-fragmenting experts: small tokens × K / N group sizes starve MXU tiles.

When candidate MoE layouts match on quality, pick the one with higher compute intensity at a similar active-parameter budget. A larger shared expert (wider d_shared) shifts work into dense, communication-free GEMMs that overlap EP dispatch/combine in training and amortize weight reads across the decode batch. Fewer, wider routed experts (halving N and K while doubling d_ff at fixed sparsity K/N) cut dispatch traffic in half and double per-expert GEMM fill. Optimal (N, K) grows with model size, and over-coarsening hurts routing diversity. Ablate expert granularity and the shared-to-routed split at your target scale rather than copying GPU co-designed models, since the TPU optimum is more shared-heavy.

Measured: On a 63B LatentMoE (ℓ = 768, K/N = 1/32, 8K context, 512 TPU v7x chips at EP = 128), halving (N, K) from (512, 16) to (256, 8) while doubling d_ff and the shared expert lifted training throughput +57% tok/s and held lower loss across 120.8B tokens:

Low-concurrency decode streams model weights every token (~300 concurrent tokens per weight shard are needed in dense FP8 to reach compute roofline). Combine two architectural levers with explicit concurrency sizing:

Norms, activations, masking, routing math and quantization scale applies run on the Vector Processing Unit (VPU), which is roughly two orders of magnitude below the MXU in throughput and executes in sequence with the matmuls unless XLA fuses it into an adjacent GEMM or we move it off the TensorCore. Therefore we drop reshapes and transposes that carry no quality, standardize on fusable RMSNorm and SwiGLU so the elementwise work folds into the neighbouring GEMM, and offload the MoE sort, gather and scatter to the SparseCore.

Note that fused VPU work is free only while its cycle count fits inside the MXU window it is fused into. A quick illustration of this concept is block-scaled fp8 GEMM.

With a scale per block of B elements along the contraction axis, the MXU emits one partial sum per output element per block and the VPU rescales each one (about three vector ops), so per output element the MXU does 2K flops and the VPU does 3·ceil(K/B) ops. The rescale hides when B ≥ 1.5 × r_mxu / r_vpu, which on v7x (fp8 peak 4,614 TFLOPS against a 19.2 TFLOPS vector peak, a ratio near 240) puts the threshold near 340. In simulation on a DeepSeek-V3 expert shape, block 512 runs at the per-tensor time, block 256 is VPU-bound at 1.2x and block 128 at 2.4x. Achieved MXU efficiency is lower than peak, which lowers the effective ratio and can make the smaller blocks workable; the way to decide is to measure the GEMM at each block instead of using peak ratio. This threshold is specific to Ironwood. TPU 8t is designed with specialized support for scaling so quantization for MXFP4 and MXFP8 formats has no overhead. Re-measure the block-size threshold there instead of reusing 340. The rule itself carries forward: keep vector work hidden inside a matmul window.

For workloads dominated by BF16-sensitive matmuls (most notably long-context quadratic attention where direct FP8 degrades accuracy), native single-pass BF16 leaves MXU capacity unused whenever hardware low-precision MXU throughput exceeds native BF16 throughput by more than the pass count. Use the multi-pass BF16 algorithm to execute BF16-accurate matmuls via multiple passes over the higher-throughput lower-precision MXU pipeline without losing numerical fidelity. This matters more as low-precision throughput pulls ahead of BF16. TPU 8t adds native FP4, which doubles MXU throughput. On Ironwood the FP8 to BF16 ratio is exactly 2:1, so two or more passes cannot beat native BF16.

Assign communication-heavy parallel axes to the fastest physical links and keep logical mesh factors aligned with torus dimensions:

Minimum-degree CP matched to kernel trade-offs: All-gather CP is simplest (though its all-gathers can sit exposed) when paired with zigzag load balancing (context_parallel_load_balance in MaxText) so causal masking does not idle tail ranks; Ring CP overlaps collectives but trades off SplashAttention chunk efficiency; Ulysses A2A CP avoids causal skew but exposes all-to-alls and caps at head count.

Google engineered TPUs from the ground up as custom ML ASICs to deliver top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Unlocking that potential requires bridging the gap between hardware architecture and model design. By standardizing on 256-aligned shapes, leveraging LatentMoE, and mapping parallel axes directly to physical links, engineering teams can maximize TPU efficiency across both training and serving workloads.

Always evaluate architectural changes in wall-clock time on a tuned software stack rather than paper FLOPs: in our Ironwood benchmarks, pure systems tuning (SparseCore offload, MXU-aligned tiles, and remat tuning) lifted an open 30B MoE from 12% to 23% MFU before any model change, and published Ironwood baselines show 256-aligned MoEs (DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-235B at 608–630 BF16 TFLOP/s/chip) running at roughly 2× the throughput of unaligned GPT-OSS-120B (330 TFLOP/s/chip).
