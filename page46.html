CFP committee needs to show its work after Alabama over UCLA ranking

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Alabama is 8-2. UCLA is 10-0. And in the latest College Football Playoff (CFP) rankings that were released on Tuesday night, the Crimson Tide came in one spot (No. 3) ahead of the Bruins (No. 4) for a second straight week.

That is just one of the many things that has fueled debate the last couple weeks about whether the CFP committee favors the SEC over the Big Ten, Big 12 and ACC. I don't believe the answer is as simple as them being biased, but I also understand why people could think they are.

Because much like when Nate Bargatze was asked how many liters are in a gallon, or how many yards are in a mile in the "Washington's Dream" sketch on Saturday Night Live, "nobody knows" what the CFP committee values when it comes to their rankings.

The fix for this should be simple: tell everyone how you go about determining the rankings. Or, more bluntly, which advanced metrics are used to help rank the top-25 teams.

When asked about that very thing earlier this week by The Athletic, CFP executive director Rich Clark danced around the question a bit and ultimately said this:

"Just to let you know, we have not provided those publicly. Basically, there's so many metrics to really give that out publicly with an explanation of them isn't really feasible right now. It is something that we're considering. We're looking at this in the offseason to see what metrics and how we would present those potentially in the coming seasons."

The committee is not hiding the fact it uses metrics to help set their rankings, but why not tell fans how many, and which, metrics are used? And which ones are the most important? And how were those metrics determined to be more important than the others?

And how is it not "feasible" to simply say which metrics you use? Perhaps Clark means it isn't feasible to show every metric in a clean, TV-friendly graphic. Maybe not. But with ESPN holding the CFP's media rights through the 2031 season, it's fair to wonder how much input the network has in what the public sees.

One metric we know the CFP values is strength of schedule. Committee Chair Hunter Yurachek — the athletic director for Arkansas — has defended Alabama's ranking due to the Crimson Tide's schedule. They rank sixth in strength of record and sixth in strength of schedule on ESPN's Football Power Index (FPI).

That may be a fine and dandy explanation to a casual college football fan, but ESPN owns the media rights to the CFP, so using ESPN's own metrics is really suspect.

Other than that, what else does the committee value? Head-to-head results don't really appear to be one of them. CBS Sports noted the committee has valued head-to-head results in some cases, like Alabama over Kentucky, but not in others.

One prime example is…you guessed it…Alabama being six spots ahead of Tennessee despite the Volunteers beating the Tide by 16 points this season. Yurachek explained that decision by citing Alabama's three wins over teams in the committee's top-25.

Now, to be fair, the metrics do back up the strength of schedule debate in Alabama's favor. UCLA is first in strength of record, but just 67th in strength of schedule. With a non-conference schedule that featured teams like San Diego State (96th in FPI) Cal (97th) and Nevada (98th), that isn't incredibly surprising.

Alabama's non-conference schedule wasn't much better. Florida State (No. 40) is a shell of what it once was and is in the midst of a coaching search, and East Carolina (No. 89) and FCS Chattanooga don't move the needle either. At least not to the point where they should definitively be ahead of the Bruins with two losses.

If the strength of schedule and strength of record metrics are what the committee favors more than others, just say it. A published methodology protects the committee more than this current approach does. Instead, every close call we get currently in the rankings can look like a favor to ESPN and the SEC.

But perhaps it isn't the strength of schedule that matters; perhaps it's strength of schedule within a team's conference. ESPN's Heather Dinich said on Tuesday night's show that there is a "new metric" the committee is using this year to help them rank each team. And the metric certainly seems to favor schools where football "just means more."

So the reason No. 21 South Carolina is still ranked — even after losing last week, 34-6, to an Arkansas team (Yurachek's Arkansas team, just to remind you) that was 3-6 and had lost four of its last five games before that game — is because…the SEC's schedule is tougher? According to who, exactly? Who determines how difficult a conference's schedule really is?

Nobody knows, but it must also be the reason three other four-loss teams — No. 22 Oklahoma, No. 24 Florida, No. 25 Ole Miss — are also ranked.

Ultimately, the CFP Committee needs to be transparent before the college football season even begins. Members of the committee change every year, so the way they go about developing the rankings should be made available every offseason so we all know exactly what to expect. Then, if the committee changes their criteria midseason, those changes should be made public as well, along with legitimate reasons for why those changes were made.

Show your work, CFP Committee. Fans can handle it.

Huge New Nvidia Bug Is Ruining Gaming Hardware, Users Say

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia has not been having a good couple of weeks

Nvidia is in rough shape. As the hardware company scrambles to replace its CEO and co-founder, Jensen Huang, after a series of scandals, it seems the needs of lowly gamers aren’t high on the priority list. Earlier this year, the company ran into controversy over its ugly generative AI-fueled DLSS 5 technology. If you need more evidence that Nvidia is focused on cranking out chips for generative AI rather than supporting its gaming hardware, the company has failed to fix a bug reportedly capable of damaging users’ PCs by writing excessive amounts of data to solid-state drives (SSDs).

The bug was found in the last two driver updates for Nvidia graphics cards — specifically, the 621.14 and 621.24 updates for the Nvidia GeForce Game Ready Driver. The 621.24 update went live on Nov. 12. As first reported by TroubleGrid, the bug causes constant writes (saving data) on a user’s SSD, degrading the hardware while potentially causing stutters and mouse pointer hitches. Specifically, the bug apparently causes NVDisplay.Container.exe to write an excessive amount of information to an Nvidia log file, resulting in as much as 30 GB being written per hour.

While this executable already wrote to log files before the most recent driver update, versions 621.14 and 621.24 can cause dramatically more data to be written. It usually takes years for an SSD to degrade naturally, but the excessive data logged by the Nvidia bug could speed up this process. The bug seems to be causing performance issues for some users as data is written in the background. The easiest fix is to downgrade your graphics card driver to version 621.01, which is the last known patch that didn’t have the problem.

Users have reported the bug on most major PC gaming subreddits, and they’re not happy. “Trying to destroy our computers so we stop hoarding all their RAM,” one user said, referencing a now-infamous quote from OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. “AMD and NVIDIA releasing firmware that prematurely kills SSD’s when SSD’s have become 7x more expensive than they were 18 months ago [thinking emoji],” another wrote, conspiratorially implying that these bugs were deliberately created to damage users’ computers and make them buy expensive new SSDs. “Makes you wonder if it’s AI code incompetence or a concerted effort to kill personal computing,” someone else replied.

As this comment suggests, some have suggested that the bug may be a byproduct of Nvidia’s increasing use of AI-generated code. Earlier this year, Huang said he wanted his developers to use AI chatbots and agents more often to help with programming. In that context, this bug may be a Hanlon’s razor situation rather than a conspiracy: "Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity."

Europe Spent €16.5 Million Proving Again That Hydrogen Burns in a Turbine

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hydrogen burns. Turbines spin. Their exhaust can make steam. Kawasaki demonstrated hydrogen-fired electricity and heat production at Kobe Port Island in 2018, before Europe funded the first HYFLEXPOWER programme. Six years later, Siemens Energy is still issuing announcements about hydrogen combustion at a single turbine in a French paper mill. European and British taxpayers have supplied roughly €16.5 million for the two research programmes, and the same installation has generated at least 78 identified press appearances. Neither the grants nor the publicity have produced a commercial reason to make hydrogen from electricity and burn it for industrial electricity and steam.

The latest Siemens Energy announcement, dated November 16, 2026, describes an SGT-400 turbine at Smurfit Westrock’s Saillat-sur-Vienne paper mill operating on pure hydrogen through startup, load changes and full-load cogeneration. The equipment can operate under those conditions. Hydrogen combustion and heat recovery were already established. Extending the operating range of one manufacturer’s turbine has not changed hydrogen’s production cost, the losses involved in making it or the available alternatives. No second commercial installation has emerged from the programme.

Siemens’ own March 2024 technical article supplied the most important numbers. The electrolyzer installed at the paper mill produces 16.4 kg of hydrogen per hour. The turbine consumes about 810 kg per hour when producing 10 MW of electricity. Onsite production covers 2.02% of hourly fuel demand. Nearly 50 times that electrolyzer capacity would be needed to supply the turbine continuously at the stated operating point. Later testing used hydrogen brought to the site.

The 2023 campaign accumulated about 90 hours of hydrogen and natural-gas blending tests, including just over four hours on pure hydrogen. Siemens identified limited hydrogen availability as the constraint. The 2026 announcement supplies no cumulative full-load hydrogen operating hours, delivered fuel price or routine cost of electricity and steam. These are the figures an industrial customer needs before buying equipment. Announcing another combustion test avoids the question of who will supply hundreds of kilograms of affordable hydrogen every hour for years.

My complete TFIE Strategy Briefing investigation examines the funding records, Siemens’ published operating figures, the energy balance and an audited chronology of 78 press appearances surrounding the same turbine.

At an illustrative 50 kWh of electricity per kilogram of hydrogen, manufacturing the turbine’s 810 kg of hourly fuel requires approximately 40.5 MW of electricity. The turbine returns about 10 MW of electricity plus usable exhaust heat. Compression, storage and delivery add equipment and expense. The mill needs electricity and process steam; converting electricity into gas and burning it to recover those services introduces losses and costs that direct electrification avoids.

France is an especially poor location for this detour. RTE’s 2025 electricity review reports that 95.2% of French generation was low-carbon, with average generation emissions of 19.6 gCO₂e/kWh. Direct grid power can supply the electrical load. Industrial heat pumps, mechanical vapour recompression, electric boilers and thermal storage can supply process heat and steam in combinations designed around the mill’s operating requirements. The programme should have compared these complete systems before committing public money to more hydrogen turbine retrofits.

A paper mill in Finland is already doing the industrial work. Delfort’s Tervakoski plant has operated a Turboden heat pump and mechanical vapour-recompression installation since January 2026. It supplies about 12 MW of process steam at temperatures up to 170°C, with a reported coefficient of performance around two. It delivers useful steam without a hydrogen-production plant, hydrogen deliveries or a hydrogen-fired turbine. The exact plant integration will vary, but the alternative is operating commercially while HyCoFlex continues issuing test announcements.

The funding was directed at the wrong problem. HYFLEXPOWER received €10.48 million in EU support. HyCoFlex received another €4.44 million. A Siemens presentation identifies approximately €1.56 million more in UK support, bringing the public contribution across the programmes to about €16.48 million. Both programmes were designed around making existing gas-turbine cogeneration equipment compatible with hydrogen. The grants paid for additional turbine operating conditions instead of establishing that hydrogen was a sensible way to provide the plant’s energy.

The programme’s economics were weak before the second award. In October 2023, Siemens presented a scenario requiring electricity below €35/MWh to bring hydrogen fuel costs into parity with natural gas under its 2022 cost assumptions. The HYFLEXPOWER final workshop later examined favourable operating cases with hydrogen substitution and electrolyzer utilization both above 90%. European reporting also identified renewable electricity below €40/MWh as a condition for favourable deployment economics. France’s 2025 wholesale electricity averaged €61.1/MWh. Its negative-price hours were occasional. They could not supply a plant operating more than 90% of the time. These conditions were set out before the research programme ended, yet funding continued for more combustion trials.

Siemens also has a clear commercial interest in preserving turbine markets. Its 2025 presentation explicitly described protecting existing assets and future gas-turbine investments in a decarbonizing world as a benefit of hydrogen compatibility. A turbine supplier is entitled to pursue its products with its own money. The purpose of public industrial-decarbonization funding is to reduce emissions and costs, not to protect combustion-equipment sales from direct-electric competition.

The TFIE media audit counted 19 press appearances in 2020, 13 in the 2022 installation and blending phase, 35 around the 2023 tests and award, four in 2024 and seven through November 16, 2026. Those 78 appearances trace back to a handful of announcements about one installation. They are not 78 independent engineering advances or 78 deployments. The headline count swelled while the operating fleet did not.

Public funders should require equipment-neutral comparisons of delivered industrial energy services before underwriting another hydrogen retrofit. The evidence needed is familiar: a year’s reliable electricity and steam, actual operating cost, lifecycle emissions, fuel contracts, and performance against direct-electric alternatives. HyCoFlex has supplied more hydrogen-combustion publicity without establishing those outcomes. The problem was not that Europe failed to make a turbine burn hydrogen. It was that public money kept flowing after the relevant energy and economic questions already had answers.
