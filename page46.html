Jaafar Jackson shared how he celebrates the holidays with his fellow family members during an exclusive interview with E! News at the CCA's 9th Annual Celebration of Black Cinema & Television.

Jaafar Jackson will always remember having a good time around the holidays.

In fact, as the Michael star—son to Jermaine Jackson and Alejandra Genevieve Oaziaza—explained, his family finds more than one way to rock with one another during the festivities.

“It’s a lot of great music playing, there’s a lot of good food,” he exclusively told E! Not Ribeye: Bobby Flay Recommends This Steak Cut For The Best Tacos

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When taco night rolls around, you'll want to head to the butcher and grab some good meat. If you're making carne asada, your instinct might be to pick the ribeye. While it might be the gold standard of steak, tacos and ribeye are an inherent mismatch. Tacos are one of the most democratic foods out there, while ribeye is made for deep pockets and expensive tastes. Even Food Network's Bobby Flay recommends skipping it. Instead, he swaps the pricey cut for the seriously underrated skirt steak.

Ribeye is America's favorite steak thanks to its rich marbling and tender texture, but you'll rarely find it in taquerias. Beyond being pricey, ribeye's assets are its downfall in tacos. In an interview with Food and Wine, Flay revealed the reason why he often chooses to skip ribeye altogether: "[Ribeye] can be inconsistent because the fat doesn't always melt even when cooked properly." This is a real problem in tacos. Encased in a taco, you don't have the option to trim around a rubbery pocket of fat or leave it on the plate. Even if you trim the fat before adding the steak to your tacos, it's so unevenly distributed that you risk ending up with wildly inconsistent slices.

Unlike ribeye, skirt is cut from the plate primal, a hard-working area near the diaphragm. Rather than being streaked with generous ribbons of fat, it's comprised of long, coarse muscle fibers, which give it a chewier, more fibrous bite along with a bold, beef-forward flavor. Its thin profile may not win it fans at white tablecloth restaurants, but it's exactly why taquerias can't get enough of it. All that surface area compared to its thickness means skirt steak cooks quickly, soaks up marinades fast, and slices evenly, which is everything you want from taco meat.

There's no cut of beef more well-suited to carne asada than skirt. Literally translating to "grilled meat," the beef is usually marinated in a citrus and garlic blend, with the acidic ingredients helping to tenderize the surface of the meat. After being left to soak up the flavor for a couple of hours, it's then seared or grilled over an open flame or charcoal to impart a smoky, charred flavor (an air fryer also works if you can't grill). Because skirt is such a thin cut, it doesn't enjoy spending too much time over high heat; it only needs around two to four minutes per side. Skirt can be prone to drying out, so it would be wise to avoid cooking past a medium doneness.

How you cut skirt is just as important as how you cook it. Compared to other cuts, skirt has particularly long, dense muscle fibers, and if you slice them with the grain, you'll leave them intact, resulting in chewy, rubbery bites. Slicing against the grain shortens these fibers, turning them into more satisfying, bite-sized strands. To identify the grain, look closely at the surface of the meat to spot the direction of the muscle fibers running through the beef.

Although skirt is beloved by taquerias, that doesn't make it suited to all beef tacos. One popular style where we would skip skirt is birria, the slow-cooked, tender beef marinated in a chili-and-spice adobo served with a flavorful broth for dipping. The whole concept of birria revolves around slow-braising, requiring tough, collagen-rich cuts like chuck or beef cheek, which break down into tender, shreddable meat over several hours. While skirt does have some connective tissue, it's simply too thin and lean to survive hours of prolonged heat.

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Recently, I was scrolling through the Billboard Year-End Hot 100 singles of 1980. I was 14 years old that year, making it the absolute peak of my "musical reminiscence bump," the developmental window when our brains permanently wire the music of our youth to our deepest emotions.

While perusing the list, I realized there's one song that always stops me dead in my tracks and hits surprisingly hard whenever it catches me off guard in a random place, like a supermarket checkout line. That song is Elton John's "Little Jeannie."

Lyrically, this song doesn't say anything profoundly moving until near the end. And the refrain "I want you to be my acrobat, I want you to be my lover" always seems like a throwaway line. On paper, the song could easily be categorized as a seriously schmaltzy, mid-tempo early '80s pop song.

Nevertheless, every time I hear the opening percussion and cascading drum intro, I get verklempt. During the 20-second, lyric-free introduction that might sound like "Muzak-y" elevator music to some, my emotions swell and I'm filled with a deep sense of, for lack of a better word, "wistfulness" that doesn't make sense to me logically.

Curious if I was alone in having such a poignant response to a somewhat cheesy pop song, I looked at the YouTube comments to see if other listeners echoed my sentiment.

Notably, for many of the "Little Jeannie" versions on YouTube, all of which are sung in English by Elton John, the comment sections are dominated by foreign-language responses (lots of Portuguese!). According to Google Translate, these comments convey that even among non-English speakers or ESL listeners, "Little Jeannie" hits hard in bittersweet ways that seem to catch people off guard.

Why does a song that isn't inherently sad make someone cry? And how can a listener feel nostalgia for an era they didn't live through? As it turns out, the answers lie in a fascinating intersection of behavioral psychology, imagination, and neuroscience.

A new study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) on November 16, 2026, investigated the sociocultural dimensions of music-evoked memories. The researchers played 30-second clips of billboard-topping hits to 223 college students, utilizing songs from their own teenage years (the 2010s) and their parents' teenage years (the 1980s and 1990s).

Using natural language processing to analyze the memories these songs evoked, the researchers found that certain songs reliably generated strikingly similar memory scenarios among strangers from comparable backgrounds, driven by culturally learned "scripts."

To find out where that emotion was coming from, the researchers stripped away the music and gave a separate group of participants just a song's lyrics to read. Without music accompanying the words to a song, the shared sociocultural dimensions of people's autobiographical memories and associations diminished.

Based on this observation, the researchers concluded that the lyrics alone don't drive our intense reactions to pop music. Instead, the emotional scripts are embedded in the musical sound and genre itself.

When a YouTube commenter notes that "Little Jeannie" isn't a sad song but makes their eyes well up with tears, it isn't necessarily the lyrics driving that reaction. It's the specific acoustic architecture created by an old Fender Rhodes electric piano, alto saxophone, and flugelhorn, all wrapped in layered vocal harmonies. This unique musical alchemy tugs directly at the heartstrings, completely bypassing the analytical mind where words reside.

How does neuroscience explain the enigma of a young listener feeling "ridiculously melancholic" when hearing a song released decades before they were born?

One possible explanation: While we experience a primary surge of memory attachment for the music of our own adolescence, we also develop a secondary attachment to the music of our parents and caregivers from earlier generations. As the authors explain in their recent PNAS paper:

"Interestingly, however, music-evoked autobiographical memories (MEAMs) are characterized by a potentially unique phenomenon known as 'cascading reminiscence bumps,' in which they exhibit a secondary reminiscence bump associated with music that was popular before listeners were born."

As children, we're often captive audiences in the backseat of a car or the family living room. Long before music was siloed into individual earbuds via streaming apps, "hard copy" formats like vinyl records, CDs, and mixtapes filled these shared spaces. Played aloud on common speakers, we passively absorbed our parents' adolescent soundtracks by osmosis.

Yet, even as modern listening habits shift toward isolated headphones, this intergenerational phenomenon persists. This new (2026) study suggests that these cultural "scripts" are so deeply wired into us that even if a younger participant had never heard a specific older song before, their brain can use the song's genre to automatically retrieve a feeling that fits the cultural vibe.

Because these acoustic architectures activate shared neural representations, the emotional script transcends both the generation and the delivery method.

Another recent study by Princeton's Elizabeth Margulis and colleagues, published in Nature Communications on August 18, 2026, used fMRI to explore the neural representations of imagined narratives that participants conjured up while listening to unfamiliar music.

During the experiment, researchers scanned people's brains while they listened to completely instrumental tracks. The scans revealed that across different test subjects, the music produced highly organized, shared patterns in the brain's default mode network (DMN). As the authors explain:

"This work demonstrates that music elicits detectable, modality-general representations, a kind of higher-order 'meaning', integrating both narrative imagery and its associated emotional profile, specific to particular excerpts."

Interestingly, Margulis's team found that the DMN doesn't need semantic language to engage in profound meaning-making. This helps to explain why it's almost impossible to pinpoint why certain music evokes shared imagined narratives; these emotions bubble up outside the realm of words (i.e., lexical semantics).

According to the latest 2026 research, music has the power to tap into a cultural frequency that bypasses our critical minds, reminding us that oftentimes, profound meaning is found in things we feel deeply but can't articulate.

OMCO Solar offers solar-powered tracker control system

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

OMCO Solar now offers a version of its OMCO Star Tracker Control System powered entirely by solar energy, allowing tracker controls to operate without AC power at the site.

The system, first launched last year, is designed for OMCO Origin Factory-Direct Trackers. Its tracker controllers, array controllers and sensor bridges use all-weather battery packs charged by solar modules.

According to OMCO, the technology eliminates the need for AC power to tracker control components and an uninterruptible power supply. It also allows tracker controls to operate and protects modules against wind, snow and hail before AC power is available.

“Our industry has solved so many problems as we have grown – we’re now the leading source for new electric energy capacity in the US. This addition to the OMCO Star product line solves one more – how to protect modules if there’s no AC power to the site even for a short time. We’re excited to offer this solution to our existing customers and to new partners,” said Matt Kesler, vice president of solar technology at OMCO Solar, in a statement.

The OMCO Star system uses LoRa, or long-range, wireless technology with a communication range greater than 1 mile. According to OMCO, this reduces the number of network controllers required and allows direct communication with every tracker without relying on a mesh network.

The company says LoRa is 100 times less susceptible to electromagnetic noise than Zigbee and better able to connect to distant trackers despite obstacles.

OMCO says its all-weather battery packs allow the solar-powered controls to operate reliably in cold climates. The system includes field-replaceable batteries, a phone app that eliminates the need for buttons on controllers and a remote monitoring and control portal.

The portal allows operators to move trackers for activities such as mowing, snow plowing and module cleaning. The system is developed, manufactured and supported by OMCO Solar in the United States.

“We are excited about bringing this innovation to the Utility Scale and DG tracker markets. Over the past few years, OMCO has expanded its capabilities beyond just being a US steel manufacturer to now being a true technology leader in our space. We look forward to supporting our customers/partners with the OMCO Star Tracker Control System, improving reliability, and reducing the labor and materials needed to build their projects,” said Eric Goodwin, vice president of business development at OMCO Solar.

OMCO Solar will display the all-DC tracker control system at RE+ in Las Vegas, November 17–19, in the North Hall, booth N516.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scottish football has been dominated by two teams since 1986, with only Celtic and Rangers winning the league titles in the last 40 years.

Celtic have been the more dominant of the great Glasgow rivals, winning 22 titles compared to Rangers' 18 in that time, although the green side of the city has the benefit of being in the division the entire time, while Rangers spent four years climbing back into the top flight following administration and liquidation in 2012, which saw them restart in the fourth tier.

Celtic, meanwhile, have been able to build a number of dominant sides over the years, including during the period of 2000 and 2006, where they won four league titles.

That squad, managed by Martin O'Neill and then Gordon Strachan, was stacked with some incredible talent. The likes of Henrik Larsson, John Hartson, Alan Thompson, Chris Sutton, Bobo Balde, Stillian Petrov were all key members of the squad, and all wrote themselves into Celtic folklore in that period.

But someone who is often overlooked is winger Didier Agathe, who made 122 appearances for the club after joining in a deal worth just £27,000 from Hibernian.

Once described as 'incredible' by Sutton after playing alongside him, the Frenchman was one of the quickest players in Celtic's history, and is fondly remembered for his time at Parkhead.

But these days, the 51-year-old lives an incredibly different life as a bar owner in Thailand.

According to Eddie Murphy, the owner of 'The Irish House' in Pattaya, Agathe is now a part-owner of the Celtic-themed bar.

Murphy opened the bar in Thailand last year, and revealed to Glasgow Live that they have had some very familiar faces visit, including Hoops legend Henrik Larsson.

“I'd had the bar for around a year, before he wanted to buy into the bar and improve it.

“We've had Tyson Fury and Henrik Larsson in it, and when Didier Agathe came in, he loved it and he wanted to buy it. When he came, he phoned (Lubomir) Moravcik and said, 'You need to see this bar, it's unbelievable'.

According to Murphy, the bar is the only Celtic-themed bar of its kind in Thailand, and offers tourists a taste of home by offering traditional pub food and Guinness.

Agathe is someone who is fondly remembered in Glasgow for his role in their success during his six seasons with the club, and it's clear that he has taken that affection for the club with him into his post-football career choices.

Now based in Thailand, the ex-winger owns 50% of the bar and is constantly in contact with Mr. Murphy in order to help run the business and improve it.

While he only scored 11 goals in his 122 appearances, it was his pace and direct running that often caught the eye, and helped the team transition from defence to attack very quickly.

Signed after a brief stint at Hibernian, Agathe went on to become a regular under O'Neill at Celtic where he won three Scottish Premiership titles, three Scottish Cups, a Scottish League Cup, and also reached the UEFA Cup final in 2003, where the Bhoys were beaten by Jose Mourinho's Porto side.

He Wore Southampton's Number 7 Shirt After Le Tissier - Then He Was Given 2-Year Prison Sentence

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Southampton are currently aiming to win promotion back to the Premier League this season under Tonda Eckert following the Spygate scandal last term, and it was a similar story in the 2005-06 campaign.

After an absolute mess of a 2004-05 season, Southampton were relegated to England's second tier after spending the previous 27 years in the top flight.

The great work done by the likes of Matthew Le Tissier in keeping the club in the Premier League was undone, and it was up to Harry Redknapp to secure an immediate return the following season having failed to save the club from the drop.

As ever when a club suffers relegation, there were huge changes to the playing squad at St Mary's, with the likes of Peter Crouch, Kevin Phillips and Fabrice Fernandes departing, and those three were among 24 departures during the whole campaign.

With so many outgoings, it was clear that there would be a lot of incomings too, especially with Redknapp at the helm, and 15 players arrived at St Mary's to boost their promotion push.

Saints barely spent any money either as they counted the cost of relegation, with Ricardo Fuller joining from arch-rivals Portsmouth for a bargain £90,000.

On the same day, the south coast outfit also completed the signing of experienced Polish centre-back Tomasz Hajto, who moved to England after spells in Germany with MSV Duisburg, Schalke and FC Nurnberg.

The then 32-year-old had more than 60 caps for his country at that point, and his signing was seen as a coup for Southampton as they looked to achieve instant promotion back to the promised land.

Speaking on the day he signed, Hajto said:

Southampton are a big club. Over the years I have watched a lot of Premier League football and they have always been there and I hope to help them get back there.

"They are among the favourites to go up and they have a lot of ambition to do that. They had 27 years at that level and deserve to be there."

Strangely, despite being a central defender, Hajto was handed the number seven shirt by the club, which had famously been worn by Matthew Le Tissier, one of the greatest Premier League players of all time, just a few years before.

While he was a centre-back by trade, Hajto was actually often used as a right-back in the early months of his Saints career, having been viewed as a back-up option by Redknapp in the middle.

The early indications were promising as Hajto showed that as well as being an assured defender, he was also comfortable on the ball too, as he liked to produce crossfield passes to his teammates.

He began to be left out of the squad before Redknapp left the club in December, and his replacement George Burley agreed to cancel his contract in January to allow him to join Derby County instead.

The Meaning and Value of Stress

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

For the last 60 years or so, we've been warned about stress and told to do everything we can to avoid it.

We've been taught how to meditate our way out of stress, down-regulate from it, "hack our cortisol," reduce our adrenaline, manage our vagus nerve, calm ourselves, numb ourselves, distract ourselves, and halt our stress responses in as many ways as we can.

We've also been taught to view many of the difficulties in our lives as always-negative exterior forces, or stressors, that act upon us without our knowledge or permission. Stress has been presented to us as a dangerously unwanted thing with no value whatsoever.

Thankfully, newer research into stressors and our stress response has found the opposite to be true.

In part 1 of this series, I focused on the weasel word of stress, because our stress response is an emotional response. In my work, I help people understand the emotions that form the foundation of our stress response. We learn how to name these emotions with precision and work directly with them to access the essential intelligence they contribute to us.

Three of the main emotions I see in our stress response are: anxiety (the emotion of motivation and task completion), anger (which helps us identify and uphold what we value), and panic (which steps forward when our lives are in danger).

Each of these emotions helps us meet stressors with intelligence and focus. When we know which emotions are supporting us, and we know how to work with those emotions (instead of against them), stressful situations can teach us who we are, what's important to us, and how we can meet life's challenges with focus, skills, and resilience.

But if we don't know how to identify or work with our emotions, we may feel overwhelmed as our bodies increase our activation to help us meet the necessary stressors we face. We may also learn two unhelpful lessons:

If we believe that stressors are foreign and that our emotions are problems, we'll create unnecessary suffering, not just in response to the stressors but because our skills are missing in action.

Psychological researcher Kelly McGonigal has been focusing on stress for many years, and in her 2016 book, The Upside of Stress: Why Stress Is Good for You, and How to Get Good at It, she shares information from decades of stress research. Her book is full of surprising facts that turn our understanding of stress on its ear. For instance:

If I told you that the price of tiger chow has gone up by 40 percent, you might have little to no reaction (assuming you aren't a tiger keeper). This is a big price increase, but because it has no meaning to you and does not impact any part of your life, your stress response will be quiet—as it should be; there's nothing for you to do about this, and your emotions don't need to be involved.

But if I told you that your share of the cost for your child's monthly doctor's visits has gone up by 40 percent, it's likely that your stress response would engage. Your emotions, most notably anxiety and anger, might intensify to bring you the energy and focus you need to work the problem. Your stress response would help you know that this situation is important, that it's worth your time and focus, and that you need its increased energy to help you make new plans to support your child's well-being.

Sadly, most stress management approaches would have you try to calm yourself or suppress your emotions (or merely observe them), as if the activation you feel is a problem in and of itself. But this old, tired idea is not only wrong; it's counterproductive, because the strength of your stress response is a key to your success and even mastery of the situation.

Of all the surprising things in stress research, this may be the most delightful: The strength of your stress response—your emotional intensity, your hyper-focus, your "nerves"—is directly connected to your ability to thrive. And this continues to be true even when you're well-acquainted with a stressor and no longer see it as surprising or shocking.

Researchers studied new and seasoned skydivers, certain that they would see a reduced stress response (reduced cortisol, adrenaline, and heart rate, less "stress") as the skydivers became accustomed to throwing themselves out of planes. Strangely, there wasn't any reduction in the markers of stress in the seasoned skydivers; in fact, the most skilled skydivers continued to exhibit very high stress responses, often higher than people jumping out of planes for the first time.

What they did find was that the seasoned skydivers had reframed their stress responses as excitement, readiness, stimulation, and even zest for life. This reframing helped them treat their activation as a necessary and fun part of skydiving, even though they might have experienced the exact same stress response as overwhelming the first time they leapt out of a plane.

This seemingly paradoxical relationship holds true for other high-performers such as athletes, surgeons, artists, actors, and musicians. A higher stress response does not predict failure; it is intimately connected to skill and mastery.

So, the key is not to avoid stressors or your stress response, and it's not to become an endlessly chilled-out and calm person.

The key to developing your resilience alongside your skills is to learn how to reframe your natural responses to stressors and welcome the stimulation, focus, and preparedness they provide.

If you've ever wondered what an NFL player's paycheck looks like, we now know the answer thanks to Rashee Rice. The Chiefs wide receiver recently filed for bankruptcy, and as part of the court filing, he had to include a copy of his pay stub that he gets from the team each week.

The paycheck offers a rare inside look into where all the money goes for an NFL player. Rice is currently in the fourth and final year of a rookie deal that's paying him $1.635 million in base salary this year, but when it comes to his take-home pay, it's much more complicated than just looking at his base salary.

Like the rest of us, Rice has to pay taxes and he also has to pay his NFLPA dues. Not to mention, his paycheck is also being garnished to pay off several civil liabilities that he's facing.

For the week of Oct. 19-25, Rice got paid $43,353.08, but he only got to take home a small portion of that.

Let's take a look at where the money went.

It's certainly interesting to see how little Rice ends up taking home here.

After all the deductions were made, Rice's total net pay was $14,665.25. That means he only got to take home 33.9% of his paycheck. Most players don't have to deal with garnishments or fines, so let's take those out real quick. If Rice did NOT have to pay those two things, he still would have taken home only 52.7% of his paycheck ($22,860.96).

Another interesting thing about this pay stub is that you can see all the ways a player gets paid besides their base salary. For instance, Rice has been paid over $350,000 in performance pay along with a $75,000 workout bonus this year. Rice also earned $20,500 just for attending training camp. A player's salary only applies to games they play in, but they still do earn a stipend for training camp. Under the current CBA, veterans get paid $3,500 per week during training camp and this pay is equal for everyone, so Patrick Mahomes and Rice would make the same amount.

Although Rice is only making $43,000 per paycheck right now, that number will likely shoot up starting next year. The 26-year-old is in the final year of his rookie deal, and he'll definitely be cashing in this offseason, whether that's in free agency or signing an extension with the Chiefs.
