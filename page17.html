Paulson Adebo gets shot at Giants redemption after up-and-down season

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Paulson Adebo looked out of place at practice last week as the NFL's highest-paid "don't-forget-about-me" trying to find his place on the punt team.

Two days later, Adebo improbably looked totally at home as a shutdown cornerback whose wild ride this season is about to accelerate further because he has risen from purgatory to again be considered one of the Giants' most important players.

"Obviously, it's not where I wanted to be going into the season," Adebo said after Tuesday's meetings. "You prepare every week like you're going to play. If you're the third [cornerback], you're one play away from going in every game, so you owe it to yourself and you owe it to your teammates to be prepared."

Adebo, whose $24.1 million salary cap charge is the highest on the Giants, lost his starting job to Deonte Banks and Greg Newsome II in training camp. He was picked on off the bench in Week 1, missed the next five games due to injury, returned to play well against the Texans as Banks' fill-in, was a healthy scratch against the Commanders and then saved the day with four passes defended and an interception after Newsome was injured against the Jaguars.

With Newsome now expected to miss four to six weeks due to ankle surgery, Adebo will start opposite Banks on Sunday against the Colts. The Giants will face three of the NFL's top six passing attacks in that six-game window.

New York Giants cornerback Paulson Adebo (21) runs off the field after an interception against the Jacksonville Jaguars in East Rutherford, N.J., Nov. 22, 2026. IMAGN IMAGES via Reuters Connect

"I know what type of player I am," Adebo said. "I know what I can do on the field. I know what type of person I am, so I don't think it's anything new necessarily that I've learned about myself, but certainly just staying prepared and ready to go when you're called upon."

Only 20 months after signing a three-year, $54 million contract as the prize of the Giants' 2025 free agent class, Adebo was inactive against the Commanders because backup cornerbacks Colton Hood and Nic Jones offered more special teams value. Adebo played 13 total special teams snaps from 2022-25 as he developed into a starting corner for the Saints and then the Giants' No. 1 option against elite receivers.

Head coach John Harbaugh pulled aside Adebo last week to keep him engaged with a message that turned out to be prescient.

New York Giants' Greg Newsome II (20) runs during training camp at the Greenbrier in White Sulphur Springs, W.Va., Saturday, Aug. 1, 2026. Corey Sipkin for the NY POST

"I said, 'In my mind, you're a starter in this league. And when you started here, you've done really well. And you're going to have to start again,' " Harbaugh said Nov. 18. "So when that time comes, he's in a good place."

The time came Sunday when Adebo allowed two catches on eight targets. His fourth-quarter interception at the 28-yard line secured three points (to extend to a 14-point lead) at a time when the offense couldn't move the ball.

"I mean, I'm glad that [Harbaugh] thinks that," Adebo said. "I certainly think highly of myself. So, kind of been thinking that way the whole season. So, it wasn't anything new per se."

The stretch run of the season suddenly becomes a proving ground for Adebo, who recently looked like a lock to be a salary cap casualty ($14 million saved against $6.3 million dead money) in 2027. Could the Giants feel comfortable with Adebo and Newsome as starters next year if Banks leaves in free agency?

"He's a baller," safety Jevón Holland said. "He hasn't been playing that much this year, but it's just how the game goes. When they called his name, he went out and balled. I'm excited for him."

People snickered when Newsome prematurely declared the Giants cornerback room the best in the NFL during the preseason.

It's become harder to argue with his assessment as time has passed. The performance against Trevor Lawrence was a definitive argument because Banks added an interception and Dru Phillips made three of the most important plays (all breakups) of the game.

"I think I said it in training camp about how we have three, four guys that are really starters in this league," Adebo said. "So, there really is that mindset that we all know we can compete. So, just keep on executing, keep on doing what we've been doing."

The Oscar race for visual effects is down to 20, and the road to the 99th Academy Awards runs through Ithaca, Motunui and a galaxy far, far away.

The Academy’s Visual Effects Branch notified the artisans behind the 20 films advancing to the next round ahead of the Thanksgiving holiday. Through multiple sources, Variety has confirmed 19 of the finalists, including Denis Villeneuve’s “Dune: Part Three,” Christopher Nolan’s “The Odyssey,” Phil Lord and Christopher Miller’s “Project Hail Mary,” Steven Spielberg’s “Disclosure Day” and Marvel Studios’ “Avengers: Doomsday” from Anthony and Joe Russo. The Academy does not release the top 20 externally and declines to comment or confirm which films advanced.

Disney leads the confirmed titles with four. Alongside “Avengers: Doomsday,” the studio lands Jon Favreau’s “Star Wars: The Mandalorian and Grogu,” 20th Century Studios’ “Whalefall” and Thomas Kail’s live-action “Moana,” which brings Dwayne Johnson back as the demigod Maui.

Skydance (formerly Warner Bros. Discovery) took three of the spots: “Dune: Part Three,” Alejandro G. Iñárritu’s Tom Cruise-led “Digger” and Craig Gillespie’s “Supergirl” from former DC Studios heads James Gunn and Peter Safran. But the studio also sees four contenders fall short.

Villeneuve’s stakes are historic. Both “Dune” and “Dune: Part Two” won the visual effects Oscar, and in March, “Avatar: Fire and Ash” made James Cameron’s franchise three-for-three in the category. “Dune: Part Three,” which opens Dec. 18, now puts Villeneuve two steps from the same trilogy sweep.

Universal Pictures booked both of its summer event films, “The Odyssey” and “Disclosure Day,” while Amazon MGM Studios goes two-for-two with “Project Hail Mary” and Kyle Balda’s “The Sheep Detectives.”

Nolan’s track record in the category is just as formidable. “Inception,” “Interstellar” and “Tenet” each won the visual effects Oscar. However, the branch famously left “Oppenheimer” off its top 20 for the 96th Academy Awards. Spielberg has been here before, too, with “Jurassic Park,” which won the category in 1994.

Two of the more intriguing finalists come from outside the Hollywood studio system. Takashi Yamazaki’s “Godzilla Minus Zero” follows “Godzilla Minus One,” which won the category at the 96th Oscars and delivered the franchise its first Academy Award. Laika’s stop-motion “Wildwood,” directed by Travis Knight, gives the studio a chance to return to a category it cracked with Knight’s “Kubo and the Two Strings,” one of only two animated films ever nominated for visual effects, along with “The Nightmare Before Christmas.”

Sony lands three big movies in the field. Leading the charge is Destin Daniel Cretton’s “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” and the still-to-be-released “Jumanji: Open World,” advancing alongside “Ramayana: Rise of a Legend,” the Indian mythological epic. “Ramayana” follows S.S. Rajamouli’s “RRR,” which reached the top 20 for the 95th Oscars and signals the branch’s continued appetite for large-scale spectacle from outside Hollywood.

A24 also lands a spot with “Backrooms,” the feature directorial debut of 21-year-old Kane Parsons, whose YouTube series turned the internet’s liminal-space legend into a box office phenomenon, grossing more than $400 million worldwide.

Paramount received a split verdict. David Ayer’s survival thriller “Heart of the Beast,” starring Brad Pitt, advanced, but Variety confirmed that two of the studio’s other contenders, “Ebenezer” with Johnny Depp and the video game adaptation “Street Fighter,” did not make the top 20.

Netflix also breaks through with Peter Berg’s epic war drama “The Mosquito Bowl,” one year after the streamer placed “Frankenstein” and “The Electric State” on the shortlist.

That leaves one spot unconfirmed, with a few titles still awaiting confirmation. Variety will update this story as additional finalists are confirmed.

The preliminary voting period for the Oscars shortlist begins Monday, Dec. 7, at 9 a.m. PT and ends Friday, Dec. 11, at 5 p.m. PT. The shortlists in all categories will be announced Tuesday, Dec. 15. Afterward, the VFX bake-offs will take place during the Academy’s Jan. 8-10 voting events, when the 10 shortlisted films will be presented to branch members for final nomination consideration. Nominations voting runs Jan. 11-15, with the nominations announced Thursday, Jan. 21.

The 99th Academy Awards will be held Sunday, March 14, at the Dolby Theatre at Ovation Hollywood and will air live on ABC.

Why asking Trae Young to score less is a band-aid, not a fix for the Wizards' self-inflicted problem

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

The Washington Wizards entered Tuesday with a 7-11 record. In their seven wins, Trae Young is averaging just under 11.3 shot attempts per game. In those 11 losses, he's averaging 13.3. It's a small difference, but a notable one. The Wizards came into play Tuesday on a three-game winning streak, and a meaningful part of that streak has been Young spending less time on the ball.

In Washington's first 15 games, he averaged around 6.6 minutes of possession per game, tied for the third-most in the league. He's down to 4.5 in his last three games. He's dribbling around one fewer time per touch, and his average possession per touch is down around a second. These aren't glaring changes, but they've been helpful ones. It's not a coincidence, for example, that No. 1 pick AJ Dybantsa's two best offensive games have come in this stretch.

Young, 28, has been a mess as a scorer this season. He's shooting just 36.4% from the floor and 32.5% on 3s. He's barely getting to the basket and struggling mightily when he's in the paint. He can't beat defenders nearly as easily off the dribble as he once could. Getting the ball out of his hands a bit more has been essential.

It's been an intentional shift on Washington's part. Wizards officials have asked Young to focus less on his own scoring and instead emphasize his playmaking, according to The Athletic. Washington certainly won't complain about a 24-to-7 assist-to-turnover ratio in that stretch. Young, despite his noticeable decline as a scorer, remains a stellar passer. That's typically how star decline works. It comes in bits and pieces. Even as essential components of a player's game degrade, years of accumulated basketball IQ allow him to continue finding ways to contribute. After years of abject tanking, the Wizards seemingly wanted an offensive organizer around to help give their younger players some structure. Young can still do that.

But we're in an increasingly financially unforgiving NBA, and the Wizards aren't paying Young a contributor's salary. They're paying him star money, and they chose to do so after just five relatively quiet games with him a season ago. His four-year, $212 million max contract will pay him at the top of the market through the end of Dybantsa's rookie deal. With Anthony Davis on the books through the end of Kyshawn George and Alex Sarr's rookie deals, the Wizards effectively traded the roster-building flexibility those cheap rookie deals afforded them for two declining stars.

Davis has justified the investment, at least as an asset. They haven't extended him. They could potentially flip him for more than they paid him. Young has not.

And that's the dilemma the Wizards are now in.

Clearly, less Young leads to more winning for the Wizards. But getting enough out of him to justify that enormous contract almost certainly requires more than they're currently getting. The entire value proposition of employing Young rests on him being a high-usage offensive engine. He just doesn't provide enough off-ball offensive value to justify what he gives up defensively at a max price point.

Take his shooting, for example. "Trae's got so much gravity," Wizards coach Brian Keefe told The Athletic. "When he plays off the ball, he's going to draw people, and then we're getting into actions with him on the second side that have been really good." There's something to this in theory, especially since gravity tends to rest more on reputation than reality. But Young has never been a consistently reliable catch-and-shoot player. He's averaging less than two catch-and-shoot 3-point attempts per game this season and has made around 28% of them. He's never averaged more than two catch-and-shoot 3-point attempts per game in a season.

Maybe he improves if his role demands more of these shots, but this is his ninth NBA season. That sort of shift this late in a career is rare.

It's a daunting change. Young spent most of his seven-and-a-half-year Atlanta Hawks run as almost entirely an on-ball player. He's never scored more than 39 points off of cuts in a single season. He has none this season. He's never been an especially active mover or screener, and even if he's grown on that front over the last few years, that's from a starting point of essentially zero. Asking Young to be less of a heliocentric shot creator is essentially asking Young to be a different player entirely.

That was the danger in paying Young sight unseen. They had no idea how he'd fit on this roster when they ponied up a max deal. If he'd picked up his player option and made this a prove-it season, they could have worked out the kinks and tried to sign him to a more appropriate deal next summer. The obvious argument here is that Young might have left had the Wizards refused to give him a long-term extension, but the counter here is that the Wizards would almost certainly have been better off if that had happened.

Stephen Noh's salary model estimates that Young is worth $26.3 million so far this season. That's a bit less than half of what he's getting. If that persists over the life of the deal, it's among the NBA's worst contracts. If his scoring continues to decline as he loses burst with age, it's a borderline crisis. Young may remain a passing genius, but passing skill is not a one-to-one match with passing value. It's easier to set teammates up when you're a dangerous enough scorer to consistently draw help. If Young isn't generating the same sort of advantages he once did, the passes that were once available to him suddenly become harder.

All of this puts the Wizards in an extremely precarious long-term position. They had to get the ball out of Young's hands more in the present. Too much of their offense involved players more important to the organization's long-term plans -- Dybantsa chief among them -- watching him dribble. A lower-usage Young probably makes the Wizards a better team simply by spreading usage to players who need more of it.

But that doesn't mean that a lower-usage Young is especially valuable. On-ball creation is where much of Young's value is generated. His scoring has declined so much that he's not generating the same value he used to, so all of this has stuck the Wizards between a rock and a hard place. They can try to maximize Young's skill set at the expense of everyone else, or they can try to maximize the team as a whole. But in doing so, they're not getting nearly enough out of a player they're paying to be a centerpiece. The latter is probably the right decision, but it's still a choice between two bad options. It's a band-aid on a self-inflicted wound.

Mister Rogers' Son John Rogers Dead at 65

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Fred Rogers' son John Rogers—who was raised by the creator and star of Mister Rogers' Neighborhood and wife Joanne Byrd—died at 65 years old, more than two decades after the late TV host.

Mister Rogers' neighborhood is in mourning.

Beloved TV host Fred Rogers' youngest son, John Rogers, died at 65 years old, his family confirmed to TMZ. His cause of death was not shared.

John was born in 1961 to the puppeteer—who made television history as America's neighbor on Mister Rogers' Neighborhood from 1968 to 2001—and his wife Joanne Byrd Rogers following the arrival of son James Byrd Rogers in 1959.

John appeared with his brother on the children's show during their childhood in Pittsburgh, Pa., but went on to live a life outside of the public eye, which included a move to Florida in his 30s. However, he has occasionally returned to the spotlight to celebrate his father's legacy following his 2003 death, such as accepting a star on the Pittsburgh Walk of Fame on Fred's behalf.

"How many people have said 'It's a beautiful day in the neighborhood?'" John quoted the series' iconic opening line at the event. "What an honor this is, firstly for my dad."

Though Fred's show ended in 2001, John noted how much his dad's impact lived on with new generations.

In his words, "It's hard to believe he's been gone since 2003. His memory and his work live on beyond belief."

And having grown up in the city where his dad became an ordained Presbyterian Minister, John felt endeared to return back home over the years, noting, "Pittsburgh sort of reminds me of him."

"I can't find nicer people than Pittsburgh, and that's why I visit here quite often," he continued. "It's a lot of Mister Rogers-type people that live here that really are caring and wonderful, loving people."

In addition to accepting the posthumous honor, John met with Tom Hanks on the set of the biopic about Fred's life,  A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood. The late 74-year-old's wife Joanne was closely involved in the making of the 2019 film, before her death two years later.

"We're happy about the movie. Thought it was wonderful. I thought Tom Hanks' portrayal of the movie was just fabulous,” John gushed on Fox and Friends in 2019. "And when I heard that Tom was going to do it, I said in baseball terms, well, that's a grand slam, home run."

This show of relative strength has been a theme for the QNT token since Saturday, the 21st of November. The altcoin was up 21.35% since the previous week’s low, compared to Bitcoin’s 9.67% bounce, or the altcoin market cap’s modest 7% rebound.

Coinalyze data revealed a steady Spot CVD uptick over the weekend that continued on Monday. Alongside the rest of the crypto market, QNT also retraced the Nvidia news-related bounce on Monday.

Yet, its Open Interest was up slightly, while the funding rates remained negative. This indicated speculative traders were paying to keep their short positions open, revealing some bearish conviction.

This could work against them. A short squeeze was already past us, but sustained short selling could fuel further volatility and stop-loss hunts.

In other news, Quant’s Fusion Rollup was opened to public access on the 23rd of November. The mainnet launched in June 2026 was only available to some early users.

Fusion Rollup connects “permissionless and permissioned blockchains in a single, unified execution environment”, the announcement revealed.

The $126 support level was the 78.6% Fibonacci retracement level. AMBCrypto reported earlier this month that the level was a far-off but valid price target.

So it has proved. The altcoin was down 24.22% in November, but its deep correction has forced a retest of an imbalance on the 1-day timeframe.

This fair value gap has confluence with the Fibonacci retracement level, making a QNT recovery much more likely. The BTC and wider market pessimism could be an oppressive obstacle to the bulls.

QNT’s breakout past $160 recently, though retraced, was a notable sign of bullish intent. More gains were likely to follow.
