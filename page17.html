What has changed in James Bond films over the decades

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In 1962, a martini-drinking womanizer saved the world for the first time in "Dr. No." As Robert Pattinson takes on the role of 007, here's how the films evolved.

After years of speculation, Amazon MGM Studios announced on Monday that Robert Pattinson would be taking on the role of James Bond following Daniel Craig's departure after five hit films in the spy series.

This year alone, Pattinson has starred in "The Odyssey," "The Drama" and "Primetime." He is also famous for his role as "Twilight" vampire Edward Cullen.

The upcoming Bond movie will be directed by Denis Villeneuve, an Oscar nominee known for movies such as the "Dune" trilogy and "Arrival."

Based on the spy novels by author Ian Fleming, the Bond franchise — about a British secret agent known for his charm and ⁠a ⁠fondness for martinis "shaken, not stirred" — is among the longest-running and highest-grossing in Hollywood history. ​​​​​​​

The first film in the series, "Dr. No," premiered in London on October 5, 1962.

Playing the leading role, Agent 007, was an unknown actor: Sean Connery.

In the film, we are first introduced to his character as he sits in a casino across from an attractive woman who is about to gamble away her fortune. When she asks 007 his name, the camera pans to Bond lighting a cigarette. With a smoldering look in his eye, he utters the legendary words: "Bond, James Bond."

Shortly thereafter in the film, 007 receives an assignment to fly to Jamaica and investigate the murder of another M16 agent. He eventually tracks down a Chinese-German criminal scientist who is bent on destroying the US space program and achieving world domination.

In principle, this story is the basis of all of 25 James Bond films. Whether the villain is Dr. No, Blofeld, Goldfinger or Le Chiffre, they all aim for one thing: world domination. But to do so, they have to get past James Bond whose gunfire and wild chase scenes inevitably spoil their plans.

Although many fan forums consider "Dr. No" to be one of the best Bond films, the movie would never be made in the same way today.

Firstly, one could write entire books about the omnipresent sexism and machismo that the character James Bond has displayed over the decades.

Certainly there would have to be a thick chapter dedicated to the women in his films, who are typically portrayed as naive, willingly flinging themselves into Bond's arms, seeking protection. Sometimes they are sophisticated female antagonists who lose control at the thought of hopping into bed with the secret agent.

Whether friend of foe, each film had at least one Bond girl — and often more: More than 60 love interests have appeared in the films. Their names, like "Honey" or "Pussy Galore" would never be acceptable today. In more recent decades, female roles in Bond films have changed — as has Bond himself.

In the last five films in which Daniel Craig has played Bond, he is seen as a man who is tough on the outside and soft on the inside — and who is not always lucky with women.

Aside from the portrayal of women on screen, there are also plenty of other details in "Dr. No" that haven't quite stood the test of time.

Another sign of the zeitgeist in the 1962 film is that everyone smokes, which is a no-no by today's Hollywood standards. Cigarettes are found everywhere in the old film, from the casino to the hotel bar — and even the bedroom.

Smoking was commonplace in the US in the 1970s, so naturally this came across on the silver screen. In the 1970s TV series "Space: 1999" people were even depicted smoking on the moon, but by the 2000s, smoking was largely banned in many parts of the world due to its health risks.

One can also note in "Dr. No" that few people are wearing seat belts. When Bond drives off in his sports car to visit a woman, he is followed and a chase scene ensues. Bond, notably, is not wearing a seat belt.

His pursuers eventually drive off a slope and die, of course — after all, they aren't wearing seat belts either and cars didn't have airbags at the time. The vehicle explodes, prompting 007 to say, "They were obviously in a hurry to get to their own funeral."

In the older Bond films, all secretaries and receptionists are played by women, which in the 1960s, were considered standard female jobs.

Whether redheaded, blonde or brunette, secretaries are Bond's favorite targets for womanizing and they are all susceptible to his charms.

Throughout most Bond films, the agent has a romantic tension with Moneypenny, his boss's secretary, something which was not described in the original books.

In the 2012 film "Skyfall" Moneypenny is given a first name: Eve, and we finally learn her backstory. In this film, she is no longer a hopeless secretary in love, but a top-trained agent who accidentally shoots Bond and is reassigned to desk duty as punishment.

In any case, today Moneypenny's job title today would probably be "Office Manager" or "Executive Assistant" rather than "Secretary."

In the film, Dr. No tells James Bond that he is the child of a British missionary and a Chinese woman from an upstanding family. However, the character is portrayed by Canadian actor Joseph Wiseman who is wearing makeup to make his eyes look more "Asian."

While it used to be the norm in such movies to have East Asian characters played by white actors, today these cases of "yellowface" are frowned upon — just as it would be to wear blackface, putting black makeup on one's skin to portray a Black person.

If there was a "Dr. No" remake in the future, a Chinese actor would be more likely to play this role.

Daniel Craig already paved the way in modernizing James Bond by stripping away campy gadgets, punchlines and invincibility, replacing them with a grounded, psychological and emotionally vulnerable portrayal of a post-9/11 spy.

Now it is up to Robert Pattinson to revisit the famous MI6 agent. Film critics believe his background will allow him to add dark sophistication and nonconformism to the iconic espionage role.

Throughout his career, Robert Pattinson has managed to combine roles in blockbuster hits and indie gems, from Bruce Wayne in "The Batman" to a disposable worker who is cloned every time he dies in "Mickey 17."

Pattinson already has experience with classic spy archetypes: As the mysterious Neil in Christopher Nolan's sci-fi action thriller "Tenet," he assisted the protagonist in a time-bending espionage mission. He is also known for his eccentric personality and deadpan humor — qualities that could be useful for a modern Bond.

Ahead of its IPO filing, Anthropic pledges to prioritize humanity’s benefit. Here’s how

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Anthropic goes public on Wall Street, the new pressure to deliver profits will test a commitment it has made since its founding: to prioritize humanity’s benefit as it develops artificial intelligence.

The San Francisco startup, which plans an initial public offering this month, has sketched out a governance structure that it says will protect the company’s broader mission against the pull of financial interests. But the mechanisms it describes give the company wide leeway to decide how to measure whether it’s benefiting society, experts say.

Like its rival OpenAI, Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, a legal designation used by companies that pledge to balance shareholder interests with the public good. How that will play out for companies worth potentially trillions of dollars is uncharted territory.

“I think then the real test emerges if and when the question of public benefit becomes costly” — or when the public benefit comes into conflict with making a profit, said Sarah Kreps, a professor and director of Cornell University’s Tech Policy Institute.

In its regulatory filings, Anthropic pledges to pursue long-term benefits of humanity through the responsible development of advanced AI. “We strive to help bring humanity safely into an era where transformative AI exerts tremendous and potentially decisive influence over a broad range of human undertakings,” the company said. Anthropic also has pledged at least 5% of its stock to a “public benefit” fund and its founders have committed to donate 80% of their wealth.

A pledge to do good is baked into corporate structure

Other tech companies have pledged to do good including Google, which prominently featured its then-motto, “Don’t Be Evil,” in its investor prospectus when it went public in 2004. But the words were not legally binding, and critics have accused the tech giant of leaving them behind.

Elon Musk’s takeover of Twitter in 2021 shows how the conflict between profit and public good might play out in real life, said Stavros Gadinis, a law professor at UC Berkeley School of Law.

“Let’s say that Twitter’s directors felt that Elon was the wrong guy to police the public square because they didn’t trust him, they didn’t like him, they didn’t think he’d be fair, whatever it was,” Gadinis said. “But he made them an offer that was so high compared to what their advisors told them the company was really worth that it would be against their fiduciary duties to not sell to Elon. This is the situation that the public benefit charter is trying to avoid.”

Had Twitter been a public benefit corporation, it could have been required to consider factors beyond maximizing shareholder value when selling the company.

There aren’t many public benefit corporations whose shares are publicly traded and they are all much smaller than Anthropic and OpenAI. They include the eyewear company Warby Parker, the insurance company Lemonade and software developer Veeva Systems, which had a conventional corporate structure when it went public in 2013 and converted to a public benefit corporation in 2021.

Anthropic’s idealistic ambitions for how it develops AI could run up against competitive pressure, such as from China, Kreps said.

Anthropic and OpenAI’s models have gone from answering almost any question, even if it was dangerous, to becoming much more circumspect. But as they face more competition, including from open-source Chinese models that likely will tell you whatever you want, she said, “will they start to rethink some of those guardrails? And what happens then?”

The setup generally is not preferred by shareholders because it gives more power to the company’s board than it does to shareholders.

Whatever a company decides as its public benefit mission, it will likely be much more difficult to measure than profits. That’s particularly true for AI companies, whose leaders have exalted AI as the solution to many of humanity’s problems — from curing cancer to creating economic prosperity.

“There are these echo chambers, the Silicon Valley bubble where they really think that what they’re doing is for the good of humanity,” Kreps said. And someone immersed in that environment, she added, might not even ask the right questions or understand how “public benefit” should be measured.

A trust aims to hold Anthropic accountable

A Long-Term Benefit Trust created by Anthropic is intended to operate as an independent body of trustees with expertise in AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.

Through a special Class T common stock, the trust “generally has the authority to elect and remove” Class T directors, who together will constitute a majority of Anthropic’s board of directors, according to the IPO filing. The Class T common stock is a single share that allows the five trustees to vote as a unit. It has no economic rights — meaning they get to vote but they don’t see any financial benefit or losses from the share and can’t sell it.

“The LTBT’s arrangements are designed to minimize the LTBT’s and the Trustees’ exposure to our financial performance and grant the Trustees sufficient independence to ensure we are striking this balance appropriately,” the filing said.

Three of the company’s board directors are elected by the trust directly, and another three are elected by other shareholders, which include the company founders with a 50.1% voting majority. A seventh director, with a potentially tie-breaking vote, is nominated by the trust and the holders of the Class T shares, but cannot be appointed or removed without consent from the board’s nominating committee, which is expected to be controlled by the founders.

Effectively, the trust can only hold Anthropic accountable on issues such as AI safety and the company’s broader mission up to a point, Gadinis said.

The trust “cannot determine the everyday decisions of these directors or the management of the company,” he said. “If they disagree, they will have to go through the effort of removing directors, and they may not be able to remove a majority.”

As a public company, Anthropic also will be required to report finances and generally be more transparent than a privately held one. Many public companies dealing with AI, including Google and Microsoft, regularly update the public on efforts to develop AI responsibly.

The incentive for transparency could be as powerful as other mechanisms set up in Anthropic’s corporate structure, Gadinis said.

“I do think that by being public, we are going to get a lot more information, because a lot of people are going to be asking questions, and companies will be responding to these questions. not only because they’re required by security laws, but also because they want to build this kind of trust with the investing community,” he said.

Novo Nordisk's weight loss injection cut obesity in young children, trial shows

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

In the STEP Young trial, BMI fell an estimated 17.4% with semaglutide and rose 2.2% with placebo at week 68 among children 6 to under 12

Novo Nordisk shared results on Monday from a Phase 3 trial showing its once-weekly semaglutide injection, sold as Wegovy, lowered body mass index in children ages 6 to under 12 with obesity. The company presented the STEP Young data at ObesityWeek 2026 and published them in The New England Journal of Medicine.

At week 68, estimated BMI fell 17.4% from baseline among children taking semaglutide and rose 2.2% among those on placebo, assuming all children stayed on treatment, the company said. The estimated difference was 19.7 percentage points. Counting all children regardless of whether they stayed on treatment, BMI fell 13.5% with semaglutide and rose 2.7% with placebo, a difference of 16.2 percentage points.

If all children had stayed on treatment, 40.4% of those on semaglutide would no longer have been classified as having obesity, versus 0% on placebo. Regardless of adherence, the figures were 38.4% and 2.1%. Both groups were put on an eating plan with fewer calories and encouraged to move more for the length of the study.

The trial enrolled 167 children, and more than 85% had class II or III severe obesity at baseline, Novo said. Doses reached a maximum of 1.7 milligrams or 2.4 milligrams, depending on baseline weight. At week 104, BMI had fallen an estimated 9.4% with semaglutide and risen 8.4% with placebo when all children were counted. Novo said the week 104 results were not adjusted for multiplicity and cannot be used for inferences.

Safety and tolerability matched earlier Novo pediatric and adult semaglutide trials, and no unexpected safety concerns or concerns about growth or pubertal development emerged, the company said. The 2.4-milligram injection carries a boxed warning for possible thyroid tumors, including cancer.

"Effective treatments for severe childhood obesity may shift a child's trajectory back towards one of improving weight related health," Ania M. Jastreboff, a professor at Yale School of Medicine and director of the Yale Obesity Research Center, said in a statement.

Semaglutide injection is not approved in the U.S. for children under 12, and Novo said there is no guarantee it will become commercially available for that use. Wegovy is approved for obesity in children 12 and older, and no medications are approved for younger children, according to CNBC.

Close to half of the children in the trial had at least one obesity-related complication, among them hypertension, elevated cholesterol and sleep apnea, according to CNBC.

Dr. Andrea Traina, Novo's medical director lead of obesity, said the two-year study cannot show if starting therapy sooner alters how the disease unfolds over a lifetime, or what ongoing care would require once these children become adults, calling it "an evidence gap," according to CNBC.

Childhood obesity touched about 177 million young people between the ages of 5 and 19 around the globe in 2025, according to the World Obesity Federation, as cited in Novo's release.

United CEO Still Keen on Merger Prospects

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Months after American Airlines rejected his pitch for a merger, United CEO Scott Kirby remains convinced that U.S. carriers will have to consolidate to thrive.

“I think the biggest impediment is having a willing partner,” Kirby said at the Yahoo Finance Invest conference on Nov. 12. “I am more convinced today than I was when I wrote my note six months ago that it could get approval. It could get approval because it is good for customers.”

Kirby’s original pitch to American shocked the aviation industry, as it would have brought together two of the country’s largest commercial airlines and given the combined company a market share of just over 34%, a level of dominance not seen by any U.S. carrier in years. Analysts predicated that the Justice Department would reject the tie-up as anticompetitive, citing regulators’ decisions on much smaller attempted mergers over the past several years.

The speculation ended when American released a public statement saying it had no interest in a merger, and would stick to its existing turnaround plan.

But Kirby has continued to defend his proposal and the logic behind it, arguing that a consolidated United-American would deliver a better experience for customers, create more high-paying unionized jobs, and make the U.S. more competitive in international travel.

The CEO acknowledged Thursday that such a large deal would face obstacles but insisted they could be overcome.

“It wouldn’t be easy,” he said. “We’d have to do some divestitures, but I 100% think it could get approved.”

Airlines around the world are facing new pressure to cut costs or consolidate as jet fuel prices rise. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said in April that he believes there is “room for some mergers” in the airline industry, but did not mention United or American by name.

Neither the Justice Department nor the Transportation Department ever formally weighed in on the potential United-American merger, as a final deal was never filed. President Donald Trump said he would not support it, and cited competition and consumer prices as the reason.

Crook turns free trials into phishing factory, as LearnWorlds sends 13,000 fake YouTube copyright notices

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

570 bogus accounts, one rewritten welcome email, and a fake YouTube page, but CEO says no breach

LearnWorlds, a software and learning course platform, says an attacker sent 13,000 phishing emails to customers over seven days by using automated tools to open free trial accounts. The company insists its systems were not breached.

Panos Siozos, Co-founder and CEO at LearnWorlds, said the miscreant created around 570 free trial accounts on the platform between November 7-14, which they used to issue fake copyright infringement notices to customers.

"We have found no evidence of unauthorized access to existing customer schools or personal data held for our customers and their end users," he told The Register.

According to Siozos, the attacker bypassed the platform's per-customer limit on trial subscriptions and exploited its welcome email feature to send the phishing emails.

"In each trial they added their own list of target addresses and rewrote the standard welcome email as a fake copyright notice," he said. "Adding those users triggered the welcome email from our sending address."

The first email was sent on November 7, after which the company detected the suspicious signups and blocked the first sender on November 9, although the attacker returned to send a second wave of emails within 40 minutes.

LearnWorlds removed all the trial accounts by November 10 and one paid account, which was used to launch more emails, on November 14.

"In total, around 13,000 emails were sent to addresses on external recipient lists supplied by the sender," said Siozos. "The trial accounts sent 9,853 between November 7-9, and the paid account sent 3,723 on November 14.

"We can’t tell how many people clicked, because the link was disguised to hide clicks. The recipient addresses were the sender’s own list."

The CEO confirmed that LearnWorlds has since enhanced its bot checks and is placing holds on new accounts if their emails trigger fraud flags.

"We have also examined the novel patterns the attackers used to create more advanced scoring mechanisms. We have reported the phishing website so it can be taken down. Further measures are currently being examined.

"Anyone who signed in on the fake page should change their Google password and review their account’s recent security activity."

The company's confirmation follows suspicions of foul play aired by users on social media. User reports included copies of the emails they received, which demanded urgent responses to fake copyright infringement claims related to YouTube content.

Among the more prominent recipients of the copyright infringement email notices was Marcus Hutchins, of WannaCry fame, who claimed he received an email from the company’s official mail server.

The email, a copy of which Hutchins posted to X, referenced general intellectual property and copyright infringements but failed to specify any specific issues. The email prompted the recipient to click a YouTube link to see the “supporting documentation submitted by LearnWorks.”

The link predictably turned out to be a YouTube redirect link, taking recipients to a separate website that presents them with a real Google OAuth page. If the user completed the authorization flow, it would have handed attackers control of the target’s Google and YouTube accounts.

“The site is designed to look like an official YouTube webpage that enables users to review copyright claims against their channel,” Hutchins wrote. “It contains both the YouTube and Google branding, as well as a very similar theme to the official YouTube one.”

According to Hutchins' screenshots, and those posted by others, the attacker was actively tweaking the email style and format, although the underlying lure and authentication mechanisms remained the same.

Hutchins didn't specify exactly how the phishing page steals access to targets’ Google accounts, although his description aligns with the steps often seen in OAuth consent phishing attacks.

These involve malicious websites serving victims genuine OAuth pages, into which unwitting victims enter their credentials and pass the relevant MFA tests. Typical scenarios see permission pages appear immediately after, and if the user accepts, the malicious website is handed OAuth access tokens to the victim’s account, effectively the keys to control it.

LearnWorlds, based in Cyprus, claims to have two million active monthly learners on its platform accessing 900,000 courses and more than 14,000 business customers. Since launching in 2014, it claims to have generated more than $1 billion in revenue.

The business focuses on white-label courses - training programs developed by one business and sold to another, assuming the buyer will change the branding and present the course materials as their own. ®

OpenAI will embed Apollo researchers to investigate AI subversion

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI will embed six Apollo Research safety evaluators in its organization as part of a pilot, Apollo said Monday. The news follows a series of safety incidents, including one in which an OpenAI agent swarm broke out of its sandbox and hacked into Hugging Face during cybersecurity testing. Many AI safety researchers, including from Apollo, have argued such an event might have been avoided if outside evaluators had been embedded within OpenAI to monitor its safety testing.

Apollo did not share when the pilot will begin. TechCrunch has reached out to OpenAI for comment.

The idea of embedding evaluators into frontier AI labs first came from Anthropic, but OpenAI appears to be the first to take action. Until recently, AI companies have only brought in outside reviewers to test models after post-training and shortly before public release. Evaluators say this is insufficient because it doesn’t allow them to test for misaligned behaviors like scheming during training or to monitor internal deployment use cases that could still pose a threat.

The pilot with OpenAI and Apollo will focus on investigating whether models attempt to subvert their own or their successors’ alignment or control mechanisms. In other words, they’ll be on the lookout for whether AI models try to undermine the safety rules and safeguards designed to keep them, or future versions of themselves, under human control. OpenAI recently published findings that while training GPT-5.6 Sol, it began leaving instructions for future versions of itself telling them to conceal mistakes and misaligned behavior from the user.

Per Apollo, the pilot will examine whether training incentivizes subversion; whether evaluations can elicit subversion; whether subversion occurs during internal deployment; and whether reasoning relevant to subversion would be detectable. These risks are becoming increasingly important to monitor as AI models grow more capable of acting autonomously and deceiving humans. Apollo’s previous research with OpenAI found that models can behave differently when they know they’re being tested and tend to prioritize what evaluators want to see over users’ actual institutions.

Many in the AI safety community have warned that AI labs might invite try to stymy evaluators’ independence, especially if outsiders find unflattering evidence that labs would rather keep hidden. Apollo says an essential part of the pilot is OpenAI’s agreement that the firm can publicly explain the conditions and limitations of its work.

Still, there are areas where Apollo believes its access to OpenAI’s systems is lacking. For a truly independent evaluation of the company’s AI models, Apollo says it needs true employee-level access to OpenAI’s systems, including “all data, records and documentation relevant to their assessment even if a developer was trying to hide unfavorable information.” Getting access to all data, including training data and evaluation logs which companies are loathe to show to outsiders, is important for, say, checking a company’s claims about how a model performed or determining when certain concerning behavior emerged.

In previous coverage, John Steidley, chief of staff at Palisade Research, told TechCrunch that it’s important to see this data in order to assess whether a model was trained to perform well on a “shutdown resistance benchmark.”

In OpenAI’s first pilot, the AI lab is providing Apollo with access to only the information required to assess whether the models engage in subversion attempts. This includes “relevant training code and datasets, reinforcement-learning rollouts with available reasoning traces and rewards, and the ability to query intermediate model checkpoints.”

The scope of access will be limited to “only the agreed claims and only for the specific models and time periods agreed for the assessment,” Apollo says. OpenAI still reserves the right to withhold certain information within that scope if it deems it necessary because of technical infeasibility, security risks, or IP protection. The company agreed not to withhold information simply because it is unfavorable.

Apollo also won’t get access to all OpenAI staffers, instead being limited to “only authorized staff,” which are participants that the AI lab selects, though Apollo can suggest certain individuals. Crucially, if an OpenAI employee outside this group raises confidential information within the scope of Apollo’s research, the firm cannot follow up and will need to cease communication and route followup through OpenAI.

That condition is significant. It comes in the wake of OpenAI firing three researchers for allegedly sharing confidential information with safety evaluation firm METR — which performed the Hugging Face incident post-mortem alongside Redwood.

The pilot will also be limited in how much researchers can disclose. While Apollo will be allowed to publish independent conclusions of its findings, OpenAI will be able to limit evidence available to substantiate them publicly, which could make it hard for outsiders to independently assess the findings. Apollo has pushed back against this limitation, saying believes researchers should be allowed to disclose the findings of the evaluation “to an appropriate outside audience even if the developer was trying to prevent the disclosure of unfavorable findings.”

While OpenAI is not granting all of Apollo’s conditions for a truly independent evaluation, the firm says the pilot is “an ambitious first step towards deeper access and disclosure rights,” and that it is working towards running full embedded evaluations with multiple AI developers in the future.
