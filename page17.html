'Worst Possible News': Cybertruck Fans Melt Down After Tesla Cancels Its Wheeled Electric Dumpster

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As anyone with even a passive knowledge of our existence could have told you, Elon Musk's announcement that Tesla will kill the Cybertruck next year was big news here at Jalopnik. After three-ish years, we finally defeated our mortal enemy. The Cybertruck will die, and Jalopnik still lives. Of course, you had to know there'd be at least a few die-hard Cybertruck fans who chugged the Kool-Aid and may have cried when they heard the news. Thankfully, our friends over at Wired tracked down many of the more enjoyable meltdowns, purely for your entertainment.

Before we dive too deep into the a few examples, though, can we just take a second to appreciate how perfectly Wired journalist Miles Klee summed up the Cybertruck's woes? It's beautiful:

Automotive industry observers were quick to surmise that the vehicle's weak sales, delayed deliveries, inflated pricing, numerous recalls, and connection to Musk's far-right MAGA politics may also have factored into the decision. Longtime detractors of the polarizing steel-paneled truck, originally positioned as an electric rival to classic gas-powered pickups, jeered Musk and Tesla fans over its demise, noting with some pleasure that the Cybertruck would exit the market within four years of its November 2023 rollout.

I don't get the feeling he's one of those Cybertruck fans left brokenhearted following the cancellation announcement, which is good. Miles gets an invite to my upcoming Cybertruck Is Dead cookout next year. Anyway, onto the schadenfreude. Here's a thread he found from a Cybertruck owners' forum that you'll definitely want to scroll through. "Worst possible news," one poster wrote, adding, "No chance to buy another in future years for a newer model or after a car accident. No more aftermarket mods. No more service or parts."

Don't be too mean to that commenter, though. Those aftermarket parts are important when you own a truck that loses body panels like a toddler loses socks. In their world, maybe it really is the worst possible news. Meanwhile, another Cybertruck owner who goes by the name The Cybertruck Guy on Twitter posted, "I... have no words. Devastating. I can't help but think this is Tesla's own unwillingness (or incompetence) to make Cybertruck a success. Where was the price cuts? Where were the advertisements? Where is range extender? Where is the future of autonomous cargo transport?"

I… have no words. Devastating.I can’t help but think this is Tesla’s own unwillingness (or incompetence) to make Cybertruck a success. Where was the price cuts? Where were the advertisements? Where is range extender? Where is the future of autonomous cargo transport?

In addition to highlighting a few of the best sad reactions, Wired also spoke with Cybertruck fans to hear their thoughts directly. "So everybody in my group, they are not happy," a contractor in Palm Springs name Michael Goldman told Wired. "I've had people reaching out to me through the group, asking me, like, 'What are you going to do?' And I'm like, 'Dude, I don't know.'" That group, by the way, is called Cybertruck Owners Only and has more than 82,000 members, every single one of whom we can assume is equally depressed now.

The article also uses the "betrayal" and "disappointment" to describe how many owners feel, which always feels good to see. As for Goldman, he told Wired he nearly bought a second Cybertruck to have for when his first one dies. Unfortunately for Mr. Goldman here, that didn't work out because Tesla pulled the ultimate Tesla move on him right before he pulled the trigger. "I'll be honest with you, I was going pull the trigger on a [Cyberbeast] about two days ago, and I have a reservation already in my phone," Goldman told Klee. That price increase wasn't personal, though. Per Car and Driver, while the Cybertruck will reportedly stay in production through some point in 2027, the only version you can now buy costs $112,000. So that's not exactly cheap. Then again, who expects consistent pricing out of Tesla? You may not have to negotiate with a dealer salesperson, but Tesla pricing is still an expensive game of Russian roulette, and it has been for years.

Speaking with Wired, Troy Meekhof (the man behind the Cybertruck Guy Twitter account) said, "I absolutely do think this is the wrong move for the Tesla brand, and it's not necessarily because of the removal of an under-performing product, but it's a clear symbol of Tesla's aggression towards transitioning to autonomous vehicles only," adding, "If you ask me, it is far too aggressive, and I have a growing conviction that, at least in America, the population and legislators are not ready nor interested in autonomous vehicles today en masse." Huh.

Why Meekhof would feel disappointed to see Tesla abandon vehicle production as it pursues its AI/robotics future, I have no idea. We've known that was the plan since 2024, when Elon literally told us that was the plan. While Musk is a proven liar with a long track record of saying he'll do things he never does, his actions since making that announcement have only demonstrated a willingness to pursue his plan, which also involves attempting to merge Tesla with SpaceX. Elon killed both the Model S and Model X back in January, too.

Then again, that's the problem with thinking you're special. You can look at Tesla cutting everything that isn't the Model 3 or Model 3 hatchback, refusing to redesign its product, and killing the long-rumored $25,000 "cheap Tesla," and you'll still assume Elon will keep building his slow-selling truck just for you and your Cybertruck-driving friends. Being forced to face the reality that they aren't Elon's favorite customers who are basically his best friends has apparently been rough for some of these guys.

Cybertruck Owners Only groupmember Drew Million told Wired, "I've lost a lot of love for Tesla over this," before complaining about the electric automaker's "very stale consumer options." Million also added, "After Cybertruck is gone, there isn't a flagship vehicle. The Cybertruck started that," apparently still confused about whether or not Tesla is still a car company. Nvidia and Dyania bring AI chart review onto hospital servers

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Dyania's Synapsis AI runs on the hospital's own Nvidia servers, so patient records never leave the building.

Dyania’s Synapsis AI links findings across years of a patient’s records, according to Nvidia.

Dyania Health’s Synapsis AI reviewed the records of more than 7,800 Cleveland Clinic heart-valve patients, running on the hospital’s own Nvidia servers. The data was used to build a model predicting when replacement valves fail. Doctors still make every decision.

An AI system has read up to 16 years of records for more than 7,800 Cleveland Clinic heart patients, Nvidia said on Friday. It ran entirely on the hospital’s own servers. The software, Synapsis AI, is made by New Jersey startup Dyania Health.

Much of a patient’s history sits in doctors’ notes and scan reports rather than tidy data fields. Synapsis reads those notes and answers clinical questions, linking each answer back to the record it came from. It runs on Nvidia hardware inside the hospital’s network, so records never leave the building, much like a UK NHS triage AI built with Nvidia.

For the study, it answered 88 questions about each patient who had an aortic valve replaced between 2005 and 2021. The resulting data was used to build a model that predicts the risk of a replacement valve failing three, five and ten years after surgery. Nvidia says the chart review would have taken clinicians years by hand.

Lars Svensson, head of Cleveland Clinic’s heart institute, called it “a promising step towards enhanced outcomes and more personalized care for patients.”

Doctors still review the AI’s evidence and make every decision. The study shows the tool can build a dataset and predict risk, not that it improves care, and Dyania says it still needs outside validation. Cleveland Clinic is also an investor in Dyania, it disclosed last year when it rolled the tool out for trial recruitment.

Big tech is pushing hard into hospital records. Google Cloud launched Gemini for healthcare this week, and Bunkerhill raised $55M for hospital AI agents. But proven gains for patients remain harder to find, and doctors have pushed back on bold claims.

However tightly curated, Huelva’s lineup also allows a broader vision of which ways Latin American cinema and film industries are heading in Latin America. A few brief provisional takes as the 52nd Huelva Ibero-American Film Festival unpools in Andalusia, running through Nov. 21:

Where’s Latin America’s cinema at? Some of its most exciting movies, as in world cinema at large, is genre bending and blending. “The Riverbank” “traverses the classic erotic thriller to experiment with tropical noir, crime drama, magical realism and even a hint of whodunnit,” Variety observes, all inspired by and exploring the reality of Recife life, director Enock Carvalho notes. “The Bovine with the Curved Horns” braids myth, oral tradition, Colombia’s armed conflict and downbeat daily life. “Days Off,” Argentine Luciana Mariani’s surprising debut melds coming of-age and sci-fi beats, as Bego, 12, experiments with shifting – the move to an alternative reality – during a solar eclipse.

García’s receives this year’s City of Huelva Prize, the festival’s highest honor. She is “a funny and resilient force of nature” in “Gloria,” but equally she can act a gravely ailing mother in Huelva 2026 title “Julia’s Flowers,” fading, irascible, frail, seemingly light-bodied, child-like but still, at times, capable of cognizance.

No country in Latin America has broken out to international recognition more than Brazil. Last year Walter Salles’ “I’m Still Here” won an Oscar. This year, Kleber Mendonça Filho’s “The Secret Agent” scored four Academy Award nominations. Brazil has no film of such scale this year. What it does have, however, is underscored at Huelva. It has funding from its government, “Funk” supported by a classic financing combination of public sector bank BRDE, fund FSA and the Ancine national agency. Brazil also has vibrant regional and municipal film scenes. “Funk” is set in Rio, supports by RioFilme. “The Riverbank” forms part of Recife’s vibrant film scene. The consequences? Films which have the singularity and relevance of auteur cinema but high production standards.

Or it’s got there already. Sometimes the partners align in a single territory, such as “The Bovine with the Curved Horns,” produced by Colombia’s La Fortaleza Films, Congo Films, Universidad del Magdalena, Lightbox, Pablo Mondragón, De La Tierra Producciones and Soretti Films. Or co-producers stack up internationally, such as on Lucila Mariani’s “The Days Off,” produced by Argentina’s Maravillacine (“Marilyn,” “Under the Flags, the Sun”), alongside Brazil’s Persona Non Grata Pictures and Vulcana Films (“Hard Paint,” “Night Stage”), with Mexico’s Woo Films (“Pedro Páramo,” “Noise”) and Valiente Films as co-producers. Huelva is, however, something of an exception when it comes to festivals and women’s films and the world. LGBTQ freedoms remains a barometer of larger freedoms in many Latin American films, such as “Riverbank,” whose leads face the brutal antagonism of members of an Evangelical church.

“We attempt to program films which have a variety of themes, such as might be LGBTQ, mourning or mental health, subjects which can engage and concern society,” says Martín. Huelva’s lineup, as so much of Latin American cinema, deals with issues. In Latin America, the issues are impossible to ignore, Nobel Prize Winning novelist Mario Vargas Llosa said decades ago. That remains true, however, down to this day.

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Vault will eventually open to public eyes, and it’ll be worth a visit once it does.

For decades, Ford’s internal policy was to not keep examples of cars it’s made. Once their useful lifespan was up, they were to be sold, donated, or trashed. That being the case, there’s never been an official museum of cars past and present operated by the Blue Oval here in the U.S. Until now, that is. Say hi to the Ford Vault.

In the same sense that Porsche has the Porsche Museum and Mercedes-Benz has the Mercedes-Benz Museum, the Ford Vault is a place to admire the long history of Ford and Lincoln. It’s not open to the general public yet – Ford intends to ultimately open its doors to anyone – but we got a sneak preview of what to expect when Ford’s finished with this large hall of vehicles.

As of now, there are 125 cars and trucks inside this warehouse-like building in Romulus, Michigan. Situated about 20 or so miles from Ford World Headquarters in Dearborn, this only exists because of Jim Farley’s blessing. Thanks to orders from the top to change Ford’s vehicle retention policy, a collection of historical vehicles finally became possible. It’s taken about three years of buying special vehicles and unearthing some that were squirreled away, but Ford finally has an array of cars worth showing off. A small fraction of them (about 15 at this stage) are on loan from folks happy to show their cars off in Ford’s space. It’s a practice that Ford says it intends to continue, giving new visitors something fresh to see each time they return.

We got a chance to walk through the Vault this week. Read on for some highlights of what you can expect should you find yourself in the Ford Vault one day.

This Mustang doesn’t look like much from the outside, but it houses one of the most ambitious skunkworks engine projects from Ford that never made it past the prototype phase. Back when Ford was developing the 2005 Ford GT, it was searching for the right powertrain for the job. Engineers thought a V-10 could fit the bill, so it combined two modular 4.6-liter V-8 engine blocks to create a short deck V-10 measuring 351 cubic inches. Hilariously, it fit in the engine bay of the Mustang. This prototype in particular has been kept off the books for decades since to ensure it lived on, and now it’s proudly displayed in the Vault.

Speaking of Ford GTs, the Vault is home to a huge number of them. One of the particularly special ones is this concept that debuted at the 2002 Detroit Auto Show. Nicknamed “Petunia,” it’s an example of a concept that was truly representative of the production car to follow. That said, look close, and you’ll spot the differences. The headlight and fog light designs, for instance, are different on the final car. Same goes for various vents and openings throughout the body. It’s close, but not identical to the 2005 GT that came out a few years later.

The Ka being present here in the Vault foreshadows that it won’t just be American-sold Fords and Lincolns that will make their way into the collection. This particular Ka is the last first-gen example to roll off the line, and it has less than 10 miles on the odometer. It’s also a charming aside from the barrage of Ford Mustangs and GTs on display here.

These three are on loan to Ford for the time being to coincide with the launch of the brand’s new Le Mans Hypercar. As you likely already know, they’re the three GT40s that swept the podium at the 1966 Le Mans. Ford rightly has them lined up in the feature spot inside the Vault. There’s no guarantee these pieces of history will be there when Ford decides to open up to the public, but it sure is a great way to kick this collection off with a bang.

Ford has about 30 different concept cars from across the years on display right now, and one of the funkiest is the Ute Concept. Ford’s EVB Metal Shop crafted this creation using a Ranger bed and the original Mustang decklid as the tailgate. Obviously, it was only ever made as a one-off.

Again, it’s not all Mustangs and GTs, and thankfully, Ford found some space for its affordable, entry-level enthusiast cars. Not pictured here, but across the hall, Ford has both a Fiesta ST and Focus ST on display, too. It’s not often you see perfectly kept Focus and Contour SVTs, so it’s great to see Ford found some to preserve.

It’s not just on-road race cars that made the cut for the Vault, and Ford found a special one with this Baja 1000 Bronco. Prepared by Bill Stroppe for the 1967 Mexican 1000, it’s the first of four Broncos that Stroppe built to compete in off-road rallies and competitions. You might recall its color scheme from today’s Bronco Stroppe Edition made as a tribute to the original truck.

Leno has an absurd number of cars, but did you ever wonder which was his first? Ford is keeping it safe and dry for Leno for the time being, but it will eventually be shipped back out to California for a future episode of Jay Leno’s Garage.

Ford is seemingly bringing back the Cobra name in a big way soon, and it’s highlighting the trim’s history with a collection of three Cobra Rs here at the Vault. Each one of these Cobra Rs (1993, 1995, and 2000) are the first production examples off the line for each generation. The 2000 Cobra R with its big wing and independent rear suspension is the most well-known of the bunch, and we have high expectations for the continuation of the Cobra line.

‘We don’t have a security problem anymore. We have a data problem now’

Chalk up a new record for vulns. The number of Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) found and disclosed so far in 2026 surpassed 100,000 on Friday - more than double 2025’s total - and the reason can be summed up in two letters: AI.

“It’s kind of wild,” Josh Bressers, VulnCheck’s director of coordinated vulnerability disclosure, told The Register. “The thing that almost scares the bejesus out of me is while 100K sounds like a lot, in a couple years we're going to say, remember when it was only 100K?”

As of the time of our interview, about 4 pm UTC on Friday, VulnCheck counted 100,222 CVEs, he said. Microsoft alone added 1,094 security flaws to the total count on Tuesday, following 1,474 vulnerabilities the month before.

“There's no denying the fact that vulnerability hunting with AI is driving a majority of what we're seeing.” All the major CVE Numbering Authorities (CNAs) - these are the organizations authorized by the CVE program to assign unique identifiers to security vulnerabilities - like VulnCheck, Microsoft, Oracle, GitHub, and other large enterprise IT vendors “are just screaming into the atmosphere right now,” Bressers said. “It’s AI.”

Meanwhile, the percentage of these bugs that are exploited in the wild remains relatively consistent. About 1.4 percent (1,363) of the CVEs published this year have been added to VulnCheck’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.

“But obviously, as the number of CVEs grows, [1 percent] of 1,000 is a lot different than [1 percent] of 100,000,” Bressers said.

Plus, as other security researchers have documented, the window between the public release of a security patch and attackers developing working exploits – often by reverse engineering the patch while end users are still applying the fix – is rapidly shrinking. This is also thanks to AI.

From January to June, 88 percent of the exploitation observed by CrowdStrike using public proof-of-concept (PoC) code occurred within 48 hours of the code's release. The security firm said China-linked groups such as Vault Panda and Genesis Panda moved even faster, launching attacks within 24 hours of disclosure.

Similarly, VulnCheck found the median time from CVE publication to exploitation falling from 120 days in 2025 to 80 days during the first half of 2026. This window has closed even further during the second half of the year, which also seems to coincide with the AI-induced vulnpocalypse.

“It’s often just hours,” Bressers said, noting 14 make-me-root vulnerabilities that VulnCheck disclosed on Friday in Copeland refrigeration controllers. Two are under active exploitation. These are the devices that Iranian hackers abused to spoil food and spark fires in supermarkets across Europe.

“We published those CVEs, and literally minutes later, we were seeing hits on our honey pots,” Bressers said about the Copeland device vulnerabilities. “We’ve hit a point where with anything that looks interesting, someone is taking that information and seeing what they can do with it. Is it AI driven? Probably. It'd be hard to say it's not, because we've seen it really ramp up recently.”

This influx of software bugs requires a sea change in how the entire industry approaches vulnerability disclosure and patching.

“We don't have a security problem anymore,” Bressers said. “We have a data problem now. Instead of obsessing over vulnerabilities, we need to start looking at things like heat maps for infrastructure, and figure out the vulnerabilities that actually affect you. The CISA BOD helps with that.”

The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) in June issued a cybersecurity directive that replaces flat-deadline vulnerability management policies. Instead of prioritizing patching based solely on CVSS severity scores, the directive instructs organizations to prioritize based on specific risk criteria, including whether the asset is internet-exposed, under active exploitation, and whether an attacker can automate the exploit.

“It's telling people to figure out the vulnerabilities that actually affect you, and then put those at the top of the list, instead of just saying we're going to fix all the criticals,” Bressers said.

AI-generated vulnerability disclosures also create huge headaches for open source software developers and maintainers, which Bressers describes as a security problem “super close to my heart.”

“You've got individual developers that are drowning in vulnerability reports,” he said. “You have foundations being crushed by AI agents scraping their data or downloading their packages, millions of times a day, and it's straining the seams absolutely everywhere you look. The one thing I can say with certainty is: The way we currently do things has to change, and it's going to have to change fairly soon.”

Different open source projects have taken different approaches to solving this issue. The Eclipse Foundation, which houses the Open VSX Registry, in April launched a “managed tier” for enterprise customers and other organizations running Open VSX at scale.

Instead of using the free, open source version, the managed version guarantees a 99.95 percent uptime SLA backed by service credits. Its customers include AWS, IBM, Google, Cursor, and Windsurf. This paid option means commercial users who rely on the registry help fund its ongoing maintenance - and their own platforms’ security and performance.

Meanwhile, curl’s maintainer, Daniel Stenberg, citing an “avalanche of vulnerability reports," updated its bug reporting rules last month. As of October 26, all low- and medium-severity reports go directly into the public GitHub repository, with only the high- and critical-flaws receiving private handling.

Stenberg already shuttered curl's bug bounty program this year as AI-generated submissions piled work onto its security team.

“A lot more needs to happen, and I think it is going to be a challenge to enact some of this change,”  Bressers said. “When a package registry drastically changes something, people start freaking out, and the world is going to end, and people on LinkedIn are complaining - a lot of people are hesitant. No one wants to be the first to jump into the alligator-filled pool. That's one of our challenges.”®
