Starting on January 26, 2027, it will no longer be possible to sign into your Nintendo account, or to create a new one, with Google and Apple as a log-in method. Nintendo didn't say why it's removing Google and Apple sign-ins, and whether it has security concerns with them. Whatever Nintendo's reason for it, you may want to get your log-in sorted if you want uninterrupted access to your games and to the eShop.

Why Elvis Presley’s and Eminem’s music owners are suing Suno

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Presley suit adds a claim under Tennessee’s ELVIS Act over his voice. With Universal, Sony and Jason Isbell also suing Suno, the AI music company says it blocks artist-name prompts.

Elvis Presley’s music owners and Eminem’s publisher have filed two more lawsuits against Suno, the $5.4bn AI music generator. They join a growing line of rights holders suing Suno. Their complaints spell out two different grievances: the songs, and the voice.

The plaintiffs filed both suits on 6 November in federal court in Northern California, as Variety first reported. Courthouse News published the complaints.

The first comes from Elvis Presley Enterprises and other owners of Presley’s songs. Raleigh Music Publishing and several individual rights holders join them. Together they say Suno infringed 859 works, from “Can’t Help Falling in Love” to a Chubby Checker hit.

The second comes from Eight Mile Style and Martin Affiliated, which control 269 Eminem compositions. Eminem himself is not a party. This suit is about copyright only.

Both quote Suno’s own answer to an earlier suit by the major labels, describing its training data.

“Essentially all music files of reasonable quality that are accessible on the open Internet,” Suno said in that filing.

Both complaints accuse Suno of “stream ripping”, copying audio from YouTube with tools that get around its encryption. They cite leaked code that they say shows Suno collected 2.06 million YouTube Music artist records. The code also logs 6.98 million song pages from the lyrics site Genius, they say. Suno gathered some of it through the data firm Bright Data, they allege.

Only the Presley suit claims the voice. It accuses Suno of breaking Tennessee’s ELVIS Act. The 2024 law added a person’s voice to the state’s protections for name and likeness. The complaint says the law’s backers asked Presley’s rights holders for permission before naming it after him.

“All of the humanity, history, despair, and triumph that constitutes Elvis’ identity was stolen,” the plaintiffs wrote.

Their test is simple. On 7 October, they say, they gave Suno only a few lyrics with his name spelled out letter by letter. It produced two songs in his voice. They attach 38 recordings they say copy his voice. One, “Elvis in The Jailhouse Rock”, has more than 125,000 plays.

In a separate case this month, led by the singer Jason Isbell, Suno told a court in Massachusetts this:

Suno outputs “do not replicate the auditory characteristics of any real artist’s voice to the point that someone would confuse a Suno creation with one actually sung by a particular famous musician,” the company wrote.

The Presley owners call that statement false.

The Eminem complaint prints lyrics side by side. One Suno track, “Lose Yourself By Eminem”, copies the song’s verse and chorus almost word for word, it says. It lists similar copies of “Till I Collapse”, “The Real Slim Shady” and “Houdini”.

Both suits say Suno’s block on artist names is easy to get around. The Eminem suit cites a $29 guide from a third-party site that sells “Eminem-style prompts for Suno”. Both point to a creator in Suno’s paid affiliate scheme who teaches users to sound like an artist without naming them.

“Suno exists to help people create new, original music, not to trade on anyone’s name or likeness, and we prohibit any unauthorized use of an artist’s work,” a Suno spokesperson said in a statement.

The company says it blocks prompts with artists’ names and songs. Outside providers screen uploaded audio and lyrics, it says. It has argued in court that training on music is fair use. It has also licensed music: Warner Music Group settled with Suno, and BMG and the distributor Believe signed deals that feed its v6 model.

Universal Music Group and Sony Music sued Suno again in September. Round Hill Music and Gerencia 360 have sued in California, and Isbell leads a proposed class action in Massachusetts. Abroad, a Munich court ruled in July that Suno broke copyright in a case brought by GEMA, and collecting societies in Denmark and Canada have sued too. Several of the same Presley owners sued Anthropic in October.

Catalogues carry big values too. Paul McCartney’s largest asset is his song publisher MPL, worth at least $250M, Forbes estimated this week. It owns “Blue Suede Shoes”, the song Presley made famous.

The Presley group seeks up to $128.85M in statutory copyright damages and more than $50M under the ELVIS Act, which it wants tripled because Presley served in the US Army. Eight Mile Style seeks up to $40.35M. Both want a jury trial, and the destruction of copies held in Suno’s training data and model weights.

Faulty Wedding Cake pavilion to be dismantled at Waddesdon Manor

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Photography by Chris Lacey and Merriman Photography

A 12-metre-high pavilion shaped like a wedding cake is set to be dismantled at Waddesdon Manor in Buckinghamshire, UK, after it was closed over health and safety concerns.

A spokesperson for the Grade I-listed country house told the BBC that the pavilion, which was installed in 2023, was not “open for quite as long as hoped” due to faults in its ceramic tiles.

The sculptural pavilion, simply named Wedding Cake, was designed by Portuguese artist Joana Vasconcelos as a permanent garden pavilion for the grounds of Waddesdon Manor.

Its three tiers are wrapped in glazed, pastel-toned ceramic tiles made by the historic Portuguese tile manufacturer Viúva Lamego and intended to look like cake icing. They are now faulty, forcing the structure to close because of concerns about their safety.

According to the BBC, a report by a Buckinghamshire Council officer found that no viable solution to repair the faulty tiles had been identified.

As such, the council has approved a change to the listed building consent, allowing the sculpture to be removed. As the manor and its grounds are protected sites, permission must be obtained to carry out such work.

The structure must now be removed no later than January 2036. However, the Rothschild Foundation, which manages the estate, wants the sculpture removed as soon as possible, according to the BBC.

The revised consent also requires the surrounding landscape to be returned to grass within six months of its removal.

When the pavilion was completed, its designer Vasconcelos told Dezeen it was her “most ambitious project to date”.

Her use of tiles reflected her ambition to use ceramics and textiles to challenge traditional hierarchies of what she called “noble materials”, such as marble, in grand architectural settings.

The manufacturer Viúva Lamego’s standard 14-by-14 centimetre tiles determined the size of the overall structure. It has an 11-metre diameter, which was the smallest that could be achieved using the tiles.

The pavilion is the fourth project by Vasconcelos created for the estate, which was built in the 1870s by Baron Ferdinand de Rothschild to entertain guests and showcase his ceramic collections.

A spokesperson for the manor said it plans to continue to commission artwork for its grounds.

“We are hoping to continue to showcase art in the landscape, including plans for a new contemporary sculpture trail in the grounds of Waddesdon Manor, which will open in July 2027,” they said.

Other recent projects by Joana Vasconcelos featured on Dezeen include a 24-metre-long textile tentacle installation for Dior’s Autumn Winter 2023 catwalk show.

Elsewhere, we recently reported on the dismantling of Rike Concert Hall, designed by Italian practice Studio Fuksas. It is being demolished less than 15 years after it was completed, and before ever opening to the public.

Democrats won Congress. What might happen to Trump’s construction projects now?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The future of President Donald Trump’s construction and renovation projects in Washington, D.C., could soon come into question, after Democrats take control of Congress in January.

Trump has powered his pet projects through by sheer force of will since retaking office last year, setting a breakneck pace for ideas moving from blueprint to construction site. Disregarding legally mandated approval processes and protocols, his administration has knocked down buildings and torn up trees without permission.

Though legal challenges have, at times, slowed projects like the White House ballroom, arch monument, a D.C. golf course, and John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts renovations, they haven’t been effective.

The landscape is shifting, however. Once the the next Democrat-led Congress is in place, changes to the built environment will face oversight that Republicans didn’t previously provide. This is how it might shake out.

3 new roadblocks to Trump’s projects

First, Democrats will get two ex officio seats on the 12-person National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC)—the federal government’s D.C.-area planning agency—that are reserved for U.S. Senate and House of Representatives members. The president appoints some members of the commission, and there are others who are there because of their job titles.

Democrats will replace Senator Rand Paul and Representative James Comer, both Kentucky Republicans, with their own people. Those two new members of the commission could join with the District of Columbia’s four members, who have consistently voted against Trump’s building priorities, to block his projects from passing with a tied 6-6 vote.

Second, Democrats will have standing to sue in federal court on behalf of the Senate and House, using Senate and House counsel. Leveraging congressional subpoenas and investigations, lawmakers could learn more about private donations Trump used to fund the projects.

Third, and most important, Democrats will hold the purse strings in the appropriations process, which means they can restrict or defund executive branch activities. In addition to accepting private donations, Trump has also funded his construction with public, taxpayer funds. Under Democrats, that practice could soon come to an end.

What Democrats are saying

These potential roadblocks could be why Trump seems to be rushing to get construction work done before the next Congress is sworn in. The day after the midterm elections, Trump signed and raised a beam at the ballroom site. Meanwhile, the NCPC approved plans for his arch the following day, though a judge issued a temporary restraining order shortly after.

The restraining order lasts just 14 days, and Democratic lawmakers are gearing up to fight it long term.

Speaking about Trump’s arch, Representative Don Beyer, the Democrat whose northern Virginia district includes Arlington National Cemetery, said at a press conference Friday that he’s “never seen a project with such profound, widespread opposition from the very people who it will affect most.”

To fight the project, Beyer authored the Arlington National Cemetery Viewshed Protection Act, which would block the arch’s construction outright. Introduced in May, the bill hasn’t been brought to a vote under Republican House leadership. Under Democrats, that and similar legislation to defund or explicitly ban Trump projects could more easily make it to a vote.

Representative Jared Huffman, a California Democrat and ranking member on the House Natural Resources Committee, which oversees the Department of the Interior, said “the ground between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington belongs to the American people and the families who have buried loved ones there.” He warned officials who keep the project moving forward or firms that sign on to build the arch to “understand the legal risk they’re taking on.”

“If Trump wants a tribute to himself, he can build one at one of his glitzy resorts or private golf clubs,” Huffman said Friday in a statement. “Come January, a new Congress will demand answers from the people enabling this abuse of our public lands and our laws.”

For two years, Trump hasn’t faced pushback from Congress over his construction projects. That’s about to change.

Football and the jungle have always been a natural fit. Ever since John Fashanu swapped the dressing room for the campfire in 2003, a steady stream of players, managers and pundits have signed up for 'I'm A Celebrity... Get Me Out Of Here!', with wildly different results.

Some were crowned royalty whilst others were the first to be sent packing. Here is every football figure to enter the jungle, ranked from worst to best.

The biggest football name the show has ever landed. Manchester United's all-time record goalscorer is reported to be following wife Coleen, who finished runner-up in 2024 on a reported record fee of £1.5million.

Jimmy Bullard arrived as the camp's resident banter merchant, with few expecting him to be first out. Yet the former Hull City and Fulham midfielder was the first campmate evicted in 2014, lasting 17 days. His relentless ribbing of X Factor singer Jake Quickenden turned viewers against him, though Bullard insisted it was just "banter" and celebrated his exit with a fry-up.

A 2026 return for the South Africa All Stars series went no better: he shouted the famous catchphrase to quit a trial, then reignited his feud with eventual winner Adam Thomas in a chaotic live final.

Few football figures have entered the jungle with a bigger broadcasting profile than Alex Scott. The former Arsenal and England defender, now a fixture of BBC Sport, became the first campmate voted out of the 2025 series after around two weeks in camp.

Her exit sparked a backlash, with many viewers arguing she had been starved of airtime rather than rejected by the public. Scott then broke with tradition by flying straight home rather than attending the wrap party, choosing to be with partner Jess Glynne during a family emergency.

Neil 'Razor' Ruddock entered the third series as one of the early favourites, but the public never warmed to the former Liverpool and Tottenham defender's abrasive camp persona. He faced only one Bushtucker Trial, alongside Katie Price, and banked every star on offer. That was not enough.

Ian Wright went in as one of the most beloved figures in British football broadcasting, reportedly on a £400,000 fee, and came out with that image a little dented. A series of camp conflicts saw the Arsenal great become the fifth campmate voted out after 19 days.

To his credit, he was candid on exit, admitting he had "lost it too quickly" and had plenty to work on. Wright later said he wished he could take the experience back, feeling the final edit never showed his vulnerable side.

Rodney Marsh entered the jungle two years after Sky Sports sacked him over an on-air joke about the Asian tsunami, and he brought the same spikiness with him. The former QPR and Manchester City forward sparked a sexism row in camp, insisting his remarks were a "joke", and was later rushed to hospital after a fall injured his leg.

Even then, he refused a stretcher because "people watching in England" would see. Marsh became the fifth campmate voted out, finishing seventh after 15 days.

The former Chelsea captain earned a reported five-figure fee, modest by football standards, and was rarely far from controversy. Viewers accused Wise of bullying radio host Iain Lee after he argued Lee should not take on the Tutankha-Doom trial, having already failed two previous tasks.

The backlash stuck, and the public never came round. Wise lasted 19 days before losing out to boxer Amir Khan in the public vote, finishing sixth. Lee, the target of his frustration, went on to finish runner-up.

Wayne Bridge was a slow burner, but a likeable one. Reportedly paid £150,000, the former Chelsea and England left-back took time to come out of his shell before becoming a steady presence in camp and tackling six Bushtucker Trials.

He also conquered a fear of spiders, declaring afterwards that he would now be the one getting rid of them at home. His run ended after 20 days in a surprise double elimination, finishing fifth, one place behind hockey Olympian Sam Quek.

Catching rogue agents and preventing cyberattacks—quantum computing is about to have its ‘ChatGPT moment’

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A boardroom’s job is to see around corners. Ten years ago, large language models were the approaching challenge, a fledgling technology with a wide range of plausible applications. Today it is quantum computing.

Quantum will change the way we consider computing power. At present, classical computing encodes everything using “bits”. Bits only have two states, 0 or 1. Quantum uses quantum bits, or qubits, which are more adventurous. They can be 0 or 1 or a mixture of both, called “superposition.” This flexibility allows them to perform many more tasks than classical computing at a speed high enough to revolutionize how we work.

This has advantages as well as risks. Most of life is more complicated than a series of ones and zeros. Understanding the delicacy of a human protein and its relationship to disease, how the climate works or the interaction of sub-atomic particles in fluids requires significant amounts of computing power. In the battle to keep business and personal data safe from bad actors, the more powerful the technical smarts at our fingertips, the better. Financial services firms already have quant capabilities to help them trade and would like more.

But qubits can be erratic and prone to error. Small fluctuations in temperature and atmospheric pressure can cause malfunctions. Expensive isolation chambers are the norm. We might all like to drive a supercar, but sometimes a basic family runaround—classic computing—is more reliable.

Many companies are working hard to make quantum computing the next ubiquitous technology. IBM, Google, and Microsoft have significant quantum divisions. Pure play operators such as D-Wave Quantum, Rigetti Computing, and IonQ are seeing valuations in the multiple billions of dollars.

Europe is getting in on the scene. In June, Oxford Quantum Circuits (OQC) executed the biggest C-series raise for a quantum computing company in Europe at £260 million ($345 million). The next stage is commercial applications with a clear return on investment.

“Some of the earliest use cases of quantum are the ability to conduct vulnerability analysis on your network, and also to be able to detect threats,” OQC’s CEO Gerald Mullally told me. “And that’s really where the possibility of strengthening our cyber security defenses through the earliest use of quantum compute becomes possible.”

“Quantum can have a profound impact on being able to identify the anomalous behavior of rogue agents.”

The good guys need to get there first. A scenario where quantum computing becomes so powerful it can eat through all classical computer encryption defenses is not outside the realms of possibility. By then—and it could be as little as five years away—every company and individual will need powerful quant on their side.

“The first area where we generate advantage is anomaly detection—the ability to be able to spot something different in a pattern of data,” Mullally said. “Fraud detection and anti-money laundering are two of the very first use cases in financial services.

“Classical compute can really struggle to identify patterns or the relationships between data. Quantum moves that data to a higher dimensional plane, like a sphere, rather than two points. If it’s a sphere, you can then see more easily that there’s a relationship between data that you wouldn’t be able to spot if it was classical compute.”

When it comes to encryption, quantum will give businesses an “offensive capability” sufficient to head off the threat.

Fortunately, quantum computing’s capabilities are becoming stronger by the day. Average quant algorithms can regularly execute a thousand steps before being overwhelmed by errors. Soon that number will grow to a million steps.

“We will hit millions of steps before an error takes over in the years 2028 to 2031,” Mullally said. “That’s the point where we move past the capabilities of classical compute in those narrow areas like anomaly detection. That will be our version of the ChatGPT moment, and that will be the point where the confidence will grow. We’re going to hit billions, and we’re going to hit trillions of quantum operations, and we’ll start to then have this real commercial advantage for a corporate.”

Many executives are tired of tech bros bearing gifts and have spent considerable amounts of money experimenting with artificial intelligence. Bottom lines have remained resolutely flat.

Quantum is different, a mechanism for problem solving which comes closer to understanding the complexities of the world. OQC already has applications helping data centers operate more efficiently. As AI goes rogue with greater regularity, executives will be glad of a new, well-armed hunter to defeat the errant agent.

“I think quantum can have a profound impact on being able to identify the anomalous behavior of certain agents, and that’s the rogue agent type,” Mullally said. Nervous CEOs won’t be the only ones heaving a sigh of relief.

Hyperscalers have got a huge PR problem with data centers—this regulation academic has figured out the playbook for what they should do next

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s a question that will define the next phase of the economy’s AI transformation: How will tech giants solve their PR problem with data centers? The demand for data centers in the U.S. alone is huge, as is the capital expenditure on them. McKinsey estimates that between 2024 and 2030, demand for data centers will nearly quadruple, from 25 GW to more than 80 GW.In the first half of last year, AI-related spending accounted for almost one percentage point of real U.S. GDP growth, Goldman Sachs noted, “Without it, U.S. GDP growth would have almost flatlined.”

But there’s a snag: Communities don’t want data centers on their doorsteps. A Pew Research study from September suggests environmental concerns are a leading factor in the negative perception of the builds, followed by home energy costs and the quality of life for people living nearby.

Some experts say that the tension between an aversion to data centers and the economic need will find its own balance. Professor Jeremy Siegel, emeritus professor of finance at Wharton, suggests: “If one community rejects a data center, another will welcome the investment, jobs, and tax revenues.”

But Stavros Gadinis, a law professor at UC Berkeley, suggests history has already provided a “playbook” for next steps. Gadinis, faculty director of the Berkeley Center for Law and Business, says there is a “better way.”

Speaking exclusively to Fortune on the sidelines of Web Summit in Lisbon, Professor Gadinis explains: “So, that playbook involves, first off, outreach to impacted parties and impacted communities. I don’t mean just the local [communities]—although obviously these are key—but also nonprofits, academics.”

Concerned parties need to be given a stake in the development, he added, but the impetus needs to come from large investors who have the power to lay down conditions before putting their hands in their pockets. Only when these terms are met can builders “get the ball rolling” on developments, Professor Gadinis added.

“The final part, I think, has to come through accountability from these companies to try to show how they respected the policies and the goals, and how they’re moving ahead and what they’re contributing.”

Public pushback to data centers is spreading rapidly across the U.S.—even in Texas, home to Elon Musk’s SpaceX, which merged with Musk’s AI-specific firm, xAI, earlier this year.

If conflicts and permit delays are bubbling up in Texas, Professor Gadinis adds, such gridlock is “possible everywhere.” The author of Corporate Ordering added that neither AI companies nor the White House want to see a breakdown, explaining, “The biggest advantage that we have against our global competitors is our ability to build compute, of which data centers are a very important part. So we actually need to take some steps to bring everyone closer together in that question.”

The other question hanging over the AI sector is regulation.

The Democratic landslide at the midterms is likely to result in more scrutiny of companies that have enjoyed significant freedoms under the Trump administration, with the market sliding last week on those prospects, analysts suggested.

A back-and-forth has been played out between AI hyperscalers and policymakers in Washington, D.C.: Some founders want government regulation, but figures like Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have insisted the government will not become a “liability shield” for tech giants.

Professor Gadinis suggested that whether the government or AI sector acts first to establish frameworks is largely irrelevant, as long as “it is built on the right foundations.”

“My prediction would be that once practice is crystallized, then the government is going to say: ‘OK, we found it, now do this, and everybody does it,” he added.

Indeed, it’s already too late to decide whether the liability buck stops with AI companies or the government, the academic added: “The investment we have is so huge as a percentage of the [U.S.] GDP that they’re already too big to fail. They became too big to fail almost instantly in 2023.”

It’s “very hard” to imagine the government not being a backstop in any potential crisis, he added, “especially in an issue that the government itself has elevated into a national security concern. If a batch of subprime mortgages can cause a financial crisis, AI is looming large.

“So I think it’s too late, and I think that realization also explains why people are asking for more regulation, because to some extent we’re all in it—whether we want it or not.”
