25-Episode Netflix Adaptation Of A Classic Book Series Delivers Perfect Worldbuilding

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Worldbuilding is one of the most important ingredients in any TV show, but it’s easier with some genres than others. For fantasy and sci-fi, spectacular elements like magical kingdoms and distant planets make for great foundations to build lore on. Comedies and dramas grounded in the real world, however, have much less to work with. Making the familiar feel distinctive is no easy task.

That makes one Netflix adaptation particularly deserving of recognition. Across three seasons between 2017 and 2019, it created a setting with more personality than many fictional universes that can boast elaborate maps and centuries of invented history. Its secretive world feels vast and governed by peculiar rules that make it seem quite unlike our own reality despite being set there. Even more impressively, it achieved all this across just 25 episodes. That show is Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events.

Based on Daniel Handler’s 13 novels, published under the name Lemony Snicket, A Series of Unfortunate Events follows the three Baudelaire orphans as they bounce between adopted parents and foster families all while pursued by the murderous Count Olaf (Neil Patrick Harris). It has the sinister humor and theatrical atmosphere of a quirky fantasy, yet there’s no magic or supernatural elements to be found. Despite this, the world of A Series of Unfortunate Events feels distinct and different, and how the Netflix show turned everyday reality into somewhere so convincingly alien deserves celebration.

Netflix's A Series of Unfortunate Events may take place in the real world, but it doesn’t feel like it. It feels like a nightmare-like inversion of reality, both in terms of its aesthetics and in how the lives of the Baudelaire orphans play out following the death of their parents. Violet (Malina Weissman), Klaus (Louis Hynes), and baby Sunny (Presley Smith) are delivered from one incompetent caregiver to another, all while the authorities ignore the blatant schemes of Count Olaf and fail to keep them safe from him. They’re thrown into a system that definitely overlaps with the foster-care institutions of the real world, but operates with a disturbing incompetence that’s far removed from the genuine article.

As far as the aesthetics are concerned, A Series of Unfortunate Events creates its own world by making everything and everywhere look and feel slightly wrong despite being familiar. Its exaggerated sets make ordinary environments like a cheerily decorated living room seem capable of swallowing children whole. The unease this creates isn’t helped by the fact that the adults treat the obvious environmental dangers as perfectly reasonable.

The visual elements of A Series of Unfortunate Events’ worldbuilding are helped by the show’s deliberately scrambled relationship with history. There’s fashion, architecture, and technologies from various technologies, and this is once again presented as totally normal. Telegraphs and walkie-talkies, for example, are both treated like contemporary devices. The modern appears side-by-side with the antique in ways that technically could happen in the real world, but obviously aren’t the norm.

Patrick Warburton’s Lemony Snicket is the final masterstroke that ensures the reality presented by the Netflix show feels unique. He delivers his solemn narration like someone documenting an existing world, complete with personal regrets and information he cannot yet share. Every digression suggests another story happening beyond the children’s immediate predicament. Snicket’s musings make it clear that this world has a past just as rich as its present, making the absurdity of A Series of Unfortunate Events feel bizarrely plausible.

The convincing worldbuilding of A Series of Unfortunate events is definitely an achievement. However, the world of the Baudelaire orphans wasn’t created for the series. It came from Daniel Handler’s novels. This doesn’t make the creative vision of the show less noteworthy, however. Quite the opposite. Just as much as Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events stands out for its small-screen worldbuilding, it’s also an example of a near flawless literary adaptation. The Netflix show completely understands why the A Series of Unfortunate Events books are so beloved.

Netflix’s A Series of Unfortunate Events succeeds as a show because it recognizes that the appeal of the books extends far beyond eccentric locations and elaborate misfortune (a mistake made by the divisive 2004 movie). The world of Handler’s books is a thematic exploration of children’s frustration with adults who possess authority without the intelligence or compassion to deserve it. It’s a setting whose central logic is that bureaucratic procedures are the governing power in all things, even when it blatantly puts the lives of children at risk. Any successful adaptation needs to bring this thematic core to screens, which the Netflix series definitely did.

The recurring failures of characters like Mr. Poe makes the world of both the A Series of Unfortunate Events Netflix show and books terrifying without requiring anything supernatural. The fact that those responsible for protecting Violet, Klaus, and Sunny repeatedly refuse to see danger that readers can is an essential part of why Handler’s novels are so engaging. The Netflix series recognized the importance of this, and that it was much more essential than the timeline of specific events or established visual details.

The show itself proves this, too, since the A Series of Unfortunate Events TV show makes multiple substantial changes to the timeline of the narrative. For example, it brings the Baudelaire’s connections to the shadowy V.F.D. organization forward so that their influence can be felt from the moment Violet, Klaus, and Sunny meet Count Olaf. Many other TV adaptations of books would have been lambasted for making such a huge alteration to the established story. A Series of Unfortunate Events gets away with it because the change helps to support a small-screen version of the narrative whose spiritual heart is a flawless recreation of everything that makes the source material so beloved.

Two fantastic devices, but which is the best for reading?

I recently reviewed the iPad mini 8, and it has quickly become one of my favorite devices for reading. That’s saying a lot, considering how I’ve been an avid Kindle reader for over ten years. That said, the Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition also offers a brilliant reading experience.

The iPad mini 8 has many great features for reading. It’s slimmer and lighter, making it easier to hold for long reading sessions. The new OLED panel helps text and color images pop, and the nano-texture display option gives the panel a more paper-like feel.

Even if the iPad mini 8 is more feature-rich, the Kindle Colorsoft is still a fantastic e-reader thanks to its light and sturdy build, large color display, and smooth integration with the Kindle ecosystem. And even the more expensive Signature Edition is still hundreds of dollars less than an iPad mini 8.

You can read my iPad mini 8 review and Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition review to learn more — but suffice it to say, these are among the best for reading. But which one is right for you? I’ll break down everything you need to know to help you decide.

The iPad mini 8 is the most significant upgrade to Apple’s smallest tablet. The thinner, lighter design makes it more portable than ever, while the new 8.5-inch OLED panel delivers stunning image quality for videos and books. Powered by the A20 Pro chip, it also delivers fast performance and more-than-all-day battery life. Though pricier and missing features like Face ID and a 120Hz refresh rate, it still delivers a first-rate iPad experience.

The Amazon Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition (2026) improves on its predecessor with a thinner, lighter design and more vibrant color display. It’s arguably the best color e-reader right now, especially for Kindle users, though its steep price may deter some. But for what it delivers as a color e-reader, it’s hard to top.

The iPad mini 8 starts at $699 for the 128GB model with a glossy screen. You can upgrade to a model with the glare-reducing nano-texture display, but that requires upgrading to 256GB, which adds $200 to the overall cost.

Conversely, the Amazon Kindle Signature Edition costs $319 with 32GB of storage and no ad support. The standard Kindle Colorsoft with 16GB of storage and without the aluminum back housing costs $289.

The price difference between the iPad mini 8 and Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition is substantial. Between the two, the Kindle is the more affordable option, though both are relatively expensive compared to their previous models.

The new iPad mini 8 and Kindle Colorsoft have been redesigned to be thinner and lighter than their respective predecessors. As a result, they're easy to read for extended periods.

Of the two, the iPad mini 8 is larger and heavier, measuring 7.69 x 5.22 x 0.21 inches and weighing 8.9 ounces. The Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition is still pretty sizeable at 6.9 x 5.0 x 0.3 inches and 8.1 ounces, but it’s decidedly smaller in comparison.

The iPad mini 8 is one single piece of machined aluminum, whereas the only aluminum part of the Kindle Colorsoft is its back housing. Still, both feel sturdy and smooth in hand. Both devices are also waterproof, with the iPad mini 8 rated IP67 and the Colorsoft rated IPX8. Colorwise, Apple’s miniature tablet comes in Midnight and Starlight, while the Kindle Colorsoft comes in Fig and Graphite.

The iPad mini 8 has the more premium design overall, but both are elegant slabs in their own right.

Here’s where things diverge sharply. Since we’re discussing a digital ink display against an OLED panel, which is admittedly not a one-to-one comparison, I’ll instead focus on the actual reading experience each provides to make things fairer.

The iPad mini 8’s display is a big improvement over the past, thanks to its larger 8.5-inch display, thinner bezels, and higher resolution of 2316 x 1512 pixels at 326 pixels per inch (ppi). Its OLED panel also delivers excellent contrast and vivid colors. Novels, comics, and everything else look fantastic.

Apple sent us a review unit with a nano-texture display, which makes the screen more comparable to a Kindle for reading by reducing glare. Plus, the texture itself gives it a more paper-like feel. While it stings that you have to pay $200 more for the 256GB model to get the nano-texture option, it’s a great feature.

The Kindle Colorsoft has the same 7-inch 300ppi color display as the previous model, but Amazon has employed a new reverse-stack display construction to make the screen-to-bezel transition more seamless.

While black-and-white content looks brighter and bolder on a Kindle Paperwhite due to this reader’s color layer, colors are bolder than before. Whether it’s books or comics, everything looks great. However, color reading drops the resolution to 150ppi.

Though the iPad mini 8 might give you more eye strain since it can get significantly brighter, it still has the more vibrant display of the two. However, if you want a more paper-like experience, the Colorosoft beats the iPad.

Battery life is the one area where the Kindle Colorsoft clearly beats the iPad mini 8. Apple rates the iPad mini 8 as having up to 10 hours of battery life while surfing the web on Wi-Fi. Meanwhile, Amazon says the Colorsoft can last up to eight weeks on a single charge.

I’ve used the iPad mini 8 for nearly two weeks and have charged it about once every other day. Conversely, after almost three weeks, I still haven't charged the Kindle Colorsoft since I unboxed it. At the time of writing, it is at 60% battery life!

Kindles have always boasted long battery life since they are dedicated e-readers, unlike an iPad, which is a more demanding computing device. It’s no surprise that the Colorsoft can last much longer than the iPad mini 8.

This is another area where things don’t match one-to-one. You can read on both, but that’s about it on the Colorsoft, while the iPad mini 8 is basically a computer that can do so much more. To keep things fair though, I’ll focus on the respective Kindle ecosystem experiences the devices offer.

Naturally, the Kindle Colorsoft integrates seamlessly with the Kindle Store and Goodreads. I can easily purchase new books right on the Kindle and also upload my reading status to Goodreads.

UI-wise, the Home page shows all the books you’re currently reading or have read, a list of recommended books, and top Kindle Unlimited recommendations based on your reading history. You can also change the font, theme, and more within a book’s dedicated settings.

The iPadOS version of the Kindle app has all these features, except that the UI is arranged differently to fit an iPad’s screen size. One downside is that you can’t buy books directly in the Kindle app. To do that, you’ll have to open a web browser and log in to Amazon. That said, the Kindle app still syncs your reading status across Kindles and other devices.

One of Colorsoft’s major advantages over the iPad mini 8 is its truly distraction-free reading experience. You’ll never see pop-up notifications on a Kindle, whereas you’ll need to manually disable notifications on an iPad. It’s also much easier to wander off to another app on the iPad because everything is so easily accessible. On a Kindle, you can only read or browse for books.

Both the iPad mini 8 and Kindle Colorsoft are fantastic for reading. You can’t go wrong with either for that, but depending on what you’re looking for, one might suit you better than the other.

If you want an enhanced version of the classic Kindle experience, then the Kindle Colorsoft is the device to get. Its new design is superb, and colors look better than ever. Yes, it’s now the second-priciest Kindle after the Kindle Scribe, but there’s no denying Amazon delivered a more refined color e-reader.

Conversely, the iPad mini 8 impresses thanks to its premium design and mesmerizing OLED panel. Both black-and-white text and color images look absolutely stunning on its display. The lighter design also makes it more Kindle-like to hold. And if you want to do more than read, the iPad mini delivers strong performance for work, light gaming, and streaming videos.

This is the meeting that all of Spain is waiting for. FC Barcelona welcomes Real Madrid this Sunday at 9 p.m. at Camp Nou for a Clasico which could already weigh heavily in the title race. The Blaugranas will want to take advantage of home advantage to gain the upper hand over their historic rival, while the Merengues will look to leave Catalonia with a positive result to move up the standings. For this poster of the 10th day of La Liga, Barça should rely on Joan García in goals. In front of him, Xavi Espart, Pau Cubarsí, Gerard Martín and João Cancelo are expected to compose the defense. A rearguard who will have to be vigilant against Madrid’s offensive power, even in the absence of Vinicius Jr, suspended for this meeting.

In the midfield, FC Barcelona should rely on a trio composed of Rodri, Pedri and Fermín López. The technical mastery and ability of these three players to circulate the ball will be essential to impose the rhythm of the Catalans. Up front, Lamine Yamal should occupy the right side, with Raphinha on the left flank and Anthony Gordon up front. The young Spanish international will be particularly expected on the eve of the big Ballon d’Or ceremony in London where he is one of the credible candidates. For Real Madrid, Thibaut Courtois was announced as the starter but a last minute injury changed Madrid’s plans. It will therefore be Andriy Lunin who will guard the cages, with a defense made up of Federico Valverde, Ibrahima Konaté, Antonio Rüdiger and Marc Cucurella. The French international will be one of Madrid’s main offensive threats and will be responsible for weighing on the Catalan defense in a match that is always special for both clubs. The absence of Vinicius Jr, suspended, should modify the offensive habits of Real Madrid, who will have to find other solutions to make the difference in the final meters. With Mbappé at the forefront and Güler in creation, José Mourinho’s men nevertheless have several options to put Barça in difficulty. Liverpool are reportedly keeping a close watch on Aston Villa goalkeeper Zion Suzuki as they begin planning for life beyond Alisson Becker.

The Japan international has impressed since moving to Villa Park in the summer and is already attracting attention from several of Europe’s biggest clubs.

Liverpool’s interest is particularly significant because Alisson is in the final year of his contract, leaving the Reds with an important decision to make over one of the most influential players of their recent era.

According to TEAMtalk, Liverpool have checked on Suzuki while assessing their long-term goalkeeping options.

The 24-year-old joined Aston Villa for around £30million in the summer and has quickly impressed with his shot-stopping, distribution and ability to play as a modern sweeper-keeper. Real Madrid, Paris Saint-Germain, Arsenal, Manchester City and Tottenham are also monitoring him.

A CaughtOffside report also confirms Liverpool’s interest and notes that Suzuki is being viewed as a potential long-term successor to Alisson.

Liverpool would be wise to explore their options now rather than wait until Alisson actually leaves.

Liverpool already have Giorgi Mamardashvili and highly-rated youngster Lucca Brughmans in their plans, but TEAMtalk reports that Mamardashvili is not currently viewed as Alisson’s definitive long-term replacement.

That makes Suzuki an understandable target. He is young, already Premier League-tested and possesses the distribution required in Iraola’s system.

The difficulty will be price. Villa have little reason to sell, and Liverpool could face competition from several elite clubs.

Still, if Alisson does depart in 2027, Suzuki looks like exactly the type of goalkeeper Liverpool should be considering.

Ripple's Schwartz Speaks Out About His Influence Over XRP Price

📅 2026-11-08  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

David Schwartz, former chief technology officer of Ripple and one of the original XRP Ledger architects, has responded to claims that he is partially to blame for XRP's poor performance.

In a recent interaction on the X social media platform, David Schwartz was challenged about his possible impact on the price of the Ripple-linked cryptocurrency.

"Don't run from this. You can honestly say you don't have anything to do with it? It's not unfounded anger," the user wrote.

Although David Schwartz does not entirely deny that he can have an impact on the price of XRP, he is clear that it is minimal.

"Can I honestly say that I have nothing to do with the price of XRP? I guess probably not nothing. But very little," David Schwartz responded.

The response from Schwartz comes in the wake of mounting anger within the XRP community about the token's long-running price slump.

Indeed, XRP has seen roughly a 50% decline in value over the past year, per data from CoinMarketCap. During this period, the token has seen several temporary spikes, but the overall trend has been downward.

Of course, XRP is not the only token experiencing such a dramatic drop in value.

Stellar (XLM), another prominent cryptocurrency that seeks to enable payments, has seen a very similar price trend over the same time period.

The interaction came on the back of a long post in which Schwartz lamented the rise in hostile replies he receives on X.

Schwartz, a Ripple veteran, disclosed that some users are actually following him into other discussions and then proceeding to either label him a fraud or criticize him for his work on XRP.

The Ripple veteran noted that he has now made the decision to block such users, as irrelevant crypto-related posts are disrupting his discussions with other users.

Nonetheless, he stressed that the decision affects both critics and fans. Even if someone makes a positive comment about XRP but in an unsuitable place, they might also get blocked.

He also emphasized that he has no issue with honest criticism or accusations that make sense and come with good arguments.

He is mostly annoyed with users who keep hijacking unrelated discussions to vent their frustration.

In fact, Schwartz even offered to unblock users who commit to not doing so in the future.

Schwartz stepped away from his day-to-day duties at Ripple at the end of 2025. He had worked at the firm for over a decade. However, he has not completely left Ripple.

Earlier this month, Schwartz clarified that he's still on Ripple's board of directors and holds the honorary title of CTO Emeritus.

Furthermore, he disclosed that he still meets with his old team twice a month.

I've always been a little bit confused about the launch of Camera Control on the iPhone 16. It's one of those features that generally feels pretty redundant, especially since there are already better ways to quick launch the camera app that Apple hasn't bothered with yet. Well the good news is that the standalone Camera Control button could be on its way out, or at least it might be according to one leaker.

The leaker Instant Digital posted about this on their Weibo account, noting that the Camera Control and Action Buttons could be merged as soon as next year's iPhone 20. The new button will apparently launch the camera with a single tap, while press-and-hold will fire up whatever Action Button setting you have mapped. There's no mention of touch sensitivity, or any of the other Camera Control features that are currently available.

Instant Digital clarifies that this means the Action Button will be rolled into Camera Control, rather than the other way around. This presumably means the button will remain on the bottom right side of the phones, in the spot Camera Control currently occupies. That said, whichever way this might happen, I am all for it, because I've never liked Camera Control.

I've been using iPhones on and off for years, since before the Camera Control button was added to the iPhone 16 series two years ago. Apple marketed this button as a way to instantly access the camera with a single click and adjust camera features with your finger — rather than the touchscreen controls.

Apple didn't come up with the idea of a dedicated camera button, those have existed on smartphones for years, but mostly fell out of favor. Instead phones came up with better ways to access the camera with the existing button layout — including the well known "double tap" gesture on an Android phone's power button.

Admittedly Camera Control was meant for more. It was a way to access Visual Intelligence, until it got rolled into the camera app as "Siri Mode," and to adjust various different camera levels without having to poke around on the screen.

Though, in my experience with recent iPhones, that was never particularly appealing. On-screen controls always felt faster and simpler, especially since I could adjust things like zoom without taking my hands off the phone itself.

It's really not that difficult to do, and I've never felt the need to actually utilize the Camera Control button to the fullest extent of its capabilities. The older ways were easier and simpler to do, and didn't require much in the way of thinking first. Plus, the volume keys were (and still are) a valid way to activate the shutter for photos and video.

I've never fully understood why Apple felt the need to add that extra button to the side of the iPhone, when better alternatives were already being used. Especially after all the rumors that suggested Apple was trying to eliminate buttons altogether.

I'll admit, in the same way that I do not care for the Camera Control button, there are plenty of people who do. In fact some of them have already taken to social media sites like Reddit to discuss their own feelings about both the Action Button and Camera Control — many of them lamenting the fact that the current iterations may be lost.

I get it to an extent, but it still feels as though iPhones are getting a little too cluttered for my liking, especially on the smaller models. That's assuming you ignore all those rumors about a possible third button on the iPhone 17, which didn't come to pass because it would have been a step too far.

The features that are on offer aren't necessarily bad, but there are better ways to implement them. Combining Camera Control and the Action Button together is one good way of doing it. After all, the Action Button is triggered by a long press, while the Camera Control opens your camera with a single or double press. There's no reason why the two can't live in harmony as a single dual-purpose button.

Of course turning the Camera Control button into an Action Button will mean Siri Mode needs a new shortcut. At the moment, pressing and holding the Camera button launches the camera app in Apple's AI camera mode, just like how it launched Visual Intelligence before the release of iOS 27.

Likewise you won't be able to use the Action Button while the camera is open, since that action is already reserved for recording video — and nobody wants to get rid of that. I'm guessing that the emphasis on the claim that the Action Button will become part of Camera Control means the latter's touch-sensitive controls will stick around for people that actually use them.

Sure, it will take some getting used to, regardless of how old your current iPhone might be, but I think that it will end up being the more efficient option in the long run. I just hope they pick a more sensible spot, because the bottom right side of an iPhone doesn't really scream "Action" to me.

The one thing that has to be made very clear is that this is an unverified rumor. Instant Digital doesn't have a bad reputation, but that doesn't mean they have a 100% track record, and we're still pretty early in the iPhone 20 development cycle. So, in other words, there's no guarantee that Apple will actually make this change with the iPhone 20.

Especially when you remember all the other Camera Control rumors that never came true, and the fact the iPhone 20 is also rumored to swap out buttons for haptic sensors. If every phone rumor we heard turned out to be true, these devices would be a complete mess.

Still, I would quite like to see this happen. The Action Button is useful, and I don't want to see that go away, but there are plenty of people who have grown to like the Camera Control button too — even if I don't really care for it myself. If you're going to keep both, you might as well merge them into a single, easy to use button.

At the very least it's going to look and feel neater than plastering the iPhone with more buttons than a keyboard.
