Add This Spicy Ingredient To Ground Beef For A Burger That's 10x Juicier

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If you're looking for a burger patty that not only has a bit of kick, but also plenty of juice, you need 'nduja, a legendary Italian sausage. It is not only extremely flavorful, but also quite high in fat and moisture, making it a perfect addition no matter how you like to cook your burgers.

'Nduja is technically a type of salami, except it's far more decadent and incorporates a hefty dose of Calabrian chili peppers for next-level taste. Its high fat-to-lean meat ratio makes it perfect for introducing more moisture, even in small amounts. Even if you only have exceptionally lean meat on hand, one part 'nduja to four parts beef keeps your patties tender and juicy, and even helps develop a better crust.

The idea is similar to many meatball recipes from around the world that incorporate both pork and beef. Whereas beef provides body and a hearty texture, pork can provide extra fat, flavor, and moisture, working together to cook up something truly delicious. The peppers also create a slightly savory and spicy flavor profile that, while immediately noticeable, isn't so overwhelming that you might need to change up your preferred toppings. Each bite will drip with flavorful juices that mix with your condiments for the most moisture-rich burger possible.

Incorporating anything into a burger is a bit of a tightrope walk. You want to disperse your ingredients evenly but not work the muscle fibers so much that your patties end up dense or tough. 'Nduja's texture makes this quite easy, but you'll want to be careful about what type of meat you add it to.

Since it has a spreadable, pâté-like texture, working 'nduja directly into the meat is quite easy. Whether you mix it in thoroughly or squeeze pinches into already formed patties for pockets of flavor, you get plenty of fatty juices. Still, if you're worried about overworking your patties or want to add the salty meat at the very last minute, 'nduja is typically safe to eat right out of the package. Treat it like cheese and add a dollop after flipping your patties, letting it melt over the whole thing for a juicy entree.

If you want a custom burger blend, you have to be mindful of the proper fat-to-meat ratio. 'Nduja's exceptional fat content means it's best to add it to already lean blends so you don't end up with anything too greasy. The leaner your beef, the more 'nduja you can incorporate, so it's a balance between how much of its flavor you want included. Something that's at least 85% lean is ideal, letting you incorporate enough to taste without adding so much that you can't appreciate the beef's natural flavor.

Modern life is changing the DNA of bacteria in our guts

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria can acquire genes from neighboring microbes, adding to the genetic differences linked to how people live.

Diet, antibiotics, and indoor plumbing help shape which bacteria settle into a person’s gut. That much has been clear for years.

What researchers couldn’t say is whether the bacteria themselves have been evolving in response.

A study of nearly 6,000 gut bacterial genomes finds that they have.

Across the species examined, strains from industrialized communities carried more genes than those from non-industrialized communities.

Most of the extra genes arrived recently. The bacteria didn’t inherit them from a parent cell. They took them from other microbes living alongside them.

So modern life hasn’t only changed which bacteria live in the gut. The same species now carries a different set of genes depending on how its host lives.

Researchers at Kiel University’s Institute of Clinical Molecular Biology and MIT’s Department of Biological Engineering ran the work, with the evolutionary modeling done in Japan.

Everything here was measured in bacterial genomes, so none of it shows that a bigger bacterial genome makes a person healthier or sicker.

All of the genomes come from the Global Microbiome Conservancy, a non-profit effort to bank living strains from communities that microbiome research has mostly passed over.

For this study, 20 volunteers in Cameroon, Ghana, Malaysia, Nigeria and Rwanda gave fresh stool samples. None had taken antibiotics recently.

Scientists processed each sample on site within three hours. The samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen at minus 321°F (minus 196°C).

In the lab, the material went onto plates inside an oxygen-free chamber. Colonies grew at 99°F (37°C) for one to two weeks. Single colonies were then picked and sequenced.

The cultures yielded 1,841 new bacterial genomes. Added to 4,140 strains already banked, the collection reached 5,981 genomes. They came from 56 donors in nine countries.

Researchers classified donors as industrialized or non-industrialized using a development score for where they lived, compared with their country’s median.

From that collection, the team picked ten species sampled well on both sides of the divide, with at least eight strains from each side. Most are Bacteroides and close relatives, the workhorses of the lower gut.

E. coli was left out on purpose. Its genetic diversity has already been mapped in detail.

In all ten species, strains from industrialized guts carried more protein-coding genes. The difference was statistically significant in eight of the ten species.

Core genes, the ones every strain of a species has, showed no difference. The extra material was all optional genes, the kind one strain carries and the next one doesn’t.

The optional sets also varied more from strain to strain in industrialized hosts, again in all ten species. The team then widened the comparison to 34 species and saw the same pattern.

A bigger genome could mean two different things. Either these strains picked up genes lately, or strains that already had big genomes happened to do well in modern guts.

Separating the two fell to a group in Okinawa, Japan. They compared the family tree of a single gene with the family tree of the species carrying it.

A mismatch between the two marks the spot where a gene was gained, lost or handed over.

Gene gains clustered near the tips of the evolutionary trees, indicating that they occurred relatively recently.

They also piled up on the branches belonging to industrialized hosts.

Between 80% and 90% of those gains were transfers from other microbes. Only a small share came down from a parent cell.

Many arrived packaged inside plasmids and viruses that infect bacteria.

Both deliver several genes at once. Swapping of this kind goes on well beyond the gut, in soil, seawater and thawing permafrost.

Gergely Szöllősi heads the Model-Based Evolutionary Genomics Unit at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology.

His group built these tools for a much older problem, and Lénárd Szánthó, a senior bioinformatician in the unit, adapted them to a far shorter stretch of time.

“These methods were built to look back hundreds of millions and billions of years, so it was not obvious that they would tell us anything about something this recent,” said Szöllősi.

“Lénárd made them work for this problem, and the picture that comes out is that bacteria in industrialized hosts have been acquiring genes from their neighbors recently, and this is what makes their genomes larger.”

About 5% of the gene families in a typical species collection tracked with the host’s way of life.

The team checked that against bacterial DNA read straight from the stool of 1,015 people, and 83% of the signals pointed the same way.

A few gene families turned up in more than one species, which is harder to put down to chance.

Two of them help build the outer coat of the cell, the part that other microbes and the immune system meet.

Another repeat hit was an enzyme that breaks down penicillin-type drugs. Genes like that ride the same mobile DNA now spreading antibiotic resistance through cities and farms.

The team also counted genes where protein-changing mutations had built up faster than mutations that did not change the protein. That pattern is the usual sign of a change being favored.

About 1.4% of the genes examined qualified. One of them helps a cell hold its outer envelope together under stress, and it carried the signature in three separate species.

In the five species with the most genomes, the researchers tracked single-letter DNA differences in core genes.

The variants tied to industrialized hosts kept landing on the same few jobs: building the cell wall, handling stress and making vitamin B12.

A companion study from the same group found more vitamin B12 production in the gut communities of people more exposed to industrialized environments.

Another cluster of changes hit the genes that build a molecule on the bacterial surface. The human immune system reads that molecule and reacts to it.

Researchers have not tested whether any of this changes how a person’s immune system behaves. The authors wrote that it is a possibility worth following up.

“I hope our results will inspire further studies to use this knowledge in supporting us to live a healthier life in our changing environment,” Szánthó said.

The analysis can’t say which part of industrialized living is responsible. Diet, medicine, sanitation and exposure to outdoor microbes all travel together, and this data can’t pull them apart.

Bigger genomes may not be an adaptation at all.

Extra genes build up on their own when the pressure to discard useless DNA eases, and the authors couldn’t rule that out.

Ten species is a thin slice of a gut that holds hundreds, and most of the ten belong to one bacterial group.

Species that have never been grown in a lab are missing here, which matters because industrialized guts hold fewer bacterial species to begin with.

The study itself doesn’t test what those extra genes actually do inside a person. Getting that answer will take experiments rather than more genomes.

The authors call for controlled experiments with bacteria, laboratory studies of evolution, and more detailed measurements of people’s living conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Cell.

Claude Personal Finance vs. ChatGPT Finances: What sets them apart?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both AI tools offer to delve deep into your bank accounts, but they differ in privacy settings.

Ahead of its plans to go public with a potentially record-breaking valuation, Anthropic recently announced a new feature for Claude called Personal Finance. It's an obvious rival to OpenAI's Finances feature within ChatGPT, which debuted in May. Both options from these AI giants are meant to provide users with a better understanding of their finances and are not designed to offer expert financial advice, but there are slight variations in how they do it that might make you lean towards one or the other. To help you decide which AI chatbot to go with for more clarity on your finances, we've laid out the major similarities and differences of Claude's Personal Finance and ChatGPT's Finances features.

Covering a broad spectrum of personal finance topics, there's a lot of crossover in what both OpenAI and Anthropic's offerings can do. Both of their AI chatbots can address general questions about financial goals, like buying a house, planning an expensive family vacation or creating a savings plan. Claude and ChatGPT will also generate dashboards to offer users better visuals at things like monthly expenses, investments and spending habits.

To activate either Claude's Personal Finance tool or ChatGPT's Finances feature, you have to link your bank account. Both AI companies partnered with Plaid to make this happen, allowing connections with the most popular financial institutions, like Bank of America, Capital One or Chase. Once set up, both Claude and ChatGPT only have "read-only" access to your financial accounts, meaning they can only view the info provided and would not be able to make any changes.

Anthropic specified that Claude's Personal Finance feature can only see your bank name, account name, account type, last four account digits, balances, credit limits and up to two years of transactions. Similarly, OpenAI said that its Finances tool can see balances, transactions, investments and liabilities.

While the core function of Anthropic's Personal Finance and OpenAI's Finances are very similar, the major difference between the two has to do with privacy settings. By default, Anthropic said, "when you chat with Claude in the Personal Finance hub, those conversations aren't used to train our models." Anthropic added that Claude will no longer see any data that you previously provided to its Personal Finance feature if you unlink your bank account.

On the other hand, OpenAI said that "your conversations with connected financial accounts follow the same model training settings you choose across ChatGPT." In this case, you have to opt out of your data being used by navigating to Data controls in ChatGPT's settings and turning off the option called Improve the model for everyone, which is on by default. When you disconnect your bank accounts from OpenAI, the company said that "your synced account data will be deleted from OpenAI's systems within 30 days."

To differentiate itself from OpenAI's alternative, Anthropic said that its Personal Finance feature "has no ads or sponsored recommendations, and it doesn't offer credit cards, loans or savings accounts of its own." While OpenAI doesn't explicitly mention ads with ChatGPT Finances, the company has been testing out ads for its cheaper tiers as of January.

OpenAI said it's looking to bring more features to ChatGPT's Finances by partnering with Intuit to cover more bases like credit card applications or tax questions. As for Anthropic's next steps with Personal Finance, the company wants to hear from banks, wealth managers and financial services companies to help improve the AI tool.

President Donald Trump offered UFC president and CEO Dana White the job of press secretary on Saturday.

In a post on Truth Social, Trump said: "I am pleased to announce that the Great Dana White, a violent Fighter and Warrior, has been offered the job of Press Secretary of the United States."

"We need these people to defend us against the Lunatic Left," Trump wrote. "Dana will be a Press Secretary like none other. Dana, please take the job!"

CNN, citing a source, reported White had declined the president's offer.

Earlier Saturday, White posted a picture of himself on Instagram stories standing behind the press secretary's lectern in the White House, with the caption, "On to the next chapter."

It was unclear whether White had accepted the position or was simply acknowledging the president's request as a joke.

Trump said that he was offering White the job of "Press Secretary of the United States," but the president's spokesperson is called the "White House press secretary." Trump also recently speculated about considering Trump loyalist and rapper Nicki Minaj as press secretary, in an interview with podcaster Benny Johnson.

Trump said the job offer to White extended to his assistants, "even though some are not from our Country." The so-called assistants Trump named included current and former UFC mixed martial arts fighters from countries such as Brazil and Russia.

In October, White House press secretary Katie Zacharia left the position just five days after her stint as Karoline Leavitt's replacement began.

The president told reporters he suggested Zacharia quit after her first and only press briefing, where, in her six minutes answering questions behind the lectern, she only took questions from three conservative outlets.

"I just spoke to her about it. I said, 'Katie, is this for you?'" Trump said outside the White House on Oct. 16.

The president's appeal to White follows Friday's announcement that Trump's deputy chief of staff, Stephen Miller, would be promoted to chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles.

Trump's stunning shake-up came just three days after Democrats won a landslide victory in the midterm elections, gaining majority control in the House and Senate.

The president and White have a longstanding friendship. During Trump's first campaign in 2016, White praised the then-Republican nominee and called him a "fighter."

"Nobody took us seriously. Nobody. Except Donald Trump. Donald was the first guy to recognize the potential we saw in the UFC and encouraged us to build our business," White said.

And for his 80th birthday in June, President Trump hosted a UFC match — dubbed UFC Freedom 250 — on the White House's South Lawn.

The White House and the UFC didn't immediately respond to a request for comment.

‘Ferritin Face’: What Low Iron Actually Does (and Doesn't Do) to Your Appearance

Social media influencers claim that having a “ferritin face” may indicate you’re low in iron.

Ferritin is a protein that helps your body store iron. You need iron in order to make healthy red blood cells, which carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Iron is also important for bone marrow, organs, healthy muscles, and brain development.

Users have also shared images of their face before and after taking iron supplements or getting an iron infusion. Videos about “ferritin face” have garnered millions of views on various platforms.

However, medical professionals state that your appearance alone is not enough to diagnose iron deficiency.

“It’s important to understand that ferritin face is not a medical diagnosis, nor is it a confirmed symptom of iron deficiency. While some people with iron deficiency can appear pale due to low hemoglobin levels in their red blood cells, there are many other symptoms to consider,” said Daniel Atkinson, a general practitioner in the United Kingdom and clinical lead at Treated.

“Notably, these symptoms are also present in many different conditions, and their presence does not necessarily mean iron deficiency anemia or even low ferritin levels. Similar features are seen in people with iron processing and absorption issues, such as gluten intolerance, or in urgent sudden blood loss scenarios,” added Jonathan Jennings, MD, board certified internist at Medical Offices of Manhattan.

In some ways, the social media videos are correct. Your skin and face can change when your iron levels are low.

“The facial signs people point to, like pale skin and a washed-out look, mostly come from anaemia, when your haemoglobin has dropped. But iron deficiency and anaemia aren’t the same thing,” said Thom Phillips, a general practitioner in the United Kingdom and Chief Medical Officer at Forth. “By the time low iron shows on your face, your stores are usually close to empty.”

It is important to speak with a healthcare professional, who will run blood tests such as a complete blood count and ferritin levels.

It is also important not to take iron supplements without first checking with your healthcare professional, because having too much iron in your body can also cause negative effects.

Too little and too much iron could raise your risk of infection, which is why it’s important not to take supplements without medical supervision.

“These symptoms typically occur because your body produces fewer red blood cells, which results in less oxygen being delivered throughout your body, making you feel tired and weak,” said Atkinson.

While your facial appearance can be a sign that something isn’t right, healthcare professionals consider other symptoms and how you feel overall.

They also rely on blood tests to confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment.

“The misinformation is the idea that you can spot it yourself. That leads to two problems: people with low iron who look fine assume they’re OK, and people who look tired start taking iron supplements they don’t need,” said Phillips.

Atkinson also noted that factors such as different settings and environmental conditions can affect how your face appears.

That being said, the “ferritin face” trend may help bring awareness to certain symptoms and when to get them checked.

“[This] trend may raise some awareness about iron deficiency anemia and how low iron levels can impact daily life,” said Atkinson.

“Fatigue and lightheadedness are common side effects of low iron, but it manifests in other ways too, such as paler skin or increased hair shedding, which can be concerning for many people. If you experience these symptoms, you should let your doctor know so they can help treat the condition appropriately,” he said.

Hims & Hers inks deal with Eli Lilly to offer branded weight loss drugs" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Hims & Hers struck a deal with Eli Lilly to offer the drugmaker’s branded obesity treatments through its online health and wellness platform.

Under the agreement, Eli Lilly will give Hims & Hers pharmacies direct access to its U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved GLP-1 medications at self-pay prices. Eligible patients on the Hims & Hers platform can be prescribed and order Eli Lilly's injectable Zepbound (tirzepatide) and daily oral medication Foundayo (orforglipron) without leaving the company's platform.

Prior to this agreement, Lilly drugs were available to patients on Hims & Hers only if they left the site and went to Lilly's direct-to-consumer platform, LillyDirect.

Shares of Hims & Hers jumped 8% Friday following news of the Eli Lilly partnership.

The agreement brings together Lilly’s obesity medicines and Hims & Hers’ consumer platform to expand consumers' access to FDA-approved weight loss drugs, the companies said.

"Patients can complete a consultation, receive a prescription if their provider determines it is appropriate and have an FDA-approved Lilly medicine shipped directly to their home, all in one seamless experience," the companies said in a press release.

Self-pay pricing starts at $149 per month for Foundayo and $299 per month for Zepbound.

"At Lilly, we are always looking for new ways to help more people get the medicines they need, and that means meeting patients where they already are," said Ilya Yuffa, executive vice president and president, Lilly USA and global customer capabilities, Eli Lilly and Company, in a statement. "Millions of people turn to Hims & Hers for their care, and through this agreement, those patients now have a simple, more direct way to get FDA-approved Lilly medicine at an affordable price if prescribed."

Hims & Hers' technology platform supports provider outreach, follow-up care and treatment planning. Weight loss patients on Hims & Hers receive a membership experience that includes 24/7 access to a care team, nutrition guidance, regular clinical check-ins and access to the Hims & Hers Weight Loss community, according to the company.

The agreement with Eli Lilly mirrors Hims & Hers' partnership with Novo Nordisk, which has been selling its branded weight loss drugs Wegovy and Ozempic through Hims & Hers since March. Novo had previously sued the telehealth company for offering compounded versions of its medications. Hims & Hers' agreement with Novo marked a shift in its obesity strategy toward FDA-approved branded GLP-1 treatments and away from cheaper compounded GLP-1 drugs.

Earlier this year, Hims & Hers took heat from the pharmaceutical manufacturer and federal regulators after announcing plans to launch a knockoff of Novo's oral pill. It backed down from those plans under pressure.

Hims & Hers stated that it never offered access to compounded copies of Lilly’s GLP-1 drugs and has committed not to do so in the future.

As part of the agreement, the companies have committed to protecting independent clinical decision-making, so that providers on the Hims & Hers platform continue to prescribe the treatment they determine is right for each patient.

“Lilly is one of the most innovative pharmaceutical companies in history, and I’m incredibly proud that Hims & Hers is a part of making sure more people can access the medicines they’ve created,” said Andrew Dudum, co-founder and CEO of Hims & Hers, in a statement. “By working together, we’re not just reaching more people with obesity with seamless, personal access to FDA-approved Lilly medicine. We’re showing the industry what innovators can achieve for customers everywhere when we work together to make the world well.”

Hims & Hers has faced financial pressure as it shifts from high-margin compounded GLP-1s to lower-margin branded weight-loss drugs. The company posted a $92 million loss in Q1, including $33 million in restructuring costs, largely from write-downs of its compounded GLP-1 supply chain, which executives said faced “risk of obsolescence.”

In Q2, Hims & Hers reported an $86 million loss, compared with a $43 million profit a year earlier. The quarter included $81 million in one-time costs, including expenses tied to its exit from compounded GLP-1s.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/pharma/cms-makes-it-official-codifying-medicare-drug-price-negotiations-rolled-out-under-ira" hreflang="en">CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Against a backdrop of repeated industry challenges in court, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) is locking in the drug price negotiation framework established under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act (IRA).

In turn, the process under which CMS selects certain high-spend Medicare Part B or Part D drugs and negotiates lower prices with manufacturers will carry on into 2029 and beyond. The rule codifies and expands policies previously established through guidance for the initial 2026-28 period.

The final rule is slated to go into effect Dec. 5, CMS said in a fact sheet accompanying Thursday’s announcement. By Feb. 1 of next year, CMS will select up to 20 drugs covered under Medicare Part B or Part D for price negotiations taking effect in 2029, with the potential to also select medicines included in previous rounds for renegotiation.

That upcoming group of drugs will make up the fourth cycle of pricing negotiations, with negotiated prices taking effect in 2029. CMS asserted that the rule gives both patients and drugmakers “a clear path forward as the agency builds on the program’s results and continues negotiating prices for high-cost drugs.”

The initial rounds of price negotiations have already included a slew of prominent brands, including Novo's semaglutide franchise of Wegovy and Ozempic and cancer meds like Bristol Myers Squibb’s Pomalyst, Pfizer’s Ibrance and AstraZeneca’s Calquence.

Meanwhile, the latest tranche of drugs selected earlier this year for negotiation, whose new prices would go into effect in 2028, includes notable entrants like Gilead’s HIV treatment Biktarvy, AbbVie’s Botox and Eli Lilly’s diabetes drug Trulicity, to name just a few.

Beyond codifying the framework for price negotiations in 2029 and subsequent years, CMS has also taken steps to fine-tune several elements of the program.

Namely, the rule finalizes a policy on how CMS classifies certain fixed-combination drugs, with the aim of addressing “potential program integrity concerns” that could encourage drugmakers to evade negotiated prices by steering patients toward new product formulations.

The rule also includes a provision establishing a temporary “small biotech floor” that prevents CMS from offering or accepting a counteroffer below a specified price floor for certain eligible small biotech drugs in 2029 and 2030. It also codifies two policies related to Medicare Part D intended to ensure plan coverage of medicines with a maximum fair price in effect and limit the prices plans pay pharmacies and other dispensing entities.

The IRA and the Medicare price negotiations it empowered have been frequent targets of the biopharma industry, which has broadly alleged that the policy is unconstitutional and that the pricing talks aren’t as voluntary as advertised. But attempts by major drugmakers and powerful trade organizations like the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) to push back on Medicare price negotiations have repeatedly stalled out.

In the interim, the U.S. government has made a separate and more recent push to rein in high prescription drug costs by leaning on President Donald Trump’s “most favored nation” (MFN) strategy.

“President Trump directed us to lower drug costs for Americans using every tool at our disposal,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a statement announcing the new rule. “This rule provides additional tools to help CMS negotiate better deals, achieve greater savings for taxpayers and beneficiaries, and improve health outcomes for seniors who rely on the Medicare Prescription Drug Program.”

MFN, for its part, has yielded at times, controversial deals with more than two dozen drugmakers, generally involving commitments to price new medicines in line with prices in high-income comparator countries and offer MFN pricing on certain existing drugs through Medicaid.

Through those deals, which have also included investment commitments in the U.S., drugmakers have been able to secure temporary exemptions from the Trump administration’s threatened pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Around this week’s midterm elections, several Big Pharma executives have signaled that they expect little immediate change to their thinking around MFN. Nevertheless, the lack of transparency surrounding the deals has already drawn scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers. With Democrats set to control both chambers of Congress beginning in January, the party may soon press harder to uncover the details of those agreements.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/ai-and-machine-learning/equiti-acquires-lexi-ai-combine-ai-human-medical-interpretation" hreflang="en">Equiti acquires Lexi AI to combine AI, human medical interpretation</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Equiti acquires Lexi AI to combine AI, human medical interpretation" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Equiti is acquiring Lexi AI to build a hybrid language-access model that combines AI language support with human medical interpreters.

Equiti, which sells medical interpretation services to hospitals and health systems through its Martti business, provides 24/7 access to qualified medical interpreters across more than 250 languages and dialects. Lexi provides multilingual, AI-powered medical interpretation. The acquisition deal merges Lexi's immediate AI language support, covering over 35 languages and dialects, with Martti and its network of qualified medical interpreters to help healthcare organizations expand language access.

The acquisition was announced October 28. Financial details of the deal were not disclosed.

With this acquisition, Equiti is building on Martti’s work pioneering video remote interpretation in healthcare more than two decades ago, according to executives. The use of AI can expand where and when language services can be offered, particularly for everyday interactions that may not currently receive interpretation support, Equiti executives said.

Martti was built alongside healthcare organizations to make qualified interpreters available by video. Lexi, in turn, has been developed with healthcare customers to extend immediate language support into more workflows. The acquisition brings those capabilities together, with human-in-the-loop design, patient safety and patient experience at its center, Equiti executives said.

The company saw an opportunity to combine its services with Lexi's AI expertise rather than build a healthcare AI interpretation product internally, Maureen Huber, Equiti CEO, told Fierce Healthcare.

"Lexi brings specialized expertise in AI, engineering and healthcare product design, along with experience putting that technology into the hands of care teams. That combination takes time to build," Huber said. "For more than 20 years, our work through Martti has been about making qualified human interpretation accessible within healthcare. We understand the service, the clinical environment and what it takes to support healthcare organizations every day. Developing AI and machine learning technology calls for additional, specialized expertise. We wanted people who had already been doing that work with healthcare in mind."

She added, "We spent time evaluating the category and learning from customers. Lexi brought together the people, product and approach we were seeking. Combining their expertise with ours gives us a stronger foundation to expand language access responsibly."

Combining AI and human interpretation makes language support more accessible across the patient journey, helping organizations improve care delivery while making better use of their resources, according to Equiti executives.

Healthcare organizations are increasingly interested in AI interpretation capabilities and are looking for guidance, Huber noted.

"Customers were asking: 'Where could AI help? How do we evaluate it? What is appropriate for our patients?' Healthcare leaders see the same advances in consumer AI that everyone else sees. They also must think about what happens when those tools enter a care environment, sometimes before an organization has decided how they should be used. They want a partner who understands both the possibilities and the responsibilities," she said.

She added, "I would describe that as inquiry and evaluation. There is real curiosity about extending language support to more of the patient experience, particularly before and after a visit. Our job is to help customers identify those opportunities while keeping qualified human interpretation central to care delivery."

Equiti calls this approach Modality Matters, a connected model that allows healthcare organizations to match each encounter with the appropriate modality — immediate, healthcare-purpose-built AI support for approved use cases and qualified medical interpretation where clinical complexity, regulatory requirements, organizational policy or patient preference calls for a human interpreter. With this approach, organizations have the flexibility to shift between modalities as clinical context and communication needs change in real time.

The combined portfolio of Equiti and Lexi's capabilities will extend language access into everyday interactions that often go unsupported, Huber noted.

Equiti is focusing Lexi's AI-powered medical interpretation on routine patient engagement, including before and after care, such as appointment communications, scheduling and nonclinical follow-up logistics.

"We see a significant opportunity for Lexi in that communication before and after care: helping patients exchange messages, arrange appointments and stay connected in their preferred language. Language access must extend beyond the clinical encounter. Think about what happens after a patient leaves the clinic. They receive a message about a follow-up appointment, but they need to ask whether it is at the same location, how to reschedule, or whom to contact. Those may be simple questions for the organization, but the answers determine whether the patient can take the next step," she said.

Qualified human interpreters remain the foundation for care delivery, Huber noted. "In conversations such as those about a diagnosis, informed consent, treatment or palliative care, the ability to clarify meaning, recognize cultural context and respond with empathy is essential. A medically qualified interpreter helps people understand one another through conversations that can be complex and deeply personal," she said.

Equiti said it can work with customers to define appropriate uses within their policies and applicable requirements, preserving patient choice and access to a qualified human interpreter.

Language access is also a strategic performance driver for healthcare organizations, as effective communication influences patient understanding and experience, clinical outcomes, workforce efficiency and financial performance.

Equiti's customers include Wellstar Health System, Corewell Health, NYU Langone and Lee Health. Lexi works with a growing pipeline of federally qualified health centers and community providers including Lowell Community Health Center, which serves one of the most multilingual communities in Massachusetts.

As general-purpose AI gets better at translation, a healthcare-specific interpretation platform has distinct advantages, Huber asserted.

"Healthcare communication involves clinical meaning, cultural context, jargon and acronyms during situations where people may be frightened, vulnerable or unsure how to ask a question. Our job is to remove those communication barriers, and that requires understanding the care environment, fitting into clinical workflows and knowing when a qualified human interpreter needs to be involved," she said. "That is where Equiti’s experience matters. We bring the relationships, service infrastructure and human expertise developed through more than two decades of healthcare interpretation, alongside Lexi’s technology and team."

Huber said she envisions broader uses of Lexi's AI language support in healthcare workflows. "A patient’s need to communicate continues after the conversation with a clinician ends. Lexi already supports text-based messaging, including translation of text provided by a care team, and can even leave voicemail messages for patients. That creates opportunities to help patients access information and communicate between visits. Helping someone navigate follow-up care is a natural direction for that work, like understanding the next step, connecting with the right office or arranging an appointment. The value is in making it easier for patients to act on the care plan they have been given and ask for help along the way," she said.

"We built Lexi to fit the way healthcare teams actually work, bringing immediate language support into the devices and workflows they already use,” Siddharth UR, co-founder and chief product officer of Lexi, said in a statement. “As part of Equiti, we can extend that experience across the patient journey and connect it to a broader language access model built around the needs of each encounter.”
