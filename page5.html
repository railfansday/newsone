Renee Slegers knows Arsenal will have to "suffer" if they are to get the better of Barcelona on Thursday, in a repeat of the teams' 2025 Champions League final matchup.

Though the Gunners have struggled domestically this campaign, sitting sixth in the Women's Super League with just three wins in nine matches, their European form has been better.

They have taken seven points from their opening three games in the Champions League, putting themselves firmly in the hunt for a top-four finish and automatic quarter-final qualification.

Arsenal are unbeaten in each of their last six league or group-stage games in the Champions League (W5 D1), their longest run without defeat since this stage of the competition was introduced in 2021-22.

But reigning European champions Barca were the only side to win their first three matches and are themselves unbeaten in 14 league or group-stage matches (W13 D1); winning each of their last two by a margin of at least six goals.

Barca also lead all teams in the 2026-27 edition for goals (19), expected goals (11.7 xG), shots on target (35) and shot conversion rate (28.8%), leading Slegers to expect a tough test.

"We will have to suffer. We'll have to put bodies on the line," she said. "We'll have to be really disciplined. But we have a lot of quality in our team as well.

"We are in a good position in the league phase and we're looking forward to the game against Barcelona. It's going to be a big game with two really good teams."

The Gunners did, of course, beat Barca 1-0 to lift the Champions League trophy in 2024-25, with Stina Blackstenius netting the all-important goal in that final clash.

Only Lyon (67%) have a better win rate in Champions League games against Barcelona than Arsenal's 60% (3/5), and Slegers looks back on that victory as one of her career highlights.

"That performance was immense and it had so many facets to it," Slegers said. "It was a great moment, of course, a very proud moment for us."

Ethical disruptive innovators identify and solve problems through prosocial and creative means that often unsettle group norms.

Disruption can be interpreted both as destructive and divine, depending on the professional culture. For example, part-time Sherwin-Williams employee Tony Piloseno, a senior at the time at Ohio University, used his popular TikTok channel to share his paint-mixing acumen, a clever contribution meant to bolster the company’s products, yet instead of accolades, he was served termination papers for violating company policy. However, instead of accepting one of the multiple job offers spurred by the news coverage, he poured his energy into starting his own paint company, a bold innovator’s move (Tonester Paints, n.d.).

Organizational norms govern how employees typically behave at work, but norms can be messy business, for institutions possess multiple forms. There are injunctive norms, which are the “ought to” behaviors, and descriptive norms, which detail what people actually do. When a gap exists between the two, the Focus Theory of Normative Conduct suggests we follow the norms we see others live out (Cialdini et al., 1991).

For example, let’s say that during faculty meetings, university administrators regularly profess that they value employee well-being (injunctive norms), yet faculty workload dictates 70-hour work weeks, and the university gym is free to students, but not to employees. Faculty will work longer hours and exercise fewer hours, putting their health at risk, because the descriptive norm is what plays out on campus despite injunctive norms to the contrary (Cialdini et al., 1990; Roswag et al., 2026; Skogstad et al., 2017).

We are shaped by those with whom we share our social spaces, so what happens when an employee decides to think past tradition, explore new ideas, and forge unique connections in a quest to innovate novel solutions to organizational problems (Lawande, 2023)?

Dyer and colleagues (2011) studied just that, interviewing creators of groundbreaking products and founders and leaders of visionary companies. They shared their insights in the book The Innovator’s DNA: Mastering the Five Skills of Disruptive Innovators. Another intriguing finding was that, though many companies’ leaders facilitated processes that encouraged innovation, they themselves did not engage in innovative work. Conversely, in highly innovative organizations, making up just 15 percent of Dyer et al.’s (2009) research, top executives were among the ones actually doing, not delegating, innovation. Therefore, the injunctive (ought to) and descriptive (actually doing) organizational norms were in alignment, thus providing a clear pathway for others to join in or initiate creative work.

Again, we tend to emulate what we see, and leaders set that agenda. But what happens to innovative employees in organizations where leadership views their innovator’s DNA as a threat, not an asset?

Ethical disruption awakens innovation, but as Tony experienced, shaking trees has its costs; keepers of the status quo sometimes go to considerable lengths to neutralize norm-breakers. Norm violations are categorized as either constructive, such as crafting innovative products that surpass current offerings, or destructive, like purposefully denigrating a colleague’s reputation. The disruptor’s intentions and the innovation’s success also factor into how the norm is classified (Bennett et al., 2024). Though organizations often profess a desire for “out-of-the-box” thinkers, many nonconformists face roadblocks and repercussions. Whether the disruptor receives praise or pushback is, in large part, reflective of leadership.

According to McGregor’s (1960) seminal book, The Human Side of Enterprise, leaders adopt one of two theories when developing their style of management. Leaders with a Theory X view of management assume employees are lazy and shirk responsibility; therefore, they must be micromanaged, leaving little room for innovation. In contrast, leaders who view management through a Theory Y framework believe work can be fun, employees are internally motivated to do a good job, and that when given autonomy and resources, they can and will engage in creative work. Thus, under Theory Y leadership, innovators innovate.

Another way to think about leadership approaches that stymie innovation is through what researchers call control appraisal. Leaders with a low control appraisal struggle with self-doubt and believe they have limited ability to control outcomes. This group is unsettled by innovators, viewing them as unpredictable and destabilizing. Thus, they are quick to shut them down, often using withdrawal of opportunities and ostracization as containment strategies. By contrast, leaders with high control appraisal are self-confident and possess a strong sense of personal agency, or trust that they have the skills and fortitude to impact outcomes. These leaders are intrigued by innovators and work to form genuine connections, believing disruptors’ creativity is an asset, thus supporting and celebrating their work (Peng et al., 2026).

Social comparison theory is another helpful tool for considering leaders’ level of support for ethical disruptive innovators. It posits that leaders with a low core self-evaluation, or a propensity to question their ability to steer the ship, often become jealous of employees who exhibit high leadership potential (HLP), worried their star might surpass their own, and thus attempt to eliminate the competition by withdrawing opportunities. Conversely, comparison is minimal to nonexistent in leaders with a high core self-evaluation, and this positioning calls them to support innovative projects and proposals with the potential to bolster the institution’s success and more effectively serve communities (Yu et al., 2025).

Lastly, when faced with ethically disruptive innovators who don’t conform to the prevailing work culture, knowingly or unknowingly breaking group norms, leaders tend to react in one of five ways: forgive, reward, punish, ignore, or manipulate. According to research, however, only the reward reaction ensures the creative continues to engage in innovative behaviors. So, when a leader shuts down an innovator, the employee and the organization both lose out (Lin et al., 2016).

So, what does this all tell us about the types of leaders innovators need to engage in trailblazing work? First, innovators need leaders who clearly articulate and live into their mission, ensuring their injunctive norms (ought tos) and descriptive norms (what people actually do) are in alignment. Second, innovators benefit from leaders who build authentic relationships with their employees, not just rule-followers in their inner circle. Third, innovators thrive when working for leaders who personally engage in innovative work, and by that example, the institution develops a collective culture of creativity and care.
