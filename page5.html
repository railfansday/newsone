7 Action Shows Better Than 'The Terminal List'

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One of the most dominant genres on television is the action thriller. Action shows are popular because they deliver universal, high-energy entertainment that triggers a natural surge of excitement and escapism. With every network and streaming getting in on the thrilling game, it's opening up a world of endless options that will keep your adrenaline pumping from start to finish. One such series is The Terminal List. Now in its second season, the Prime Video series, based on the Jack Carr novel, follows Chris Pratt as James Reece, a U.S. Navy SEAL who uncovers a military and government conspiracy.

A high-octane show, The Terminal List scratches the itch for most action lovers. But if it’s not quite up to par with your expectations, there are seven action shows that are considered better. From a classic early 2000s thriller to masterful page-to-screen adaptations, the seven titles on this list fulfill the action quota you found in The Terminal List but amplify it in more profound and exciting ways. While it likely comes down to taste, what’s clear is these shows deliver the thrills you desire.

The early 2000s world of action was defined by the signature Fox series 24. The ticking-clock political thriller created by Joel Surnow and Robert Cochran follows counter-terrorism agent Jack Bauer (Kiefer Sutherland) as he races against the clock to stop global threats. Bauer must navigate high-stakes political conspiracies, assassination attempts, nuclear plots, and deep ethical compromises to protect national security. Each season unfolds in a unique "real-time" format, where every one-hour episode represents one hour of a single, chaotic 24-hour day. 24 dazzled viewers for nine seasons, plus a movie and a single-season revival, thanks to its high-pressure, real-time format, relentless narrative momentum, and top-tier action hero lead.

Whether you watched or not, 24 is defined by its iconic digital countdown clock and beep audio signal commercial breaks, building constant anxiety and urgency. From there, Sutherland was in charge. Bauer takes an "ends justify the means" approach: lying, torturing, and sacrificing his personal life to stop catastrophic threats. The show popularized a multi-panel split-screen technique that tracked simultaneous events across different locations and made phone calls or operational command rooms feel dynamic and fast-paced. Tapping into real-world anxieties surrounding terrorism, national security, and presidential politics as it premiered just after 9/11, 24 turned counter-terrorism operations into a high-octane spectacle. While both shows examine drastically different eras of action, 24 has broader stakes compared to the one-man vendetta machine. While The Terminal List prides itself on hyper-specific military hardware and Tier-1 operator accuracy, 24 focuses heavily on psychological pressure, interrogation standoffs, and the physical toll taken on its lead character. 24 pioneered the modern television event, opening doors for shows like The Terminal List.

If there's one show that proves women can be just as incredible in the action genre, it's Lioness. Created by Taylor Sheridan, the espionage thriller, also known as Special Ops: Lioness, follows Joe McNamara (Zoe Saldaña), a CIA station chief who manages a covert military program deploying female undercover operatives to infiltrate international terrorist networks. Throughout their missions, both the handlers and the undercover assets must navigate extreme psychological pressure and intense personal sacrifices. The show centers on these elite operatives as they establish close relationships with high-target individuals to dismantle dangerous organizations from within. The compelling, high-stakes action thriller brings gritty realism, high-voltage tactical sequences, and a deep emotional toll.

Whether tracking targets across international hotspots like the Middle East or Europe, or plunging into intense tactical firefights, the pacing keeps the tension tightly wound. Lioness shines for its practical stunts, realistic gunfights, and grounded military maneuvers that give the combat sequences a heavy, visceral pulse. Saldaña is exceptional, leading an extraordinary ensemble that also features Laysla De Oliveira, Nicole Kidman, Michael Kelly, and Morgan Freeman. With a heavyweight supporting cast, Lioness adds institutional and political gravity to the chain of command. The series interweaves the adrenaline of black-ops intelligence work with the quiet, destabilizing strain it places on family life. If you prefer an ensemble-driven series, Lioness is Sheridan doing what he does best. With a bigger cinematic scale, Lioness has a blockbuster sensibility that moves beyond a straightforward, black-and-white revenge quest.

It's safe to say that Reacher is the official king of modern action television. Based on the Lee Child novel series, Reacher follows Jack Reacher (Alan Ritchson), a massive, highly intelligent former U.S. Army military police investigator who lives as a nomadic drifter. As he travels across the United States with nothing but the clothes on his back, he is continuously pulled into deadly conspiracies and criminal rings. Driven by a strict personal moral code, he uses his formidable physical strength and sharp analytical skills to dismantle corruption and deliver aggressive street justice. Reacher stands at the top of the heap as it leans into old-school pulp thrills, pairs a physically faithful lead with smart investigative instincts, and delivers grounded, visceral set pieces.

Reacher takes the ideals of an action blockbuster and gives it the room to breathe. It lets allies like Oscar Finlay (Malcolm Goodwin), Roscoe Conklin (Willa Fitzgerald), and Frances Neagley (Maria Sten) develop properly as the narrative builds organic tension through a multi-episode mystery. Ritchson carries the weight of the world, matching Child’s literary description of the titular character. He combines an imposing, human-wrecking-ball presence with a deadpan, quiet confidence and sharp dry wit. Ritchson performs many of his own stunts, giving the action a tangible feel. Reacher versus The Terminal List is a battle between pulp fun and grim realism. Ritchson's hulking Sherlock Holmes gives Reacher brilliant depth, while Pratt brings trauma, physical degradation, and a darker, more obsessive mindset. Reacher is a more straightforward drama with street justice, corruption-busting, and self-contained town mysteries, while The Terminal List is a bit denser. If you prefer a classically tinged action adventure, Reacher is most certainly for you.

Based on the Frederick Forsyth novel and 1973 film of the same name, the Peacock iteration of The Day of the Jackal is peak spy thriller. Created by Ronan Bennett, The Day of the Jackal follows an elusive, cold-blooded contract assassin, code name Jackie (Eddie Redmayne), who carries out high-profile hits for astronomical fees. After a high-risk assignment draws the attention of Bianca Pullman (Lashana Lynch), a tenacious British intelligence officer, a destructive, international cat-and-mouse chase ensues across Europe. As both professionals risk their personal lives to outsmart each other, global forces pull strings from the shadows. The series works as a gripping action-thriller because it takes the novel's sharp mechanics and expands them into a high-stakes, modern cat-and-mouse game.

The 10-episode series gives the narrative room to explore how a high-end assassin operates and survives in an era dominated by digital tracking, global intelligence networks, and modern surveillance. It's a tour-de-force acting phenomenon. Redmayne plays the Jackal as an icy, calculating chameleon, going completely against type. He's the antagonist, and yet watching him succeed is half the fun. The show treats his process like an actor's playground, focusing on prosthetic disguises, accent changes, operational craft, and psychological precision, giving Redmayne room to showcase his vast skill set. Lynch matches with more heroic tenacity, obsession, and ruthlessness. Both characters operate in a morally gray world where their professional drives relentlessly clash with and threaten their personal lives. The latter more so in devastating fashion. Filmed across international locations like London, Budapest, Vienna, and Croatia, the series delivers tightly choreographed sniper hits, tense standoffs, and explosive action sequences. The two series are quite different, highlighting a grounded, cat-and-mouse European espionage thriller and a high-octane military-revenge action saga. While The Terminal List focuses on Reece, The Day of the Jackal champions the antagonist. This villain-centric perspective creates a darker, morally ambiguous tone, and sometimes that's a juicier watch. ​​​​​​​

One of the most-watched Netflix series of the 2020s is the political action thriller The Night Agent. Created by Shawn Ryan and based on the novel of the same name by Matthew Quirk, The Night Agent follows Peter Sutherland (Gabriel Basso), a low-level FBI agent working in the White House basement. When an emergency phone he monitors finally rings, he is thrust into a dangerous, high-stakes conspiracy reaching the highest levels of the U.S. government. Alongside a cybersecurity expert named Rose Larkin (Luciane Buchanan), he must race against time to uncover a deadly mole and protect American power. A thoroughly engaging thrill ride, The Night Agent kicks off with an irresistible hook and unrelenting action that never slows down.

The Night Agent is built around tight, bingeworthy episodes; almost every installment ends with a suspenseful twist, new evidence, or a cliffhanger that drives you straight into the next hour. It features practical stunts, intense shootouts, hand-to-hand combat, and nerve-wracking chases without getting bogged down by overly dense or confusing mechanics. The narrative scales from a localized murder mystery into a sprawling political conspiracy threatening the Oval Office and national security, keeping the tension high across its multi-season run. The two shows approach action differently: propulsive, high-concept political paranoia versus gritty, military-authentic revenge. The Night Agent thrives as a pulpy thriller, driven by rapid action and instant gratification. The narrative moves briskly from a basement White House emergency phone line straight into a sprawling government conspiracy. Peter emerges as a beloved hero that you instantly cheer on. The Terminal List tightly centers on a singular, visceral path of personal retribution of a man who isn't as likable as Peter. ​​​​​​​

Even if you know the character from the comics, The Punisher blends Marvel ideals with bone-crunching action into a brazen two-season thriller. The Steve Lightfoot-created series follows former Marine Frank Castle (Jon Bernthal) as he tracks down those responsible for the murder of his family. After avenging his loved ones, Castle uncovers a deep military and government conspiracy that forces him to operate as a brutal, lethal vigilante while teaming up with allies to take out his rivals. The Punisher is a character-driven drama because it grounds its brutal spectacle in raw human grief, moral grey areas, and visceral realism. Unlike traditional comic book shows with mystical enemies or high-tech lasers, the street-level action is gritty, messy, and focused on hand-to-hand combat, gunplay, and sheer endurance.

Frank operates with a brutal, no-compromise efficiency that sets him apart from heroes who rely on mercy or repeated incarceration. Bernthal embodies Frank Castle as a man constantly on edge, consumed by rage, pain, and exhaustion. The story treats Castle's existence as a tragedy, showing the heavy toll of war and loss. What allows the series to stand out is its brilliant approach to pertinent themes revolving around post-traumatic stress disorder, the struggles of returning military veterans, and institutional hypocrisy. Like The Terminal List, this story is about a one-man vengeance machine. The difference is the emotional depth that Bernthal brings compared to Pratt. The Punisher explores the grim reality of unchecked vigilantism, military conspiracies, and the ethics of lethal force within a flawed justice system. By contrast, The Terminal List is more of a straightforward, single-minded anti-hero revenge trip where Reece systematically clears a list of targets. Plus, the first-season dynamic between Bernthal and his The Bear costar, Ebon Moss-Bachrach, is worth the watch.

If there's one character who's emerged as a timeless action hero, it's Jack Ryan. Played by a bevy of stars over the years, the latest iteration has many fans noting it’s the best. In Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan, CIA analyst Jack Ryan (John Krasinski) is pulled from his desk job into high-stakes field operations after discovering suspicious bank transfers. As he tracks a rising terrorist figurehead across Europe and the Middle East, he evolves into a field operative, uncovering global conspiracies and internal corruption. Based on the iconic book series, Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan blends high-stakes geopolitical scope with an everyman hero who relies on brainpower as much as brawn.

Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan weaves through realistic financial tracking, intelligence tradecraft, and moral gray areas, grounding the story in stellar geopolitical narratives. With a massive episodic budget, the series delivers movie-tier action sequences, globetrotting locations and tense tactical set pieces. Krasinski portrays Jack Ryan as a vulnerable, brainy CIA financial analyst who gets dragged out of a desk job and has to learn and adapt on the fly. By giving him an everyman essence, he becomes a highly accessible character. The dynamic, organic bromance and working partnership between Jack Ryan and his mentor/boss James Greer (Wendell Pierce) add levity to the weighty action. If you're seeking cinematic-level action, Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan is for you. It's a much brainier action series that maintains a bit more structural optimism, with Ryan working within and trying to fix or protect larger institutional frameworks even when bad actors infiltrate them. Over its four seasons, Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan offers more variety. While both shows feature two standout sitcom stars, it's Krasinski's transition to an action hero that is more enthralling to watch.

10 Best Sci-Fi Reads Better Than Black Mirror

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Charlie Brooker's Black Mirror is technically science fiction, but it's so much more than that. It shines a light on our society and technology in a way that's disturbingly relatable. Watching it feels uncomfortable because, as unsettling as the stories it portrays are, sometimes they're not too far off from the lives we actually live.

If you've binged the entire show and are still looking for more content with the same vibes, there are many science fiction books that feel like reading a Black Mirror episode. They coat social commentary in a tense and unsettling atmosphere. After reading these, you might come away understanding society a bit better than when you started... for better or worse.

Every day, there's an increasingly large number of mouths to feed. In Tender Is the Flesh by Agustina Bazterrica (available for purchase here), the problem becomes even worse when a vius makes all animal meat poisonous to humans.

The solution that the government in the novel comes up with is raising humans as livestock. The protagonist, Marcos, works at a processing plant for this "special meat." When he's gifted a live woman for his consumption, he starts to warm up to her and begins to do the unthinkable: Treat her like a human being.

We live at a time when human beings are increasingly looking to artificial intelligence for companionship. Annie Bot by Sierra Greer (available for purchase here) gives a fresh take to the concept of the perfect computer-powered girlfriend, which has been seen in media and movies plenty of times.

The story is told from the point of view of Annie Bot, the AI woman who was created to be the perfect companion for Doug. She sees to his every whim, keeps the house clean, and tries to give him what he wants.

But the more human she becomes, the less perfect she gets. Slowly, it becomes apparent that Doug might not really know what he even wants, and that Annie Bot might be able to look beyond her role as companion.

Everyday things have been disappearing in The Memory Police by Yoko Ogawa (available for purchase here). It starts with small things, like hats, roses, birds. These objects don't just vanish from the world, but even from people's memories, like they never even existed in the first place.

The protagonist is a young woman who's one of the few with the power to remember the lost objects. This puts her at risk from the overbearing Memory Police, who exist to make sure that the forgotten remains forgotten, at any cost.

Little by little, more serious things start to disappear with horrible repercussions. All the protagonist can do is watch as life changes completely, while everyone around her remains oblivious that anything wrong is even happening: a situation that would feel right at home on Black Mirror.

When considering how technology interacts with society, it's impossible not to mention the classic Do Androids Dream Of Electric Sheep? by Philip K Dick (available for purchase here). This sci-fi novel inspired the Blade Runner series and remains one of the most beloved sci-fi novels.

The book takes place in a post-apocalyptic world that's shrouded in a cloud of radioactive dust, and most of humanity has escaped to Mars. Rick Deckard remains on Earth with his pet electric sheep to do a job: He's an android killer who tracks down and kills rogue robots for the police.

His latest assignment is to track and destroy six androids on the run from authorities. The only problem is that these androids are very smart, and they look and sound exactly like regular people.

There Is No Antimemetics Division by an author known only as qntm (available for purchase here) is an SCP novel. If you're unfamiliar, the fictional SCP Foundation secures, contains, and protects anomalies and strange supernatural phenomena and entities.

The titular Antimemetics Division is a branch of the SCP that deals with anti-memes, or ideas that can't be remembered or spread. These entities mess with people's minds and prevents them from remembering or sharing information about them, obviously making it extremely difficult to trap or contain them.

The story is told over the course of several seemingly unconnected short stories. Eventually, you realize two things: everything is connected, and there's a horrible threat looming over humanity, but no one can remember that it exists. This is a strange and unsettling read that comes together in a very satisfying way and explores the way human beings think and act, much like an episode of Black Mirror.

Many of the novels by John Marrs are Black Mirror-esque and explore a world where technology and science are just a little too far advanced for its own good. The sci-fi novel The One (available for purchase here) is the first entry in the Dark Future collection, and a great place to start if you're looking for your next realistic dystopian fix.

In The One, a new service has rolled out genetic matchmaking. A new DNA test can match you to your perfect partner, who you're the most genetically compatible with. The book follows five people from all walks of life who find out that being matched to their soul mates might not be all it's cracked up to be.

Blake Crouch is a much more action-focused sci-fi novel than many of the others on this list, but Dark Matter (available for purchase here) still feels very much like a story straight from Black Mirror. Jason Dessen is a physics professor on his way home when he's knocked out by a mysterious person. When he wakes up, he discovers that his life is completely different.

His wife isn't married to him, his son doesn't exist, and his work is a lot more significant than his teaching was. In this world, Jason has done something impossible, but he doesn't want the fame and status. All he wants is to go back home to his wife and son. This sends him on an incredible journey, pursued by an enemy that might be indestructible.

The Test by Sylvain Neuvel (available for purchase here) is a short read, but it's one that will stay on your mind long after you've finished it. Written by one of the contributing writers for Black Mirror, The Test asks what it takes to be a good contributing member of society.

The book's protagonist, Idir, is facing the British Citizenship Test: a brief questionnaire of just 25 multiple-choice questions. It begins simple enough, with a few easy questions. But before long, the testing descends into a dystopian life or death situation that will make you question how you value life and what makes a good citizen.

Exhalation by Ted Chiang (available for purchase here) is an anthology consisting of nine short stories. Each tale has the dreamy, slightly "off" feel of a Black Mirror episode, and scenarios that feel like you get not one but nine whole new episodes.

The collection explores moral and philosophical questions. If you had the option to go back in time and fix some mistakes, would you? How would multiple universes change the way we see free will and choice? These profound and speculative stories will make you reexamine what you think about the world and human nature.

The movie version of Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun—a story about a solar-poweed, child-like companion android—has recently brought the author's dream-like writing to the public eye. Never Let Me Go (available for purchase here) is another story worth checking out for fans of Black Mirror.

In this book, we spend some time learning about Kathy, a 31-year-old woman with fond memories of her exclusive private school. As she remembers her life as a student, the memories are tinged with nostalgia and an air of unease.

There's something very wrong about the school, that has unsettling ramifications for Kathy's future. If you're a fan of the eerie atmosphere of Black Mirror, this one might be just right for you.

Why New Boeing Planes Are Green Before Delivery, But Airbus Aircraft Aren't

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The observation that pre-delivery Boeing aircraft are green, and Airbus aluminum aircraft aren't, is partially true, but it is far from the full picture. There are significant differences in the substances that Boeing and Airbus' various divisions and subcontractors use, and this is partially driven by environmental regulations differing by location. But unfinished Boeing aircraft are also typically photographed at an earlier stage of development compared with Airbus.

After the peelable Temporary Protective Coating (TPC / "Spraylat") is removed, Boeing aircraft look different, but they are rarely photographed at that stage. This is the stage that Airbus aircraft are typically photographed. Importantly, the 'green' conversation requires aluminum aircraft, as composite material structures are treated differently. Here is what to know about Boeing and Airbus coatings.

One of the most distinctive features of new aircraft is the stage they go through when they are in their primary coats of paint. Boeing aircraft are green as they roll out of the assembly facility. The green coating is a corrosion-protective primer applied to the aircraft structure before its final exterior paint scheme. The layer also helps subsequent coatings to adhere to the aircraft. Other aircraft manufacturers such as Airbus, Embraer, ATR, and COMAC use broadly comparable surface-protection coatings, although they are not quite the same.

It is also important to distinguish between the aircraft's three basic stages of coating. Aircraft receive structural primer, intermediate coating, and final decorative topcoats. What looks like a single coat of green paint on Boeing aircraft is actually a combination of two distinct coatings, obviously not the final decorative one. What is seen is typically the Temporary Protective Coating (TPC / "Spraylat") and the Corrosion-Inhibiting Primer (The True Undercoat).

This does not apply to the composite-material-constructed 787 Dreamliner (more on that below). Instead, it is applied to traditional aluminum structures. It is worth noting that while the Boeing 737 is now in its fourth (MAX) generation, its fuselage remains a fundamentally aluminum structure and a 1960s design. Something similar is also true of the heavily updated Boeing 777X soon to enter service. It may come with new wings, but its fuselage remains primarily aluminum.

Boeing's clean-sheet Boeing 787 is its only 'new' aircraft in the narrow sense of being a clean-sheet design able to take full advantage of today's advanced composite materials. The only 'new' Airbus analog is the Airbus A350. The A330neo and A320neo series are upgraded older aluminum fuselage aircraft. The Boeing 787's fuselage and wings are built with carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) rather than aluminum and so do not suffer from galvanic or atmospheric metal corrosion.

Consequently, Dreamliners do not come with green TPC or green chromate primers. As they roll out of the assembly line, they appear white or light-gray/beige, being coated with conductive/UV-protective primer coats. The Dreamliner's primary competitor, the A350, comes with a tan/beige primer coating, which serves a similar function. Sections in white or beige are usually composite materials, which don't corrode and don't need the coating. That's why the composite-bodied 787 looks less green than the aluminum 737.

This is perhaps the most striking on the Boeing 777X. As stated, it retained the 1990s-era aluminum fuselage. Accordingly, the fuselage is painted green in the signature glossy emerald-green Temporary Protective Coating (TPC). Meanwhile, the wings are made of composite and so receive a white or light-gray UV-protective topcoat that matches the Boeing 787. Boeing has also applied a strippable green film to its early-built 777-9s, which have been stored outdoors awaiting certification for years.

A closer look at the paint and production processes of Boeing airliners.

Airbus' aluminum aircraft are similar, but also different. These receive chromate-free exterior primers, corrosion-protective coatings, and decorative topcoats. But while the basic process is the same, they are still chromate-free. Airbus started introducing chromate-free exterior primers on the A380 in 2016, almost a decade after the Superjumbo entered service. While the Superjumbo was a clean-sheet aircraft, it was not a 'new' aircraft in the sense of the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350.

The A380 was built using the traditional aluminum-alloy fuselage. One of the criticisms of the program was that it was a big airplane more than it was a new one. Airbus and Boeing operate in different regulatory environments. The European Union has strict REACH chemical regulations that are phasing out hexavalent chromium more aggressively than in the United States.

In 2025, CIRS reported, "the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) unveiled a proposal to restrict the use of carcinogenic hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) substances across the EU. The measure aims to safeguard human health and the environment from the cancer risks associated with these chemicals." This has pushed Airbus to adopt chromate-free primers faster than Boeing. These typically appear tan, beige, or pale yellow. Airbus generally doesn't use Boeing's glossy, peelable emerald green 'Spraylat' transport film. The structural primer is therefore directly visible.

Another distinctive feature of Airbus aircraft is that when assembled, their coatings are a patchwork of shades. Boeing is a national company; Airbus is a multinational company formed from the merger of major European aerospace companies. It has four home countries (the UK, France, Germany, and Spain). Accordingly, it uses a distributed European manufacturing model.

Barrels, wings, and tails are fabricated, anodized, and primed at different sites and at different subsidiaries across these countries. Each facility applies its own corrosion-inhibiting primer before they are shipped to be assembled. Those primers have slightly different chemistries, resulting in different shades. When the aircraft is pieced together, it looks like something of a patchwork of mismatched greens, yellows, and tans.

Major contractors include GKN Aerospace (Fokker Aerostructures) in the UK and the Netherlands, Latecoere in France, and Spirit AeroSystems. Airbus radomes are often matte gray or tan (made of composite materials), the nose and forward base are pale yellow-green, the center fuselage is slightly darker olive or lime green, the belly fairing is tan or beige, the wings are light gray over green primer, and the aft is olive green aluminum or tan/gray in composite areas.

The XWB-97's maturity is becoming an advantage over the rival new GE9X turbofan, helping the program to progress and be perceived as less risky.

Unlike Airbus, Boeing uses a peelable green coating to protect the aircraft's aluminum skin. The aluminum skin is relatively thin, and even minor scratches and indentations can require inspection. It protects against environmental damage during manufacturing and transport. Like Airbus, Boeing has components manufactured across a broad geological footprint, and these require transportation.

While Airbus transports many of its components on climate-controlled BelugaXL aircraft (and ferries), Boeing relies on the Open-Air Cross-Country Rail Transit for its core 737 aircraft. Those fuselages are built in Wichita, Kansas, and need to be transported 2,000 miles (3,200 km) by open railcar to Renton and Everett. Boeing's highly modified Boeing 747 Dreamlifters are used for the 787, not normally the 737. The train route is primarily operated by BNSF Railway, with the journey going through Nebraska, Wyoming, Montana, to Washington via Spokane.

The journey takes around ten to 12 days, with trains taking three to four fuselages at a time (six maximum). In 2018, Aviation Week reported, "In today’s world on the massive airplane you see going down the rail or you see entering the Renton factory the fuselage is already integrated and they are able to start the same day installing the blankets and wiring and going through the production process without having to do any structural work to the airplane."

Another key factor to consider when comparing Boeing and Airbus during their manufacturing process is that they are typically photographed at different stages of their manufacturing cycle. This creates an apples-to-oranges mismatch. Most familiar photos of green Boeing aircraft show them with the TPC peelable coating, as they are on the railcars or parked at Renton/Everett facilities.

Airbus photos are typically taken when they are being unloaded from a BelugaXL or rolling off the Final Assembly Line. They may be conducting their first customer acceptance flights. This is a later stage than the Boeing aircraft, and they typically sport a raw structural epoxy primer that gives them a flat, chalky, matte finish.

Other factors also explain why Boeing and Airbus aircraft may appear different. Boeing aircraft parked in Washington are frequently pictured in overcast skies, while Airbus aircraft in Toulouse are often in warm Mediterranean sunlight. Even the digital camera may react differently. For example, if a planespotter fills much of the frame with a green fuselage, the camera's Auto White Balance (AWB) may automatically shift the image to magenta and warm tones.

Trump doesn’t plan to raise Nvidia, spy issue during China trip: U.S. officials

President Donald Trump is not expected to raise allegations of a Chinese intelligence operation involving former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang during his upcoming meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping, senior U.S. officials said Friday.

Officials also said that Elon Musk, the CEO of Tesla and SpaceX, will accompany Trump on the trip to Shenzhen, China, for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit on Nov. 18 and 19.

The White House, earlier Friday, said Trump plans to have a three-way summit there with Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

Jensen Huang's relationship with the suspected spy, Alice Huang, who is not related to him, has raised concerns that a Singapore-based company where she was an executive diverted restricted Nvidia chips to China.

"The situation with Nvidia should not really have any bearing on the trip," an official said on the press call, speaking on the condition that they not be identified.

"In all of our discussions and negotiations with the Chinese, we do not negotiate American export controls ... and we don't expect it to figure at all in any of our conversations with the Chinese while we're there."

Asked if Trump would raise the allegations against Alice Huang to Chinese leader Xi Jinping, the official said, "The focus of a meeting with President Xi, at least on the economic side, is going to be about implementation of the trade agreement."

"When it comes to some of these national security matters and examinations and investigations that are going on in the U.S., [that is] probably not something that we're going to read the Chinese in on at this moment," the official said.

Another official said, "I don't expect the president to raise it — the Alice Huang issue."

China has called the allegations against Alice Huang baseless.

The scandal has rocked Nvidia and raised questions about whether the Commerce Department, which is led by Howard Lutnick, has been aggressive enough at blocking advanced chips from ending up in China.

SpaceX launches Starship in key test flight of massive rocket

📅 2026-11-13  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a key test flight on Friday, SpaceX tried but failed to recover both stages of its massive Starship rocket.

The rocket lifted off from the company's South Texas launch facility at 4:47 p.m. local time, or 5:47 p.m. ET. SpaceX had to abort its plans to catch and reuse the booster, instead diverting it to splash into the Gulf of Mexico, where it burst into flames.

Test Flight 15, the most ambitious Starship mission to date, was the first time Elon Musk's company attempted to catch both the booster and upper stage, or spacecraft portion, of the massive rocket.

Earlier on Friday, SpaceX obtained authorization from the Federal Aviation Administration. Hours later, the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop after reporting communication problems affecting "multiple" air traffic control centers. The SpaceX license wasn't effected by the flight stoppage, a spokesperson for the FAA told CNBC.

The upper stage of the Starship continued its trajectory into orbit after the company failed to catch and salvage the booster. The rocket is expected to fly around the Earth fourteen times in total. If successful, SpaceX will land the upper stage back at its Starbase facility on Saturday.

Starship's success is critical to SpaceX and its ambitions to eventually lift cargo and people to the surface of the Moon and Mars. In the near-term, SpaceX needs to send more satellites into orbit to expands its Starlink satellite internet service, which is currently the company's profit center.

The company's constellation already includes over 11,000 active satellites in low Earth orbit. One Starship rocket has the capacity to carry up to 60 Starlink v3 satellites into orbit, the company has said, representing about 20 times the capacity of earlier Falcon 9 rockets.

In the previous Starship test flight, the company released 26 v3 satellites, and released another 26 in the Friday test flight, with the goal of "Starlink becoming 51% of the internet," SpaceX said in a post on X.

When it comes to launches, "SpaceX is likely a full decade ahead of peers on this front," William Blair analysts wrote in a note out on Friday. "Even if tonight's catch is unsuccessful, a delay by a few months is not that consequential."

Meanwhile, SpaceX is facing fresh criticism over restrictions imposed on its Starshield satellite internet service in Ukraine. Starshield is built on Starlink technology and is the military-tier of SpaceX's satellite internet service.

Earlier this week, Ukraine President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said his country's military forces had experienced limits on Starshield without warning again, harming their ability to defend against Russia's ongoing aerial attacks. Similar limits on Starshield in Ukraine were temporarily imposed last month.

Some U.S. senators have condemned the restrictions, and urged Congress to investigate SpaceX.
