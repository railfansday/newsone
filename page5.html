For a family trip of three nights or more, compare a hotel suite, extended-stay room, or rental with a kitchen to a standard room. Add up the kitchen’s extra cost for the whole stay, any cleaning fee included, and book the one with the kitchen if cooking breakfast and a few dinners would save your family more than that.

That extra cost shows up in the nightly rate. The savings show up at every meal your family doesn’t buy at a restaurant.

Say a room with a kitchen costs your family of four $30 a night more than one without. Breakfast out costs $50 and dinner $100, against $15 and $30 for the same meals from a grocery store. Cooking breakfast saves $35 a day, which covers the $30 on its own.

Over five nights, cooking every breakfast and two dinners saves $315, or $165 after the kitchen’s extra $150. On a stay of a night or two, the kitchen often doesn’t pay off, because some costs come once per stay, such as the coffee, oil, and spices from your first grocery run. A rental’s cleaning fee is one of them. A $100 fee adds $50 a night to a two-night stay, more than the $35 a day you save by cooking breakfast, but only $20 a night to a five-night one.

Extended-stay chains put a kitchen in every room. Extended Stay America’s suites all have full kitchens, and the chain discounts longer stays. Candlewood Suites’ studios and suites come with full kitchens too. Some extended-stay chains also serve a free breakfast, so there the kitchen saves you money only on the other meals you cook. Check for a stovetop, since some kitchenettes have only a microwave.

On Airbnb or Vrbo, compare a rental’s total price for your dates with a hotel’s. Under the Federal Trade Commission’s fee rule, that total must include the cleaning fee, as a hotel’s must include any resort fee, though taxes can be added before you pay.

Shop on day one for breakfasts and two or three dinners that share ingredients, such as taco meat one night and burrito bowls the next. Plan a dinner out every day or two so nobody spends the trip at the stove.

If cooking breakfast saves your family more than the kitchen’s extra cost per night, with any cleaning fee divided across your nights, the kitchen pays for itself before you cook a single dinner.

10 Greatest Film Noir Movies of the '80s, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Film noir has been the go-to for compelling entertainment since the Golden Age of Hollywood — back when this genre was first coined in the 1940s and '50s, when stylish black-and-white classics like Double Indemnity, Sunset Boulevard, and The Maltese Falcon captivated audiences with their blend of crime and drama, becoming timeless staples of the genre for decades. Today, it's still a current filmmaking style that has a huge impact on pop culture.

There's no shortage of compelling film noir classics throughout the decade. Many, as mentioned before, are from the early years of its inception. But one decade has stood out spectacularly for reinventing the genre and incorporating its thrilling potential into the creative mix of the time period. That was the glorious 1980s, a decade marked by cinematic excellence, where blockbusters ruled, and it was just an absolute blast to be a movie buff. The 10 below define the 1980s with their unique use of film noir mixed with their equally complex stories, proving that this was a time marked by this genre.

Best described as a rock-and-roll action roller-coaster ride of 1980s awesomeness, Walter Hill's cult classic Streets of Fire takes neo-noir filmmaking to new extremes. This kick-ass flick just screams the '80s, featuring a thrilling soundtrack and a unique, rebellious style that perfectly encapsulates the time period. Michael Paré, Diane Lane, and Willem Dafoe lead in this memorable tale of a dangerous ex-soldier on a mission to rescue his singer girlfriend kidnapped by a biker gang and taken somewhere in a dystopian city.

Though a box office flop and critically mixed upon initial reception, Streets of Fire has garnered a cult following that many other '80s films could only dream of. It's stylish, one-of-a-kind, and above all, very entertaining, featuring a perfect mix of action, sci-fi, and film noir to provide viewers with an unforgettable experience. This embodies the youthful spirit and angst of '80s culture and tries to be nothing else.

Hitting all the right notes to make one of the more fascinating and memorable neo-noir classics of the decade, Peter Wier's 1985 film Witness is a must-watch for audiences due to how motivating and emotional its story beats are. Acting icon Harrison Ford steals the show as always, this time in, to date, his only Oscar-nominated performance as a Philadelphia police detective who infiltrates an Amish community to watch over a young boy who witnessed an undercover cop's murder.

The film is perfectly shot, written, and notably acted, as well as tightly paced, providing decent thrills, a compelling mystery, and enough drama and heart to satisfy just about anyone. Witness was a sleeper hit at the box office, racking in an impressive number, and, of course, was a hit with critics, citing its excellent blend of film noir and drama genres, and for its mature storytelling.

A darkly erotic, one-of-a-kind nailbiter, Brian De Palma's cult classic Body Double became one of the most original entries in the film noir genre to come out of the '80s. Though there's a heavy emphasis on shocking and risqué storytelling, it's much in line with a classic Alfred Hitchcock film, following a voyeuristic young man who's pulled into a bizarre murder conspiracy after spying on a beautiful neighbor one night outside an apartment window.

This has the makings of a classic suspenseful set-up, much like Hitchcock's Rear Window, which further explores the themes of voyeuristic obsession as this film does. Except, here the suspense is much more tense, the shock and violence really crank things up a notch, and the unique '80s style really sets it apart and gives it a charm. Body Double offers an edge-of-your-seat experience with great thrills and mysterious presence, making it one of the more fascinating film noir classics that perfectly represent this decade.

Featuring the triumphant feature-film debut of Academy Award-winning filmmakers Joel and Ethan Coen, as well as cinematographer and future director Barry Sonnenfeld and Oscar-winning actress Francis McDormand, the 1984 neo-noir crime thriller Blood Simple is quite simply one of the most underappreciated but masterful cult favorites to enter the decade. McDormand shone, showcasing what she would later give to more prominent roles in a twisted story of romance and murder as a scorned saloon owner hires a private investigator to kill his wife and the bartender she's been cheating with.

This unique, stylish thriller not only showcased the talent of its up-and-coming stars but also became a slow-growing fan favorite among movie buffs over the years for its tense pacing and great concept. It perfectly fits into the genre, complete with a decent mix of crime and drama to grip viewers and disturb them with its creeping suspense. Not to mention, it's a Coen film, which, of course, features their signature filmmaking style, which is visually stellar and hard not to appreciate. Overall, it's a must-watch that would delight any film noir fan.

Based on the legendary Thomas Harris novel Red Dragon, 1986's Manhunter was the first compelling look into the exciting yet terrifying world of the Hannibal Lecter series before becoming more mainstream when Jonathan Demme's The Silence of the Lambs shocked the world. Directed by Michael Mann (yes, the man behind Heat), it delivers a tightly paced, slow-building thriller which sees an ace FBI profiler, Will Graham (William Petersen), as he asks the infamous Dr. Lecter (Brian Cox) to assist in his hunt to catch the elusive Tooth Fairy Killer (Tom Noonan).

Though it's a whole different beast compared to The Silence of the Lambs, Manhunter is a perfectly made and epically gripping classic in its own right. The performances are near flawless, especially the late Tom Noonan as the killer, who can easily give audiences the creeps even now. Not to mention, its mystery and suspense are well-paced and build to a satisfying payoff, which has steadily made this film such a rewatchable and adored cult classic that's gotten more appreciation over the years.

Packing a powerful mix of neo-noir storytelling and tense suspense, William Friedkin's 1985 classic To Live and Die in L.A. provides cinematic excitement, which was what the '80s were all about. William Petersen brings his services again to the genre in this action spectacular, which sees him as a vengeful U.S. Secret Service Agent who crosses the line to eliminate the counterfeit artist (Willem Dafoe) who killed his partner.

From its uniquely '80s style, the kick-ass Wang Chung soundtrack to the heavy, awesome fight sequences and tense chases, To Live and Die in L.A. is an absolute blast of entertainment, one that's not just one of the best of the film noir genre, but just one of the most exciting features of the 1980s, period. It instantly hooks you in, never letting go for even a moment, and fascinates you with its tale of revenge and justice.

Truly one of the most remarkable, bold, and sexiest noir thrillers to come out of the '80s was 1981's Body Heat, a film directed by Lawrence Kasdan and starring the late William Hurt and Kathleen Turner. A neat, twisted little spin on the genre, which in itself is an homage to 1944's Double Indemnity, it follows a naive lawyer manipulated into a murder plot by a seductive femme fatale who has a few tricks up her sleeves.

It's tensely paced, perfectly acted, competently shot, and doesn't lose your attention for a second. Body Heat is a highly rewatchable masterwork that pays tribute to the classic film noir genre, as well as standing in its own right, telling a dark and alluring crime tale that has to be experienced. Just a few minutes of this classic can instantly hook you in.

Michael Mann returns, this time with arguably one of his greatest, most captivating works, Thief, a 1981 neo-noir thriller starring the late James Caan. It follows Caan as a professional thief who is on the path to turning his life around, but his pursuit of this dream becomes far more difficult as he desperately tries to get one last big score and deals with his old associates who are unwilling to let him leave his life of crime.

A critical and commercial success, Thief was instrumental in keeping film noir hot going into the 1980s. Today, it's regarded as one of the genre's most recognizable cult favorites. From Caan's nuanced lead performance as Frank to Mann's remarkable filmmaking talents, to the overall thrilling '80s feel of the film, it's an absolute delight that keeps getting better with age.

The one and only David Lynch fascinated countless fans with his unusual charm and one-of-a-kind storytelling. The film that arguably showed the greatest potential of his talents outside of Twin Peaks was 1986's Blue Velvet, a favorite among many Lynch enthusiasts for its captivating mystery and bizarre tone. Kyle MacLachlan stars as a curious college boy who returns to his old town and is quickly dragged into the town's dark criminal underworld after finding a severed human ear in a field.

It's certainly strange, off, and borderline nonsensical at a few points, but its mystery plot is straight to the point and undeniably fascinating. That's the best way to describe Lynch's masterpiece. Blue Velvet incorporates all his best attributes, and his story is a complement as well as something new to the film noir genre, leading it to become one of the best films of the year.

But truly, at the end of it all, there was no other place for Ridley Scott's 1982 sci-fi and noir masterpiece Blade Runner than at number one. It's honestly hailed as one of the most significant movies in cinematic history, a near-perfect film with flawless visuals, competent performances, and thought-provoking themes. Harrison Ford shines once again as a former cop turned detective-for-hire who is tasked with finding and killing a group of rogue androids who have rebelled against their creator.

This is a film that's still widely talked about and appreciated today for its brilliance and complex storytelling. There's no denying Blade Runner's impact on film noir. It not only breathed new life into the genre but redefined it in every way. This continues to influence the current genre, and rightfully takes this final spot as the film that most defines the film noir genre in the 1980s.

Ronald Reagan said you can achieve anything—if you don’t care who gets the credit. Abdul El-Sayed channels that wisdom with ‘TrumpCare for all’

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Democratic legislative agenda is taking shape, and healthcare is at the top as rising medical expenses were a major driver of midterm election messaging about affordability.

But while Democrats will hold commanding majorities in the House and Senate when the new Congress convenes in January, President Donald Trump has reminded them that he is ready to veto legislation he doesn’t like.

Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed—a former Detroit health official who campaigned on Medicare for all—sees a way around that by appealing to Trump’s ego. On Nov. 12, he posted to social media a picture of a notional, gold-colored “TrumpCare” card, saying “Donald, this is on the table. Guarantee healthcare for every American, from cradle to grave.”

The next day, El-Sayed doubled down, assuring CNN in an interview that he wasn’t joking and insisting that he doesn’t care what the public insurance program called.

“What you call it is secondary,” he added. “What it does in your life, that’s the whole point. None of us ran to name things, except for Donald Trump. Most of us ran to actually solve problems in people’s lives.”

That got Trump’s attention. And on Saturday, he reposted on his Truth Social account El-Sayed’s initial Nov. 12 appeal. El-Sayed posted “Let’s get to work, then.”

The gambit echoes a famous line from President Ronald Reagan in February 1988, when he spoke at a meeting of the White House Conference for a Drug Free America:

“On my desk in the Oval Office, I have a little sign that says: There is no limit to what a man can do or where he can go if he doesn’t mind who gets the credit.”

While similar statements have been attributed to others going back more than 100 years, Reagan’s line is notable as it came during the modern era of American politics, which has grown even more intensely partisan since then.

In fact, during the midterm election campaign, Trump ramped up the partisan rancor to such a level that he called El-Sayed a “jihadist” who was “on the side of the terrorists.”

But the incoming Democratic senator is ready to set that aside for the sake of expanding health coverage. On Sunday, El-Sayed noted Trump “signaled openness to the opportunity,” telling NBC’s Meet the Press with Kristen Welker that while growing up playing lacrosse, he was taught to play the ball, not the man.

“If the president is serious about leaving a lasting legacy, there is nothing better than the ultimate Trump card, a card that they can have in their back pocket from the moment they are born to the moment that they pass away that guarantees them the health care that has been so elusive in this country without premiums, co-pays, and deductibles,” he said.

El-Sayed also predicted that if Trump says he wants TrumpCare for all and is open to signing legislation, then a lot of Republicans in Congress will vote for it.

When asked later if he has any plans to meet with Trump or Vice President JD Vance, El-Sayed replied “those plans are in the works.”

Minnesota Senator-Elect Peggy Flanagan, another Democrat who rode the blue tsunami, voiced similar willingness to capitalize on Trump’s desire to name things after himself.

“Call it whatever you want. I think people are ready for Medicare for all. If we have to call it TrumpCare in order to get it across the finish line, that’s fine by me,” she told CNN on Sunday.

The discussion over health coverage comes as open enrollment season is in full swing across the country, with workers getting sticker shock on how much premiums are going up for next year.

But Americans who get health insurance through their employers aren’t the only ones feeling the pain. The cost of coverage via Affordable Care Act plans is surging too after Congress didn’t renew the enhanced subsidies that were enacted during the pandemic.

If Trump makes a deal with Democrats to expand health coverage, it would be a 180 after he tried unsuccessfully to kill the Affordable Care Act during his first term.

High-end genealogy travel is booming, offering luxe journeys to the roots of your family tree

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The most exclusive itinerary is written in your DNA.

My great-great-grandfather came to the Tuscan ghost island of Pianosa as a prisoner. I arrived there on a luxury vacation.

The island lives up to its name, I thought as my ferry drifted toward the sole dock on this protected isola fantasma. “Pianosa” is derived from piano, Italian for “flat,” and indeed the island crouches low to the cyan Tyrrhenian Sea, its shores fringed by parasol pines and deserted prison watchtowers. From 1856 to 1998, Pianosa was a remote penal colony—and the place where my ancestor, Salvatore Giovanni Pellegrino Irace, died.

I’d learned this family lore thanks to a partnership between Ancestry, the Blackstone-owned leader in the at-home DNA-testing space, and Kensington, a bespoke travel outfitter, which invited me to test-drive its Personal Heritage Journey. After I submitted a DNA sample, Ancestry got to work filling in my Italian family tree, with researchers combing archives in Tuscany, Molise, and Campania. “So the interesting thing about Salvatore,” genealogist Kyle Betit said on our initial call, “is that he died in prison of tuberculosis in 1894—and we don’t know why he was there.”

Who doesn’t love a good family mystery? That’s part of what draws clients to Kensington’s genealogical-based trips. The journeys average $20,000 to $25,000 per person, with the company’s travel desk handling all the details in high style.

For me, that meant a chartered boat from mainland Tuscany to the island of Elba; chauffeured SUVs between regions; a private guide in Pompeii; and some spectacular accommodations, including Casa Angelina and Palazzo Avino on the Amalfi Coast. The major flex, though, was having a professional genealogist, Gabriele Musumeci, riding shotgun.

Once upon a time, possessing one’s comprehensive genealogy was a signal of wealth and pedigree. Then, in the 2000s, the rise of at-home DNA-testing kits from Ancestry and 23andMe made such knowledge commonplace. The ultrawealthy can now seek out deeper stories through first-person ancestry tourism, from China to Ghana. Kensington reports that demand has doubled and revenue has quadrupled in five years. Grand View Research put the global value of “heritage tourism”—of which ancestral travel is a segment—at $625 billion in 2025. It’s expected to reach almost a trillion by 2033.

“I think there’s a deep need in any of us to understand who we are,” Musumeci said as we descended the concrete steps to Pianosa’s white-sand beach, Cala Giovanna. “For most Americans, your story is cut.” He made a chopping motion. And then a sewing motion: “We stitch it back together.”

Most luxury travel planners now offer some variety of ancestry service. Alexandrea Padilha, director at the membership-based Fischer Unlimited, recently hired genealogists in Germany and the U.S. to trace a Louisiana tech entrepreneur’s ancestry. The results were bound into a book for the client’s father’s 80th birthday and also informed the itinerary for a trip to Germany—a way to show younger family members their roots. “I think that’s grounding for young adults today,” Padilha says, “living in a world that’s slowly slipping away.”

Existential dread is one hell of a motivator. “When it feels like the world may be shifting every second, people reach for something enduring,” says Dr. Katie Blake, a Texas-based social psychologist who specializes in travel. “The archives give you the facts, but it’s the place itself that gives you the identity pull that’s different than something you just see on paper.”

A sudden, biblical rainstorm broke open during our mile-long bike ride from Cala Giovanna to Pianosa’s imposing Agrippa Prison. We sheltered inside the Zante wing, where prisoners like Salvatore slept four to a concrete cell on metal beds, as thunder rattled the rusty gates.

My itinerary traced Salvatore’s life backward. A few days later, Musumeci and I rendezvoused in Molise, where Salvatore grew up. My great-great-great-grandfather, Vincenzo, moved his family to Montenero di Bisaccia, a mountain keep surrounded by vineyards and wheat fields sloping down to the Adriatic, in 1838, when Salvatore was a boy. At Montenero’s city hall, the clerks hauled out massive, crimson-bound books, and Musumeci leafed through until he found the birth and marriage records of Salvatore’s second son, Giuseppe—my great-grandfather.

Walking through Montenero’s hilltop puzzle of alleys brought those pieces of paper to life—and conjured a story of downward mobility. Along the town’s outer promenade, ringing the remains of medieval stone walls, a lookout faced the amber foothills where Vincenzo bought land and a farmhouse. I walked the narrow alley the family lived on after they lost the farm. Within four years, Vincenzo had spiraled from merchant and farm owner to street sweeper. He died in 1842, leaving his son on the poverty-to-prison pipeline.

I also saw the house in the old ghetto where my great-grandmother, Nicoletta, grew up. She and Giuseppe had a daughter out of wedlock, Mattia. (Nobody in my family had known Mattia existed until Musumeci discovered her.) The infant likely died during the couple’s 1900 journey to America, where, at Ellis Island, Irace became Erace. Giuseppe and Nicoletta settled in Philadelphia and had 16—yes, 16—more children, including my paternal grandfather, Michael Erace, who died in 2013.

It was like I was looking at Grandpop Mike’s long-lost brother when we met Bruno Irace, a 74-year-old Ironman triathlete and my fourth cousin, that afternoon. Smile, hair, complexion, bone structure; it was uncanny. We sipped espresso and compared photos, then visited a cemetery where “Irace” seemed to be engraved on every other gravestone. Two Iraces were listed on an obelisk in the village park honoring fallen soldiers. When it was time to leave Montenero, Bruno told us to come back soon. “You have family here,” he said.

The name Irace was also everywhere in the archives of San Luca, the family’s neighborhood church, in Praiano, on the Amalfi Coast, the final stop on my journey. This dramatic shoreline of improbable cliff-top villages and citrus groves set high above the sapphire sea is one of the most beautiful places in the world. I couldn’t help but wonder about the Iraces: Why did these fools leave? What misfortune befell my reasonably prosperous ancestors to make them abscond from this stunning seaside real estate?

Musumeci had coordinated with the pastor, who granted us free rein of his office, a charmingly cluttered room with sea views through diamond-paned windows. Hundreds of books, some dating to the 1600s, filled a tall cabinet, their yellowed pages brittle as autumn leaves. Handwritten in meticulous script were the births and baptisms, marriages and deaths of San Luca’s parishioners, which included generations of Iraces.

“Oh, wow … ” Musumeci stopped suddenly as his finger moved across a page. He pointed to Vincenzo’s marriage record. “Adam, the date … ” October 10, 1807.

It took a few seconds to dawn on me. I checked my phone: October 10, 2026. Exactly 219 years earlier, Vincenzo Irace and Mariangela Rispoli, ages 21 and 18, had stood at San Luca’s resplendent altar and exchanged vows. Until then, I’d encountered Vincenzo mostly through his losses: his farm, his livelihood, his family’s good name. Here was a record of a beginning. Whatever came later, my great-great-great-grandparents had first been two young people at the altar of a church above the sea.

I hadn’t found all the answers in Italy. We never learned why they left the Amalfi Coast, what sent their son to prison, or what compelled their grandson to cross an ocean. The private guides, chauffeured SUVs, and beautiful hotels couldn’t make the records yield answers they didn’t contain.

But the distance between my ancestors’ lives and my own no longer seemed quite so great. Standing in San Luca, I wasn’t the discerning traveler whose preferences shaped the itinerary. For all the choices I’d made on this trip, the ones that mattered most had been made two centuries earlier.

For once, the point wasn’t being made to feel important. The real privilege was the opportunity to feel very, very small.
