A Used Cybertruck Is Selling A Month Faster Than A Ford F-150

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you had to guess which used pickup found a buyer fastest in October, the Toyota Tacoma would be a sensible bet. It’s America’s best-selling midsize truck and has a reputation for holding its value. Or you might pick the super frugal Ford Maverick Hybrid. But the truck that beat them both was the Tesla Cybertruck.

The data from the new iSeeCars study covers 1.1 million used vehicles that changed hands in October, so this was before Tesla announced it would discontinue the Cybertruck in 2027. The study looked at one- to five-year-old vehicles and ranked models by how long they’d been listed before selling.

The Cybertruck surpassed all other pickups, averaging 39.1 days, compared to 65.5 days for used pickups overall. The Maverick Hybrid came closest at 43.3 days, followed by the Rivian R1T at 45.2 and the F-150 Lightning at 47.4. The Tacoma came in fifth position.

The gap between the two electric pickups at either end of the list was even more striking. A used Chevrolet Silverado EV took 107.5 days to sell, putting it last among the 24 trucks in the study. That’s a 68-day difference between two American electric pickups. Across the wider used market, EVs averaged about 46 days to find a buyer, around nine fewer than hybrids and 15 fewer than gas vehicles.

How quickly a used Cybertruck sold seems to have changed a lot since May 2024, when some sellers were cutting as much as $30,000 from their asking prices after listing their trucks for close to $200,000. By December of that year, we reported that used Cybertrucks were piling up on dealer lots, with data at the time showing they stayed for 75 days, up from 27 in May.

Then owners got a look at what Tesla itself would pay for a used Cybertruck trade-in. One year later, in May 2025, one owner of a $99,990 dual-motor Foundation Series truck got a trade-in estimate of $65,400 after driving it just 6,211 miles.

So even though the Cybertruck was the fastest-selling used pickup in iSeeCars’ new study, it didn’t report what buyers paid, so we don’t know whether owners got their asking price or had to lower it to sell.

Tesla will build the last Cybertrucks at the end of summer 2027, and this will reshape their used market. Elon Musk originally announced production would end around the middle of the year, but then extended the run to fill more orders. And if you order a Cybertruck now, the only one you can get is the $110,000 tri-motor Cyberbeast, so anyone who wants a less expensive dual-motor truck has to look for a used example.

Once the last new Cybertruck is sold, I’d expect collectors and everyday truck buyers to look at used ones differently. Someone after a clean Foundation Series truck with low miles might pay a little extra for one close to home, especially since it could be months before another comes up for sale nearby.

At the same time, someone who just needs an electric pickup to tow a trailer has more options. If a used dual-motor Cybertruck costs about the same as a Ford F-150 Lightning or a Rivian R1T, I suspect that buyer will care more about mileage, condition, and how much battery warranty is left than when Tesla built its last truck.

Is Anthropic Joining the S&P 500? Here's What Investors Need to Know.

Anthropic (ANT +1.08%) just completed the largest IPO in history, raising around $93.5 billion at $83 per share. The stock closed Nov. 20 at $88.91.

Given that it's now one of the largest publicly traded companies in the world, you might assume its addition to the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) is imminent. It isn't, actually, and won't be for a while. But the stock will show up in several other major indexes within days.

When Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.30%) debuted earlier this year, there was a lot of discussion around how the major indexes would handle it. Typically, companies go public at much smaller market caps, not with market caps over $1 trillion. Index committees needed to decide whether to "fast track" these mega IPOs into the indexes sooner, given their outsize influence.

Several providers made changes. But Standard & Poor's did not. It elected to keep the existing 12-month waiting period and generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) income requirements -- companies need to be profitable in the most recent quarter as well as the sum of the past four quarters in order to qualify.

Anthropic, so far, falls far short on the GAAP income requirement, reporting a net loss of roughly $104 billion in the first nine months of this year.

Given these requirements, the earliest we're likely to see Anthropic in the S&P 500 is late 2027.

The S&P 500 will actually be the exception, not the rule, when it comes to adding Anthropic.

The Nasdaq-100 index updated its inclusion criteria as a result of the SpaceX IPO. Now, if a company's entire market capitalization ranks within the top 40 current constituents in the index, it can be added to the index after just 15 trading days. Since Anthropic is listed on the Nasdaq, it could show up in the Invesco QQQ ETF (QQQ -0.65%) before the end of the year.

The Morningstar indexes, which acquired the widely-used CRSP indexes, could add Anthropic after the close of the fifth trading day. The Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -0.18%) would be one of the biggest funds impacted by this. FTSE Russell works on a similar timeline.

A lot of index funds will weight stocks according to the number of publicly available shares, not necessarily the total market cap. Since only about 5% of Anthropic's total market cap is now traded publicly, its starting weight will likely be small. Vanguard has projected only a 0.055% to 0.15% weighting for Anthropic in the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF.

When and if additional shares become publicly available, the anticipated weighting of these stocks would likely grow along with it.

If you really want to own Anthropic, you should probably just buy Anthropic.

The stock won't show up at all in the S&P 500 for the foreseeable future. For many of the major large-cap indexes and corresponding ETFs, the initial weight probably won't get much above 0.25%. The exception might be the Nasdaq-100, the Invesco QQQ ETF, and the Invesco Nasdaq 100 ETF (QQQM -0.66%). They could see an allocation closer to 1% given the more concentrated nature of that index.

It'll likely be a similar story to what we saw when SpaceX went public: a lot of hype but minimal impact to the ETF marketplace and your portfolio in general.

Long-term investors almost certainly shouldn't be making big asset allocation changes based on this. Even if you want to overweight tech based on a belief that Anthropic might spark the sector, your tech ETFs aren't going to meaningfully change in the future.

Three Ancient Cosmic Collisions May Explain Why the Moon Has Two Faces

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have narrowed down when three massive collisions reshaped the Moon, bringing them closer to understanding why its Earth-facing side looks so different from its far side.

Billions of years ago, a collision powerful enough to melt enormous quantities of lunar rock created the Moon’s largest impact basin. Subsequent collisions broke apart and scattered some of that ancient material across the surface, leaving behind a geological record that scientists are now piecing together.

Located on the Moon’s far side, the South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin is one of the oldest and largest impact structures in the solar system. Understanding its history could help explain why the Moon’s near and far sides are so different, what lies beneath its surface, and when major collisions occurred during its early evolution.

An international research team led by Professor Guochun Zhao, Mok Sau-King, a professor in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (DEPS) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU), and Dr. Yuqi Qian, the study’s first author, has identified two types of magnesium-rich rocks with remarkably different origins. Published in Science Advances, the research also establishes a clearer timeline of the major impacts that shaped the region.

For decades, the basin remained largely unexplored. That changed in 2019, when Chang’e-4 completed the first successful landing on the Moon’s far side, followed in 2024 by Chang’e-6, which brought the first rock samples from this largely unexplored region back to Earth.

Using Chang’e-6 samples allocated to HKU DEPS and surface measurements from both missions, the researchers obtained the first direct geological evidence from the two landing sites for reconstructing the basin’s impact history. Satellite observations and numerical simulations helped them trace the origins of rocks across the wider basin.

One group of rocks, known as moderately magnesium-rich norites, formed in the aftermath of the original SPA collision. The impact generated a thick sheet of molten rock that gradually cooled, with different minerals crystallizing at different stages. Later collisions exposed these ancient rocks at the central peaks of large craters. Some were eventually blasted toward the Chang’e-4 landing site in Von Kármán crater by the impact that formed Finsen crater.

The second group contained considerably more magnesium and likely formed through a separate process called magnesium-suite magmatism, a type of magma activity associated with the original SPA impact. These rocks were exposed along the western ring of mountain peaks within the Apollo basin before the Chaffee S impact scattered some of them toward the Chang’e-6 landing site.

Earlier research by the HKU-led team had already uncovered evidence of prolonged volcanic activity and previously unrecognized magma activity in the region. Those studies also suggested that underground accumulations of titanium-rich minerals, known as ilmenite-bearing cumulates, helped account for the contrasting volcanic histories of the Moon’s two sides.

The latest findings suggest that the South Pole-Aitken basin formed approximately 4.34 billion years ago. Another major collision created the Apollo basin around 4.2 billion years ago, followed by the formation of the Orientale basin approximately 3.87 billion years ago. Together, these three events reveal how successive collisions reshaped the Moon’s largest impact basin over hundreds of millions of years.

Reference: “Postimpact evolution of the South Pole–Aitken basin revealed by Chang’e-4 and Chang’e-6” by Yuqi Qian, Wenlei Song, James Head, Mingyu Tian, Zhuang Guo, Harald Hiesinger, Carolyn H. van der Bogert, Le Zhang, Jingyou Chen, Wanfeng Zhang, Ziqing Li, Lingzhi Sun, Xiangsong Wang, Wei-Ran Li, Jiacheng Liu, Nicolas Dauphas, Yi-Gang Xu and Guochun Zhao, 18 November 2026, Science Advances.
