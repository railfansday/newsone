Free cash flow is calculated as net cash provided by or used in operating activities less purchases of property and equipment and the capitalization of software development costs (collectively referred to as capital expenditures). This press release also includes key performance indicators, including annual recurring revenue and dollar-based retention rate.

Annual recurring revenue ("ARR") is defined as the value of subscription-based customer agreements encompassing term-based on-premise software and cloud-based software, including those offerings delivered under a recurring consumption-based model, that are in effect at the end of the reporting period. For subscription-based agreements, ARR is calculated as the total contract value of the agreement divided by the length of the agreement, measured as of the end of the period and assuming no increases or reductions to the customer's subscription. For consumption-based cloud software subscriptions, ARR is calculated as the total contract value of the subscription divided by the length of the agreement, assuming no increases or reductions in the customer's usage rate. For agreements with a term of less than 12 months, ARR is calculated as the total contract value of the agreement, without annualization. The annualized value of contracts is a legal and contractual determination made by assessing the contractual terms with our customers, including contracts for which we are actively negotiating a subscription renewal, which continue to be included in ARR until the customer notifies the Company that it does not intend to renew. ARR is not a forecast of future subscription revenue, which can be impacted by contract start and end dates and renewal rates.

Dollar-based net retention rate ("NRR") is calculated by dividing customer recurring revenue by base revenue. We define base revenue as recurring revenue we recognized from all customers with a valid license at the last quarter of the previous year period, during the four quarters ended one year prior to the date of measurement. We define our customer revenue as the recurring revenue we recognized during the four quarters ended on the date of measurement from the same customer base included in our measure of base revenue, including recurring revenue resulting from additional sales to those customers.

Criteo Announces Inclusion in MSCI Global Investable Market Indexes

First-time MSCI inclusion marks a new capital markets milestone following redomiciliation to Luxembourg

NEW YORK, Nov. 12, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Criteo (NASDAQ: CRTO) ("Criteo" or the "Company"), the global commerce intelligence platform, today announced its inclusion in the MSCI Global Investable Market Indexes. As announced by MSCI, the inclusion will take effect as of the close of November 30, 2026.

Following the completion of the Company's redomiciliation to Luxembourg, this inclusion marks a significant capital markets milestone for Criteo and strengthens its public-market profile. Within the broader MSCI Global Investable Market Indexes series, Criteo is expected to be included in several global and U.S. indexes. This is expected to increase Criteo's visibility within the institutional investment community and create an opportunity to broaden its shareholder base over time.

"Criteo's first inclusion in MSCI indexes represents a significant step in our evolution as a public company, and we believe an early benefit of our redomiciliation process," said Connor McGogney, Chief Financial Officer of Criteo. "We remain focused on disciplined execution and advancing our global commerce intelligence platform strategy to drive long-term shareholder value."

MSCI is a global index leader, providing indexes widely tracked by global institutional investors and passive funds as investment benchmarks. Following Criteo's redomiciliation to Luxembourg, the subsequent transfer of its legal domicile from Luxembourg to the U.S. is expected to be completed on January 1, 2027, subject to shareholder approval and other customary conditions.

Worker.ai acquires DeltaGen, launches with backing from billionaire Veeam founder Ratmir Timashev

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The acquisition brings DeltaGen's real-time AI technology to Worker's operating system for small businesses, as Timashev bets on AI to transform Main Street.

RALEIGH, N.C., Nov. 12, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Worker.ai today announced the acquisition of Techstars-backed DeltaGen and launched its company operating system for small businesses, with backing from Veeam founder Ratmir Timashev. Founded by former private equity operator John Roberts and DeltaGen founder Rene Bystron, Worker is targeting a painful limit on small-business growth: the owner's capacity to keep everything moving.

"You shouldn't have to sell your company to get your life back," said John Roberts, co-founder and CEO of Worker.

"You shouldn't have to sell your company to get your life back," said Roberts, co-founder and CEO of Worker. "In private equity, I saw firsthand how a strong operating system helped companies grow faster. We're using AI to put those systems and that support within reach of small teams. Owners should be able to build a bigger business without sacrificing control of the company or having to sell to an external firm."

For too many owners, growth comes with a catch. Every new customer adds decisions, exceptions, and follow-ups that eventually land back on the owner's desk. The business gets bigger. Their life gets smaller.

Timashev is backing Worker to help small businesses, such as manufacturers, service companies, and family businesses, scale and succeed. SMBs drive 43.5% of U.S. GDP and employ almost 46% of private sector workers, so investing in their success drives broader economic growth.

"Building Veeam showed me the opportunity to bring powerful technology to businesses of all sizes," said Timashev. "AI can now give small businesses more capacity without asking already stretched owners to work longer days. Worker is my bet on that shift."

Worker brings a company's plan, responsibilities, key numbers, and processes into one operating system, where employees and AI workers keep work moving. Teams can delegate recurring work, including preparing updates and following up on commitments, while keeping business decisions in human hands.

The DeltaGen acquisition brings standardization and scalability to critical business decision-making. Its real-time voice technology captures decisions, commitments and next steps as people talk. Worker turns those commitments into assigned work. When a team agrees to send a proposal after pricing is approved, for example, an AI worker drafts it and requests approval. The responsible employee approves the pricing and sends the proposal.

For Worker user Nikita Zhitov, owner of CityPlat, the difference shows up outside the office.

"I used to hesitate before taking on another project because I knew it would mean sacrificing evenings and weekends with my family," said Zhitov. "With Worker, I don't have to be in the middle of every decision to know the work is moving. That makes me more confident saying yes. And when I'm with my kids, I'm actually with my kids."

Bystron, Worker's co-founder and COO, founded DeltaGen with Michal Studnicka and Matus Ondreicka. His interest in the problem began much earlier.

"My dad ran a small construction firm. He kept working harder, but the business wasn't always growing," said Bystron. "The internet was supposed to make running a business easier. For him, it added more demands to a day that was already full. AI will have failed people like my dad if all it does is give them more work to keep up with. We're building Worker so they can grow a business and still come home with time and energy for the people they love."

Worker is a company operating system that helps small businesses grow without taking over the owner's life. Plans, responsibilities, key numbers and processes live in one shared place, where employees and AI workers work together to keep the business running. Learn more at worker.ai.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang set to be fired, with Johny Srouji listed among possible replacements

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Following reports of a potential second affair – this time with a subordinate – Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang looks certain to be fired by the company’s board, probably later today (though he may be allowed to ‘resign’).

Apple’s chief hardware officer Johny Srouji has been listed among the potential replacements, though this seems unlikely …

For anyone who’s been away from the planet for the past fortnight, Huang’s affair with a woman suspected to be a Chinese spy has been the main headline news in the tech press since it came to light at the end of last month. The New York Times broke the story on October 29th, citing it then as “a close relationship.” Nvidia initially denied that the relationship was anything out of the ordinary.

That changed on November 8th when Huang publicly admitted to the affair. Nvidia’s shares dropped 5%, and the company’s board of directors opened an independent investigation. Further details emerged over the following days.

Things got even worse yesterday when the same paper reported that Huang had also had an affair with an Nvidia executive who reports to him.

It was already looking inevitable that he would be fired, and the latest revelation adds to the urgency. The Wall Street Journal reported that the board was meeting last night to discuss the CEO’s future. That future at Nvidia is likely to be measured in hours rather than days.

The board now needs to find a replacement, and Bloomberg provides a rundown on some of the potential candidates. CFO Colette Kress looks likely to head the list for at least an interim appointment. The paper names a number of other possibilities, including Apple’s chief hardware officer, Johny Srouji.

Srouji has led one of the most successful in-house silicon programs in the industry, overseeing the custom chips that power the iPhone, Mac and other Apple devices. His track record gives him deep credibility on chip design, product integration and long-term technology roadmaps. But much of his experience has been built inside Apple’s tightly controlled ecosystem, and he hasn’t been tested in the kind of high-profile salesmanship which Huang is famous for.

Given the urgency, my money is on Nvidia announcing Kress as at least interim CEO, if not a permanent replacement. It seems exceedingly unlikely that Srouji would be appointed at this time, given the in-depth negotiations and discussions that would need to take place.

As to whether he’s a potential candidate following an interim appointment, it still seems unlikely. There were earlier reports of his previous dissatisfaction at the company, but it seems a stretch to imagine him jumping ship so soon after his promotion to chief hardware officer.

The 8 Best TV Dramas From Before the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We are currently undeniably living in the era of streaming, but for the first few decades of its existence as a medium, network television reigned supreme. Since network shows are broadcast on a strict weekly timetable, they're usually built on very particular structures potentially containing built-in breaks before cutting to commercials, cliffhangers, and very consistent pacing. As television has evolved, so too have the characteristics of network series changed.

However, there are eight particular broadcast television dramas which are easily the greatest in history prior to the advent of streaming. Whether we're talking about classics like Twin Peaks or modern masterpieces like Game of Thrones, these are the kinds of generation-defining dramas that anyone who loves small-screen content should watch at least once in their lives. There is no understanding network television without experiencing these masterpieces.

The 1990s gave birth to a second Golden Age of television, and were an absolutely groundbreaking time for auteur-driven television. The medium shifted away from traditional network-controlled episodic formulas, and instead began embracing more serialized storytelling in line with the vision of creatives like David Lynch. Indeed, Lynch and Mark Frost's Twin Peaks is one of the most foundational auteur-driven shows of all time.

It's one of those network TV shows that are visually stunning, impeccably written, masterfully directed, and pretty much impeccable in every way that matters. Though the show saw a dip in quality right in the middle of season two, this has never stopped fans from greatly enjoying the show's intentionally uncanny tone, campy supernatural elements, and brilliant mix of melodramatic soap opera tropes and Lynch's signature style of surrealism.

Never in history has the small screen seen a prequel series even remotely as incredible as Vince Gilligan and Peter Gould's Better Call Saul. Making a prequel to a network drama widely considered the single greatest TV show of all time was never going to be an easy task, and as such, fans were very much skeptical when this project was announced. In the end, though, Better Call Saul ended up being one of the best TV shows since 2010, a near-perfect masterpiece through and through.

Better Call Saul is both an exceptional prequel adding a lot of depth and texture to the Breaking Bad world, and an incredible crime drama and legal thriller in its own right. With some masterful character writing, an excellent balance between poignant tragedy and genuinely laugh-out-loud-funny comedy, and the faultless work of an ensemble led by Bob Odenkirk at the top of his game, it's no wonder why it's the most acclaimed TV prequel in history.

Genre stories were always of vital importance to network television, and science fiction in particular was a key driver of technical innovation and a great platform for social commentary throughout the broadcast era of TV. Over the years, never has the small screen seen a sci-fi show more foundational or widely influential than Rod Serling's legendary anthology series The Twilight Zone, a gem that's essential viewing for anyone who loves sci-fi.

Serling wanted to make a show that would allow him to tackle controversial yet essential social and political issues, but since network executives refused to let him write direct socially-conscious shows, he decided to circumvent that issue. So, he made a sci-fi series that disguised its treatment of taboo topics—like racism and McCarthyism—as sci-fi, fantasy, and horror. Like any anthology series, Twilight Zone had its fair share of ups and downs throughout its run, but it's packed with some of the greatest episodes in the history of anthology TV series.

HBO was launched all the way back in 1972, but it wouldn't be until the '90s and 2000s that they would become the kings of broadcast television and the pioneers of what is now called "prestige TV." Case in point: Band of Brothers, a war drama miniseries executive-produced by Tom Hanks and Steven Spielberg that has been the highest-rated miniseries in history on IMDb for quite some time. It's easy to see why: miniseries or otherwise, history has never seen a war drama quite like this on TV.

It's one of the best HBO shows shorter than 20 episodes, a hard-hitting and faithful adaptation of the non-fiction book of the same name by historian Stephen E. Ambrose. Treating World War II with the same kind of sensitivity and raw realism as Saving Private Ryan, Band of Brothers is uncompromisingly realistic and respectful toward its characters, with a potent shift in narrative perspective every episode that gives every minute of its run a consistently surprising, thought-provoking feel.

Though its penultimate season was divisive and its final few episodes were nothing short of universally panned, Game of Thrones proves that a network drama doesn't need to be absolutely perfect in order to be counted among the best in history. Indeed, it would be silly to pretend that this HBO adaptation of George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire book series, which was a gargantuan pop culture phenomenon over the course of its eight seasons, is anything less than legendary.

Where can one even begin singing the praises of such a praise-worthy masterpiece? For one, the production values are off-the-charts, including some of the greatest visuals and music in the history of fantasy television. Add to that some exceptional writing, an unrelentingly bleak and violent tone, and one of the best TV cast ensembles of the 2010s, and you get a genre-defining masterpiece whose quality far transcends any mistakes that its final season may have made.

As if any more proof were needed that there's just no matching HBO's quality and legacy when it comes to the greatest dramas in the history of network television, there's also The Wire. Creator David Simon had spent 12 years covering crime and city systems as a police reporter for The Baltimore Sun, while co-writer Ed Burns had worked inside the Baltimore Police Department as a homicide detective. Inspired by their experiences and what they'd seen, the duo co-created The Wire.

It's a scathing study and deconstruction of five particular institutional layers of Baltimore: the illegal drug trade, the port system, the government, education, and the media. But far from feeling niche or exploitative, The Wire instead delivers the most complete portrait of American urban decay ever put to screen. Full of richly layered characters and potent thematic work, it's one of the many pinnacles of 21st-century crime drama television.

Network television would simply have never become what it is today had it not been for The Sopranos. In television's history, there's a "before The Sopranos" and an "after The Sopranos," as David Chase's crime drama completely re-defined what the medium was able to do. Never before had television seen such darkly antiheroic characters, such cinematic quality, and such cleanly serialized storytelling. Thus, prestige television was born, and the small screen was never the same again.

But though it was a pioneer of this new version of network television, The Sopranos doesn't feel dated in the slightest all these many years later; on the contrary, it's still generally considered the best HBO show to date. Bolstered by the phenomenal writing, James Gandolfini's powerhouse lead performance, and some of the richest psychological depth that a broadcast drama has ever seen, it's a must-see for anyone who loves television.

Over a decade after its conclusion, most people still widely refer to Vince Gilligan's Breaking Bad as the single greatest TV drama in history, and it will take something very special to ever dethrone it. Gilligan came up with the concept after fellow writer Tom Schnauz joked that they should solve their lack of work by buying up an RV and setting up a mobile meth lab. The idea stuck in his mind for years, and would eventually transformed into the most complex, cinematic, and irresistibly riveting drama that television has ever produced.

Breaking Bad is one of those drama shows that have aged like fine wine, a masterpiece whose every element works in flawless concert with the others to deliver the most enthralling experience possible. The production values are fantastic, the ensemble is full of actors delivering career-best work, and the character writing is the dramatic fuel that pushes the narrative momentum forward throughout. From top to bottom and beginning to end, Breaking Bad is fully worthy of being officially named the greatest TV drama of all time.

Your premise isn't necessarily true. As interest rates rise, the yield of shorter-term bond funds rises much faster than the yield of long-term bond funds. How can that be? Because the shorter-term bond fund replaces its maturing, or shortest term, bonds much faster, which raises its yield at a faster pace. Take a look at the yields of bond funds in the 1970s as an example. Short-term bond fund yields rose to nearly 17% and long-term bond fund yields were only in the low teens. Intermediate-term bond funds were a better overall compromise.
