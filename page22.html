The posided magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune may stem from extreme conditions lying within their deep interior. Unravelling the mystery behind the magnetic fields of these worlds is giving us new insight into the little-known interiors of the ice giants.

Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus and Neptune, the Voyager 2 spacecraft which spend past Uranus in January 1986 and Neptune in August 1989. Voyager 2 found that their planetary magnetic fields were, to put it bluntly, wonky. Uranus’ magnetic field was offset from its rotational axis by 59 degrees and isn’t even centered on the planet’s core, but instead is only two-thirds of the way down. Neptune is similar, with a disordered magnetic field offset by 47 degrees and centered halfway between the core and its visible cloud tops.If we compare these planets to Earth, which has a stable magnetic field tilted by just 11 degrees to our planet’s rotation, we can see how extreme the magnetic fields of the ice giants are.

Various explanations for the strange magnetic fields of the ice giants have been put forward over the years. Uranus as a whole is tilted by 97 degrees and effectively rolls around the sun, and speculation is that billions of years ago it was knocked on its side by a collision with a long-lost Earth-sized planet. Could this collision have also skewed its magnetic field?

This might be a plausible explanation were Neptune’s magnetic field also not similarly skewed without having experienced a collision. Clearly, whatever the cause, it must be intrinsic to the nature of the two ice giant planets. Now, a new study by scientists in the U.S. and the UK, based on computer simulations and Voyager 2’s measurements of the two planets’ magnetic fields, may have found a solution.

The solution to the weird magnetic fields of the solar system's ice giants may come down to how planets generate their magnetic field through the dynamo effect. This arises from the swirling, convective motion of an electrically conducting liquid within a rotating body, which is able to generate and maintain a powerful magnetic field.

Inside Earth, the electrically conducting fluid is the molten iron in the outer part of our planet’s core, and the magnetic field that it generates has clear north and south poles – what we call a dipole, a phenomenon that we can also see in the two magnetic poles of a simple bar magnet.

However, the conditions inside Uranus and Neptune are somewhat different. As ice giants, they contain a lot of volatiles – ammonia, hydrogen, helium, methane, and water surrounding a probable rocky core. Furthermore, Uranus and Neptune are substantially larger than Earth, with 14.5 and 17 times the mass of our planet, respectively. This increased mass creates crushing conditions within the interior of the ice giants.

"Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, pressures and temperatures become extremely high," research team leader Chi Yan of the University of Texas at Austin, who led the new findings, said in a statement. "Under those conditions, which can cause water molecules to break apart into ions, the material may become increasingly electrically conductive with depth."

Given the increasing electrical conductivity and the compositions of the two planets, the computer simulations were able to replicate the bizarre magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune. This showed how the magnetic-field structure co-evolves alongside changes in the fluid motion driven by convection and the temperature inside the planets.

It also seems that this co-evolution might not yet be over. The simulations indicate that the magnetic fields of both planets can change quite significantly in the space of just a few decades.

"Earth’s magnetic tilt drifts by only a degree or so over 50 years," said Yan. "Our models show the ice giants can behave very differently. In some stretches, the tilt swings by tens of degrees within decades. In others, it shifts only slightly over the same span."

NASA have prioritized a new mission to return to Uranus in the early 2040s, while the China National Space Administration’s Tianwen-4 mission launching to Jupiter in 2029 will deploy a small probe to carry onwards to Uranus via a gravitational slingshot. Until then, scientists are coming up with creative ways to not have to rely only on the 40-year-old Voyager 2 measurements of the magnetic fields.

For example, Tom Stallard of Northumbria University in the UK, who is a co-author with Yan on the new research paper, has been leading James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations of the faint infrared auroral lights on both Uranus and Neptune. Aurorae are generated when charged particles on the solar wind interact with a planet’s magnetic field, driving some of those charged particles into the atmosphere above the magnetic poles. (Neptune actually has a quadrupolar component, muddling its defined north and south magnetic poles.) As such, the location of aurorae is a good indicator of what’s going on with a planet’s magnetic field.

"We are busy working on what’s contained within these observations, but the shape and structure of the aurora, as well as other weird patterns in the ionosphere [upper atmosphere], promise to provide us with a way of understanding how the magnetic fields have changed, [which is] an important stepping stone," said Stallard.

The hope is that by combining JWST’s observations of the aurora with Voyager 2’s magnetic-field measurements and the results of the computer simulations, it will be possible to estimate the configuration of the magnetic fields on Uranus and Neptune today and see how they compare to the 1980s.

This would provide an experimental test to help show whether the new model describing the magnetic fields is on the right track, although we won’t know for sure until a new mission arrives in the ice giants’ backyard.

The findings were described on 3 November in the journal Communications Physics.

US-listed spot Bitcoin funds recorded another difficult session on Monday, November 9. The updated Farside Investors flow table shows $201.0 million in net outflows across the group, extending a three-session losing streak. The important detail is that the negative total was not evenly spread across issuers: BlackRock’s IBIT recorded $213.2 million leaving, while Morgan Stanley’s MSBT attracted $22.1 million.

TL;DR: Spot Bitcoin ETFs lost a net $201.0 million on November 9, following $430.2 million on November 5 and $411.9 million on November 6. BlackRock drove Monday’s withdrawals, while one smaller fund reported inflows. The figures measure fund creations and redemptions, not the intentions of every Bitcoin investor.

The latest fully reported Farside figures put the consecutive negative sessions at roughly $1.04 billion in aggregate. Thursday’s $430.2 million loss was followed by Friday’s $411.9 million and then Monday’s $201.0 million. That progression shows persistent selling pressure, although Monday’s net outflow was smaller than either of the previous two sessions.

Our previous report on Friday’s fund activity documented a similar pattern of issuer concentration. Going back further, the November 2 breakdown shows why it is worth examining individual ETF lines rather than reading the aggregate as a single institutional vote.

On November 9, ARK Invest and 21Shares’ ARKB showed a $9.9 million outflow. The positive contribution from MSBT offset part of the selling elsewhere. Several other funds showed no daily net creation or redemption. That does not mean no shares changed hands on exchanges; secondary-market trading and flows into or out of a fund’s underlying Bitcoin exposure are different measures.

BlackRock’s fund is a large and closely watched route into Bitcoin for investors using traditional brokerage and portfolio systems. Its persistent redemptions can therefore affect the tone of market commentary disproportionately. But the identity of an ETF’s end investors is not published in a daily flow table, and a withdrawal cannot automatically be attributed to hedge funds, pensions or retail holders.

The distinction between net flows and assets under management matters as well. A fund’s reported assets can fall when Bitcoin’s price drops even without corresponding redemptions. Conversely, share creations can occur on a day when the asset price weakens. Analysts who blend these concepts can make demand appear stronger or weaker than it actually was.

The next test is whether the large-issuer selling eases over several completed reporting sessions. One positive day would interrupt the streak but would not, by itself, demonstrate that buyers have regained control. It is also worth watching whether inflows broaden across products or remain confined to a single issuer.

These numbers are current through the November 9 US trading session, not an estimate of November 10 while trading is still under way. A better measure of sentiment combines a completed ETF flow series with spot volume, derivatives positioning and the changing rates outlook. For now, the latest evidence is that listed-fund demand remains weak even as the scale of net redemptions has moderated from last week’s peaks.

Talking Point: With Big Walk, The AI-In-Game-Dev Debate Just Got A Lot Murkier

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"The line must be drawn here! This far, no further!"

Please forgive the Captain Picard First Contact quote (it's been a couple of weeks since Patrick Stewart's death and only now can I mention his most famous character without welling up) - I promise it's relevant as an illustration of an admirable, principled stance.

In the Borg, good ol' Jean-Luc had a well-defined enemy to engage, with the threat of humanity's assimilation providing a pretty clear line between good and bad. There are larger philosophical questions about the hive mind and our aversion to Star Trek's cube-riding automatons, but for the purposes of this article, let's assume we're all in agreement: Borg=baaad.

Back in the real world, though, that line between right and wrong often isn't so easy to define. Today there'll be a lot of commentators in the video game space, players and developers alike, longing for a better understanding of another encroaching, all-consuming entity.

House House's belated disclosure that LLMs were used during the development of indie hit Big Walk pours fuel on an already raging debate and brings questions over permissible AI use in game dev right back into the discourse - specifically the differences between generative AI for player-facing audio-visual assets and writing versus coding and other less obvious uses.

News Big Walk Devs Issue Statement Over AI Usage

This latest flashpoint followed a statement released by a subset of IGF Award jurors explaining why they won't be voting for games developed using any form of generative AI.

Last year, Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 — which swept The Game Awards with nine wins from 13 nominations — raised similar questions after the Indie Game Awards disqualified Sandfall Interactive's game upon discovering AI had been used in the development pipeline.

I've voiced my distaste for GenAI, and it remains a massive no-no - professionally and personally. I'm fortunate to not have corporate overlords mandating its use around these parts, which certainly isn't the case for some other outlets, nor for many creatives in the gaming space. The gulf in quality between the human-written word and generated bilge is still enormous and easily identifiable, and for now, the same is broadly true of GenAI visual artwork, too.

What this Big Walk situation highlights, however, is how difficult it can be to discern in other places, and the increasing pull it has on even the most principled developers.

Talking Point Does It Bother You That Big Games Companies Are Using GenAI?

The phrase gets bandied around a lot, but it's fair to call Big Walk a proper 'critical darling'. It garnered "Universal Acclaim" back in August, with an impressive 91 Metascore. Alex gave it a "Great" 8/10 after reviewing the Switch 2 version for us - technical hiccups took the shine off it for him, but he still loved the co-op experience. And by September it was already Nile's GOTY.

As someone who didn't play Big Walk at launch, its place in my GOTY-catch-up priorities may have shifted, but wherever you land on the GenAI spectrum, the consensus among critics and players is as unanimous as it gets: Big Walk is an excellent video game.

There are many issues to consider — not least where AI use crosses from tool to theft — but an important one relates to how this game in particular has had a profound effect on many players. Rejecting something flagged as 'suspicious' on principle is easy, especially with so many brilliant games releasing each week. But something you've played already and internalised and loved? From a dev team you respect?

For some, this revelation will feel like when a beloved artist/actor/writer/musician is revealed to be problematic, but we're not talking about illegal behaviour or allegations here.

If House House's statement is to be taken at face value, the team used a chatbot to find a solution to a coding issue with Unity, the third-party engine the game is built on. People online with real chops have already investigated the specifics of House House's single example:

took me 3 hours but I figured out the “URP culling shadowcasters behind the camera” issue House House cites in their disclosure. This culls immediately when the object is off-camera, but modifying the culling params with a custom frustrum is straightforward

this needs cleaning up but is basically the gist

This was a terrible use of 3 hours, but I know just a bit more about how to do something cool. I’m sympathetic to them wanting to ship something good but AI rots your damn brain

That's the one example the devs offer in their statement, and the language around further usage feels vague. The impression it gives is of a team searching for a solution to a specific problem using common tools, as opposed to vibe-coding huge swathes of the game.

Now, I've never programmed anything in my life, but I can appreciate that efficient code must be an art in itself. Is it any less of an art because that code is invisible to players? Then again, to me, generating great chunks of game code feels very different from using an LLM to squash a bug or find a solution when third-party documentation fails. Is one usage as 'bad' as the other, or is this slippery slope, gateway-drug territory?

(Perhaps if any programmers are reading, they'd like to share thoughts in the comments - as the headline says, this is very much a talking point and I'm keen to explore with as much nuance as the internet will permit.)

My point is that seeing exactly where to draw that line is increasingly difficult when AI tools of all types become ubiquitous. A blanket rejection of anything inevitably now means excluding not just an increasing number of games, but also artistically significant, important ones.

And with executives like former Nintendo of America president Doug Bowser advocating the use of AI for indie teams to improve workflow, it's also easy to understand how a developer might turn to the tech. The whole topic is getting greyer by the day, much to the pleasure of evangelists, I'm sure.

Like it or not, this situation forces AI sceptics to reconcile their positive feelings for the game. House House estimates "well under 0.1% of Big Walk’s code" has any trace of generative AI, but to paraphrase a certain Starfleet captain again, "How much does it take before it becomes wrong, hmm?" That is the question.

For some players who simply don't care, it's a non-issue. Some may reluctantly cut the game from their lives and GOTY lists. I suspect that others, though, disappointed or otherwise, will start reconsidering where they personally draw the AI line.

And if House House hadn't disclosed this, would anyone have ever known? I'd guess not. And if a celebrated indie team turned to AI as a problem-solver, how many other devs aren't disclosing similar usage? And how many sure-as-hell won't do so after this?

Lots of questions, no clear answers, just murk. How unsatisfying. I've heard 'ends-justify-means' sentiments online — 'hey, if the game is good, does it matter?' — which feel unsavoury given the documented, quantifiable damage AI is doing to the games industry and the wider world. It's that damage which has IGF jurors abstaining from voting from Big Walk this year: an understandable, admirable stance.

Which leads us to one final question: What would Captain Picard do?

Naturally, he'd call everyone into the Observation Lounge for a big old meeting in a calm, civilised manner, that's what. Conference.

There’s a Good Reason the NYC Marathon Winners Wore These Watches

Over 60,000 runners crossed the finish line at the New York City Marathon last week. When you think of the elites fighting for a spot on the podium, you might expect to see only the priciest, most feature-packed GPS watches money can buy—historically, this meant the most premium Garmin Forerunners. Looking at the wrists of this year's New York City Marathon podium finishers, though,  you’ll see that Garmin—the name most recreational runners reach for by default—was far from the only brand on the course.

Here are the watches I’ve been able to confirm among the top NYC Marathon finishers:

Men's winner, Andreas Almgren: Coros Pace 4 (sponsored)

Men's runner-up, Yemaneberhan Crippa: Amazfit Cheetah 2 Pro (sponsored)

Women's runner-up, Jessica McClain: Garmin Forerunner 570 (sponsored)

Women's third place, Hellen Obiri: Coros Pace 4 (sponsored)

Nonbinary winner, Ryan Montgomery: Suunto Race 3S (almost certainly sponsored, since this watch was released last month)

An important disclaimer: I haven't spoken with any of these athletes. What follows is my opinion, based on what I know running watches offer the people who wear them.

"Garmin" has long been the default answer to "what running watch should I buy?" It earned that spot with reliable GPS, excellent software, and a huge ecosystem. And on this podium, it's represented by a Forerunner 570—a great watch, but at a price tag most runners would struggle to justify.

Of course, sponsorships muddy the water, since athletes wear what their sponsors put on their wrists. But that's exactly the point: Other brands are investing in elite runners because they believe their products can stand next to Garmin's, and it looks like they’re making the right bet.

It's fair to ask whether Garmin's grip is loosening. The company has increasingly pushed toward the fanciest watches with the most features at higher and higher price points. The Forerunner 570 starts north of $500. Meanwhile, COROS, Amazfit, and others are proving that a perfectly solid, competitive watch doesn't need to cost that much. Is Garmin still the best running watch overall? For ecosystem, polish, and depth of features, it's still a strong contender. But "best" no longer means "the only serious option," and the gap between Garmin and the field is narrower than it has been in years. Sponsored or not, seeing two Coros Pace 4s and an Amazfit Cheetah 2 Pro among the top finishers is worth noting.

You don’t need an expensive watch to be elite—just look at the cheap running watches worn by the men who broke the sub-two-hour barrier at the London Marathon this past spring. Training load scores, recovery metrics, maps, music, and smart notifications are nice, but they're extras. If you're shopping for a watch, spend your money on the basics being done right, and don't feel pressured to pay for features you'll never use. Everything besides your time and pace is icing on the cake.

If they can trust those watches on their biggest days, the rest of us can probably trust them with our Saturday long runs.

For the first time we saw a peek behind the mask, one that has very often been one portraying an optimistic and positive expression. Amorim knew there was no point attempting to lie to the supporters, the media but above all to himself.

What we know is that Amorim echoed the ferocity of these sentiments when speaking in the dressing room after full-time in what was likely the first dressing down that the team got. Six players were reported to have disappointed more than the others, too.

Above all, what stood out was the fact that the head coach opted to question whether his players wanted it enough. This was no longer a question of learning an identity, rotation or an injury crisis: it became a matter of heart.

Another intriguing extract from the post-Ferencvaros tirade that Amorim went on was about the squad’s ability to balance playing in two different competitions, namely Serie A and the Europa League.

“We’re not ready to be competitive on two fronts. We need to work on our way of looking at football and how we approach matches. There are no excuses tonight, there’s nothing I can say.

“I have to focus on the league, obviously, but we also have to do well in the Europa League. We must and can win games despite the rotation. We have to be ready – we’re not now – to play and win every three days.

“But I won’t give up on this competition. But as long as the mathematics don’t condemn us, we’ll fight in this competition as we do in the others.”

Once again, Amorim offered a bit of a revelation: the league is the absolute priority for Milan this season. It may not exactly be revolutionary information, given that a top four spot remains the most accessible avenue to the main objective for 2026-27: securing a Champions League return.

And yet, in his emotional outburst, the former Sporting CP and Manchester United boss told us all in black and white that Serie A will be the focus. As a result, it’s right to expect more ups and downs in Europe, especially with such a depleted squad.

There is an element of observational realism about Amorim’s words; almost an admission of defeat in his attempts to spin two plates at the same time and get the most out of the resources at his disposal. Does it suggest a Europa League resurgence is coming? Not exactly.

Amorim has not made a habit of mentioning individuals ever since his arrivals, both in his pre-match words but especially after games in which emotions are running high. However, he brought up someone who was absent for the Ferencvaros game.

What Amorim seems keen to hammer home is that progress for this season will be judged on the ability to bring the ceiling closer to the floor. In other words, we have seen the potential of this team already with some impressive wins, yet the base level for performances remains frighteningly low.

Consistency is not just about being able to string together several positive results in a row, something the current Rossoneri do not seem capable of. It is about the repeated displaying of an idea and an identity every few days, to suggest the team are getting closer to the vision the coach has.

With numbers like these, there isn’t much for Ramos to get on the end of, and it makes the positive attacking numbers (23 goals in 11 league games) feel quite unsustainable.

This is something Amorim must work on, because while the goals are coming from other sources for now – in some cases due to the space Ramos helps create – it could all come tumbling down unless the attacking play improves more generally.

My Store Turned Into Wingdings for Exactly One Reason, and It Was 3,410 Bytes

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On 7 November 2026 I added analytics to my store and rewarded myself by breaking the entire site's text. Every word on every page rendered as garbled symbols in my browser. The build was fine. The files were fine. The words were fine, somewhere underneath whatever my browser had decided they were.

The site is a small store built with Astro, deployed on Vercel. The change that morning was tiny and boring: install Vercel Analytics, drop the component into the base layout, deploy. The kind of change you make between sips of tea.

Then I opened the store and it looked like the text had been fed through a paper shredder and reassembled by an enemy.

I checked the content files. Clean. I checked the build output locally. Clean. I rebuilt. I stared at fonts, because garbled text feels like a font problem the way a cough feels like a lung problem. It usually isn't, but the feeling is strong.

Here is what I had not checked: the actual bytes of the page my browser received.

The analytics component injects an inline script into the . My layout had the tag sitting after other elements, and the injected script landed before it. In the served HTML, the charset declaration ended up at byte offset 3,410.

When a server sends an HTML page without declaring the character encoding in the Content-Type response header, the browser has to guess. The HTML spec gives it a prescan step: it reads the beginning of the byte stream looking for a charset declaration.

The catch is the size of that beginning. The prescan only looks at roughly the first 1,024 bytes.

My charset tag was at byte 3,410. From the browser's point of view, it did not exist. So the browser fell back to a guessed encoding, guessed wrong, and rendered UTF-8 text through the wrong lens. Every multi-byte character smeared into symbols. The page was never broken. It was being read in the wrong language by a reader following its own rules correctly.

And my response headers carried no charset either, so there was no second chance. The meta tag was the only witness, and it was standing 3,410 bytes away from the only place witnesses get heard.

Move the charset meta to be the first element inside . Before the title. Before everything.

One line, relocated. Redeploy. In the new served HTML the charset sat at byte offset 44, comfortably inside the prescan window, and the store read like a store again. The checkout page returned 200, the text was clean, done.

Total code changed: the position of one tag. Total time spent suspecting my content, my fonts and my build before looking at the byte offsets: considerably longer, and I'd rather not be precise about it, which tells you everything.

Encoding bugs wear a perfect disguise. They look exactly like content bugs. The words are what's visibly wrong, so you interrogate the words, and the words are innocent, and they stay innocent no matter how many times you rebuild them.

The debugging rule I now follow, learned at byte 3,410: when text renders wrong, check how the bytes are being read before checking what the bytes say. View source. Count where the charset declaration actually sits in the served document, not where you placed it in your template, because frameworks, injectors and "helpful" components rearrange furniture in your without asking. My template had the tag in a perfectly reasonable position. The served page had it somewhere else entirely. The template is a promise; the served bytes are the truth.

This applies to anything that injects into your head section: analytics, tag managers, A/B testing scripts, consent banners. Every one of them pushes your own tags further from byte zero. Most of the time it costs nothing. The day your headers don't declare a charset, it costs you your whole site's legibility, and the bill arrives looking like a content problem.

I'm collecting these now, the bugs where the cause-to-symptom ratio is most insulting. Mine: one misplaced tag, 2,386 bytes past the limit, every word on a live store unreadable.

What's yours? The one-character typo that ate a weekend, the config line that took down the thing it was supposed to protect. Smallest cause, biggest symptom. I want to feel better, and also worse.

Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation for System Design, With Worked Examples

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 10 • Originally published at letinix.com

System design estimation is a way to turn an ambiguous product description into approximate traffic, storage, bandwidth, and capacity requirements. The goal is not to predict an exact production number. It is to expose the order of magnitude, make assumptions visible, and identify which part of the design deserves attention first.

A useful estimate follows a repeatable sequence: define the workload, calculate average and peak requests per second, split reads from writes, estimate data growth, calculate network throughput, and translate the results into design choices. The arithmetic should be simple enough to explain aloud and detailed enough for another engineer to challenge.

Note: Treat every number in the worked examples as an explicit hypothetical input, not a benchmark or a universal industry default. Replace assumptions with measured product analytics, load tests, payload samples, and retention requirements when sizing a real system.

Start by describing what the system does. “A social feed” is not a workload; “two million daily active users each request twenty feed pages and publish half a post per day on average” is a workload you can calculate.

Write down the assumptions before doing arithmetic:

Ask which values are known, which are estimates, and which are deliberately conservative. If a prompt does not provide a value, choose a round assumption, say why it is plausible for the scenario, and show how the result changes if it is wrong. In an interview, this is part of the answer—not a detour before the answer.

A day contains 86,400 seconds. For quick mental arithmetic, 100,000 seconds is a convenient approximation, but use 86,400 when showing a worked calculation. The approximation makes the result about 13.6% lower than the exact division, so it is useful for a first pass, not a substitute for checking a decision near a capacity boundary.

For traffic, remember the order of magnitude: one million requests per day is 1,000,000 ÷ 86,400 = 11.57 requests per second, or roughly 12 average requests per second. Ten million per day is about 116/s; one hundred million per day is about 1,157/s.

Be precise about data units. Decimal units use powers of 10: 1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes and 1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes. Binary units use powers of 2: 1 KiB = 1,024 bytes, 1 MiB = 1,048,576 bytes, and 1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes. NIST documents the distinction between SI and IEC binary prefixes. NIST: Definitions of the SI units — binary prefixes

Use one convention throughout a calculation. In the examples below, storage totals use decimal GB and TB; file sizes use MiB where binary units are more natural. State the convention rather than switching between MB and MiB as if they were identical.

These formulas provide a starting model. They do not capture every system cost, but they expose the main quantities that influence an initial architecture.

RPS means requests per second. QPS means queries per second; teams sometimes use the terms interchangeably, but define whether a “request” is an external API call, a database query, or an internal operation. One external request can fan out into several internal calls.

Do not assume one peak factor fits every product. Calculate it from observed time-bucketed traffic when possible: peak interval rate divided by the average rate for the same interval definition. If no telemetry exists, state a scenario assumption and test alternatives. A factor of 3 in an example means “we are modeling a peak three times the average,” not “all systems peak at 3×.”

Calculate read and write rates separately. A read-heavy product may need to serve a large volume of repeated data, while a write-heavy product may need sustained ingestion, ordering, durability, or downstream processing. A combined RPS figure can hide the real constraint.

Add indexes, metadata, compaction space, snapshots, backups, and derived data separately. A copy factor is a simplified planning input, not a universal statement about how a managed database or object store implements durability. Compression also changes the stored size; estimate it from representative data rather than assuming a fixed saving.

Calculate ingress and egress independently. Use the relevant request or response size for each path. Add protocol overhead, encryption, retries, fan-out, and retransmission where they materially affect the estimate. For large files, a small request count can still produce substantial bandwidth.

If the capacity target is below full utilization, use ceil(peak_RPS ÷ (sustainable_RPS_per_instance × target_utilization)). The per-instance rate must come from a representative load test or a defensible capacity measurement for the actual workload, software, instance type, and latency target. Do not copy a generic “requests per server” number into a design and present it as fact.

Assume a feed service has 2 million daily active users. Each user loads twenty feed pages per day and creates half a post per day on average. Model peak traffic at four times the daily average. Assume each post record occupies 1,000 bytes and each feed response transfers 12,000 bytes. These are illustrative inputs.

The workload is roughly 40 reads for every write. That ratio does not prove a cache is required, but it tells you the read path deserves more attention than the write path in the first design pass.

This is application response payload only. It excludes transport overhead and any media served separately. If feed responses include images, model image delivery as a separate path rather than hiding it inside an average API response.

The 1.095 TB figure excludes indexes, metadata, backups, and media. It is not a complete database capacity plan.

Design implications: investigate caching or precomputing frequently requested feed data, and consider read replicas only if measured database read pressure justifies them. Keep writes on a simpler path initially unless ordering, fan-out, or consistency requirements change the picture. Separate media delivery from feed API responses, and validate the assumed read distribution: a highly personalized feed may not benefit from the same cache strategy as a feed of mostly shared content.

Assume 500,000 daily active devices each emit 200 events per day. Each stored event, including its basic metadata, averages 600 bytes. Model peak ingestion at five times the average and retain events for 30 days. Use a three-copy scenario for a first storage estimate.

The 5.4 TB estimate excludes indexes, compression effects, retention gaps, backups, and temporary compaction space. If event payloads vary substantially, calculate by event class or use a measured size distribution instead of relying on one average.

The request rate is more revealing than the raw bandwidth in this example. The system must accept and durably process nearly 5,800 small events per second at the modeled peak, even though the byte rate is modest.

Design implications: consider an append-oriented ingestion path, batching, and a durable queue or log when producers and consumers need decoupling. Partitioning can distribute sustained write volume, but the partition key must avoid concentrating one hot tenant or event type on a single partition. Define ordering requirements before choosing a partition strategy: global ordering is much more restrictive than per-device or per-aggregate ordering. Use retention and compaction policies that match the actual query and audit requirements.

Assume 100,000 daily active users upload two files each per day. The average file is 8 MiB. Model upload peaks at eight times the average. Assume each uploaded file is downloaded three times on average, downloads peak at four times their average, and files are retained for 30 days. For a simplified capacity scenario, count three stored copies.

The request rate looks small. The payload size changes the capacity picture.

The conversion uses 8 MiB = 8,388,608 bytes and decimal MB for the final rate. This is a payload estimate, not a provisioned network target; protocol overhead and burst shape still matter.

Design implications: this is a data-transfer and storage problem, not simply an API request-count problem. Consider direct-to-object-storage uploads using short-lived, scoped upload authorization so application servers do not proxy every file byte. Use asynchronous processing for malware scanning, metadata extraction, and thumbnail generation when those tasks do not need to block the upload response. A CDN may reduce origin egress for repeatedly downloaded files, subject to access control and cache policy. Confirm whether “three copies” is actually how the chosen storage service bills or exposes durability; do not multiply managed storage by three if its documented storage model already accounts for redundancy.

The examples are not directly comparable capacity benchmarks: their workloads, payloads, and assumptions differ. Their purpose is to show how the same method reveals different constraints.

Run these checks before turning estimates into an architecture:

An estimate is useful only when it changes a question or decision. Use the numbers to identify what to investigate—not to select a database or cache by formula alone.

For an interview, narrate the assumptions, show the arithmetic, identify the largest constraint, and explain what you would measure next. For a real design, record the inputs, source, confidence, and owner beside the estimate. A useful next step is to put the assumptions and capacity implications into a technical design document, where reviewers can challenge the numbers before they become infrastructure commitments.
