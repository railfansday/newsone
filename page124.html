Shopify Stock Fell After Its Report. Here's the Price I'd Pay for It.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The e-commerce company's free cash flow climbed 61% last quarter, but the stock fell anyway. Is it cheap enough now?

Shopify (SHOP -8.74%) stock fell nearly 9% on Tuesday after the e-commerce software company posted its third-quarter results. At roughly $144 as I write, shares are about 20% below their Oct. 12 record close of $179.22.

The slide came even though revenue climbed 32% year over year to about $3.7 billion and free cash flow jumped 61% to $816 million.

Before the report, I wrote that I'd rather wait and see where shares settled afterward. The stock was around $157 then, or about 86 times its trailing free cash flow. Now the cash flow has come in slightly over the $800 million I'd penciled in for a strong quarter, and the price is lower. Is it finally time to buy?

Investors seemed to want more than a quarter that landed where management said it would. Shopify's guidance was for third-quarter revenue growth in the low 30s, and 32% delivered that.

But revenue had grown 34% in each of the two previous quarters, beating a low-thirties outlook for the first quarter and a high-twenties forecast for the second.

Gross merchandise volume (GMV, the value of orders placed on Shopify's platform) slowed, too. It rose 29% to $119 billion in the third quarter, after gaining 35% in the first quarter and 32% in the second. Currency explains most of that. In constant currency, GMV grew 30% in all three quarters. Gross profit, though, is where the slowdown shows. Its growth rate dipped to 28% in the third quarter, under the 32% and 31% rates in the first two quarters. And management sees high-twenties growth again in the fourth quarter.

Spending is also set to rise. Management guided fourth-quarter operating expenses to 29% to 30% of revenue. In the fourth quarter of 2025, they were about 29%. Chief financial officer Jeff Hoffmeister said on the call that the forecast reflects more marketing, including some efforts before Black Friday and Cyber Monday. A U.S. marketing test held down spending in the third quarter, and he said part of that spending will move to the fourth.

Taking the middle of each range in the guidance, I get fourth-quarter operating income growth in the high teens. That means profit growth might slow sharply from the 66% Shopify just reported.

I doubt any of these explains Tuesday's fall by itself. But together, they suggest profit growth may ease in the quarters ahead.

Shopify's free cash flow margin (the part of revenue left as cash after capital spending) was 22%, against 18% in the third quarter of 2025. This pushed trailing-12-month free cash flow to around $2.7 billion, up from nearly $2.4 billion through June.

Between the higher cash flow and the lower price, the stock's valuation looks much less stretched than it did last week. At a market cap of around $185 billion, Shopify now trades at about 70 times trailing free cash flow, against about 86 times free cash flow before the report and about 98 times free cash flow at the Oct. 12 record.

Still, not all of that improvement is likely to last. Hoffmeister said only about 1.5 percentage points of the margin gain came from lasting cost discipline. He called the rest more temporary, spread about evenly between the marketing test, the timing of some tax receivables, and an accounting change that shifted merchant cash advances (Shopify's financing for its sellers) out of operating cash flow. Management also expects the fourth-quarter free cash flow margin to roughly match last year's 19%.

Taking out the temporary piece, third-quarter free cash flow would have been closer to $725 million. That comes out to around 72 times trailing free cash flow, just slightly above 70.

Yes, the e-commerce company is still growing quickly and bringing in much more cash than it did a year ago. Shopify even spent $562 million of that cash to buy back its own shares in the third quarter.

But at around 70 times free cash flow, buyers are still paying for years of rapid growth. And they're paying it right as gross profit growth cools and management boosts its marketing.

Does the lower price make Shopify a buy? I think the drop moved the stock closer to a price I'd pay, but not all the way there. I'd start to consider shares somewhere around $115, or about 55 times trailing free cash flow.

That's about 20% under where the stock sits now, so I'm in no rush.

Dirk Kuyt, Liverpool’s big-game hero and embodiment of the Benitez era

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

English fans can be excused for never been quite sure what to expect when a striker swaps the Eredivisie for the Premier League.

Will they be the next Robin van Persie or Ruud van Nistelrooy? Or are they being sold false goods? Think Ricky van Wolfswinkel. A star in Holland with Utrecht and then in Portugal with Sporting but the scorer of precisely one goal in England during a full season with Norwich.

Dirk Kuyt had plundered 71 goals in 101 games for Feyenoord before Liverpool pressed ‘buy’ in the summer of 2006. In theory, the Reds were getting a bargain at just £10million. A proven goalscorer and, those who had seen him in action, the very antithesis of a goal hanger.

Yes, Kuyt ran around a lot. He worked for the team. He put in a shift. Ironically, that was part of the reason why his career at Liverpool will be remembered as that of honest pro rather than superstar.

Kuyt, with those solid legs and neat mop of blonde hair, made his debut in a 2-1 home win over West Ham on August 26, 2006, coming on as a substitute to replace Peter Crouch.

That 2006-07 Liverpool team could be classed as being ‘between strikers’.

Crouch brought something different to the mix and Craig Bellamy was a classy pest with a goal or two in him, while Robbie Fowler had come back for a second stint but was more nostalgia act than genuine first-teamer so there was a goalscoring void to be filled. Was Kuyt the man?

This, of course, was the Rafa Benitez era at Anfield. His tactical wizardry had guided a genuinely mixed bag of a Liverpool team to Champions League glory in 2005, but now there was the Holy Grail of trying to win the Premier League.

Anyone and everyone can chip in on cup runs, but a title assault needs a genuine and regular goalscorer. A 20-goal haul from Kuyt and solid contributions from elsewhere could have been the winning formula.

The Dutchman took a little while to settle but scored twice in four days at the end of September, helping Liverpool to home wins against Newcastle and Spurs.

Reds fans were hopeful but cautious too. On the one hand, there was a feeling that could this be the start of something. On the other, there was a nagging feeling that he was good but not great. A seven out of 10 player.

Fans are greedy. They want superstars. They want No.9s and No.10s. Kuyt looked like what he was. A £10million signing from the Dutch league.

He scored 14 goals in that first campaign, a very respectable haul but not a game-changing one.

In the next, he managed 11 while a late flurry in Liverpool’s near-miss 2008-09 season yielded 15.

It was back to 11 in the following campaign but back up to 15 in 2009-10, a tally boosted by one very special afternoon against Manchester United.

One thing was very clear. Rafa loved him. Or rather, given that the Spaniard doesn’t really do love, Rafa saw how valuable he was to the cause. From being an out-and-out striker, Benitez turned Kuyt into the ultimate workhorse, ploughing a furrow up and down one side of a front three.

READ: When Dirk Kuyt shamelessly stole Luis Suarez’s wondergoal v Man Utd

And Liverpool fans adjusted with him. The fanciful expectations of Kuyt being a world-beater had been downgraded to a different type of admiration. He wasn’t Pele, but good old Dirk would play his heart out for the club and die for the cause. And that goes a long, long way on Merseyside.

While other Reds flickered and flirted with greatness, Kuyt offered a rubber-stamped guarantee. Whatever the occasion, whatever the opponent, whatever the location, he would put in a blue-collar shift week in, week out.

That can sound patronising, but Kuyt had another trick up his sleeve which moved him into a category beyond honest trier. The man from the little coastal town of Katwijk aan See (Dutch for Clacton-on-Sea… well, not really) could score big goals in big games.

When he finally left the Big Anfield House in 2012, his ‘best bits’ were quite something:

– Scorer in the 2006-07 Champions League final against AC Milan

– Scorer of the winning penalty in the 2007 semi-final second leg against Chelsea.

– First player in 21 years to score a hat-trick against Manchester United

– Injury-time penalty to secure dramatic derby win at Everton

– Extra-time goal against Cardiff in the 2012 League Cup final

– Shootout goal against Cardiff to secure League Cup final glory

There were other important strikes against Arsenal, while he netted seven times in the 2007-08 Champions League campaign. Even the hat-trick against Manchester United had added cult value as they were all one-yard tap-ins. Those goalscoring instincts had never left him.

Not many can claim to have scored a hat-trick against Manchester United.

In many ways, Dirk Kuyt was the embodiment of the Benitez era. Tactically astute, hard-working and capable of taking it up a level on big occasions.

You don’t build a football team just by throwing 11 superstars together. Even Brazil 70 had a few in their ranks who provided solidity and balance. That was Dirk Kuyt at Liverpool. Popular, reliable and hugely valuable.

Imagine Kuyt was at his peak now. Would he make this current high-flying Liverpool team? Not as a regular starter, but Jurgen Klopp would have him as a squad player all day long.

Slight injury concern for one of the litany of superstar forwards in the squad? Two games in four days? Tough away assignment where you need to dig in but try to nick a big goal?

Deep ocean oxygen kept rising and falling long after animals appeared

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Geologists found deep ocean oxygen built up and vanished again for over 100 million years after the Cambrian, complicating how animals evolved.

Researchers often explain the Cambrian Explosion, the burst of animal life half a billion years ago, with a lasting rise in ocean oxygen. In the deep ocean, though, the oxygen didn’t last..

Geologists studied mud that settled on the seafloor between about 485 million and 380 million years ago and later hardened into rock.

They found oxygen near the bottom building up for short stretches and then disappearing again. So animals may have evolved under less deep-sea oxygen than researchers once assumed.

Chadlin Ostrander, an assistant professor in the Geology & Geophysics department at the University of Utah, led the study. His team found no evidence of sustained oxygen in the deep ocean during that span.

The team concluded that oxygen didn’t settle into the deepest water for good until sometime after roughly 380 million years ago.

“Just a decade ago, it was thought that a deep ocean oxygenation switch was flipped around 540 million years ago. Our new dataset pushes that forward in time by at least ~160 million years,” he said.

The team didn’t measure oxygen directly. Instead, it read a chemical record in the rock that follows oxygen indirectly.

Researchers have invoked oxygen in the deep ocean to explain nearly every major step in early animal history. That includes the first animals and the Cambrian Explosion. It also includes a second burst of marine diversity from about 485 to 444 million years ago.

Other scientists have raised serious counterarguments to each. A common objection is that oxygen may have risen during these events, but not high enough, or for long enough, to fill the deep ocean.

Visitors to the Grand Canyon can see some of that early history. Its walls include about 1,600 feet (500 meters) of rock that settled in a Cambrian sea.

Ostrander’s samples start right after the Cambrian ended, about 485 million years ago, so the team didn’t sample that sea.

Geologists find tiny amounts of thallium, a heavy metal, in ordinary rock. It comes in two forms that differ by a hair in weight.

When a manganese mineral settles into seafloor mud, it pulls more of the heavier form out of seawater. That mineral survives in mud only where oxygen is steadily present in the water above it, and a few days without oxygen dissolve it again.

Mud formed in oxygen-poor places keeps a sample of the surrounding seawater, including its mix of the two forms. The team read a lower value, meaning more of the lighter form, as a sign that more seafloor lay under oxygen-rich water.

The team measured 82 shale samples from the Peel River in Canada’s Yukon. The river has cut into the rock, exposing fresh layers that stayed largely unweathered in the cold.

Erik Sperling of Stanford University and Justin Strauss of Dartmouth College led the expeditions that collected the Yukon mud.

Another 44 samples came from sites including Latvia, Libya, Wales and Montana.

For most of the record, the team found the same thallium mix as in ordinary continental rock. A seafloor with little oxygen above it should leave exactly that. In Yukon mud that formed between about 405 million and 386 million years ago, the readings dropped.

The lowest value came from a Marcellus Shale sample from the Appalachian Basin that formed about 388 million years ago. By the team’s calculations, that value means oxygen-rich water covered much more than 30% of the seafloor, well past the continental shelves and into the deep ocean.

Readings from the peak of the last ice age look similar, with surface oxygen reaching the deep ocean only slightly less effectively than it does now.

That high-oxygen stretch didn’t last. By about 378 million years ago, the readings returned to ordinary values and stayed there to the top of the rock. That could mean more of the deep ocean had lost its oxygen again.

Local conditions could have faked the dip, so the team checked. The 11 Yukon samples from the dip had the same chemistry as the other Yukon samples, and the Marcellus Shale, from a separate basin, dipped the same way.

By the time of those samples, Ostrander said, marine life was already large and doing energy-expensive work.

“You have some phenomenal changes in evolutionary biology happening during this time. And none of it seems to require substantial and sustained deep ocean oxygenation,” he said.

Animal life was changing fast before that window opened.

Paleontologists working at a fossil site in southwest China have found that many animal groups already existed before the Cambrian began. Researchers who analyzed fossil trails from about 545 million years ago reached a similar conclusion.

In rock about 520 million years old, researchers have found colonies of tiny filter-feeding animals and an early relative of octopuses and squid.

The deep ocean has had steady oxygen since then. The team can’t say when that began, only that it came after about 380 million years ago. Ostrander called that the study’s one disappointment.

Gaps remain. The team has only one rock sample for roughly every 450,000 years, so the real swings may have been even larger than the mud shows.

The team also couldn’t fully rule out that thallium cycled through seawater too fast for the mud to reflect the whole ocean. Its models and the match between basins make that unlikely.

The researchers wrote that long, closely spaced oxygen records from the late Paleozoic and the Mesozoic are still needed to learn when the deep ocean filled with oxygen for good.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

Sign up for the Global Ocean Oxygen Network‘s webinars to follow the science. The UNESCO expert group on falling ocean oxygen runs monthly talks, and its short form is open to anyone.

What Trump losing power over Congress means for Iran, Ukraine and foreign policy

📅 2026-11-04  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With Democrats taking control of Congress, U.S. President Donald Trump could intensify his focus on foreign policy in his final two years in office, while facing greater scrutiny over the wars in Iran and Ukraine, experts told CNBC.

The Associated Press is projecting Democrats will have a Senate majority, flipping seats from Republicans in Iowa, Kansas, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, and Texas.

Democrats also won control of the House, giving the party greater scope to scrutinize the administration, shape spending and challenge Trump's foreign-policy agenda.

"We are going to see a president that is somewhat constrained on matters of oversight and investigation, but on foreign policy, he does still retain a lot of latitude," Laurel Rapp, director of Chatham House's U.S. and North America Programme, told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe."

Rapp said the Iran war was a "major issue" in this election cycle amid soaring fuel prices, food costs, and inflation. The war began when Trump launched joint strikes with Israel on Feb. 28.

"Republicans don't really have an off-ramp here at this stage. We know the Democrats will seek to take votes in the House and the Senate to constrain the Republicans, to restrain the administration on Iran war powers. But whether that will bind the administration is another matter," Rapp added.

She said that Democrats could exercise the "power of the purse" to limit spending on U.S. military and other Iran-related costs.

With control of both chambers, Democrats could pass legislation seeking to direct Trump to end U.S. involvement in the conflict. However, making such a measure binding over a presidential veto would require enough votes in Congress to override it.

J.C. Lintzenich, visiting senior fellow at the German Marshall Fund, said that the president is not a "lame duck."

"Remember that this election changes the Congress; it does not change the president," Lintzenich told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" on Wednesday.

"The President of the United States still has lots of power, lots of authority. We still have pending action in Iran. We still have an unresolved situation with Russia and Ukraine."

He added that while Congress has changed, foreign policy is still executed by the president and his appointed officials.

"What we'll now see is a little bit more congressional oversight, the application of funding, for example, Ukraine, that's required by statute and law," Lintzenich said.

Lintzenich said the outgoing Congress was "more willing to let the White House slide," but Democratic control of both chambers would mean a more assertive Congress, using its Article I powers over legislation, spending, and oversight to hold the White House more closely to existing law and congressional requirements.
