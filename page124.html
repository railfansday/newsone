Nvidia's first earnings report since Jensen Huang resigned, the Fed's meeting minutes, and Anthropic's massive public debut all land this week, and together they could help decide whether the pain in stocks continues or a comeback gets started.

It's been a tough month for the stock market, with the S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.92%) down 2.1% and the Nasdaq Composite down 3.6% since the end of Oct. This week could help decide if that pain continues, or if stocks will make a comeback.

First, Nvidia (NVDA -4.12%) reports earnings tomorrow after the bell. It's the company's first since its now-former CEO Jensen Huang resigned on Nov. 11.

Second, on Wednesday, the Federal Reserve releases minutes from its Oct. 27-28 meeting.

And finally, Anthropic is set for its massive public debut on Thursday, listing under the ticker ANT.

All of this lands amid an already wobbly AI trade that makes up about X% of the entire S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.92%).

Nvidia guided for revenue of about $108 billion, up from $96.2 billion last quarter. Wall Street is looking for slightly more than that, with the consensus sitting at $109 billion -- 91% growth.

But this earnings release is about much more than the numbers. After 33 years at its helm, Jensen Huang resigned last week after multiple reports of extramarital affairs, one of which was with a suspected foreign intelligence asset. The board has launched an investigation into the matter, and CFO Colette Kress will need to answer some uncomfortable questions. The company's dealings with firms that have apparently been contravening U.S. export controls will be under scrutiny. Investors will also want to know in concrete terms how Jensen's departure will affect the business.

The Fed held its benchmark rate steady on Oct. 28, choosing to leave it at 3.75% to 4.00%. However, two officials -- Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack -- dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. Since the meeting, October's Consumer Price Index (CPI) came in hotter-than-expected while jobs numbers looked better-than-expected.

The minutes, released Wednesday at 2 p.m. ET, will show more of the committee's thinking and whether any of the other officials were open to a hike, despite voting to hold rates.

Beyond the minutes, we get a round of earnings from retailers like Home Depot (HD -0.34%), Target (TGT -0.70%) and Walmart (WMT +0.25%) ahead of the holiday season. I'll be interested to see if there are signs of inflation weighing on consumer spending.

Anthropic plans to sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78, raising as much as $64 billion. That's on top of a $26 billion private placement going to Google, Nvidia, AMD (AMD -5.58%), and others. All told, Anthropic is hoping to walk away with a cool $82 billion, giving it a war chest of $212.7 billion.

Anthropic's growth has been nothing short of incredible -- $33.2 billion in revenue over the first nine months of 2026 versus $2.5 billion a year earlier. But its GAAP net loss was $104 billion (most of this was a paper charge from revaluing convertible notes and other instruments), and it has signed up for something like $900 billion in compute and infrastructure commitments, much of which is non-cancelable no matter what happens. That last part is important. If Anthropic's growth plans don't materialize the way it hopes, the Claude-maker could find itself struggling to make good on those commitments.

Soneil Spark introduces its Mobile Battery Energy Storage System

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This week, Soneil Spark rolls out its Mobile Battery Energy Storage System (Mobile BESS), the portable, modular battery storage built into a towable trailer.

The company, offering bespoke, modular energy solutions, is introducing Mobile BESS at RE+ 2026. It delivers temporary backup and off-grid power, working with what’s available by taking power from the grid, a generator, or solar.

“Wherever your business goes, the power goes with it. Simply tow it where energy demand grows,” said Sonya Jain, CEO of Soneil Spark, in a statement. “Start with one unit and add more as your needs change.”

It’s a simple set up, the company said. Mobile BESS works with no crane, no trenching, and no utility wait. That makes it a natural fit for a job site which may be months away from permanent service, a film set or event that can’t tolerate generator noise and fumes, a utility crew restoring power after a storm, or a remote mine or farm beyond the grid’s reach.

On sites that already run generators, the Mobile BESS syncs with them to cover peaks and start-up gaps, so the generators run fewer hours. Where diesel is restricted, it offers a quiet alternative.

The Packers’ offensive line regressed against the Vikings

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

We all saw the Green Bay Packers' offensive line get pushed around by the Minnesota Vikings on Sunday, but I'm sure some of you are wondering exactly why the team messed up in crucial situations over and over. Hopefully, you'll learn a little more about the game and the status of the team by the end of this article.

I want to start with this up front: I don't think the Packers' running game was actually that bad on Sunday — at least on the relative scale of Green Bay's running game. Where I really think the team regressed was in the pass protection department, where they played their worst game since at least the first three weeks of the season.

I clipped up all of the runs that the Packers' running backs had in the tweets above, if you want to check them out. There are a couple of poor calls (like the pin and pull sweep that had no chance because of Minnesota's defensive call), but for the most part, Green Bay got a hat on a hat against the Vikings, something we can't say of prior games.

Can they move anyone? No! They never have been able. They have one preferred starter on the field. Can they get in front of people, at least on the ground? The last two weeks are probably the Packers' best back-to-back performances this season.

The first big mistake the Packers made was on this duo run. The diagram above is what Green Bay's offensive line is expecting pre-snap. Because the Vikings stunted their three-technique defensive lineman outside on the play, though, right tackle Anthony Belton never gets off his block, and there's a free runner when the play should have gotten up to the safety level.

It's the second play in the Johnson cutup.

The second big mess-up was on this outside zone toss, where the team handled another stunt poorly. On paper, if everyone stays in the gaps they line up in, the diagram above is what the run should look like. Essentially, offensive linemen try to "scoop" defensive linemen to their play-side, and if there isn't a defender in that gap, they climb to the second level and try to work on backers and safeties, after first securing that no one stunted in their play-side gap pre-snap.

The problem is that the Vikings don't stay in their gaps, and they slant weak on the play. Because of this, there should be three pass-offs. The Packers don't execute a single one of them, leading to all three players getting penetration.

It's the play that you keep seeing screenshots of over and over again. It's the fourth play in the Johnson cutup.

These plays look like crap, but outside of that, the Packers mostly played their rules correctly this week — again, which has not been the case over most of the year. Over the last two weeks, they've found something that worked for them: running counter, a play that allows the offensive linemen to get on train tracks and just block downhill instead of worrying about assignments being exchanged by the Vikings' blitzes and stunts.

Everyone makes a wall of down blocks except for a kickout player (either a pulling guard or a tight end) and an insert player (again, either a pulling guard or tight end). As a reminder, the zone plays that didn't work against Minnesota are what Green Bay ran a lot of in the preseason and through Week 3 of the regular season. They went out of their way to try to get good at what they're doing the worst this year.

On counter and split zone concepts, the Packers had 10 carries for 50 yards (5 yards per carry) on the day. On other concepts, which mostly relied on changing targets post-snap, the Packers were just 6 for 6 (1 YPC).

My guess: Green Bay wanted to punish Minnesota with the ground game this week, particularly counter and split zone runs, but the game got out of hand and they had to ditch that script when they were down three scores. The team played a lot of extra tight end looks in this game, allowing the Vikings to get an extra player in the box on defense.

The passing game is really where the Packers' line struggled, in part because they were playing these multiple tight end sets that added just another variable for Green Bay's line to account for. Against Minnesota, the Packers looked completely lost on the interior offensive line, which was made up of Laken Tomlinson, John Williams and Travis Glover (left to right).

I thought we were over this. From Weeks 4-9, after the disaster that was the Atlanta Falcons game on Monday Night Football, the Packers had just a 35 pressure rate, good for the 16th-lowest in the NFL over this time. They looked like an average pass-protection unit over the last two months.

In the first three weeks of the year, the Packers cracked the 40 percent pressure rate twice, but had done it only once in the past six weeks. It seemed like they turned a corner, but then the Vikings came to town.

Yesterday, Minnesota posted a 60 percent pressure rate, the highest number that Green Bay has allowed all season. In Weeks 1-3 and 10, the Packers have allowed a 46 percent pressure rate overall, the highest mark in the league over those four weeks.

It's hard to know what Green Bay's offensive line will look like moving forward, because we just saw a huge regression after a streak of solid play.

Mind you, this all came with Love's time to throw at just 2.27 seconds against the Vikings, and they still sacked him eight times in this game. For perspective, the average NFL time to throw is around 2.8 seconds, so Love was operating about a half-second faster than the average. And he was still getting popped left and right. Had he held onto the ball at an average rate, the line's pressure rate would have been even higher.

Here are just a few clips of the Packers' offensive line blowing up plays from essentially the snap of the ball on Sunday, just to show how poor it was:

This tweet is supposed to say Belton, not Becton. I apologize to the Becton family.

All three interior offensive linemen had disaster games, looking consistently confused by Minnesota's defense. Right tackle also Anthony Belton struggled, as he's not a good athlete for the tackle position and should ideally be a guard at this level — but the injury to Zach Bako-Bewele forced him into his position this year.

Green Bay's plan to add extra bodies into the formation with multiple tight end sets kind of worked in the run game, where they were able to run counter and split zone runs pretty well against Minnesota, but they couldn't stick with the ground game once they went down multiple points — understandably. What really hurt on the ground was when the offensive line had to think and react to the Vikings' defensive front, and the line wasn't able to execute on those runs.

To make matters worse, the extra tight end (and offensive line) looks helped Minnesota bring an extra body in the box in the passing game, which adds exponentially more options for defensive coordinator Brian Flores for each body he could line up as a threat. Against those pressure fronts, the Packers' interior offensive line's heads were spinning all game.

Next time, maybe LaFleur will want to spread out the formation against those pressure looks, instead of having running backs and tight ends walk up to the line of scrimmage. It's what the Vikings did against Green Bay's pressure fronts, and Minnesota was able to get a good number of explosive plays in those situations, notably on third and long.

The Packers' offensive line just isn't good enough to deal with this many variables post-snap. They're not coached well enough. LaFleur, the head coach, just hasn't accepted that going into the second half of November. The offense looked better when they ditched their game plan in the second half of the third quarter, turning into a three-receiver spread team that ran the hurry-up no-huddle. The Packers went on a little run there and looked like they had a chance to get back into the game until they fumbled the ball on a kickoff return.

The fact that "do what you want, just play fast" led to better results than Green Bay's game plan is a damning indictment of the staff's decisions this week.

Luke Bryan & Jason Aldean Fight $1.4 Million Legal Demand From Nashville Steakhouse Landlord

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The two country stars say they aren't liable for unpaid rent following the closure of E3 Chophouse.

Luke Bryan and Jason Aldean say they aren’t responsible for a hefty real estate judgment against their now-shuttered Nashville steakhouse.

The two country stars filed a motion last month to end the claims brought against them by GBT Realty, the Tennessee property developer that was the landlord for E3 Chophouse. Bryan and Aldean opened the restaurant’s Nashville location in 2019 alongside Adam LaRoche, a retired MLB player who had founded E3 Chophouse in Colorado years earlier.

E3’s Nashville location shut down in February, leading GBT Realty to obtain a $1.4 million judgment against the restaurant for unpaid rent and legal fees. GBT then sued Bryan, Aldean and LaRoche in June, alleging they had signed E3’s lease as guarantors and thus were responsible for footing that bill.

But lawyers for Bryan, Aldean and LaRoche say they’re not on the hook for all that money. Their October court filing, obtained by Billboard, says the restaurant lease was clear that the guarantors’ rent obligations lasted for just five years — and that after that, their only liability to GBT would be for certain construction costs. This amount, they say, comes out to just $59,000.

“The formula provided by the guaranty cap requires simple arithmetic,” wrote the attorneys for Bryan, Aldean and LaRoche. “The court should determine as a matter of law that the guaranty defendants’ liability is limited to the then unamortized portion of the construction allowance.”

Bryan, Aldean and LaRoche are asking a judge to dispose of the case completely with a ruling in their favor. GBT’s reps did not immediately return a request for comment on that request on Monday (Nov. 16). The case is scheduled for a court hearing in January.

Lots of country stars own restaurants and bars in Nashville, from Morgan Wallen to Miranda Lambert to Luke Combs. But with new business ventures come new legal risks, and E3 Chophouse isn’t the only artist-founded establishment to end up in court.

Last month, less than a year after Kane Brown opened Kane Brown’s On Broadway, the restaurant was hit with a $2.4 million lawsuit for allegedly defaulting on its building purchase agreement. Kane Brown’s On Broadway denies the claims and has countersued the seller for breaching confidentiality. The singer himself is not personally involved in that dispute.

Why Broadcom Stock Got Mashed on Monday

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Investors were reallocating capital to more prominent tech stocks.

Broadcom (AVGO -3.92%) is one of the tech hardware manufacturers spearheading the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution, but you wouldn't know that from its stock's performance on Monday. Investors aggressively traded out of the custom chipmaker's equity, sending it to a nearly 4% loss on a day when the S&P 500 index posted a decline of under 2%. Reallocation into certain peer stocks was a major factor in this, as was an analyst's price target cut.

The shock of the latest Nvidia (NVDA -4.12%) developments hasn't yet worn off. This was affecting investor sentiment not only for tech hardware titles, but for the broader industry as a whole.

At the same time, however, Nvidia is the bellwether AI hardware stock for many. And since it's slated to report its next set of quarterly results tomorrow (Tuesday), many market players remain bullish on its prospects despite the tribulations surrounding co-founder and ex-CEO Jensen Huang. With that, they were selling out of some of their other tech holdings in anticipation of blowout financial results from the company. Ditto for the highly anticipated IPO of star AI model developer Anthropic, whose stock should start trading on Thursday.

These developments were exacerbated by that price target reduction. This was made in a research update by TD Cowen's Joshua Buchalter, who trimmed his fair value assessment on Broadcom to $425 per share from $475. This doesn't make him a bear on the stock, however, as he maintained his buy recommendation.

That, to me, is telling. Price target cuts are normal when stocks and industries are in a dip; this doesn't necessarily mean investors should sell out of either.

Analysis: The Fed's chairman sees an AI revolution coming. Now he has to figure out what it means

📅 2026-11-17  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence is about to sweep through the economy. The Federal Reserve is bracing for impact.

"We are at a hinge point in the U.S. economy," Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said at a conference he convened in San Francisco on Monday. But exactly when and how the economy could turn, and what that means for interest rates and inflation, isn't yet clear.

The meeting of Warsh's AI task force saw the new Fed chairman publicly asking the questions Americans will ultimately expect him to answer. He hinted at a few of his private views on whether white-collar workers could soon face massive job losses, but he largely left the speculation to an array of experts he has assembled to advise the Fed.

With the technology racing ahead, Warsh won't be able to put off giving his own answers much longer. AI lab Anthropic plans to price the shares for its initial public offering on Wednesday and could raise as much as $64 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation. Vast spending from AI firms is prompting the Fed to contemplate raising interest rates in December to slow down the rate of price increases as the tech sector heats up the economy.

Meanwhile Americans are souring on some aspects of the technology. The Nov. 3 midterms saw voters enact an array of state-level measures to slow down construction of the data centers that are essential to powering AI.

Warsh moderated three out of four sessions at Monday's event and was involved in selecting the speakers. Among them was one of the few prominent Democrats who was willing to speak out against that anti-data center trend.

It is "a very troubling sign" that so many anti-data center measures passed in the midterms, Gina Raimondo said. She is a former secretary of commerce under President Joe Biden and a former governor of Rhode Island.

Stopping AI "would be very bad for America" and for the people who voted for the measures, Raimondo said.

Warsh didn't say whether he shares that view, and how it would translate into monetary policy isn't exactly clear.

The event was an unusual one for the Fed. The central bank employs a small army of Ph.D. economists who can rapidly weigh in on anything affecting the U.S. and global economies. But in keeping with his longstanding critiques of the Fed as insular and outdated, Warsh has chosen to draw expertise from outside the institution to help it figure out how to think about what AI will do to productivity and jobs.

Warsh has assembled a task force on that issue, alongside four others devoted to aspects of Fed policymaking.

The public heard Monday from one of the three official members of the task force: venture capitalist Marc Andreessen. Warsh went to Stanford University with Andreessen's wife, and the two have been longtime friends. Another task force member, Stanford economist Chad Jones, is on leave and working at Anthropic and couldn't speak during the company's blackout period ahead of the IPO. The third, Xbox CEO Asha Sharma, didn't attend the event.

Andreessen is hugely optimistic about the promise of AI as a technology, but believes the government won't let Americans experience its full effects. Heavy regulation in areas such as healthcare and education mean technology can't replace people's jobs in those sectors, he said.

"To the extent our economy is a free-market economy, then maybe you could see the, let's say, extremely rapid displacement scenarios people are talking about," Andreessen said. "But we've mostly outlawed the kind of job transition that … both the utopians the doomers talk about."

Warsh returned repeatedly to one scenario for the economy that draws on past experience. The so-called China shock unfolded after that country joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and moved heavily into low-cost manufacturing. Americans saw prices on clothing and furniture drop rapidly, for instance, but at the cost of severe job losses. One widely cited estimate found the U.S. lost a million manufacturing jobs and 2.4 million jobs overall in the years that followed.

Warsh suggested to one of the panelists that the widespread adoption of AI might produce its own version of the China shock, this time focused on service workers.

"I don't want to hold this too tightly," Warsh said. "But if in some ways over the last generation or generation and a half, the China shock, for lack of a better word, lowered prices of goods in the United States, the way that I heard you talking about the philosophy of this new technology is it might do something for services."

In other words, AI could do, say, legal or administrative work more cheaply than a human. That could lower prices but put many people out of a job.

Warsh asked panelists whether "we should expect disinflation over the medium term, from what AI could do to services."

They largely suggested it was possible, which could mean the Fed might eventually want to lower interest rates over the long term.

But at the end of the day, it is Warsh and his fellow Fed members who will need to have conviction on the answer.

There are still many steps between the task force discussion and any kind of change in Fed policy. First, the three task force members will have to assemble their points of view into a report for Warsh sometime in the next few months. Then the Fed itself will need to debate those results and decide whether or how to use them to shape monetary policy.

But with AI growing bigger every day, the Fed may need to make up its mind sooner rather than later.
