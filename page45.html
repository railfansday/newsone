UK defence startup Agon raises $70M to simulate the battlefield

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The London company, now valued at $450M, builds virtual testing grounds for drones and other military robots.

British defence startup Agon has raised $70M to expand its software for testing military robots, valuing it at $450M. The pace is striking, as the company was founded in January and it came out of stealth earlier this year with $30M in funding.

Bloomberg first reported the round, which Northzone led with Lakestar, Lux Capital, and XYZ alongside. The UK’s Sovereign AI Fund and its National Security Strategic Investment Fund joined too.

So did two big-name angels: Roelof Botha, the former head of Sequoia Capital, and Unity co-founder David Helgason, who chairs Agon’s board.

Agon builds what it calls a synthetic battlefield. The software lets weapon makers test how their drones, sea vessels, and other robots behave in a given setting.

The idea, much like a simulator for self-driving cars, is to give robots a safe place to make their mistakes.

The new capital will pay for AI models that can run those tests in many more settings. And Agon’s plans don’t stop at its own product.

“We’re going to expand to effectively build out Europe’s defence AI lab,” co-founder and CEO Tristam Constant told Bloomberg, adding that this will be an expensive undertaking.

Constant previously worked at Applied Intuition, one of the simulation companies Agon now competes with. His co-founder Junaid Hussain helped set up Cambridge Aerospace, the British anti-missile startup valued at $3.4bn in August.

Investors have rushed into European defence tech since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine set off a spending surge.

This year they have put a record $7.4bn into the sector’s startups, according to October data from Dealroom and Resilience Media, and Stark Defence‘s €500M round was among the biggest.

In that crowd, Agon pitches itself as the neutral one. It arms nobody, but gives every weapons maker a place to put its machines through their paces.

‘MoldyRoost’ ads can steal crypto wallet recovery phrases from iPhones

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The campaign used at least 480 ad domains to target users in more than 47 countries and could infect a vulnerable iPhone with a single tap.

Hackers are using fake ads to spread malware that can steal crypto wallet recovery phrases from vulnerable iPhones, cybersecurity company Confiant said Friday.

Confiant, which calls the campaign MoldyRoost, linked at least 480 ad domains to it between May 12 and Nov. 17. The ads targeted users in more than 47 countries.

“Just loading the landing page of a MoldyRoost ad on a vulnerable iPhone is enough to get infected,” Confiant said in its report.

Google Threat Intelligence reported in March that hackers were using a tool called Coruna to target visitors to fake crypto sites. MoldyRoost bought ads to spread the same tool on sites unrelated to crypto, where tapping an ad could expose a recovery phrase saved on a vulnerable iPhone.

Coruna can search Apple Notes for wallet seed phrases and read QR codes in saved images. A thief who gets a seed phrase can use those words to open the wallet on another device and move the funds.

The campaign also used DarkSword, another tool that can steal wallet data, which Google researchers also warned of in March.

In a Nov. 6 warning about DarkSword, blockchain security firm CertiK urged iPhone users to update their software and keep seed phrases out of Notes, photos and screenshots. It also advised avoiding unfamiliar links and using a hardware wallet for larger holdings.

Elegoo's Centauri 2 Combo delivers very solid print quality and four-color printing for under $380. Inevitably given that low price, certain corners are cut. None of these are deal-breakers, but there are better printers on the market — including quite a few from Elegoo — for only a little bit more cash. Still, if you're on a tight budget or are a beginner who is interested in multi-color printing, this is a decent choice.

3D printing has been edging closer and closer to the mainstream for years now. It's cheaper than ever to buy a really good, feature-packed machine, while the various manufacturers are constantly working to try and make their products easier to use and more accessible.

Despite that, it can still feel like a comparative niche to newcomers, and getting to grips with your first 3D printer can be... intimidating. Enter Elegoo's Centauri line, which has been around since 2024. These are designed to bring 3D printing to the masses, with easy to use machines at affordable prices.

The Centauri Carbon 2 Combo launched at the start of the year and was a very impressive model for its $449 RRP, but the company has recently upped the ante with an even-more-budget offering that does most of what its predecessor can do for under $380 — and you can already find it cheaper than that in some of the sales. But does that low price point come with any downsides?

Find out in my full Elegoo Centauri 2 Combo review.

It really is quite impressive what this printer can do. It delivers easy setup, good build quality, and multi-color 3D printing at such a low price point.

The most obvious selling point of the Centauri 2 Combo is its four-color printing. You simply attach the spool holders to the top of the machine, pop on your filament and then feed them into each extruder.

If you're using official Elegoo spools with RFID tags, then the CANVAS unit (Elegoo’s filament switching system) on the top of the machine will automatically identify which color is which. You then simply feed the filament into the numbered inlets on the machine. The results are good, with well-defined color changes. My first print was a classic Benchy, which printed quickly and cleanly in 45 minutes.

Next up, I printed two Daleks from Doctor Who, based on a nice fan-made model that I found on Cults3D by user wayne_peters.

The red one was printed using the factory standard printer settings. For the yellow I tweaked several settings for surface ironing and adaptive layers in the hope of getting a smoother print. That significantly upped the print time, but didn't deliver massively different results. If you’re noticing some rough edges, those were me being a bit slapdash post print, not an issue with the printer.

One thing to note is that printing models with multiple colors will significantly increase the amount of waste filament produced, because of the way the printer has to switch between colors. That's not a criticism of the Centauri 2 Combo, it's just the nature of the technology. If you are concerned about filament wastage then printing models with multi-colored parts may not be for you.

The Centauri 2 Combo arrived securely packaged and was very easy to assemble. I'd say it was a seamless experience, except for one small but really quite frustrating aspect that added a good 15 minutes of swearing onto the build.

That was attaching the filament hub. You have to fix it into place with two tiny screws, one of which feels almost impossible to get into position because of the way the cable drag chain is positioned. I really struggled with this, almost losing the screw several times, before finally somehow managing to get it into place.

Thankfully after that, everything ran perfectly smoothly. When you first switch the machine on it runs through a self-check and calibration, as well as levelling the bed automatically. That really takes the stress out of the setup and is another reason why this is a good machine for beginners.

After setup, you need to install two pieces of software: ElegooSlicer on your computer and Elegoo Matrix on a smartphone.

ElegooSlicer is the software used to work on your 3D models (which can be downloaded from sites like Printables, Makerworld, and Elegoo's own Nexprint) before exporting them ready to be printed. It's based on the open-source OrcaSlicer and is intuitive enough for newcomers to get to grips with quickly.

Elegoo Matrix enables you to monitor and control your printer remotely and is pretty fool proof. Again, you get the sense of a system that has been designed with novices in mind.

I can't stress how straightforward this printer is, as it does most of the hard work for you automatically. If this is your first 3D printer then it won't take you very long to get used to it, and if you've used one of Elegoo's other printers before, particularly any of the Centauri models, then you'll have no worries

I printed the Halloween skeleton candlestick below, found on Nexprint, in a little over seven hours — pretty good for such an intricate design!

A curvy vase, also from Nexprint, was equally straightforward and printed in just over three hours, with no need for the supports that you would typically need to include with complex prints. I've since used the machine to print out some gaming terrain as well as for practical applications like paint storage for my Warhammer paints and storage boxes.

Over several weeks of use and around 30 prints I had only one fail. I cancelled the print, tried again and the second time it worked with no problems.

Despite the low price point, the Centauri 2 Combo doesn't feel cheap. There are some cut corners — we'll come to those in a moment — but it's a reassuringly solid piece of kit, robustly built from a die-cast aluminum frame and steel body. Once again, it recalls Elegoo's bestselling Centauri 2 Combo Carbon in its design, which feels similarly solid.

The build-plate is double-sided (textured on side A and smooth on side B) and made from magnetic spring steel that's easy to keep clean. The toolhead comes with a standard 0.4mm hardened steel nozzle, which can be swapped out for different sizes, which are sold separately. You will also need to buy a nozzle wrench to do that.

It feels almost churlish to complain about the Centauri 2 Combo as it's really very good at what it sets out to do — and there's no arguing with that aggressive price point. Even so, there are a few reasons why you might want to consider shelling out for a slightly more expensive machine.

Probably the biggest talking point with the Centauri 2 Combo is the price — but it comes at the cost of several features that you'd usually find on a 3D printer.

The most obvious of these omissions is the lack of an enclosure. The panels, glass door and hood are all absent, meaning that the print bed is exposed for all to see. In this way it echoes the design of several competitors, including Anycubic's Kobra X and Creality's 3D SparkX i7 Combo. That said, I much prefer the neat and tidy design of the Centauri 2 Combo.

There's something quite cool about being able to watch your latest print materialise right there in front of you without it being hidden away behind glass, but enclosures are useful for maintaining temperatures and containing the fumes from a print. If you're sticking to printing with standard PLA it's no biggie (though please still ventilate your space), but if you're using other types of filament you may feel more comfortable investing in a printer that comes with an enclosure.

The other downside is that you'll also have to make sure you either cover the printer after use, or dust it regularly.

Other features that are absent from this model include a camera (fun and useful for monitoring your prints while away from home, and something that is notably included on the Creality 3D SparkX i7 Combo) and internal lights.

Neither of these things are deal-breakers and I think newcomers, in particular, will find a lot to love in the Centauri 2 Combo. But if you're serious about 3D printing then you may find yourself quickly considering upgrading to a more capable machine. The Centauri 2 Combo is entering an increasingly crowded 3D printer market — with much of the competition coming from Elegoo itself.

The obvious competitor here is the almost identically titled Elegoo Centauri Carbon 2 Combo. For a mere $70 more right now you can pick that machine up and enjoy a printer with an enclosure, wider filament compatibility, camera and a HEPA + Activated Carbon filter.

Single-color printing with PLA tends to be fairly efficient, when it comes to waste. That changes significantly when you move to multi-color printing, however, because of the way the printer has to switch between colors. That's not a criticism of the Centauri 2 Combo, it's just the nature of the technology.

Basically, when the printer needs to change colors, it retracts the filament being currently used, nips the end off it and ejects the waste, before loading in the new color. If you're printing something with a lot of differently-colored parts (even if you're just using 2 or 3 colors) then it is going to do this a lot. Your waste basked will fill up quickly and if you are concerned about filament wastage (which is difficult to recycle) then printing models with multi-colored parts may not be for you. The picture above is mostly the waste from my two model Daleks!

One way to get around this, if you're printing model kits or things that can be glued together, is by printing each color separately. That will save you both print time and filament wastage, but will not be possible with everything you might want to print.

The Elegoo Centauri 2 Combo is defined by its price point. It's pretty remarkable that you can get a good four-color 3D printer for under over $400 (and sometimes a lot under that price, with Elegoo's occasional discounts). That figure may prove very tempting for those looking to get into printing for the first time.

The price, however, is also behind some of the machine's rougher edges. The lack of an enclosure may not seem like much, but it means that the Centauri 2 Combo really needs to be positioned in a well-ventilated room if you don't want to be inhaling fumes all the time. I can't quibble too much at the lack of a camera or internal lights — at this price, those are nice to have features, rather than essentials.

If you're serious about 3D printing then there are more fully-featured machines out there that may catch your eye. But if you’re new to all this and want a printer that can handle everyday use, as well as more complex prints, it really is hard to find much to grumble about here.

Endor Labs CEO warns AI is making enterprise patching more dangerous

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The pressure to patch vulnerabilities quickly is creating another risk: breaking the systems companies depend on to operate. Only 10% of upgrades or patches could be applied without additional work.

Varun Badhwar, CEO and co-founder of Endor Labs

AI is changing the economics of cybersecurity, but the consequences may not be limited to faster attacks or more sophisticated vulnerability discovery. As enterprises race to secure increasingly complex software environments, the pressure to patch vulnerabilities quickly is creating another risk: breaking the systems companies depend on to operate.

Endor Labs CEO and co-founder Varun Badhwar warned in his recent LinkedIn post that 99% of technology-forward enterprises will experience a breach in the next 12 months, while raising concerns that rushed security updates could trigger operational disruptions of their own. The prediction reflects a growing imbalance between attackers’ ability to exploit vulnerabilities and enterprises’ ability to safely address them.

Badhwar’s concern is not simply that security teams are falling behind. It is that fixing software vulnerabilities has become increasingly difficult to do without introducing new problems.

The scale of vulnerability discovery is changing dramatically. Badhwar points to the fact that the first 100,000 Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) took approximately 18 and a half years to publish, from the CVE List’s launch in September 1999 until April 2018. In 2026, another 100,000 CVEs were published in just 322 days, crossing that threshold on November 18 with weeks remaining in the year.

For enterprises already managing hundreds of thousands of vulnerabilities, the increase creates a daunting challenge. Attackers only need one successful entry point, while defenders must account for vulnerabilities across an entire environment.

The imbalance is compounded by shrinking exploitation timelines. According to Badhwar, Google Mandiant reported that the mean time to exploit had reached negative seven days, suggesting attackers were increasingly exploiting vulnerabilities before public disclosure by reverse-engineering patches.

Meanwhile, security teams are dealing with remediation timelines that stretch far longer. Badhwar cites Verizon’s 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found remediation times rising to 43 days from 32 days previously, while only 26% of vulnerabilities were fully remediated.

The result is a widening window in which attackers can act before enterprises have completed their response.

The urgency to patch is understandable, but software updates can carry their own operational risks. Enterprises often maintain applications built on years of accumulated dependencies. A security fix may require upgrading components that introduce breaking changes elsewhere in the application.

Badhwar says Endor Labs studied more than 250,000 open vulnerabilities found in open-source dependencies across customer environments, including financial services and technology organizations. The research examined whether teams could move to fixed versions without making additional changes to their applications.

The finding was that only 10% of upgrades or patches could be applied without additional work to address breaking changes. A fix may be available. But implementing it safely can require developers to modify code, test integrations and validate functionality across interconnected systems.

As Badhwar explained, “When teams are rushing one of the easiest things to bypass is testing. These are complex systems, so even minor changes can result in cascading problems.”

That creates a difficult trade-off for security leaders. Moving too slowly leaves vulnerabilities exposed, while moving too quickly can destabilize production environments, disrupt customer-facing services or introduce additional weaknesses.

Badhwar believes AI could help break this cycle, provided it is used to understand software rather than simply generate patches.

AI coding agents are increasingly capable of producing functional code, but functionality does not necessarily translate into secure remediation. In his interview, Badhwar referenced a benchmark in which Fable 5.1 achieved 87% functional code performance but only 37% secure code performance.

He argues that giving AI agents access to application context, including call graphs that show how code interacts with open-source components, can improve their ability to identify and address vulnerabilities without unnecessarily changing the wider application.

Endor Labs’ approach through AURI Fix is designed to evaluate the paths through which vulnerabilities can be reached and exploited, then determine whether the safest remediation involves upgrading a dependency, modifying application code, applying a patched library or replacing a component.

Rather than automatically pushing an extensive software upgrade, the system can make targeted changes to developers’ own code, sometimes involving fewer than 10 lines, to break an exploitable pathway while teams work on a broader fix.

For enterprise leaders, the implications extend beyond vulnerability counts or the number of tickets closed. Badhwar argues that organizations need to measure whether they are actually reducing exposure without sacrificing service availability.

He recommends tracking three indicators: how long internet-facing systems remain exposed to known exploited vulnerabilities, how often emergency fixes cause outages or require rollback, and how quickly critical services can be restored during recovery exercises.

Ultimately, the challenge facing cybersecurity leaders is no longer simply how quickly they can deploy a fix. It is how to make security improvements without creating new operational risks. As AI accelerates both vulnerability discovery and remediation, the ability to prioritize, validate and safely implement changes may become just as important as the speed at which enterprises respond.

For Badhwar, the next frontier of AI in cybersecurity is not generating more vulnerabilities or simply producing patches faster. It is helping enterprises close security gaps without compromising the systems they are trying to protect.

Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have helped people living with HIV live longer, contributing to a growing population of older adults with the virus.

In fact, a recent report published in The Lancet HIV projects that approximately 51% of people living with HIV worldwide will be over age 50 by 2040. An estimated 96% of these older adults will live in low- and middle-income countries.

The report’s authors emphasized the “pressing need” for HIV prevention and screening services tailored to older adults, who face additional age-related challenges when accessing care.

These challenges include stigma related to both age and HIV, a higher burden of chronic health conditions, taking multiple medications, delayed diagnoses, physical limitations, and increased financial and social vulnerability.

Factors like these “influence HIV risk for older adults in complex and dynamic ways,” according to the report authors.

Many adults over age 50 who are living with HIV acquired the virus during young adulthood in the late 1990s and early 2000s. During that period, some experienced prolonged periods of uncontrolled HIV and exposure to older ART medications with more severe side effects, which may have contributed to long-term inflammation and other health complications.

William Schaffner, MD, professor in the Division of Infectious Diseases at Vanderbilt University, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “That older persons with HIV are on track to outnumber younger persons with HIV infection is a double-barreled success story.”

“First, the effective treatment of persons with HIV infection has resulted in the expectation of normal or near-normal lifespans; thus, more older persons living with HIV infection,” he said. “This is a great success; both in creating effective, easy-to-use medicines as well as developing medical care systems that can reach such patients and provide lifetime care.”

Schaffner said the second success has been educating younger people about HIV prevention, helping reduce new infections.

Older adults also account for a growing proportion of new HIV diagnoses. They are more likely to receive a diagnosis later in the course of infection, which can delay treatment and increase the risk of serious health complications and death.

Mathematical models project that the average age of people living with HIV worldwide will increase from 41 in 2025 to 50 by 2040, largely driven by demographic shifts in low- and middle-income countries.

While the World Health Organization (WHO) does not specify an upper age limit for HIV testing in its published overview, U.S. guidelines focus on specific age groups.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends routine HIV testing for people ages 13 to 64, and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends testing for people ages 15 to 65.

However, these age limits don’t mean older adults shouldn’t be tested for HIV, as both U.S. organizations also recommend testing older adults who may be at risk.

Older adults can still be sexually active and have a chance of acquiring HIV. Certain physical changes that come with age, such as thinning of vaginal tissue, may also increase the risk of HIV infection.

Despite these risks, older adults are often overlooked in HIV prevention campaigns, research, and clinical trials. The report’s authors say more needs to be done to address these gaps.

“Targeted and tailored HIV prevention and screening services for older adults are a pressing global need,” the report authors stated.

Schaffner agreed with this and added, “Increasing the effectiveness of diagnosis and treatment of HIV infection in older adults will increase their expectation of a healthier, longer life.”

The company’s co:system combines sensing, motion, power and battery modules on one EtherCAT bus, with centralized real-time control and a common developer API. Published specifications list a 100 Mb/s bus, a typical 1 kHz control loop and jitter, or timing variation, below 20 microseconds.

The hardware can compose into a complete robot or integrate separately into an existing one. Coprod designs, builds and tests it in Switzerland.

The Lausanne facility will house CNC lathes, mills, lasers and electronic testing equipment. Coprod says the workshop is designed to shorten development cycles from two weeks to 24 hours while producing datasheets and digital-twin models for each component.

In an April 2, 2026 Venture Kick interview, the founders said the co:dodo robot became operational shortly before Christmas 2025 after about three months of evening and weekend work.

Coprod says its components are already used in Swiss robots, including Switzerland’s first student-built humanoid from the ETH Robotics Club, which has taken its first steps in Zurich. The company is establishing partnerships with robotics integrators and laboratories to validate prototypes ahead of a commercial launch planned for early 2027.

By the end of 2027, coprod plans to have its entire component line qualified, certified and on the market. Large-scale production would take place in Shenzhen and Texas to provide regional supply chains, while its headquarters, core team and research and development remain in Switzerland.

Valon Behrami non risparmia critiche a Djed Spence. L'ex centrocampista, oggi opinionista di DAZN, ha espresso un giudizio molto severo sul giocatore dell'Inter, soffermandosi soprattutto sull'atteggiamento mostrato al momento del suo arrivo in nerazzurro.

Secondo Behrami, Spence si sarebbe presentato con aspettative eccessive rispetto al ruolo che dovrebbe ricoprire all'interno della squadra. Il commentatore ha utilizzato un paragone particolarmente duro con il mondo dello spettacolo e con l'NBA.

LE PAROLE DI BEHRAMI – «Spence? Penso che non ci avrei fatto sicuramente amicizia… Sembra che sia arrivato qui da La La Land o da Hollywood, l'ho visto anche all'inizio, quando è arrivato all'Inter: sembrava un rapper di Hollywood… La sensazione è che qui è un gregario, e invece si sente come la star che tutti aspettavano.

Questo è stato l'atteggiamento iniziale, poi è migliorato, ci ha messo un po' di tempo, ha avuto dei problemi, ma mi sembra che stia rientrando in carreggiata. Di sicuro il primo impatto non è stato positivo: qui non è l'NBA, dove conta più l'intrattenimento che il risultato. Adesso ha la possibilità di dimostrare sul campo quello che si sente addosso, ma è difficile arrivare ai livelli che lui pensa. Non mi piace quando si presentano così. Tornando ai 32,5 come picco di velocità massima: ce ne saranno 100 che hanno raggiunto quel valore ad oggi…».

Per l'ex centrocampista, il problema riguarda soprattutto la distanza tra le aspettative con cui il calciatore si sarebbe presentato e il livello che, a suo giudizio, deve ancora dimostrare di poter raggiungere.

Et de deux pour Wadji

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

L'attaquant sénégalais de 31 ans, l'ancien Vert Ibrahima Wadji, avait  claqué son premier pion de la saison 5 minutes après son entrée en jeu lors du match nul de son équipe Turan Tovuk sur le terrain d'Araz Nakhchivan en septembre dernier. Hier, il a récidivé en parachevant, à la 87ème minute, la victoire (0-2) de son club sur le terrain du FC Shamakhi, qui lui permet d'accéder au podium du championnat d'Azerbaïdjan.

Ibrahima Wadji, désormais titulaire à la pointe de l'attaque du Turan Tovuk, avait déjà marqué contre le FC Shamakhi, lors de 32e et avant-dernière journée de la saison 2025-2026 de Premyer Liqa.

Turan Tovuk aurait dû disputer la Ligue Europa Conférence, mais l'UEFA a exclu le club azerbaïdjanais suite à l'implication de ses joueurs dans des affaires de manipulation de matchs par le passé. L'Association azerbaïdjanaise de football avait d'ailleurs déjà suspendu sept joueurs du club en 2019 pour des accusations de manipulation.
