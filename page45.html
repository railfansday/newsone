12 Best Acting Performances In Steven Spielberg Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Steven Spielberg is one of, if not the most iconic directors working today. The filmmaker’s first project dates all the way back to 1971 with the release of Duel on ABC (a direct to television film), and he has since gone on to helm countless other titles. In total, Spielberg has directed a whopping 36 projects, and is still active to this day, with the release of Disclosure Day coming earlier this year.

Many of the classic films from the last forty years can be attributed to Spielberg. The director helmed 1993s Jurassic Park, for example, and he also did the first four Indiana Jones films, along with Schindler’s List, Saving Private Ryan, Jaws, and many, many more. Impressively, Spielberg has switched genres frequently in his career, never staying in one area for too long (his two most recent projects, for example, are The Fablemans and Disclosure Day, and both are vastly different).

Spielberg isn’t just known for his pure directing talents, however. He’s also made a reputation for himself as one of the best “actor’s directors” working today; that is, having the ability to work with all kinds of unique performers and get the best out of them each and every single time.

There’s plenty of evidence to back up that claim, too. Just as some of the best movies of the last forty years have come from Spielberg, some of the best performances from actors have come in these projects as well. And while the actors naturally deserve great credit for these turns, Spielberg should also get his plaudits for being the mastermind behind these performances.

One of Spielberg’s earlier films was the iconic Jaws. Everyone knows the story of this one — a giant shark circles the waters of a coastal town, with a small group of townsfolk warning others of the dangers, but are up against a mayor who is unwilling to shut down the beaches.

Interestingly, Spielberg reportedly assembled a small group of A-list actors that he wanted to appear in the film. At the same time, the director was also hesitant to choose anyone too well known, as he didn’t want their fame to overshadow the movie. As such, the likes of Robert Redford, Paul Newman, and Steve McQueen, all names Spielberg had in mind, were not in Jaws.

One name that was in Jaws, however, is Robert Shaw. The English actor was well known at the time, both in the United Kingdom and the United States (although he was especially famous in the former, his homeland). Before Jaws, Shaw had appeared in the likes of From Russia With Love, The Taking of Pelham One Two Three, and A Man for All Seasons (for which he received an Academy Award nomination).

In Jaws, Shaw plays Quint, a grizzled and hardened former soldier who survived a massive shipwreck during World War II. He, very famously, has a strong hatred for sharks, but along with that, he also carries extensive knowledge of these animals as well. Shaw himself is arguably the standout performer of the film, especially because of his speech on the USS Indianapolis, in which he says that sharks have “lifeless eyes, black eyes, like a doll’s eyes.”

Another standout performer from Jaws is the film’s lead hero, Roy Schneider, who plays Chief Martin Brody. Like Shaw, Jaws ended up being the biggest role of Schneider’s career (and one he would reprise in future sequels, including Jaws II).

Ironically, Chief Brody has a deep-rooted fear of the water, which makes his quest for the shark even more intense. The audience can truly feel Brody’s anxiety and stress through Schneider’s performance, which, in turn, makes the movie feel more lived in and real. At the same time, Schneider does brilliantly to ensure his character isn’t defined by fear, making this one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg movie.

Just four years after Jaws, Steven Spielberg launched what ended up being another one of his most classic projects. Spielberg developed the character of Indiana Jones alongside George Lucas and Lawrence Kasdan, and then helmed Jones’ first movie, Raiders of the Lost Ark.

Harrison Ford’s first performance as Indiana Jones remains just as iconic as it was nearly fifty years ago. Ford exudes confidence and swagger, making Indiana Jones one of the very best heroes in movie history. After the first Star Wars movie in 1977, it would have been hard to predict that Ford would come up with another character just as iconic, if not more so than Han Solo, but he does just that in Raiders of the Lost Ark (thanks, in part, to Steven Spielberg’s stellar direction).

As far as intimate projects go, arguably no film in recent history has been as meaningful to its director as The Fablemans. The 2022 film serves as Spielberg’s very own origin story, showing his upbringing and how he became interested in filmmaking in the first place. His parents play big roles in the film, especially his mother, who’s played by Michelle Williams, in what is one of the finest performances of her entire career.

Williams has given many memorable turns over the years, but The Fablemans truly stands out. Williams is truly outstanding in this film, playing a character who is so conflicted (and someone that Spielberg himself clearly has complicated feelings about). It’s far from the easiest role to play, especially when factoring in that this is her director’s mother, but Williams deserves great credit.

Arguably the main attraction of Jurassic Park as a film are the dinosaurs. The special effects were exceptional in 1993 and remain so to this day, and along with Spielberg’s direction, help to make these prehistoric creatures feel truly alive. Yet, at the same time, the human characters are just as impressive in Jurassic Park, particularly Jeff Goldblum’s Dr. Ian Malcom.

Malcom is a mathematician who excels in chaos theory, and is highly skeptical about John Hammond’s Earth-shattering invention right from the get-go. Goldblum plays Malcom tremendously, making him a witty character that, at the same time, also demands to be taken seriously. Goldblum helps tie the entire movie together, in fact, making this another top-tier performance in a Steven Spielberg film.

Spielberg incredibly made not one, but two incredible films in 1993. One was the aforementioned Jurassic Park, while the other was a vastly different story — Schindler’s List. The film told the true story of a German industrialist named Oskar Schindler who put his life on the line to save thousands of Jewish people from the Holocaust.

Liam Neeson plays Schindler in what is likely the finest performance of his entire career. There’s so much emotion entrenched in Schindler’s List, and that doesn’t just come from the script or Spielberg’s direction (both of which are excellent). Neeson is extraordinary here, proving himself more than capable of delivering an iconic dramatic performance.

Another iconic 1990s film from Spielberg is Saving Private Ryan, which focuses on a mission to save Private James Francis Ryan (Matt Damon) after his three brothers were killed during the Battle of Normandy. Ryan is the core subject of the film, but Tom Hanks’ Captain John Miller is the one leading the mission.

Hanks is often labeled as one of the best actors to ever live, and Saving Private Ryan is often cited as evidence for this. It’s for good reason, too, as Hanks is this film’s standout. Despite Hanks’ immense fame, it’s easy to forget that he’s there at all, and instead, audiences are likely to simply see Captain Miller, which makes Saving Private Ryan feel all the more realistic.

Arguably the most divisive entry on this list, Tom Cruise received mixed responses to his performance as the leading protagonist in Spielberg’s War of the Worlds remake. The film itself received mixed reception, too, but Cruise does deserve a lot of credit for his performance here. Cruise plays a single dad incredibly well, and embodies what it feels like to be a parent during moments of danger.

One of the very best movies that Steven Spielberg ever made also features Tom Hanks - Catch Me if You Can. The film, which centers on Leonardo DiCaprio’s Frank Abagnale Jr., is actually a true story that you have to see to believe. DiCaprio’s Abagnale Jr. is eccentric, coming up with lies on the spot. As a result, he lives several different lives throughout the film, all with the FBI hot on his trail.

Hanks is, predictably, excellent as the FBI agent leading the charge. Yet, he’s outshone by DiCaprio, only in his early 20s at this time. And despite the fact that this was relatively early on in DiCaprio’s career, it remains one of his very best, and one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg film.

Whoopi Goldberg had her breakout role in Steven Spielberg’s 1985 adaption of The Color Purple. It’s no wonder that Goldberg became a star after, too, as she steals the film, vastly improving the story and making it more than worth the watch. A huge reason for this is the pain that Goldberg embodies throughout, making her character feel like a truly three-dimensional and real human being.

Houston Preview

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kansas played West Virginia in Las Vegas Wednesday night, and now the Jayhawks get Houston on Thursday. Two Big 12 opponents in two days, with neither game counting toward the Big 12 standings. If we win this one, do we get to hang a banner for winning the November Big 12 championship? Asking for little brother.

Anyway, Kansas survived West Virginia 86-82 Wednesday night, overcoming an 11-point second-half deficit to remain undefeated. Houston, meanwhile, knocked off #1 Florida 86-76 to advance to the Players Era 8 championship game.

Kansas: 5-0, #11 AP / #9 Coaches / #3 KenPom

Houston: 5-0, #8 AP / #7 Coaches / #1 KenPom

When: Thursday, November 19, 10:00 PM CST

Where: Michelob ULTRA Arena, Las Vegas, Nevada

Kansas leads the all-time series 7-6, but Houston won the most recent meeting 69-47 in the Big 12 Tournament semifinals last March. That wasn't particularly enjoyable.

Houston is in its 13th season (!!!) under Kelvin Sampson, who has turned the Cougars into one of the most consistently successful programs in the country. They've reached two Final Fours under Sampson, including the 2025 national championship game, and are once again among the nation's best teams. The Cougars opened this season with wins over La Salle, Cal, and Jacksonville State before beating Rutgers 85-73 and Florida 86-76 in Las Vegas.

The Florida victory was particularly impressive. Houston trailed by three at halftime, then outscored the Gators 49-36 in the second half. Dedan Thomas Jr. scored 30 points, and the Cougars held Florida to just 18 points in the paint while scoring 44 of their own.

Kansas has also faced some legitimate competition. The Jayhawks beat Kentucky by 20, handled UNLV by 25, and then needed a second-half comeback to escape West Virginia.

Both teams are undefeated. Both have already beaten quality opponents.

And one of them gets to leave Las Vegas with a 6-0 record.

Through five games, here's how the teams compare offensively (KU / Houston):

Effective field-goal percentage (eFG%): 61.1% / 55.6%

Offensive rebound rate (ORB%): 35.0% / 42.9%

Kansas has been the more efficient shooting team, but Houston has done a better job protecting the basketball and has a considerable advantage on the offensive glass. The Cougars have recovered nearly 43% of their available offensive rebounds, which is a fairly terrifying number considering that KU may be missing one of its best rebounders.

Both teams also get to the free-throw line frequently, but here's a rather alarming difference: Houston is shooting 81.9% from the charity stripe, while Kansas is making just 63.0% of its attempts. That's nearly a 19-percentage-point difference, and in a close game, it could be enormous.

And now we're up to five games of data. Progress!

1. Can Kansas score efficiently against Houston's defense?

Kansas is averaging 92.2 points per game and shooting 55.4% from the field. Those are excellent numbers, and the Jayhawks have demonstrated that they can score against legitimate competition. But Houston isn't West Virginia or UNLV. The Cougars are allowing just 63.6 points per game, holding opponents to 37.8% shooting, and forcing more than 15 turnovers per contest.

And this isn't exactly a new development. Kelvin Sampson has built his program around physical defense, relentless pressure, and making opponents work for everything they get.

Kansas scored 86 points against West Virginia despite making only three of its 11 three-point attempts. That's encouraging, particularly after the Jayhawks struggled from the perimeter against UNLV. But KU also attempted 40 free throws against the Mountaineers, making 27. I'm not sure we should count on another 40 opportunities at the line against Houston.

Tre White scored 21 against WVU, but he needed 18 field-goal attempts to get there. Trey Kaufman-Renn added 19, and Keanu Dawes contributed 18.

The good news is that Kansas found enough offense without Tyran Stokes.

The bad news is that Houston should make generating those opportunities considerably more difficult.

2. Can Kansas survive the rebounding battle?

This might be the matchup that worries me most.

Houston has collected 66 offensive rebounds through five games, compared with 56 for Kansas. The Cougars have outrebounded their opponents by nearly 12 per game and are converting missed shots into additional scoring opportunities at an impressive rate.

Joseph Tugler is the player to watch here. The 6-foot-8 senior is one of the country's best defensive players, averaging 6.8 rebounds, 1.8 steals, and 1.6 blocks per game. Against Florida, he recorded 12 points, 10 rebounds, three steals, and three assists. Delrecco Gillespie provides additional size and rebounding, while Houston's guards are willing to contribute on the glass as well.

Kansas has plenty of size of its own. Dawes grabbed 12 rebounds against West Virginia, and Kaufman-Renn and Christian Reeves give the Jayhawks legitimate frontcourt options.

But Stokes missed Wednesday's game with a bone bruise in his knee, and his availability for Houston remains uncertain. That matters. Stokes averages 8.0 rebounds per game, and Kansas could certainly use his athleticism against a team as physical as Houston.

Even if Stokes plays, the Jayhawks will have to finish defensive possessions with rebounds. Giving Houston two or three chances to score on the same possession seems like a particularly unpleasant way to spend a Thursday evening.

Dedan Thomas Jr., a senior transfer from UNLV, is averaging 20.8 points and 5.2 assists per game. He's shooting 55.0% from the field and scored 30 against Florida, making 10 of 17 shots and all eight of his free throws. He's also committed just six turnovers in five games. That's the sort of efficiency that makes life difficult for opposing defenses.

Houston isn't especially dependent on three-point shooting. Only 24.9% of its field-goal attempts have come from beyond the arc, compared with 33.2% for Kansas. The Cougars prefer to attack inside, draw fouls, and create opportunities around the basket.

Turns out, they're pretty good at it. Against Florida, Houston attempted only nine threes. The Cougars still scored 86 points, while making 25 of 27 free throws and committing just six turnovers.

Kansas just watched West Virginia freshman Miles Sadler score 34 points by repeatedly attacking the defense. Thomas is an even more established offensive threat, and he has considerably more help around him. With Melvin Council Jr. still unavailable, Taylen Kinney and Leroy Blyden Jr. will have their hands full.

Kansas doesn't necessarily need to shut Thomas down. That might not be realistic. But the Jayhawks have to make him work for his points and avoid letting Houston's offense operate comfortably for 40 minutes.

Because this isn't a team you want to spend the entire second half chasing.

Five games into the season, Kansas looks considerably better than I think most of us expected.

The Jayhawks have legitimate scoring options, improved frontcourt depth, and enough offensive versatility to win even when the three-pointers aren't falling. Wednesday's comeback against West Virginia was encouraging, particularly without Stokes. But Houston is a different animal.

The Cougars defend, rebound, take care of the basketball, and have an outstanding point guard. Case in point, they just beat the #1 team in the country, scoring 86 points while shooting 3-for-9 from behind the arc. That's what you call "concerning."

Kansas can absolutely win this game. If White and Kaufman-Renn score efficiently, the Jayhawks hold their own on the glass, and the guards handle Houston's pressure, there's no reason KU can't leave Las Vegas with the championship.

But with Stokes' availability uncertain, Council already sidelined, and both teams playing their third game in three days, I'm having a hard time talking myself into another Kansas victory.

93% of Audited Merchant Accounts Missed Visa CEDP Discounts, Verisave and Canyon Payments Analysis Finds

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Report finds most audited accounts are still paying penalty rates and traces the losses to data-flow errors merchants can fix without switching processors

SALT LAKE CITY, Nov. 19, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- A year after Visa began enforcing its Commercial Enhanced Data Program (CEDP), many businesses that accept commercial cards are still paying higher interchange rates on transactions that could qualify for discounts. A new report from Verisave, a payments optimization and cost-reduction firm, produced with Canyon Payments, a payment services and integration partner, finds that 93% of 180 merchant accounts Verisave analyzed in a single month missed CEDP discounts. Together, they lost more than $800,000 in savings that month.

The report, "State of the Industry: Visa CEDP," finds that the payments industry moved quickly to adapt when Visa introduced CEDP in 2025, but the businesses paying the fees largely did not. Visa began charging a 5-basis-point participation fee on Level 2 and Level 3 volume in April 2025 and switched on automated data validation in October 2025. Level 2 rates for small business cards ended in January 2026, and Level 2 ended for corporate and purchasing cards in April 2026.

The penalty for falling short is steep. A $500 commercial or purchasing card transaction that qualifies for Visa's verified Product 3 rate costs about $8.85 in interchange (including the 5-basis-point participation fee). Downgraded to the non-compliant rate, the same transaction still incurs the participation fee and costs $13.60, an increase of 54%. Those downgrades land on top of the record $198.25 billion U.S. merchants paid in card processing fees in 2025, according to Nilson Report data cited by the Merchants Payments Coalition.

"A year in, the businesses most affected by CEDP are the ones that have done the least about it," said Jeremy Layton, Founder of Verisave. "Many finance teams saw their costs go up and assumed it was simply the new price of accepting commercial cards. Usually it isn't. The data is breaking somewhere between the merchant's systems and the processor, and that can be fixed without switching providers."

Some businesses have responded by passing the cost on to customers. One Verisave client that was losing nearly $500,000 a month to CEDP cited the program as the main reason it began surcharging card payments. The report notes a limit to that approach: card brand rules cap surcharges at 3%, and a business that misses CEDP rates is likely to end up with an effective rate above that cap. Surcharging alone can't recover the full cost.

The report finds that most failures are structural rather than the result of merchant error or fraud.
