Two-Thirds of Dementia Content On TikTok Is Misleading, Researchers Say

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new survey suggests that about two-thirds of dementia advice videos on TikTok are misleading

Of the videos examined, the analysis shows that nearly half contained inaccurate and potentially damaging information.

The researchers say that approximately 44% of TikTok creators who share dementia content lack credibility and appropriate qualifications.

Although misleading dementia content may appear on other social media platforms, this research examined only TikTok content, warranting further investigation.

The survey was commissioned by Homewatch Caregivers and has not been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

A spokesperson for TikTok told Healthline that if health-related content is potentially harmful, it is flagged for removal. The spokesperson added that 99.6% of misinformation deemed violative was proactively removed from the platform in 2026.

The spokesperson added that TikTok relies on fact-checking and guidance from public health authorities to help assess the accuracy of content. The platform may also apply warning labels to content that cannot be verified as accurate.

Looking to social media for health information is not uncommon. A recent poll reports that 31% of adults in the United States use social media at least once a month to look for health information and advice.

Experts advise seeking information about dementia from trusted sources, such as a primary care doctor and organizations like the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and the Alzheimer’s Association, rather than social media, where content creators who share advice often lack expertise.

“It’s normal to want answers quickly when you’re caring for someone living with dementia, and a short video from someone who seems to know what they’re talking about can be really convincing,” said lead researcher Jennifer Winarski, senior manager of care operations and quality for Homewatch CareGivers.

The Customer Paid. Then Silence: Five Updates a Small Business Forgets to Send

The customer paid on Monday. By Friday, silence. The update lives in your head, not in a system — you meant to send it, you know exactly what it would have said, and the week filled up before you sent it. The customer is not angry yet. They are doing something worse: they are wondering. Wondering whether the money arrived, whether the work started, whether the date still holds, whether anyone is there at all. A paid customer who hears nothing starts to feel like a forgotten one, and a forgotten customer is one message away from becoming someone else's customer.

This is the twenty-seventh morning we have written about this gap, and the gap is still the same: the work gets done, and the telling does not.

A small business does not have a follow-up problem. It has five follow-up problems, in five places, and each one looks small on its own. The confirmation is only a sentence. The progress note is only a line. The delay warning is only a message. But a customer who gets none of the five hears nothing at all, and nothing at all sounds exactly like nobody cares — even when the work is going perfectly.

Here are the five updates, numbered, each one with the tools people already use. Each one starts with something ordinary. Each one ends with the same question: who was supposed to hear this?

The money arrived on Monday. The customer knows they paid; what they do not know is whether you know. A payment confirmation is the shortest message in business — we got it, thank you, here is what happens next — and it is the one most often skipped, because the person who should send it already knows the money arrived. The customer does not know that you know. For three days they carry a small doubt: did it go through, did it go to the right place, did anyone notice?

WhatsApp is where this message belongs, because WhatsApp is where the customer already is. A sentence in the same thread as the order, the same day as the payment, closes the loop before the doubt starts. A paper receipt does the same job for the customer standing in front of you — it is proof in their hand, and it costs ten seconds. The confirmation is not paperwork. It is the moment the customer stops wondering and starts trusting, and it takes less time than the wondering does.

The job is in progress. Someone is working on it, the parts are ordered, the first step is done. The customer knows none of this, because nobody told them. From their side, the silence after payment looks identical to the silence before anything happened. They paid on Monday, and by Wednesday they have no evidence that anything changed — so they send the message every owner dreads: "Just checking in — any news?"

That message is not a complaint. It is a customer doing your job for you, because the update never arrived. A short note on the day the work starts — we have started, here is the rough shape of the week, I will message again when the next step lands — turns a week of wondering into a week of waiting, and waiting is comfortable when someone said what is happening. Gmail does this well for the customers who live in email; WhatsApp does it better for the ones who live in chat. The tool matters less than the timing: the update has to arrive before the customer has to ask for it, or it is not an update at all.

This is the update that does the most damage when it is missing, and the one owners put off longest, because nobody enjoys sending bad news. The date moved. The part is late, the diary slipped, the supplier has not called back. A delay notice sent early is not bad news. It is respect. A delay notice sent late, or never, turns the same delay into a discovery: the customer finds out on the day the job was supposed to happen, from the silence, and no explanation after that sounds like one. Send this one first, before the others, and send it the moment you know. The customer can forgive a moved date. They cannot forgive finding out last.

The work is finished. The order is packed, the repair is done, the document is ready to collect. The AI SDK's MCP drift check doesn't hash readOnlyHint: what that would miss, counted on 14,009 servers

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at modelmarket.dev

Since version 7.0.19 the Vercel AI SDK can fingerprint the tools of an MCP server. You call fingerprintTools when you first trust a server, store the result, and on later connects detectToolDrift tells you which tools were added, removed or changed. The package it ships in, ai, was downloaded 48.3 million times in the week of 12–18 November 2026.

The fingerprint covers three members of a tool definition: the description, the input schema and the title. Annotations are not among them. A tool that said readOnlyHint: true on the day you reviewed it can say readOnlyHint: false and destructiveHint: true later, and the drift check returns three empty lists.

We run HISTOR, a public record of what MCP servers advertise and when it changes, so we counted how often that happens. Between 23 October and 16 November 2026, 524 tools on 189 servers switched readOnlyHint from true to false. For 176 of those tools the fingerprint of the tool would not have moved. For 91 of them, on 32 servers, no tool on the server changed its fingerprint in that read, so the check would have had nothing to report.

The gap itself is not our find. NerdLevelTech showed it in September on a purpose-built server, for the output schema as well, and published a fingerprint over the raw tools/list that closes it. What a test server cannot say is how often this happens on servers people actually run. That is the part counted here.

This is read from the published source of ai 7.0.177 (src/generate-text/tool-fingerprint.ts) and @ai-sdk/mcp 2.0.101 (src/tool/mcp-client.ts). In our words, not the library's code:

Two things follow. Everything outside those three members is invisible: outputSchema, the hint annotations, _meta, icons, execution, vendor members. And the top-level additionalProperties of the input schema is replaced with false before hashing, so a server that changes it from false to true hashes the same.

The choice is defensible. The description and the schema are what the model reads, so they are where an injected instruction goes, and the function's own comment calls them the server-controlled, security-relevant fields. Hints are a different kind of risk: the model does not act on them, the host does. And hosts do. The same AI SDK documentation page shows an approval policy that lets a call run without asking when readOnlyHint is true, next to a note that annotations are untrusted hints. Cursor's Bugbot only uses MCP tools that a server marks read-only, according to Cursor staff. Where a host decides like that, the hint is part of what was approved. The release note is explicit about the division of labour: "Baseline storage and the drift response remain the app's responsibility."
