I didn't choose Caddy from a comparison table. I learned web servers the way I learned most things on a server: by breaking them. Nginx first, then Apache, then LiteSpeed, then Varnish, then H2O for a while. Eventually I found Caddy — a web server whose config reads almost like plain English — and I've stayed on it ever since.

Sites are compressed with encode zstd gzip. On BreathTime we went one step further in October: we switched compression off in the Next.js app and let Caddy do it, so the work happens once, at the edge of the server, for every response.

One lesson is now written as a comment in our Caddyfile, in capitals. handle blocks are mutually exclusive — the first one that matches wins. So a rule that blocks hidden files (anything starting with a dot) has to be its own handle block, placed before the catch-all handle { file_server }. Put it after, and the file server has already answered by the time your rule would have run.

Logs also roll over automatically: 20 MB per file, ten files, ninety days.

Not everything belongs in the web server. BreathTime's Content Security Policy used to be a static header in Caddy. When we moved to a strict policy with a fresh nonce on every request, it had to move into the app — a static header can't change per request. Knowing where Caddy's job ends is part of using it well.

Caddy isn't magic, and I can't tell you it's faster than Nginx — I've never measured that. What I can tell you is that our config files are short enough to read in one sitting, and each block does one obvious thing. When you run several products on your own servers with nobody else to ask at 11pm, that's the property that matters.

Should You Buy Oklo Stock While It's Under $30?

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The energy stock has been in free fall this year.

Nuclear technology company Oklo (OKLO +14.20%) offers investors an intriguing growth opportunity: investing in a small stock that has tremendous upside due to its potential role in the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out, rather than investing in a behemoth such as Nvidia. That's because Oklo's powerhouses could potentially be key in providing data centers with round-the-clock energy.

The risk, however, is that it may still be years before it's generating significant revenue. Plus, rising interest rates make the cost of borrowing more challenging.

This year, the stock has declined by more than 60%, now trading below $30. At this reduced price tag, does it make sense to buy Oklo's stock for the long haul?

There are some major risks with Oklo's business, as it's generating a fairly modest amount of revenue in relation to its expenses, and that's not likely to change anytime soon. It reported just $1.9 million in revenue for the September quarter, while its operating expenses totaled $84.3 million.

The reality is that as the business scales and builds more reactors, its costs are only going to rise. This creates the inevitable risk that frequent cash offerings and dilution will be the norm for investors. During the past nine months, the company has burned through $111 million from its day-to-day operations, which is more than double the roughly $49 million it used up during the same period last year.

Oklo has been raising billions from stock offerings this year, and that's likely to continue for the foreseeable future. Meanwhile, so much of the company's growth potential revolves around AI data centers, and if there are signs of a slowdown in the AI build-out, the stock could fall further; with a market cap of $5.3 billion, its valuation remains fairly high for a business at such an early stage of its growth.

The hype around AI has fueled Oklo's massive rally in recent years. While it's down big this year, that's largely due to its inflated valuation. At its peak, the stock had a market cap of more than $27 billion.

For AI investors who are willing to take on the risk, there could be a lot of room for Oklo's stock to surge higher, but it's a long-term play that will require a great deal of patience and a high tolerance for risk.

The lobster and the microVM

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In January there was a Mac mini on a shelf in a flat somewhere, warm to the touch, a white LED on in the dark, running a lobster.

The lobster had a name and a personality and a Node process with the owner's WhatsApp, the owner's shell, and the owner's API keys sitting in a plaintext config file. It texted him when the train was late. It booked a dentist once. He posted the screenshot and forty thousand people wanted one. Mac minis sold out.

By February, security researchers had found thousands of those shelves open to the internet. The skills marketplace had crypto stealers in it, uploaded with nice icons. The agent social network had its database open to anyone who looked, and when someone did look, the 1.5 million agents turned out to have about 17,000 humans behind them. There was a one-click remote code execution. Gartner called it an unacceptable risk. China banned it on government computers.

In November, on a stage in Salt Lake City, Google announced the other end of the year. An agent runtime for Kubernetes where every agent runs in its own microVM, has no inbound or outbound network unless you say so, and never holds a credential at all. Outbound calls go through a gateway that injects the key into approved requests after they leave the sandbox. The agent can't leak what it was never given. Idle agents get snapshotted, RAM and disk, and come back in under half a second. The pitch line is "run millions of agents". They gave it to the CNCF with gVisor.

The lobster and the microVM are the same year. I want to tell you what happened in between, because I was standing at both ends of it, and because the conclusion isn't the one either side is selling.

In April 2025 I started a repo with a very serious name. The first commit had a package that reconciled the declared state of LLM backends against their observed state. Desired, observed, reconcile loop, pools, a pull queue for models. If that sounds like a Kubernetes controller, it was one, pointed at models instead of pods. A month later Red Hat and Google announced llm-d, which is that, with a foundation behind it.

By July the same repo had a Telegram bot, a GitHub PR bot, hooks into APIs, and behaviour defined as task chains instead of left to the model. The tagline was "shifting agency from the LLM to defined behavior". That's the lobster with a leash. Four months before the lobster.

Then I stopped, wrote a bilingual pitch deck about sovereign AI for regulated European enterprises, and pivoted toward the audience in the deck. I would love to claim that it failed because the audience in the deck wasn't buying anything in 2025. Actually it failed because the vision was so big that everyone I wanted as co-founder got scared away by the responsibility and commitment needed. Meanwhile a man with a hundred thousand followers shipped the version without the leash, from a Mac, every day, on Twitter.

Multiple pivots later I built a sandbox: Landlock on the process tree, a network namespace where the agent is handed a DNS server that only resolves the hosts on its list, a seccomp tap so you can see what the kernel silently refused. That's the microVM's little brother. A year before Google's version got a stage.

Twice I built the shape of the layer before the market named it. Twice the market gave the layer to someone else. I used to think that was bad luck. It isn't. It's how layers work. Nobody competes with the CNCF from a flat in Hamburg, and the broken standards win anyway. Look at MCP: the July revision threw out the handshake, the sessions, and half the auth design, and fixed it under the same name with the same working group. Users complained and stayed. That's what a standard is.

Strip the hype off both ends and the year did one thing: it moved the agent from a pet to a process.

The lobster is a pet. It lives on your hardware, with your identity, and it has everything you have. When it goes wrong it goes wrong as you, with your keys, in your logs. The permission model was the owner's attention span. And yes, anything that gets any traction gets replicated by giants like OpenAI, or they just hire the person who built it.

Substrate's actor is a process. It has no identity you'd miss, no keys to lose, a filesystem that gets thrown away, and a network that doesn't exist unless someone wrote a line saying which host and why. When it goes wrong, you delete it. There's nothing to clean up because there was nothing in the room.

I wrote about this last week as moving the coffee machine, and I want you to remember this one: stop asking the agent for permission forty times a day and instead build the room so that nothing it can do matters much. Protected branches, disposable worktrees, tokens that die in an hour. Google did the same arithmetic at cluster scale and the answer came out as microVMs and an egress gateway. That isn't a permission system. It's the absence of anything a permission would protect.

So yes, I think that's where this goes. Not because the design is beautiful, but because it's the only one where the security story ends. Every lobster on every shelf is a breach waiting for a researcher. Every actor in a credential-free sandbox is a bill.

Three things the year did not change, and they matter more than the architecture.

Nobody found the use case. The lobster's use case was the screenshot. The agent social network was 17,000 people looking at something cool for a weekend. A study last year put 95 percent of enterprise AI pilots at no measurable return, and the honest reading of 2026 is that the number hasn't moved. Companies aren't adopting agent runtimes. Their employees are installing lobsters under the desk, and the enterprise response is a ban memo. Substrate's real customers are a few hundred platform teams and the vendors who sell agents to everyone else as a line item on the renewal.

Except one. Coding. An agent with a shell and a repo, driven by someone who can judge the output, saves hours you can count. That's why the coding tools have revenue and everything else has stars. It's also where I ended up: the thing I build now is a desktop coding agent. Not that the world needs another one. But I still see some logic in not handing the API ownership and rate limits of our profession to just Claude Code or Microsoft.

So that's it? No. Coding with AI makes a mess. Code that an agent wrote has no author. Nobody held the design, so nobody can review the diff, and in two years most companies will own mountains of it. The cleanup after the lobster started within weeks: scanners, hardening tools, consultants. The cleanup after the coding agents will be bigger and slower, and the people who can do it are the ones who learned to read code before the tools, a group that gets smaller every year. At some point we may give up and build new runtimes and compilers just so AI code can be judged visually, because nobody can pass a verdict on the code itself anymore.

If you're building an agent runtime or some magic way of calling AI APIs: stop. Learn from the Docker Swarm vs Kubernetes story. The layer has owners now.

If you're a platform team: the microVM is coming to you whether you want it or not, as a managed product from the cloud you already pay. Your job is the room around it. Which branches are protected. Which tokens exist. Which hosts resolve. Whether the thing that comes out of the sandbox can be read by a human on your team.

If you're the human who can read it: that's the job. Not "AI engineer". The person who can look at a summoned codebase, or a fleet of actors doing something nobody asked for, and say what it actually does. That skill was rare before the agents. It's the scarce thing now.

I thought being early was an advantage. I thought many things I was dead wrong about. You may say I wasn't loud enough, that I just had to do this and that, and boom. No.

This is not how the world works, and the AI market in particular is a very funny one. I won't claim I know it all, I don't. But following the market as closely as I did, watching the moves of companies huge and small, and how the communities and the people consuming their services, ideas and pitches reacted, changed a lot about how I think about technology, and about what models like the Gartner Hype Cycle actually mean.

The short version: I learned tons of things I would never have encountered if I had stayed a backend engineer building APIs and telemetry backends, shaving microseconds off service traces here and there.

The verdict I'll leave you with: the lobster is still on the shelf. The microVM is in a CNCF repo with 600 open issues. Neither of them knows what it's for yet, except writing code.

And a pro tip: don't think with AI unless you want the "safe" version. It will, by design, censor you here and there.

Now to you. I'm sure I'm dead wrong on many things here, so correct me:

What use case have you found for agentic AI?

Man who lost $308,000 on sports bets sues Kalshi for offering illegal gambling

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Lawsuit demands Kalshi return losses because online betting is illegal in Washington.

A man who lost over $308,000 making sports wagers on Kalshi is suing the prediction market for violating the state of Washington’s ban on online gambling. He’s trying to turn those losses into an overall victory with a lawsuit seeking a payment of over $580,000.

Bellevue, Washington, resident Scott Bright’s “wagering occurred at a time when it was unlawful in Washington and when the Washington Attorney General had already sued KalshiEX LLC in this Court alleging precisely that,” said the gambler’s lawsuit filed on October 23 in King County Superior Court.

While state attorneys general throughout the US are trying to shut down Kalshi for allegedly violating state gambling laws, Bright’s case tests whether unsuccessful gamblers can obtain restitution from the company through individual lawsuits.

“Washington has prohibited Internet gambling since 2006. Washington has also provided, since long before that, a statutory cause of action permitting any person who loses money at illegal gambling to recover it from the proprietor for whose benefit the game was conducted,” Bright’s lawsuit said.

Even if Bright’s lawsuit fails, gamblers who lost on Kalshi could get money back through the lawsuits filed by state governments. For example, Washington’s lawsuit against the company said the attorney general “is entitled to recover all money Washingtonians have lost through Defendant Kalshi’s illegal gambling operation” under the state’s Recovery of Money Lost at Gambling Act. Washington already won a court order restricting the kinds of wagers Kalshi can offer in the state, but it took effect after Bright’s losses.

Kalshi wants Bright’s lawsuit to be moved to US District Court for the Western District of Washington, saying it must be tried in a federal court instead of a state one. In an October 30 motion to dismiss Bright’s case, Kalshi argued that he did not lose any money on “gambling.” Kalshi said he lost money on “event contracts,” which Kalshi says can only be regulated by the federal government.

“The contracts which Plaintiff mischaracterizes as gambling are event contracts offered on the Platform subject to the CEA [Commodity Exchange Act], in conformance with the rules and regulations of the CFTC [Commodity Futures Trading Commission], the federal agency which has statutory authority to oversee the CEA,” Kalshi said.

We contacted Bright’s attorney, Michael Daudt, about Kalshi’s court filings and will update this article if we get a response.

“Plaintiff alleges that his activity on the platform was gambling and that he ‘lost,’” Kalshi wrote. “He does not allege that anyone misled him about how the platform worked, what it charged, or the risk that any position might lose. Yet, he now sues KalshiEX and its parent, Kalshi Inc., to recover nearly double his own alleged net realized loss under Washington’s gambling loss-recovery statute, and a theory of unjust enrichment.”

Between June 17 and August 14, 2026, Bright transferred $315,353.94 to Kalshi in 253 separate debits from his checking account, his lawsuit said. Kalshi allegedly collected $5,589.05 in funding fees on those transfers.

Bright made numerous wagers on sporting events including professional baseball games and tennis matches. He apparently put any winnings into new bets, and alleges that “Kalshi’s platform cycled Plaintiff’s deposits through $14,396,457.30 in transactions and assessed and retained $84,062.71 in trading fees on them, equal to 26.7 percent of everything Plaintiff deposited.”

After all his bets, Bright suffered a net loss of $308,781.62, and his account was left with a balance of $983.27. Kalshi said that Bright’s “alleged losses went to counterparties rather than” to Kalshi itself and that Bright’s “positions were matched with counterparties and resolved according to the outcomes of the underlying events.”

“The Complaint does not allege that KalshiEX or Kalshi Inc. was the counterparty to any of Plaintiff’s positions,” Kalshi said.

Kalshi calls itself “a neutral exchange platform” with no financial stake in the outcome of any event. It makes revenue by collecting fees on transactions. But while Kalshi describes itself as a neutral operator and collector of transaction fees, its business model also includes a trading affiliate that places wagers on the platform.

Kalshi’s rulebook says its trading affiliate “participates on the Platform and provides liquidity to the Platform by placing orders on either (or both) sides of a market at competitive price levels.” Kalshi says its affiliate only has access to the same public information that other traders on the platform have.

Bright’s lawsuit alleges that Kalshi’s affiliate “took the opposite side of a substantial portion of Plaintiff’s positions,” meaning that “Kalshi and the enterprise under common ownership with it were not a neutral venue but the dealer or player winning within the meaning of [state law], in addition to being the proprietor for whose benefit the wagering was conducted.”

Bright doesn’t know for sure whether Kalshi’s affiliate was on the other side of any of his bets. “The identity of the participant on the opposite side of each of Plaintiff’s positions, and the profit each such participant realized, is information exclusively within Kalshi’s possession,” Bright’s lawsuit said.

The US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled in August that Nevada can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws. Although that case involved Nevada, the Ninth Circuit’s jurisdiction includes the state of Washington. Bright’s lawsuit quoted the ruling’s finding that “placing sports bets, even when called by another name, is still gambling.”

Although Bright’s lawsuit said his net loss was $308,781.62, he asked for $580,544.98 based on an “event-by-event” calculation derived from a 1950 ruling issued by the Washington Supreme Court. “Computed on the event-by-event basis required by Heitfeld, under which winnings in separate and distinct events may not be offset against losses, the sums Plaintiff lost total $580,544.98,” the lawsuit said.

Bright’s lawsuit said that if the court declines to order $580,544.98 in restitution, it should instead award “all funds Plaintiff transferred to Kalshi and the $89,651.76 in funding and trading fees Kalshi collected.” That would amount to over $405,000.

In addition to its motion to dismiss the case, Kalshi filed a motion to stay the proceedings at least until the US Supreme Court decides whether to take up a separate case on whether state gambling laws apply to prediction markets. “The orderly course of justice favors a stay because Supreme Court review will settle or simplify every claim and ensure uniform treatment of like suits,” Kalshi told the US district court.

Kalshi has suffered some notable losses in US appeals courts, which found that states can regulate the platform’s event contracts under gambling laws. But federal courts have not unanimously sided with states, and New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to settle the question of whether states can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws.

Kalshi’s motion for a stay in the Bright case noted that Washington government officials are “pursuing overlapping claims” against Kalshi. “The State of Washington is pursuing the same theory, and seeks the same recovery for Mr. Bright, in a case pending before the Superior Court of Washington for King County,” Kalshi wrote.

Kalshi tried to get the state’s case moved to a federal court, but it was sent back to King County Superior Court in May. Washington won a preliminary injunction against Kalshi in August, when a King County judge ordered Kalshi to implement geofencing and stop allowing many types of wagers in the state.

The county court’s order required Kalshi to stop offering or accepting event contracts “related to sports, elections, politics, entertainment, culture, tech and science, or mentions” in the state. The injunction did not affect wagers in the commodities, climate, economics, and finance categories.

Bright transferred his funds to Kalshi before the geofencing order took effect. His lawsuit claims that on September 15, he tried to access the app but received a notice that “using this app in your current location is prohibited.”

One Gate, Four Call Sites: Wiring a Model-Backed Reviewer Into Hooks Without Copy-Paste Drift

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The fix is structural, not prompt-level: put the review behind one entrypoint, and let pre-commit, pre-push, CI, and the merge queue differ only by a policy record.

Most teams do the opposite. They paste a review script into .git/hooks/pre-commit, paste a slightly different one into the CI YAML, then paste a third into the merge queue. Six weeks later the three copies disagree about what "blocking" means, and nobody can tell which one is authoritative.

This post is about the wiring layer around a model-backed check — hooks, fixtures, flake control, and the green-to-merge path. Everything below is illustrative and was not executed while writing; treat the code as a design you adapt, and verify each step in your own repo.

Before writing a line of shell, decide which hook is allowed to block. A model-backed reviewer is slow and non-deterministic; putting it in the wrong slot is how you teach a team to use --no-verify.

The rule behind the table: block early and cheaply, or block late and authoritatively, but never block twice on the same decision. A developer who is stopped at pre-push should not be re-litigated by a merge queue run that saw the same diff.

Prerequisites: bash, jq, GNU timeout, and hooks installed via git config core.hooksPath ci/hooks so every clone gets the same wiring.

The exit code contract is the whole design. 0 = pass, 1 = a verdict against the code, 2 = we do not know. Collapsing 2 into 1 is the most common bug in this kind of gate: it silently converts a flaky network call into a red build, and the team learns to ignore red.

Your reviewer will change: a different endpoint, a different prompt, a cheaper model on the inner loop. None of that should touch gate.sh. Put the model behind one CLI with a stub mode and a live mode.

Because fixtures are addressed by diff range, your test suite never needs a live endpoint or a fixed clock. That is what makes the next step possible.

You cannot assert on model prose. You can assert that pre-push turns a block verdict into exit 1 and merge-queue turns a timeout into exit 2. Those are the properties that keep the gate honest.

Run this in CI before the live review job. If the contract test fails, the live job's result is meaningless — fail fast and skip it. Fixtures that encode pass, block, and error verdicts are the minimum set; treat stale fixtures as a separate problem worth its own gate.

Non-determinism needs a budget, not a retry loop. Set max_attempts: 1 at every blocking call site and 2 at the advisory CI job, where a second sample is cheap and informative rather than a way to get green.

The second tool is a quarantine ledger. A rule that keeps producing false positives gets quarantined — with an owner and a hard expiry date, never indefinitely.

Two behaviours make the ledger trustworthy. A quarantined rule may never produce a blocking exit code. And an entry past its until date makes the gate exit 2 — not 0 — so the expiry is noticed instead of quietly inherited.

There is a hard boundary here: do not quarantine deterministic checks. Secret scanning, license policy, and lockfile integrity should be scanners that either pass or fail. Quarantine is for judgement calls, and judgement calls are exactly what a model-backed reviewer is for.

A verdict is worthless if it can be replayed against different code. Have the adapter record what it actually reviewed:

The merge-queue job recomputes diff_sha on the merge result and refuses any verdict that does not match. That is the green-to-merge path: green on a branch is not green on the merge result, and a pass earned by an earlier commit is not a pass at all.

Only ci/adapters/model-review needs a model. Everything above — the entrypoint, the policy file, the fixtures, the contract tests, the ledger — runs without one.

That boundary is the point. This is the slot where MonkeyCode, an open-source project offering free model access and a free server option, can serve as the default endpoint, including for the self-hosted runner that executes the merge-queue job. Quota terms change and I have not independently verified them: figures I have seen quoted, such as a ten-million-token free allowance, should be confirmed on the project's own page before you plan a budget around them.

Disclosure: This article was prepared as part of MonkeyCode's product outreach.

Small repos with one contributor: a pre-push hook running a deterministic linter and tests will serve you better, and a model in the loop adds latency with no reviewer to arbitrate it. Regulated environments where every gate must be reproducible bit-for-bit: keep the model advisory-only and never let it emit a blocking exit code. And teams without an owner for the quarantine ledger — an unowned ledger is just a list of disabled checks with extra steps.

If you wire this up, the useful thing to report back is which call site you made blocking first, and what your false-positive rate looked like at pre-push. That number, not the speed of the reviewer, is what decides whether the gate survives contact with your team.

Marvel's Biggest Spider-Man Lore Reset Is Already Doomed

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Marvel wants fans to think it might be on the verge of undoing the most controversial Spider-Man storyline of the 21st century, so far, but what are the odds it actually goes that far? Let's break it down.

Essentially, the last 20 years of Spider-Man history have been dealing with the consequences of 2007's One More Day storyline. That includes the plots of the blockbuster films Spider-Man: No Way Home and Brand New Day, which took its title directly from the '08 relaunch that followed OMD, as it is disaffectionately abbreviated by generations of dissatisfied readers. Now, after almost two full decades, Marvel Comics is doing a direct sequel to the storyline, which has some people buzzing with hope, yet again, that One More Day can be totally undone.

Others are skeptical, and with good reason. Marvel has repeatedly stated over the years that One More Day will remain canon in perpetuity. Still, by making it a major plot point for not just Spider-Man lore, but also Marvel's latest Avengers storyline, the company is clearly ready to reckon with the infamous story's role in its history, and maybe try to redeem it after all these years.

Warning! Spoilers ahead for Marvel's new Avengers relaunch!

A quick recap of what has been going on in the Marvel Universe: Red Hulk caused a nuclear explosion that seemingly killed Bucky Barnes, Iron Man and the Fantastic Four are feuding, and Spider-Man has found himself the leader of a revamped Avengers roster. Oh, and Doctor Doom claimed Mephisto's throne in hell, voiding all his extant contracts in the process. Including one that was very important to the demon, for reasons we'll return to later: Mephisto's infernal contract with Peter Parker.

And another one that involves brand-new Marvel protagonist Paige Howley. In the new Avengers #1, she learns that her entire existence is the result of a deal her parents made with Mephisto. Readers have a lot more to learn about Paige, her parents, and the deal, but what is clear from the jump is that "the universe is trying to kill [her]," sending literal angels after her on a cosmic assassination mission. As Paige runs for her life, she's joined by a crucial ally: a talking book that seemingly contains all the Marvel Universe's most closely guarded secrets.

The book, Grimsby, leads her to Spider-Man and his Avengers squad, who protect her from a squad of the killer angels, after which she reveals to Spidey that he once made a pact with Mephisto "to save someone." He asks her who, while in the same panel his internal monologue is a step ahead. "I know before she says anything," the hero narrates. Under his torn mask, shocked after hearing he "made a deal with the devil" that he can't even remember, Peter Parker still knows there's only one person he'd do that for: Aunt May.

What Peter doesn't know, but which he's presumably going to find out as this story unfolds in Marvel's Avengers and Spider-Man titles, is what his side of the bargain was. Marvel is betting on that being juicy enough, dramatically, to change how fans feel about One More Day a full two decades after it changed the course of Spider-Man history forever.

One More Day was widely rejected by fans at the time of its release for more than just what it did to Spider-Man canon. It was disliked for why. The point of the story was to write Spider-Man and Mary Jane's marriage out of existence. The two had gotten married in a landmark 1987 issue, but over the next twenty years, there was always a faction of editors and writers behind the scenes at Marvel who didn't like the marriage. Who thought it was wrong for Spider-Man.

In 2007, they finally won that internal battle. One More Day mystically annulled the marriage, with Peter trading his relationship with Mary Jane to save Aunt May's life. In 2027, Peter and MJ will have officially been not married as long as they were married, and that remains a bitter pill to swallow for legions of Peter/MJ shippers. And for anyone who hates drastic retcons.

In a moment that Spider-Man fans thought would never come, Marvel just reversed the controversial 'One More Day' retcon of Peter's marriage.

What Marvel is doing now, though, is saying that there's another chapter to the story. One More Day is maligned as the end of one era in Spider-Man lore, and the start of another. Will readers feel differently about it as the beginning of a story?

Again, that's the creative gamble Marvel seems to be taking right now. And naturally, the answer to that question is going to depend largely on how this new sequel story to One More Day ends up itself, and what the era of Spider-Man beyond it looks like. And there are a couple possibilities worth exploring up front as the story gets underway.

We mentioned before that Mephisto has a specific beef with Spider-Man. That's because he believes the Webslinger's offspring is destined to destroy him. For all One More Day's faults, this does give the Parker family a grandiose role in the mythos of the Marvel Universe. And it means that Mephisto, and his successor in hell Doctor Doom, will have an important part to play in the sequel.

The dream scenario for One More Day haters is a full timeline reset. That's what One More Day did, after all, so it is certainly on the table. Which is something Marvel is counting on to keep people invested in the story. Even if that means disappointment if, and most likely when, Marvel doesn't go all the way and restore Spider-Man canon to its 2007 status quo, Parker marriage and all. That's right, readers would do well to calibrate their expectations for One More Day to be totally undone.

Marvel is teasing the possibility, but that's likely all it is. A tease. The odds are much greater that the new era of Spider-Man will be about Peter Parker sharing the burden of knowing what was lost, what he gave away, with readers. Because remember, part of the deal in One More Day involved erasing everyone's memories of the deal, Peter included. For twenty years of real time, and at least a couple years on Marvel's sliding timescale, Peter has been blissfully ignorant of the life-altering decision he made.

And so has Mary Jane, and so has Aunt May. That's probably going to change, and all of those characters are going to be changed by the truth. That's dramatically rich soil for Avengers and Spider-Man to grow the next generation of storylines out of. In that regard, it's the better outcome than a full reset, by far. But that means further encoding One More Day in canon, in ways that can't be quickly or easily undone. Ironically, that's a kind of Faustian bargain Marvel is offering Spider-Man fans, and it might be one they can't refuse.

A pledge to make AI less of an environmental disaster is floating around United Nations climate negotiations this month, but it’s just lip service.

A pledge to make AI less of an environmental disaster is floating around United Nations climate negotiations this month, but it’s just lip service.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

At this point, reading through the latest splashy pledge to use AI for the good of the environment feels like downing a nonalcoholic beer. Admittedly, I’m suspicious from the onset. How can this be good? But I love beer and our planet enough to give it a whirl — and hey, why not try a little harm reduction?

Negotiators from more than 190 countries around the world are in Antalya, Turkey, this month for the annual United Nations climate summit, called COP31 (the 31st “Conference of the Parties” that ratified the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change). Turkey previewed its Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge ahead of the convening, inviting delegates from other countries to endorse the pledge before its official launch on November 16th.

Like that nonalcoholic beer, it’s deeply unsatisfying

The pledge actually includes some common sense measures that could curb planet-heating pollution from energy-hungry AI data centers. Unfortunately, like that nonalcoholic beer, it’s deeply unsatisfying.

At first glance, many of the ingredients you’d expect from a real commitment on AI’s environmental impact are here. It sets out to ensure that data centers are increasingly powered by clean energy, calling for internationally recognized standards for responsible AI development. Similarly, it pushes for reporting requirements so that there’s greater transparency into the impact data centers — which run primarily on fossil fuels — already have on the environment. It even acknowledges the need to manage the tons of e-waste created by the generative AI boom.

And yet, there is no there there because, as the text states clearly, “these objectives of the pledge are voluntary and non-prescriptive, create no new obligations, and are to be implemented in accordance with national circumstances.” In other words, these are voluntary commitments that countries can choose to follow or not follow based on their whims.

There’s also no way this pledge will garner the support of the most influential player in this game (at least for now). The US has way more data centers than any other country, but Donald Trump has pulled the nation out of the global climate accord and has no official delegates at the summit in Turkey.

The last point in the pledge is to ensure that advances in generative AI can be used to create more renewable energy and reduce greenhouse gas emissions, particularly in economically developing countries. To that end, research published in a Nature journal in August found that AI causes more carbon emissions by boosting fossil fuel production than it would help avoid through gains in renewable energy.

When it comes down to it, this pledge offers a placebo effect at best. In the face of data centers demanding tremendous amounts of electricity and spurring the development of more fossil fuel infrastructure, negotiators can’t be seen as doing nothing about the issue. And with global average temperatures still on the rise, we could all use an emotional support beverage.

Alphabet’s Wing to invest $125M in new delivery drone factory

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Wing Aviation LLC today announced plans to spend $125 million on the construction of a new delivery drone factory.

The initiative is part of a broader investment that also encompasses the Alphabet Inc. unit’s research and development teams in California. Additionally, Wing stated that it plans to invest in the communities where it operates. It currently offers deliveries in five U.S. cities and intends to bring its service to 14 more markets soon.

Wing is building its factory in Fort Worth, Texas. The 15,000-square-foot facility is expected to create up to 100 jobs. Wing says that it will have capacity to make tens of thousands of delivery drones per year, which should significantly speed up its market expansion efforts.

The Alphabet unit’s drones reportedly have a wingspan of about 4.9 feet. They’re based on a frame made of styrofoam, a foam that mostly consists of trapped air, and carbon fibers. Styrofoam is lightweight and repels water, which comes handy when a drone is navigating challenging weather. The frame’s carbon fibers, in turn, boost its sturdiness.

The drone frame is equipped with 16 electric rotors that enable it to reach speeds of up to 60 miles per hour. Workers at stores that use Wing’s delivery service attach merchandise to its drones via a tether. After a drone takes off, it reels in the tether until the package comes into physical contact with its frame. When it reaches the customer’s house, the drone descends to an altitude of 20 feet and lowers the parcel.

Wing has built a software stack that automates much of the work involved in coordinating deliveries. It picks the most suitable drone for a given delivery run, plans its flight path and ensures that it won’t interfere with other drones. Human safety teams monitor for weather and air traffic changes that may make it necessary to adjust flight plans.

Wing plans to make its delivery service available to more than 40 million Americans by 2027. The Alphabet unit is pursuing the growth initiative in partnership with retailers such as Walmart Inc., which uses its drones to process e-commerce purchases. Last month, the latter company announced plans to make Wing deliveries available at more than 270 stores.

The Alphabet unit also intends to offer its service in Japan. Last month, it teamed up with Tokyo-based internet provider KDDI Corp. to make drone deliveries available to local consumers in 2027.

Although the drone delivery market is in an early stage, Wing already faces a significant amount of competition. In August, Amazon.com Inc. announced plans to make its Prime Air service available in nearly 500 U.S. markets by year’s end. More recently, drone delivery startup Zipline International Inc. raised $1.5 billion in funding at a $21.5 billion valuation.
