Ad Platform Can't Handle Traffic Spikes? What to Fix First and Who Can Help

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 23 • Originally published at ambrain.org

At traffic spikes, queues and retries make an ad platform answer after the deadline. Measure the peak and cut that late work before adding servers.

If your ad platform fails at traffic spikes, the engineers who can help are the ones who measure it during a real peak and then fix it in a set order. They stop work that will finish after the deadline, limit retries and the traffic the platform accepts, and move budget and frequency counters off the request path. Then they add capacity before the peaks you can predict. Code is rewritten last, and only where the measurements point.

The short answer: in real-time bidding, a bid that misses the exchange's deadline is lost. At a peak, queues, retries and shared budget counters make more bids miss it. Whoever you hire should ask for your timeouts per partner and queue depth per service at the busiest minute before proposing more servers or a rewrite.

A traffic spike shows different symptoms in each part of an ad platform:

Timeouts and empty slots show up during the peak. Event and budget problems can stay hidden until delayed events reach reports and billing.

In OpenRTB, the IAB Tech Lab protocol for real-time bidding, the exchange can state the deadline in the request itself: the “maximum time in milliseconds the exchange allows for bids to be received including Internet latency to avoid timeout”. Google's Authorized Buyers documentation says the deadline typically ranges from 80 to 1000 ms. Google requires 85 percent of responses to arrive within it, as seen from the trading location, and throttles bidders that cannot consistently achieve this. A bidder that slows down at a peak loses the late auctions and can then receive less traffic.

Near capacity, a service starts to queue requests. Google's Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) book notes that “queued requests consume memory and increase latency”, and that servers spend resources on requests that will miss their deadline anyway. Unless the code checks the deadline, a request that has waited too long is still processed in full and its answer discarded.

When a call to a database, a cache or a partner times out, the caller tries again, and the retries arrive when the system can least absorb them. The SRE book gives the arithmetic of a retry storm: “100 QPS of retries in the first second leads to 200 QPS, then to 300 QPS, and so on.”

An exchange or SSP sends each request to many bidders, and the auction either waits for the slowest answer or closes without it. Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso of Google put numbers on this in Communications of the ACM in 2013. In their example, each server typically answers in 10 ms but takes a second on one request in a hundred. A request that must collect answers from 100 such servers in parallel then takes more than a second 63 percent of the time. An auction does not wait that long. By the same calculation, if each of 100 bidders is late on one request in a hundred, about 63 percent of auctions close with at least one answer missing.

Autoscaling that reacts to load adds copies of a service only after it has measured that load. Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler, for example, checks the load every 15 seconds by default and adds new copies in limited steps. Each new copy then has to start, pass its checks and fill its caches, and the SRE book notes that processes are often slower right after starting than in steady state. A spike measured in seconds can end before the new capacity carries real traffic.

Southampton returned to winning ways with a convincing 2-0 win over Derby County on Saturday, but one selection decision made by Tonda Eckert will have one player really questioning his role in the Saints squad.

One of the main contributors to Southampton's strong start this season has been their injury bill, which has, for the most part, been impeccable.

That's left some fringe players without the minutes they'd ideally want, but on Saturday, there was a big chance for Mads Roerslev to finally stake a claim for some starts in the future, as James Bree was sidelined with a minor injury.

However, the Dane was once again left on the bench, despite being the only fit recognised right-back in Tonda Eckert's squad. Even if he still managed to get his first league minutes of the season off the bench, the writing is surely on the wall regarding the former Brentford man's status in this Saints side.

Roerslev was an intriguing signing for Southampton last season. Arriving from Brentford for £3 million, he went under the radar in a window that also saw big-money feespaid for the likes of Finn Azaz, Casper Jander, Tom Fellows and Leo Scienza.

At Brentford, though, the Dane was a useful squad player in their rise to the Premier League and the years that followed under Thomas Frank. He started every game of their successful play-off run in 2021, and in the 2023/24 campaign, he played in all but four Premier League games.

Unfortunately, Roerslev's time at Southampton so far has been largely associated with how terrible the Saints were under Will Still at the start of last season. All of the 27-year-old's starts at St Mary's came under the Belgian.

When Still was sacked in early November last year, it presented a good opportunity for the squad to impress under new, young, exciting interim coach Tonda Eckert, but Roerslev was almost immediately sidelined.

He played in Eckert's first two games, wins against QPR and Sheffield Wednesday, before going down with an Achilles injury, which would see him out for the rest of the season.

Roerslev had to watch on from the sidelines as Southampton were rampant in the second half of the season under Eckert. The Saints didn't lose a league game from January 17th until the end of the campaign, and made the FA Cup semi-finals, too.

During that time, James Bree became an unlikely hero at St Mary's, coming back from his loan spell at Charlton Athletic in January and immediately being thrown back into Saints contention under Eckert.

The 28-year-old went into this season as the first choice at right-back, but a returning Roerslev from injury made for a good backup in theory, keeping with the theme at Southampton currently that there are two top players in each position.

In practice, that hasn't been the case with Bree out, though, as the former one-time Danish international has remained on the fringes, even when the position has been open.

Against Norwich City last month, Bree was rested after playing every minute of the first nine league games of the season. In his place, though, came natural centre-back, Zach Abbott, who has also been a victim of the pecking order so far this season.

With Abbott coming in to play in the middle of the back four against Derby on Saturday and with Bree out injured, though, it gave Roerslev a big chance for his first start in over a year. But in came natural midfielder Flynn Downes, with the Dane having to settle for a nine-minute cameo.

34 Years Later, Quentin Tarantino's Crime Thriller Masterpiece Is Officially Free to Stream

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Rarely does a filmmaker’s first movie also become their breakout hit. Before Jaws made Steven Spielberg a household name, he directed the little-remembered The Sugarland Express. The Coen Brothers started with the indie thriller Blood Simple, then enjoyed a handful of moderately successful films before Fargo exploded. But with Quentin Tarantino, this was not the case.

Tarantino worked as a video store clerk while writing Reservoir Dogs, which he planned to shoot independently with some friends on a meager $30,000 budget. That changed when his producer Lawrence Bender gave the script to his acting teacher, who then passed it along to actor Harvey Keitel. Keitel was impressed and agreed to help produce the film. He also agreed to star as Mr. White.

Keitel then helped Tarantino and Bender raise money for the film and hold casting sessions which got them the likes of Steve Buscemi, who played Mr. Pink; Michael Madsen, who played Mr. Blonde; and Tim Roth, who played Mr. Orange. The film was shot in Los Angeles in just 35 days. It then debuted at Sundance in January 1992, where it became a massive hit. Reservoir Dogs was released in theaters that October and grossed $22 million (an impressive haul for a film that cost about $1.5 million). It became an even bigger hit on home video.

Reservoir Dogs is about a crew of thieves pulling off a jewel heist organized by a crime boss named Joe Cabot (Lawrence Tierney). The six men in the crew don’t know one another, and each is given an alias (Mr. White, Mr. Pink, etc). The job, however, goes horribly wrong and results in a shootout with police, which also claims civilian lives. The surviving crew members take refuge at the prearranged warehouse where they were supposed to reconvene after the job. As Mr. Orange lay dying on the warehouse floor, Mr. Pink and Mr. White try to make sense of what happened. Mr. Pink is convinced there is a rat in the crew, which would explain how the police got there so quickly.

The funny thing about Reservoir Dogs is that you never see the heist itself, and that’s what’s so good about it.​​​​​​​ The movie jumps back and forth between the post-robbery setting and a series of flashbacks​​​​​​​ as the guys are hired by Joe and prepare for the job. It’s up to the viewer to put all the puzzle pieces together, and while we never get all the pieces, we get enough to assemble an entirely unpredictable narrative that includes bloody deaths​​​​​​​ that are impossible to see coming on a first viewing (and still shocking during repeat viewings).

In addition to the non-linear story structure of Reservoir Dogs, what made the movie so distinct was its characters. Keitel’s Mr. White is a surprisingly compassionate criminal, who shows his tender side with the dying Mr. Orange, played by Roth, who is equally compelling as a clearly doomed man. Steve Buscemi’s Mr. Pink is the funniest character in the film​​​​​​​, as he whines about his assigned alias and the lack of professionalism among the other thieves​​​​​​​. Michael Madsen’s Mr. Blonde is a downright frightening character who enjoys torturing people and is the reason why the whole heist goes wrong. Lawrence Tierney is also an effectively foreboding presence, which is offset by his onscreen son “Nice Guy Eddie” played by Chris Penn, who goes from funny to frightening and back again multiple times.

Reservoir Dogs set a high bar for all future Tarantino films, many of which had their own unique story structure and equally magnetic characters. Some would become bigger hits than Reservoir Dogs was​​​​​​​, but Reservoir Dogs has never been overshadowed or forgotten about, no matter how impressive Tarantino’s body of work has become. The simple reason for that is that it’s just that good a film where it simply will never, ever go away.

Reservoir Dogs is free to stream on Pluto TV.

Sandisk Just Split Its Stock 8-for-1. Is It Time to Buy?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sandisk stock is incredibly cheap right now.

By Keithen Drury – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:00AM EST

If you're wondering why Sandisk (SNDK -16.05%) just saw its share price get a huge cut, it's not time to panic. Although the stock is valued at an eighth of what it was, that was on purpose.

Sandisk enacted an 8-for-1 stock split on Nov. 19, which replaced one share with eight — each of them costing an eighth as much as a pre-split share. This is a mostly cosmetic effect, but it does let the company issue stock options to employees at more reasonable prices.

With most brokerages offering access to fractional shares, stock price really doesn't matter much to investors. What matters more is the business behind it. For a company to justify a split, its share price must have increased significantly. That only happens to companies that are doing well, and Sandisk fits that bill.

Sandisk makes memory chips, which are in short supply. The artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure build-out has consumed all available production capacity for memory, with different effects on suppliers and clients.

For Sandisk, the lack of supply means it can charge premium prices for its products. None of its input costs have changed significantly, so this is almost pure profit for Sandisk. This has shown up in a big way in its most recent results, with profit margins skyrocketing in recent quarters.

SNDK Profit Margin (Quarterly) data by YCharts

A 73% profit margin is absurd, and it shows just how profitable Sandisk is.

While Sandisk and its investors are thrilled about this, not everyone is. Consumers who want to buy laptops, phones, or external storage devices face prices several times higher than they were just a couple of years ago. The AI hyperscalers are experiencing the same thing, paying a premium to secure memory for data centers.

With the AI build-out expected to last several more years, it's unlikely that Sandisk and its peers' supply constraint issues are fixed. Although many in the industry are planning production expansion (Sandisk is too), there are questions about whether that will be enough to meet demand. After all, there is a huge risk of overbuilding production capacity.

Sandisk and its memory chip peers are cyclical companies, which means their businesses ebb and flow. The AI build-out is the largest demand surge this industry has ever seen, and they were woefully underprepared for it in terms of capacity.

But for most of their existence, memory chip companies have significantly underutilized the capacity at their disposal. The AI build-out won't last forever, so Sandisk must be careful not to overbuild; otherwise, it could find itself in a rough spot when the build-out slows.

However, the build-out may not wrap up for several more years, so some capacity investments are warranted. Still, the market is pricing Sandisk's stock like it's going to collapse tomorrow, and that's a perfect opportunity to buy.

Very few companies ever trade at a forward valuation like this.

Sandisk shares go for a mere 3.4 times forward earnings. That is an incredibly cheap valuation, and it conveys the market's message that it doesn't think Sandisk will be able to maintain its earnings growth.

However, this flies in the face of what major AI companies have said, so something has to give. I think it's more likely than not that the market is misvaluing Sandisk's stock, and today it represents an excellent buying opportunity. I think that Sandisk has a ton of potential, and with it being an important component supplier for the AI build-out, the stock is well worth buying.

Readers with back pain probably recall the much-discussed 1996 chess challenge, wherein the world champion Gerry Kasparov faced off against IBM's Deep Blue chess-optimized computer. The nascent clanker lost the match with a 4-2 total, but still stunned the world by being the first piece of electric sand to win a single game against a grandmaster. Modern chess engines will utterly destroy any human, but Anthropic engineer Nicholas Carlini wondered if granting old hardware new bytes would give the machine a better chance. Spoiler: yes, indeed it would.

To understand the task, here's a quick primer on chess scoreboards. A player is rated worldwide with an Elo system (first devised for chess), wherein they lose or gain points in proportion to the difference to their opponent's rating — meaning that winning a match against Magnus Carlsen would net you a lot of points versus defeating me, who only learned about the en passant rule a handful of years ago.

At his prime, Kasparov ranked about 2800 points. Given that Deep Blue eventually barely won against him in a subsequent rematch, that is a good starting point for any. Contemporary chess engines like Stockfish have an estimated Elo of about 3,300, far higher than any human — guaranteeing it'll win most matches against a human.

Nicholas Carlini got hold of a 1995-era machine, packing a Pentium Pro CPU with all of its 200 MHz of clock speed, and 64 MB of RAM. For his first experiment, he grabbed Crafty, a 1996 chess engine, and measured how well it would do. It came in at 2,000 Elo with 1 second allotted, and 2,400 @ 10s. Running that software on a Ryzen 7 resulted in a 2,600 rating — good, but not enough.

Since Crafty's source code is available, he got Claude to mod it with modern features, and eventually reached a ceiling of 3,000 Elo @ 10s per move. That's quite an impressive feat already, especially considering that the lone Pentium Pro represented but a small fraction of the hardware that Deep Blue had. An Elo delta of +200 means this iteration would defeat Kasparov three out of every four games, while a +400 delta means a victory nine out of ten times.

Carlini then pulled out the big gun: Stockfish. He had to ask Claude to modify it to use only 32-bit arithmetic instead of 64-bit, and limit its workspace to the measly 64 MB of RAM, minus whatever the system harness took. Right off the bat, the figures were revealing: 2,500 @ 1s, and 3000 @ 10s.

The engineer performed a few optimization passes, and eventually got 2,850 @ 1s; 3,100 @ 10s; and almost 3,200 @ 30s — enough to put every human in their place, especially in a grandmaster tournament setting where there's no fixed time per move. Goodbye, World: The End of Programmers

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Becoming a programmer was, for me, a compromise. Midway through my junior year of college, it became clear that, as an international student from Korea, I likely wouldn’t be able to stay long after graduation unless I changed my major from philosophy to a STEM field that made me eligible for a visa extension. The obvious choice was computer science, which was already my minor.

The switch came with a sense of shame, which in retrospect had a whiff of melodrama. I suppose I was afflicted by what Ian McEwan once called “modern forms of snobbery” in describing a character who, after her bookish college education, came to consider “a man with a degree in chemistry incomplete as a human being.” Chemistry, computer science—same diff. It’s safe to say I did not have many friends in the CS department.

But I don’t think my squeamishness was entirely misplaced. In the post-Facebook-IPO ferment of the 2010s, this country’s computer science departments were newly flush with a certain breed of striver who in another era would’ve gone to Wall Street. My theory is that many in America who acquired a hostility toward technology acquired it by way of a hostility toward these upstarts. That is to say, they aren’t so much anti-technology as anti-technologist.

In any event, I half-assed my way through a good many CS assignments, which meant I was never in danger of going to grad school for AI. But by virtue of a robust public math education in Korea, my entry into the subject was, mercifully, not too bumpy. If anything, theoretical computer science and distributed systems seemed like a continuation of philosophy.

But I could not dispel the sense that programming was a frivolous waste of energy. Every line of code I had to read, every line of code I had to write, was a theft of the time I could’ve spent reading something I cared about. Perhaps it didn’t help that my first two languages were Java (hideous and tedious) and C (admirable but unforgiving). To this day, Java code is like a poisoned madeleine that brings back unwelcome memories.

After college, my visa dictated that I take a job directly tied to my major, and I found a tech company willing to sponsor my green card. The issue was, it could be any number of years until I got the green card—four, it’d turn out—so I figured I might as well find a way to make programming pleasurable.

The first couple of months on the job were joyless. I worked at a health care startup, which I thought gave me a serviceable sense of moral cover against the ongoing techlash and a way to indemnify myself later as a writer. But I liked my coworkers, some of whom also harbored artistic ambitions, and after work we’d regularly adjourn to literary events at the McNally Jackson, a block from our office.

Then, one day, I discovered a language called Haskell. I was tasked with rewriting a code base using a library that borrowed concepts from it. My vague impression of Haskell had been that it was a difficult language involving a branch of mathematics called category theory. There was something seductive about the intimidating aura around it, and when I tried my hand at writing basic programs, the language was indeed challenging, but appealingly so.

I started lurking in Haskell forums where researchers in the field of programming languages—“PL,” as it’s known—flaunted their lavish command of the language. PL researchers might as well be the poets of computer science: a small community of attentive monomaniacs who engaged in a largely unprofitable enterprise—a rarity in computer science—with all the attendant beef and infighting. The unprofitability meant that, unlike AI, the field had an incorruptible dignity to it.

Soon, to my surprise, I found myself programming outside work. After months of tinkering with Haskell, I discovered a book titled Writing a Compiler in Go. Turns out you could even invent a language yourself. I made a few toy languages, useless in practice but invaluable for demystifying how programming languages work.

There was also an oblique way coding could help writing. In Draft No. 4, John McPhee, the journalist’s journalist, says that he still uses an ancient text editor called Kedit, recommended to him in 1984 by a programmer at Princeton. (“I have never used a word processor,” he writes.) When I switched to a text editor myself, I could see why he’d stuck with it. With open source code, I could add silly but useful features, such as highlighting “TK”—a common shorthand in journalism for placeholder text—in a conspicuous color so that it was easy to locate later.

If nothing else, I came to appreciate that programming was a reliable way to enter that elusive mode called flow, a state in which we’re somehow forgetful and alert at once.

In the industry, there’s a general impression that fussing over programming languages is juvenile. If a job candidate expresses enthusiasm for languages, it could very well be a red flag. Being a “polyglot” is no virtue if you can read a menu in five languages but can’t write decent prose in one. And per the popular and influential essay “Choose Boring Technology,” using fancy, novel languages is at odds with serious, robust engineering—akin to how avant-garde poetics are unfit for drafting legal contracts.

Unfortunately for my employers, I had precisely zero fucks to give about serious engineering. I did, however, give a great many fucks about having a pleasurable time at my day job until I received the green card. I took on projects if they’d give me an opportunity to use a new language, which is a dumb reason, and for the same reason even volunteered to change teams, usually a bad career decision. At the obligatory company hackathon weeks, I hacked together domain-specific languages—those specialized for a narrow purpose. I made a meager public contribution by submitting a couple of low-hanging patches to the official TypeScript compiler. I picked up a few gold badges on Stack Overflow. The years went by and, what do you know, I became a pretty decent programmer.

Then everything changed, as it did for everyone else, in November 2022. When ChatGPT arrived, it was a wild card whose impact on the industry was far from obvious. Using neural networks to write code wasn’t new. Early readers of Andrej Karpathy’s blog will remember the 2015 post featuring plausible-looking C code generated by an LSTM—a precursor of the transformer—which nonetheless amounted to little more than a parlor trick.

I remember tasking GPT-4 with rewriting a simple JavaScript program in different languages. It performed like Google Translate, instantly and passably. I likened ChatGPT to the monkey’s paw from the eponymous horror story, the accursed object that grants your wishes but not without casualties. In dutiful pursuit of the given objective—and only that objective—LLMs produced a locally correct solution while mangling essential code around it.

Then, in early 2025, AI command-line tools were released. At first, what I saw wasn’t so much a leap in quality as a greater eagerness to volunteer help. Then they improved—at this point I should quote Hemingway’s line about how one goes bankrupt—gradually, then suddenly. Within a year, they became massively, belittlingly better at my job.

Some engineers still labor a great deal to remain unimpressed, quibbling about how “it’s good at X but not yet at Y.” Please. The flaws are there, sure, and guardrails are essential. But a better heuristic for their usefulness would be: If you had to choose, for the rest of your life, between pair-programming with a competent colleague and these tools, which would you want? Any programmer worth their salt must acknowledge that they won’t be as useful to their colleague as these tools are. Even if the colleague were Linus Torvalds, I would choose the LLMs, since they won’t give me hell for asking dumb questions.

For someone who feels no allegiance to tech, the usefulness of these coding tools triggers contradictory emotions. I find ChatGPT’s indecorous entry into the literary world intolerable, and I remember feeling a creeping nihilism about the writing profession when it was released. I know firsthand what a reliance on AI can do. As I’ve come to rely more on coding tools to hand me solutions, my ability to code has gone through a swift and decisive decline. Depend on it to write sentences and it will, like a neurodegenerative disease, blunt the finer senses that writers must protect the way pianists protect their hands.

Earlier this year, it was reported that more than 90 percent of code at companies like Anthropic and Coinbase is written with AI. As most people are prompting rather than hand-coding, much of modern software engineering has become machine-operated, not unlike Mitch McConnell these days.

While this may not be the end of programming, it’ll be the end of programmers. To keep calling those in this profession “programmers” would be incorrect, since the entity that does the programming is a platoon of agents, with humans at desks prompting something out of them. A more flattering term might be meta-programming, but it’d be more accurate to say that programmers have become operators of sorts.

An operator is someone a degree removed from the thing they manipulate. (Unlike someone chipping at rock with a pickaxe, an operator of mining equipment never touches it.) Perhaps one way to describe how the nature of the profession has changed is that it’s as if cooks had stopped making food themselves and taken to writing recipes instead. If you’ve spent any time around AI Twitter, you might be forgiven for thinking that software engineering is now about looking impressed by markdown files.

This isn’t necessarily something to be defeatist about, and I’m not pessimistic about the quality of engineering. When coding agents were first released, I wasn’t sure whether this was just another natural progression, since the story of programming is a story of ever higher levels of abstraction. From bytes to assembly to higher-level languages like Python, we’ve built tools to domesticate the complexity. And with AI, we’ve finally handed off tasks that are constitutionally unfit for the human brain.

But as programmers cede control over the final unit of manipulation, programming no longer qualifies as a craft. Hence my disagreement with Simon Willison, otherwise a voice of reason among witless clout chasers in AI discourse, who wrote, “Quitting programming as a career right now because of LLMs would be like quitting carpentry as a career thanks to the invention of the table saw.” With all due respect, this is a flawed analogy, as carpenters working with a table saw are still craftspeople.

This means there won’t be great individual programmers in the way that someone like Brian W. Kernighan was: the programmer laureate who cowrote what is possibly the greatest book on programming, The C Programming Language, by which we measure programming languages to this day. (It is the book that popularized the “Hello, World” convention.)

Another loss is that programming no longer induces the flow state—or it induces a shallow sort of flow at best. There’s still a certain “hookedness” to the activity, but whereas tackling a gnarly bug once meant begging every individual neuron in my brain to do its part, the work has become suspiciously effortless. That hookedness may be closer to what the cultural anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll called, in her 2012 book Addiction by Design, the “machine zone,” the trancelike state of the Las Vegas gambler.

Even though I’ve stopped coding myself, I find I’ve been producing more code than ever before. The features I’d built for my open source editor in the past were unserious enough that I could bang out a quick program on a weekend afternoon or two. But with Claude Code, I’ve become addicted to making what has essentially become a heavyweight word processor, a juiced-up Scrivener. (My changelog tells me I’ve built 239 features and 17 extensions, mapped to 141 keyboard shortcuts.)

One Sunday this summer, I was disconcerted to discover that I’d spent 10 hours in Claude Code. How do I know? I used Claude Code to build a time-tracker for Claude Code usage. I resolved to cut back. I spent eight hours the next day. This compelled me to write a program, using Claude Code, to block myself from Claude Code except for two days a week, which I of course tried to undo with Claude Code, only to be valiantly defended by Claude Code.

With this borrowed competence has come a total loss of interest in learning new languages. A couple of years ago, an iOS meditation timer app I’d used for years started nudging me with ads, and I resolved to one day learn Swift, the language Apple created for iPhone and Mac apps, and build a simple version myself. But I now know that’s never going to happen, because I’ve already built it knowing zilch about Swift syntax, along with 12 more iOS and Mac apps: The machine helped me fork the long-unmaintained SelfControl into a more granular internet blocker; by reverse-engineering a clipboard manager—I was never sure it wasn’t leaking my input—it built a new one with an auditable security profile.

I suspect, going forward, that I’ll still mess around with code, but no longer as a professional. Recently, it became clear that I needed to quit my day job as a programmer to finish a book I’d set out to write. I once told a writer friend that I’d try to be a mole in this industry as long as I was in it. I did get to see some things while I stuck it out and, along the way, coaxed that green card from my employer.

I’m not too sorry to leave this profession at this moment of nuttiness. But I’ll admit that, after all those languages I enlisted to make my detour more tolerable, I came to appreciate the pleasures of polyglot programming. As humans cede control to the machine, it’ll be a joy lost to us all.

She refocused some of her energies on collage in recent years, and her visual work featured this past spring in solo exhibitions at Picture Theory in Chelsea and at the American Academy of Arts and Letters in upper Manhattan, where an early collage from the ’70s joined works made since 2020. From her home in upstate New York, Sante spoke with A.i.A. about learning to look through magazines, moving her way into fine-art collage, and finding poetry in images she gives new life.

What do you remember about your early collages that made you want to keep making them?

I had two sources of materials for my earliest collages: a stack of Arizona Highways magazines from the 1950s and early ’60s, and my parents’ subscription to Life. I remember a collage that I made featuring Jimi Hendrix and a mushroom cloud: That was hip. I was imitating collages I saw—not artistic collages, but collages in popular media. In the early Rolling Stone, on the letters page, almost every week they’d have a collage by this graphic designer named Wilfried Sätty, whose house I stayed in years later, after his death in 1982. He had a particular style: outer space plus old engravings.

I’m colorblind, and that was a big problem for me. I drew, but had to work in black and white. I wasn’t that bad—just not as good as I wanted to be. In high school, I started making band flyers, which I continued when I was living on the Lower East Side in the 1970s and ’80s. I made several for the Del-Byzanteens, a band I also wrote lyrics for. Many people in the band became celebrated in one way or another: Jim Jarmusch as a filmmaker, Jamie Nares as a painter, and Phil Kline as a composer.

How did your work at the Strand Bookstore find its way into your collages?

We started a collective publication called Stranded that was originally going to be like a workers’ gripe sheet. But instead, we published art and poetry and stuff like that. I had collage-like things in all four issues. Within a week of my starting, Fred Bass [fabled owner of the Strand] named me the paperback department—I was the entire department all by myself. Bookmen in that period did not take paperbacks seriously and just put them out in the 25-cent carts. That meant I had to deal with romance books, and when I put those out on Thursdays, they were mobbed by old ladies, and it was a nightmare. But it also meant that whenever they bought a library, they would throw me everything that wasn’t a hardcover book, including magazines, maps, postcards, and all kinds of stuff that I took home and still have. I made collages recently with material I acquired back then at the Strand.

What was the Strand like as a downtown institution then compared to now?

Nobody was very literary on the Lower East Side in the ’70s. People were starting bands and making movies. There wasn’t even painting at the time. We had to wait for Basquiat. In the ’70s, we had some bad poets, a few bad essayists. Nobody [in the neighborhood] wrote fiction. But at that point, the sociology was nothing to compare to now, because you could waltz into New York City hitchhiking from Denver or somewhere and find yourself a cheap apartment and get a job at the Strand. Your salary would pay your rent, and you had money left over to go clubbing. That’s long gone now.

What were you looking for? What made something conducive to collage?

I liked texture and was really interested in contrasting eras. I liked faded color. I was taking inspiration from a lot of different sources. Of the flyers I made for the Del-Byzanteens, one of them [drew from] big posters that a syndicate put out to try to promote a science-fiction comic strip. I X-Acto–knifed out the thought balloons and filled them in with other material. That was because I had a friend who’d just come over from Paris with a Situationist International book, and I got inspired by that.

You mentioned beginning with an interest in popular-media collage. What art world collagists interested you later on?

I was inspired by Max Ernst and Robert Rauschenberg. I found a book by Jess, Robert Duncan’s partner and a great collage artist. I discovered Bruce Conner and, of course, the Surrealists and Dadaists—Berlin Dada, especially, with John Heartfield and Hannah Höch. And the Russians, like El Lissitzky and Alexander Rodchenko. There’s a whole world of collage out there.

How would you describe your decision-making process?

There are collages that I don’t quite know how to resolve. They might need something else, but I don’t know what. Often they’re just made on the spot.

One work that especially caught my eye in your Arts and Letters show was De La (2023). How did you arrive upon the composition for that?

That’s my favorite work of mine. That’s one I’m keeping. It just came together in a particular way, and it zinged. And I can take you through exactly how I made it, because I do remember. The materials go back to 1981, which was the tail end of people selling shit on the street on the Lower East Side. There had been this kind of ad hoc 24/7 flea market around Astor Place. One night, I was walking down St. Marks Place, and there was a guy selling damaged posters. I bought a shopping bag’s worth for 10 bucks; they were all falling to pieces. The yellow and white in De La is from a poster for an early movie by the Italian director Elio Petri, and the lettering comes from miscellaneous papers from a local auction up here [in upstate New York]. It had all kinds of crap, including a poster for a touring Laurel and Hardy anthology film in Spanish. The tire at the top comes from a French car magazine from the ’30s, and the chrysanthemum comes from a seed-company ad in Life. The glove is from a 1950s Punch, a humor magazine that had wonderful advertising. And then the star of the show is the woman with the fingerprints on her back, which I’d been saving up for the longest time. It comes from a French soft-porn magazine from the ’30s. It was magic how they all came together.

What do letters and different bits of text do for you in collage work?

There’s lettering in, I’d say, 95 percent of my collages, and that’s partly because I kind of think of them as movie posters. One of my original inspirations was the posters I saw when I first started going to the movies as a child, for films like Ben Hur (1959) and Hawaii (1966). They had enormous, sculpted letters for the title, and then there would be little figures swirling around them illustrating the scope of the story. In a way, everything I do refers back to those.

Are there writerly aspects to the collage-making process for you? They can’t help but suggest some sort of narrative for the viewer, but I don’t know about the maker, necessarily…

It’s ambiguity central. I started life, so to speak, as a poet. I later realized I was a prose writer, but poetry is at the heart of everything for me. Among other things, that means making a structure from a sort of lean-to of ambiguities—one ambiguity resting on another, all suggesting a story and all suggesting a meaning but never actually giving it to you. If I make a collage and it seems to have a legible message, I have failed.

What makes you turn to collage instead of writing when a creative impulse strikes?

It’s something I feel in my hands—like, “By the pricking of my thumbs, something wicked this way comes.” It’s the feeling that I need to make something. Being a writer does not involve the body the way that making visual art does, and I have that craving. I wanted to be an artist before I ever thought of being a writer.

Because I have so much to do right now, I’m not running down to the basement to make more collages. But in the meantime, I’ve bought things on eBay. I bought a bunch of 19th-century chromolithographs I’m eager to use, and somebody sold me these incredibly dirty comics—I don’t mean dirty like sexy, but dirty like they were lying in a crawl space for 75 years. I like dirt. I like fading. I like things that are crinkled, things that are ripped. I prefer things that are damaged, because they carry their histories. They establish a custodial chain and have a lot of dead people’s fingerprints on them. If something looks like it comes out of the box, even if it was published in 1922, I’m far less interested than if it’s fucked up.

Young Adults Living With Parents Surge Fastest in America’s Once-Affordable Regions

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Young adults living with their parents have become a familiar symbol of America's housing affordability crisis. But the geography of that living arrangement is changing, according to new research.

A record 25.2 million Americans aged 18 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, or roughly 1 in 3 young adults. Nationally, that share barely budged between the pooled 2020–22 and 2023–25 periods, according to The Geography of the Crowded Nest, a new report from Realtor.com®.

Beneath that apparent stability, however, the South and Midwest—long considered the nation's affordability havens—recorded the largest year-over-year increases in 2025, with the share living at home rising 1.4 percentage points to 32.3% and 31.7%, respectively.

Meanwhile, in the notoriously expensive Northeast—where living with parents has historically been most common—the rate fell 2.3 percentage points to 36.9%, its lowest level since 2010.

It pushes back against conventional logic that cheaper housing markets offer young adults an easier path to independence.

"This is not a coastal problem that has stayed coastal; it has spread to even the more-accessible parts of the country," writes Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com and author of the report.

Perhaps the clearest illustration comes from two metros that, at first glance, seem worlds apart: San Antonio, TX, and Boston, MA.

One is steeped in cowboy culture; the other, Ivy League academia. In October, the median asking rent in Boston was $2,954, more than double the $1,117 in San Antonio. Yet the share of young adults living with their parents was nearly identical: 33.6% in the Alamo City and 34.3% in Beantown.

“The short answer is that different affordability constraints can produce the same outcome,” says Jones.

Take the Midwest, where the median home listing price is nearly $183,000 below the Northeast's. It's a sizable gap that obscures the fact that prices in both regions have climbed at nearly the same pace since before the pandemic: 48.4% and 50.8%, respectively, according to data from Realtor.com.

Even excluding 18- to 24-year-olds, who may still be establishing their careers and are therefore more vulnerable to economic pressures, the Midwest reached a record: 14.8% of those ages 25 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, the highest rate for that age group in 25 years.

At the same time, many affordable markets didn’t see the same run-up in local wages as they did in home prices, which only deepened the divide.

"In San Antonio, the problem is the wage-price gap," says Jones.

Geoffrey Sanzenbacher, an economist at Boston College who has studied the rise in young adults living with parents, points to another side of that relationship.

"The same things that might make an area expensive—lots of good jobs with good pay—also serve to keep people out of their parents' home," he tells Realtor.com.

"Areas with hot labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't afford an apartment. Areas with poor labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't find a job," he adds.

His September research found that higher rents were associated with greater co-residence among adults aged 25 to 34. Stronger employment and wages were associated with lower rates among men, although the relationship was not statistically significant among women.

Of the 20 major metros examined, 12 recorded increases between the pooled periods, while eight saw declines.

Las Vegas experienced the largest increase, followed closely by San Francisco and San Jose, CA—markets with vastly different housing costs. Even in Texas, San Antonio's rate climbed while Houston's fell.

One possible explanation lies in who benefits when housing becomes more available.

In San Antonio, active home listings are now more than 40% above pre-pandemic levels, according to Jones. Yet the metro recorded one of the largest increases in young adults living with their parents.

"By and large, the market has homes available, but local young adults can't qualify for them at current prices and rates," Jones says, noting that higher-earning newcomers and existing homeowners looking to trade up are better positioned to purchase those properties.

But San Antonio's rental market has also improved. Approximately 6,900 apartments were completed in 2025, according to Northmarq. By year's end, vacancies had reached 11.8% and asking rents had fallen 4.5%.

Boston offers a counterpoint. Jones suggests that new rental construction, combined with the metro's higher-paying industries, may have helped some young adults establish independent households despite a much tighter housing market.

"Markets where supply growth is concentrated in the mid-to-upper tiers still see entry-level households struggle to keep up," Jones says. "The lesson for other markets is about the type of supply that moves the co-residence needle."

There's one last side to the story of young adults moving back home: A growing number are opening their own homes to their parents.

In his research, Sanzenbacher found that the share of adults aged 25 to 34 hosting their parents increased from 1.6% in 1996 to 4% in 2025—a living arrangement outside the scope of the new co-residence figures from Realtor.com.

In Boston, George Sarkis, co-founder and CEO of The Sarkis Team at Douglas Elliman, says some parents are helping adult children with down payments, while other families are considering shared living arrangements.

"It can also work in the other direction, with adult children helping aging parents while allowing everyone to remain close," he says.

Sarkis adds he's seeing buyers considering how a property can serve different generations over time. That can mean finding homes with private suites or separate living quarters that allow family members to maintain their independence.

"Living under the same roof doesn't necessarily mean sacrificing independence. With the right property and layout, it can be a strategic financial decision that benefits multiple generations," he adds.

But those arrangements can introduce another financial hurdle.

A separate analysis from Realtor.com found that homes marketed for multigenerational living in San Antonio received 65.2% more page views than standard listings in 2025. Yet their median asking price was $573,105—nearly 69% higher than standard homes.

How Richmond, Virginia, schools are tackling a $680M facilities funding shortfall

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Filardo said that schools’ HVAC systems are complicated. They’re not just for temperature control, but also air filtration and dehumidification. Retrofitting older schools also presents unique challenges.

“In the 1950s, there would be one outlet in a classroom, maybe,” she said. “If you were lucky, one on each wall.”

From the states’ perspectives, Filardo sees a wide range of approaches to start addressing needs. Wyoming, for example, has used coal lease bonuses — mining company payments for extraction permits — and Massachusetts has implemented a statewide sales tax.

Meanwhile, in Virginia, Richmond was one of many seeking to boost facilities budgets. The same measure failed to pass in 32 of the 47 localities statewide.

Kristen Nye, executive director of Virginians for Modern Schools, an organization supporting the measure, said voter education proved to be very important in Richmond’s case. She added that the measure opened up opportunities that may have previously seemed out of reach.

87% of educators use personal devices in teaching. The next great 1:1 investment might be the one at the front of the room.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even in districts that issue laptops to staff, there is a good chance a teacher’s personal laptop, tablet or phone is carrying some of the workload. In fact, a recent study by the University of Arizona found that 87% of educators surveyed use their personal devices for at least some teaching-related tasks. Those personal devices were purchased, set up and managed by themselves, with two-thirds reporting that they download institutional documents (for example student data, grade books) onto them.

While districts are providing teaching devices, these numbers suggest that alongside the school-issued laptop, a share of teaching work, from grade books to student records, ends up on devices the district cannot see or manage.

In the movement around enabling technology in classrooms for students, this study presents a larger conversation on how technology is being used by educators who are working closest to them.

Over the last two decades, classroom devices have gone from shared computer labs to one-to-one deployment. Today, 96% of teachers say their students use learning devices in class. This did not happen overnight. It took years of planning from administrators, careful execution by IT teams, significant investment and district grants. As a result, student devices have a tried-and-true playbook. Districts know what to expect with refresh cycles, IT teams understand device management and superintendents can anticipate their budgets and funding for new devices.

Teachers are the cornerstone of classroom tech, and it is time their devices got a playbook too. As they regularly carry heavy computing loads, in everything from running lessons, online grading, to joining calls, they deserve the same level of detail and detail to device deployment and management. This is more handing out a laptop. It means giving teachers a device capable enough that they don’t need to reach for their own.

This is even more important as teachers regularly handle sensitive data. As the study found, more than half of participants who downloaded institutional documents onto personal devices, reported that their devices had automatic cloud backups through personal accounts.

While using a personal device for teaching might seem harmless in nature, the device is not under the districts' scope. Updates, encryption and sign-in guidelines are not under the control of the district, rather, this responsibility falls to the teacher.

This is alarming as education remains the fourth most targeted sector for cyber-attacks. Robust student, teacher and parent data combined with increased digitalization and lack of cybersecurity resources have left devices and information vulnerable to malicious attacks.

Paired with the right training on cybersecurity best-practices, a well-managed district-deployed device overseen by IT teams helps to enable schools to secure sensitive student data. These devices deploy continuous updates, encourage fast and secure sign-in and ensure private information stays secure.

The ASUS P5 featuring the Intel® Core™ Ultra 7 Series 3 Processor, is a strong example of a device that can be managed at scale with the performance to multitask through a full teaching day. Engineered to seamlessly juggle grade books, lesson plans and video calls, Intel processors deliver all-day battery life and built-in hardware-based security that helps protect student data — that’s the power of Intel Inside®. For IT teams, the device supports centralized management tools that let them deploy updates, monitor device health and enforce policies across a district’s fleet.

The planning, budgeting and IT expertise that has put a device in nearly every student’s hands in the United States is ready to extend to teachers. With the right devices, they can do even more and districts can protect the data that matters most. The next great 1:1 investment might be the one at the front of the room.
