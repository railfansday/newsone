‘It’s Like Facebook Again’: How AI IPOs Could Further Upend the Real Estate Market

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A robotic hand presents a house icon with a percentage symbol, symbolizing growth and investment in a modern office space, merging technology with real estate. Synapse

(TNS)– The deluge of wealth the artificial intelligence boom has brought into San Francisco has already pushed luxury home prices to record heights. Now, with Anthropic’s initial public offering poised to bring even more money into the city, could things get even worse?

Real estate professionals say yes—but that a lot of the growth is already baked in.

Let’s take stock of what’s already happened. San Francisco’s home prices have risen most sharply in neighborhoods that were already exclusive, such as Pacific Heights or the Marina. The median home price for luxury homes in the San Francisco metropolitan area—properties sold in San Francisco and San Mateo counties at or above the 95th percentile price—was nearly $7.5 million from July to September 2026, Redfin data shows. That was up a whopping 23% from the same period in 2025, and up more than 60% from 2019.

Now, some experts suggest that AI IPOs could cause demand to spill even further, raising prices for the few home types—or communities—in the Bay Area where middle-income families might still afford a property.

Already, some data indicates that even non-luxury homes in the city have seen strong appreciation over the past year.

“Typical” San Francisco area homes—those in the 35th-to-65th percentile sale price range — saw a record median sale price of $1.6 million in September 2026, an increase of 6.5% from 2025. While that’s a much lower jump compared to the San Francisco area’s luxury market, it still diverges sharply from that of the rest of the United States, where high mortgage rates and economic woes have caused prices to dip.

Redfin economist Daryl Fairweather believes the heat in San Francisco will spread to other parts of the region. In some ways, it already has; the city’s surging rents have caused many tenants to seek refuge in relatively more affordable cities like Oakland, pushing up prices there.

“This has been the story for at least … 15 years: San Francisco prices and rents going up and people being displaced,” Fairweather said.

Demand for fancy homes will likely keep surging

Some real estate agents say that the AI workers and investors fighting bidding wars in San Francisco often don’t want to give up on their dream house, even as prices rise. That’s part of the reason demand has flowed into other expensive cities in Marin County or the Peninsula, where there are more fancy homes for the buying. Phil Chen, a real estate broker at Christie’s International Real Estate Sereno, said that demand for Hillsborough has soared, with buyers citing a desire for strong schools — and security.

“It’s my first time hearing, ‘Is the glass bulletproof and are there panic rooms?’” Chen said.

Zillow data indicates that home values in Hillsborough, Menlo Park and Piedmont have risen faster over the past year than any other Bay Area city with at least 10,000 people except San Francisco, suggesting that some wealthy buyers are expanding their search radius. In some cities, such as in Piedmont, starter home values—those in the 5th to 35th percentile—have actually climbed more than top-tier values. Still, a “starter home” in Piedmont still costs more than a mid-tier property in San Francisco.

It’s unclear how much demand could rise for homes that aren’t luxury properties or in expensive neighborhoods. One 2019 study found that IPOs increase demand for high-priced homes near the company’s headquarters, but not for low-priced properties. The study’s results also indicated IPOs increased the probability low-income residents moved to lower-income neighborhoods.

But Fairweather said that junior tech workers with smaller windfalls than their senior peers could start to make offers in parts of the Bay Area where their money will go further. Vanessa Hatvany Kitchen, a real estate agent with Compass’ Team Hatvany Kitchen, said her team has seen more people buying condos and smaller single-family homes in San Francisco, fearing they won’t be able to get a foothold after companies like Anthropic go public.

But much of the demand increase is baked in

If there’s any silver lining for buyers, though, it’s that a lot of those companies’ impact on the Bay Area real estate market is already here. Arrian Binnings, a San Francisco real estate agent at Christie’s International Real Estate Sereno, said many AI workers have been able to get cash by borrowing against their stocks or selling their shares via company-approved tender offers, while investors have benefitted from the strength of the public equity market. That’s consistent with some research showing that merely filing for IPOs can raise home prices near a company’s headquarters.

“I don’t see the IPO as the starting gun of a new race. I see it as … we’re probably already at the 50-yard line,” Binnings said. “But I do still see plenty of room to run.”

IPOs don’t always result in an explosion of wealth. In the months after Uber went public in 2019, the company’s stock price plummeted, erasing billions of dollars in value. A similar fate for a public Anthropic or OpenAI would send a chill through the real estate market, some real estate agents say.

But many buyers are optimistic. Chen said some of his clients plan to sell their newly purchased homes in five years, expecting to increase their budgets from $10 million to $25 million. after the IPO. Those buyers, he added, are relatively junior-level employees.

“It’s like Facebook again, but 10x,” Chen said.

Nobody Said The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Was This Funny

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Though I’ve never played The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time until now, I’ve heard people sing its praises for years. Mostly, this revolved around its impossibly high score of 99 on Metacritic, its influential open world and combat design, and its memorable dungeons. But none of those ardent fans bothered to tell me that this classic game has some genuinely hilarious moments. Because the game was originally released 28 years ago, a spoiler-free experience is pretty much off the table for me. But the goofs and gags? Those are all 100% fresh.

I love a zingy insult or quip as much as anybody else, but some of the best video game humor leans into the unique qualities of the medium. These gags go beyond writing to incorporate at least some element of interaction — or a conspicuous lack thereof. An early example of this in Ocarina of Time comes when you show a message in a bottle to King Zora, which reveals his daughter is currently stranded inside the massive fish Jabu-Jabu. The grateful king moves aside to allow you access to the entrance of the fish’s belly — very, very slowly. No button-mashing through this one. Just like you would for a real-life monarch (or older person), you must patiently wait for the scooting to be done. With each incremental booty-shuffle, he makes a little noise that sounds like “moo–ee.”

Once you venture inside the giant fish, Princess Ruto must be carried everywhere by Link, making it impossible for you to use your sword or shield while holding her on your shoulders and moving from room to room. Thankfully, you can use Ruto as a literal dead weight to press down switches to open doors. Better still, you can toss her at enemies like a rock, and she won’t take any damage. (She will complain about it, though.)

Later in the game, you‘re tasked with rescuing nine Gorons from the Fire Temple. Some of them will give you a small morsel of information about how to proceed through the dungeon. Others will simply wish you well. Either way, one freed from their cells, a lilting, cheerful flute ditty plays as they prance away home — leaving a stone-faced Link to continue grinding his way through the dungeon. This happens nine times. Ocarina repeats the bit as you’re freeing the four construction workers in the Gerudo Fortress, and it’s somehow even funnier the 13th time you see it.

I’m not the only one discovering the humor of this classic game for the first time. Folks on social media are gleefully posting about Moblin butts, the sphincter-like doors inside Jabu-Jabu’s dungeon, and discovering a cow in Link’s treehouse. Others are making their own fun, by sequence breaking the game and discovering horrible tween Link or playing "We Are Charlie Kirk" to the frogs near Zora’s Domain. Come for the generational video game experience, stay for the sweet, sweet memes.

The ‘Midlife Crisis’ Isn’t Real. The Actual Risk Is Believing the Myth.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Knowing the reality can benefit your physical and mental health.

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

If you ask someone what comes to mind when they hear the word midlife, 99.9 percent of the time the answer will be very specific: crisis. Ideas about middle age tend to skew negative, no matter the cause.

As a midlife researcher, this is worrying to me because I know just how pernicious these legacy beliefs can be. Yet the most prevalent negative interpretations of midlife are not supported by the research. The truth is so much more hopeful and, frankly, interesting.

While it’s hard to predict exactly who will experience this, it’s likely that 10 to 20 percent of people in midlife will go through some intense period of challenge and growth. For those folks, it’s important to remember that this isn’t a bad thing. First of all, the “crisis” is only part of the story, and not even the most interesting part. Whatever challenges you face are likely to be followed by changes to your life that will help you become freer, stronger, and more tuned in to what you really want for yourself in the second half of your life.

Debunking the myth of the midlife crisis isn’t just about correcting the record—it’s about harm reduction. Cultural notions have a real-time effect on our perception of our health and well-being in our middle years, and if the “information” is more myth than reality, that can have negative effects on our physical, psychological, and cognitive health.

Research done by Becca Levy, PhD, a professor of public health and psychology at Yale, and summarized in her book Breaking the Age Code, has shown the power of both negative and positive beliefs about aging to impact our healthspan and lifespan. Levy found that those who had more positive images of aging (e.g., being healthy and active) lived longer than those with the more stereotypical views about older adults as weak, forgetful, and slow. She also found that those with a more positive mindset about aging were healthier, with lower blood pressure and a lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease.

Beliefs about aging can eclipse the truth in many arenas. If a woman in her 50s whose children have just left the nest goes to see her physician about feeling excessively fatigued, depressed, and sleepless, the cliché of the midlife crisis makes it more likely that her concerns will be dismissed. This can lead to misdiagnosis for more serious conditions such as depression, autoimmune disorders, or hormonal deficiencies, which if recognized can be treated properly.

One of the best ways to set ourselves up for success when the sea of life starts to get choppy is to pay close attention to the way we are thinking about our story. Whether we know it or not, we are always telling ourselves stories in order to make meaning out of what happens to us and how it makes us feel. When we’re going through a rough patch, it helps immensely to take control of the narrative—moving from a negative frame of mind to a more hopeful one that emphasizes agency and opportunity. On the opposite end of the spectrum from folks who enter midlife ready for battle, many enter midlife on autopilot. Some assume they are stuck with the decisions made early in adulthood. Others want out but don’t imagine the alternatives or can’t see ways to improve circumstances.

Either way, it’s important to remember that it is possible to make tweaks or small changes in midlife; they don’t have to be radical ones. One of my colleagues on the MacArthur Foundation Research Network on Successful Midlife Development used to say, “Some people are so fed up at work that they quit their jobs in frustration, when all they really needed was a two-week vacation.” When we sleepwalk through midlife, at best it’s a missed opportunity for growth, and at worst it simply delays the challenges that will inevitably surface. What we know from the research is that middle age—whether or not you go through a “crisis”—is the time to reflect and make change.

I like to think of midlife as halftime in sports: Some of the important plays have been made, but not all. While a little discomfort with the poor choices of our past isn’t a bad thing, getting mired in regret isn’t something that helps anybody. You have the opportunity to breathe, examine what has come before, and start analyzing what you need to do next to reach your goals or modify them. There is plenty of time left after midlife, so reflection can help people see how they might move in different directions.

For those who don’t know where to begin when it comes to reflecting on their life story and even changing it, there is good research to suggest that a “life review” can help. When done well, a midlife review can help you make sense of the past and bring clarity to your goals moving forward (find out more about how to do it below).

One strategy is to do life reviews at the decade milestones (40, 50, 60) and possibly at the halfway marks (45, 55, 65). Another strategy is to do it when there are significant life events that might require some readjustments, such as going through a divorce, a job loss, or having a child leave home. Midlife is a great time to revisit old stories and even consider some rewrites and new beginnings.

It is important to keep in mind that it is unlikely that you will have accomplished everything you expected to by age 40, 50, or even 60. And it is unrealistic to think you will be happy with all areas of your life. If you think you are having a midlife crisis, ask yourself whether it’s really a crisis or if it’s just that you’re not happy with how some things are going.
