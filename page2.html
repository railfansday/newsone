The FAA Nationwide Ground Stop Is Over

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Instability in a system that allows communications between the Federal Aviation Administration, air traffic controllers, and pilots was responsible for the country’s most disastrous nationwide ground stop in air traffic since September 11. The ground stop lasted five hours.

Several outlets reported that a failure in the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure or FTI network, was linked to the FAA’s decision to declare a ground stop. The FTI is the private communications backbone for the air traffic control system. The system is in the midst of a major, multi-year modernization project, which involves replacing a decades-old network of copper wire with high-speed fiber-optic cables. The FAA has said the update will reduce outages and outage-related delays, and make the system safer. It will also be equipped to more easily handle newer aircraft, including drones, electric vertical take-off and landing aircraft (eVTOLs), and other “air taxis.”

At the time the ground stop was lifted on Friday evening, over 6,300 flights within, into, or out of the US had been canceled, according to the aviation tech company FlightAware; 9,500 had been delayed. Freight and mail flights have also been affected. It will likely take days, if not weeks, for airlines to reposition aircraft and crew and get their schedules back on track.

Delays will continue, US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said in a Friday evening briefing at the White House. “It will take us some time to unwind what has happened.” He blamed “old switches, old routers” and a “data overload” for the communications systems failure. He said a “legacy system”—likely referring to FTI—was involved.

The issue was caused by “putting patches into the system” as new equipment was being installed, US Department of Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said at the White House briefing. “The new technology was overwhelming the older system,” he said.

Mullin said there were no indications that the outage was related to a cyberattack, but “there’s not complete certainty right now.”

The system is managed by the government contractor L3Harris Technologies. On its website, L3Harris says that its “team’s deep knowledge of the US national airspace allows us to upgrade systems without disrupting air traffic operations.” A spokesperson for the company did not respond to a request for comment.

In May, L3Harris said it had completed 50 percent of its modernization project. Seventy percent of FAA sites have been upgraded to fiber, the company said earlier this month. The Trump Administration has said it aims to overhaul the nation's Air Traffic Control System by the end of 2028.

In August, the FAA tapped AT&T to upgrade the FTI, the private FAA communication network, to an entirely new system. That contract is worth $73 million, though its worth is likely to climb into the billions over the years. Previously, Verizon had been selected to build and operate this new system. L2Harris is meant to operate the legacy system until the upgrades are in place.

Earlier Friday, before the ground stop, US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy sent a letter to congressional leaders asking them to approve his department’s request for $30.1 billion in funding to further upgrade the air traffic control system, and to improve airports. On Friday, he upped the ask: “I want $50 billion,” he said during a briefing at the White House.

A September report published by the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) raised concerns that the FAA’s attempt to accelerate long-delayed modernization plans “could be prioritizing speed over costs and air traffic disruption.” It found that the FAA does not have a “well-documented” lifecycle cost estimate for its programs, and that, rather than a master schedule guiding the sprawling project, has 11,389 schedules that are not integrated.

US lifts nationwide ground stop for airports hours after halting all departures

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US federal aviation regulator has lifted a nationwide ground stop for all airports. Federal officials blamed a communications outage and said technicians are still investigating.

The US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) canceled a ground stop on all national flights hours after stopping departures at US airports on Friday.

"We have old switches, old routers, we use 1980s old copper lines. We use an old system," US Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy said. "It was a legacy network problem."

US Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no indication the communications breakdown resulted from a cyberattack or artificial intelligence and that the ⁠public ​was ​never ​in danger.

Over 4,500 flights were canceled Friday and about 700 for Saturday and over 10,000 flights within, into, or out of the United States were delayed, according to the FlightAware aviation tracker. With some 11,000 flights scheduled to depart from US airports after 4 p.m. local time (2200 UTC), more cancellations were expected, according to Cirium, an aviation analytics company.

"All departing traffic should remain groundstopped until further notice," the FAA had said earlier Friday.

The agency said the ground stop was "due to extraordinary circumstances and reasons for safety." The FAA cited a temporary telecommunication outage and said, "Every flight has been accounted for and international flights will be handled on a case by case basis."

The agency said flights were halted "out of abundance of caution."

In a post on X, Duffy said the stop was due to a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers.

"FAA technicians are investigating the issue. We will provide immediate updates as we have them," he wrote.

The European Organisation for the Safety of Air Navigation, Eurocontrol, said all flights departing for US airports from its 42 member states were suspended following a request from the FAA to deal with technical issues.

Canada's air navigation service provider, NAV Canada, said it is closely monitoring the US ground stop and later said, "It was implementing traffic management measures at Toronto Pearson, Montreal-Trudeau, Calgary and Vancouver to safely manage traffic volumes."

"Travelers should expect delays and check with their airline. Our systems are operating normally," NAV Canada added.

Toronto Pearson Airport said flights between Toronto and the US have been impacted by the ground stop.

The FAA ground stop order came a day after Duffy asked Congress for $30 billion to upgrade and modernize the US Air Traffic Control System.

"This investment is critical to complete the modernization of our national airspace — delivering a resilient telecommunications backbone for air traffic controllers, modern hardware and software, state-of-the-art control towers, and world-class airport infrastructure," Duffy and Office of Management and Budget Director Russell Vought wrote in a letter to lawmakers.

It's the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023, when the FAA dealt with a system outage. Prior to that, the last ground stop was after the September 11, 2001 terror attacks.

Apple TV’s 5-Season Sci-Fi Spectacle Delivers a Masterclass in Worldbuilding

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Julio is a Senior Author for Collider. He studied History and International Relations at university, but found his calling in cultural journalism. When he isn't writing, Julio also teaches English at a nearby school. He has lived in São Paulo most of his life, where he covers CCXP and other big events. Having loved movies, music, and TV from an early age, he prides himself in knowing every minute detail about the things he loves. When he is older, he dreams of owning a movie theater in a small countryside town.

When watching science fiction, feeling immersed in the story's universe goes a long way in getting the viewer to like it — if you feel like you're part of that world, you'll never forget it. That's the power of worldbuilding, the narrative process through which storytellers create the settings of their stories, from the environment to its "rules." And, right now, there is no better sci-fi work to properly understand worldbuilding than Apple TV's For All Mankind. It's set right here, on Earth, over a timeframe that comprises most of our lifetimes, but in a completely different timeline. While everything is familiar enough for the viewer to feel "at home," its world is wildly imaginative in how it's built, making this series a true masterclass in this subject.

Right now, the world is experiencing a sort of "space age renaissance" of sorts, but in For All Mankind, the Space Race never ended. The series begins with the USSR landing a cosmonaut on the Moon before the US, which is enough to set that world on a completely different path from our own. Now, NASA and the government need not only to understand why they were beaten, but also to double down on achieving the next big milestone and take the lead. The Soviets also don't want to be overtaken, and so the space race continues.

Over five seasons, For All Mankind explores the increasingly dramatic consequences of this historical divergence. Each season is set in a different decade, focusing on the next steps of space exploration. In Season 2, the Moon becomes another battleground in the Cold War. By Season 3, the focus shifts to Mars, with several players competing to become the first to reach it. In the fourth season, humanity attempts to establish a more permanent presence there, while the fifth examines all the implications of human ambitions beyond Earth.

All that might sound like a lot, but it's actually what the series is really all about: imagining what humanity might have accomplished if the technological and political momentum of the 1960s had continued uninterrupted. Its greatest achievement is making this hypothetical and increasingly extraordinary future feel like the natural consequence of events that began with a single different historical moment. The characters and their stories are the vehicle that takes us there, but the world itself is the main sight.

With such a huge timeframe, For All Mankind is more of a generational saga rather than a single individual story, and it's through these people's eyes — especially astronaut Ed Baldwin (Joel Kinnaman) and his family — that we experience how different their world becomes from ours. A lot must happen before any rocket can leave the ground, though. That's where most of the worldbuilding takes place and what makes this series so special, as it spends just as much time setting all that up on Earth as it does among the stars.

The Soviets arriving first on the Moon has some serious ramifications. No US President wants to be the one who "lost the Moon," so investments in space exploration increase on both sides of the Cold War. This leads to technological advancements coming to the population much earlier than they do in real life. Personal computers and electric cars are on the market by the 1980s, for example, as well as screens becoming the norm for long-distance communication instead of phones.

Naturally, the political arena is impacted, too. Space exploration sees the rise of some unlikely heroes on both sides, leading to some crucial (and rather weird) differences from our world. Progressives and conservatives trade places multiple times, with some unlikely candidates becoming world leaders instead of those we learn about in school. Ted Kennedy beats Richard Nixon by cancelling his trip to Chappaquiddick, show character Ellen Wilson (Jodi Balfour) wins the 1992 election over Bill Clinton, and because Ellen wins the election she also pushes forward the legalizing of same-sex marriage in 1998. The series leans into all of the what-ifs of American history without ever lingering too long in any one major change.

With every new season of For All Mankind, an archival-style montage helps to ease the viewers into the new decade, getting them up to speed about everything that happened in the world between one season and the other. With every major development, the series' timeline grows more and more distant from ours, but everything is still rooted in real historical foundations. Regardless of how different things might be, there is always some common ground between fiction and reality, including political personalities and NASA personnel.

Among the many what-ifs, it's particularly interesting how the series uses real-world events like the rise of tech billionaires in the private sector to add new players to its imagined Space Race. By the 1990s, they are already a key part of exploration, with enough power to stand up to both the US and the USSR, sometimes even forcing them to cooperate. Soon enough, other space-faring countries also rise to prominence, just as developing countries like the BRICS became important in the international arena in the 2000s, too.

Worldbuilding in alternate history, like For All Mankind, is a challenge in itself, as it demands a solid foundation in reality. Finding the balance between what is real and its plausible imagined ramifications can be tricky, but For All Mankind does it in a way that allows it to become an inspiration instead of a dark mirror. Instead of feeling detached from our world, this makes us even more connected; what separates us from that are only a few different outcomes and decisions, so why not chase them?

A Realistic View of What Kirk Cousins 2.0 Brings to the Vikings

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Minnesota Vikings made the move they had to make after a half-season of underwhelming quarterback performance in trading for Kirk Cousins. He will be the starter as soon as possible, which is likely to be this Sunday against the Packers. His arrival has come with much fanfare as the savior of the Vikings offense who, combined with the Vikings dominant defense and good special teams, could position the Vikings for a deep run in the post-season.

But a quarterback switching teams in mid-season is not without its difficulties, even with Cousins' familiarity with Kevin O'Connell's scheme and some of the key players around him from Justin Jefferson, Jordan Addison, and T.J. Hockenson to Brian O'Neill, Blake Brandel, and Christian Darrisaw. It's also an advantage that Cousins has been playing the entire season, so he doesn't have to get acclimated to playing again, as a backup quarterback who hasn't played would need to do.

If Cousins plays on Sunday (as expected at this point), there could be some hiccups as he works out timing and other rapport issues in his first game back after three years. But Cousins has been playing in the same type of offense under Klint Kubiak in Las Vegas, and is probably the most well-versed quarterback in Kevin O'Connell's scheme in the league- and one of the most well-suited to execute it.

The first graphic compares Cousins' rate and EPA efficiency in the play-action passing game, which is central to KOC's offense. He's been one of the more effective quarterbacks out of play-action for most of his career, and has run it at a relatively high rate for most of his 15-year career. He's also played at a relatively high rate from under center, which KOC also likes to do to make the play-action game more effective. Cousins' career passer rating from under center is 107.2, and in play-action it is 110.0. This season, Cousins' passer rating with play-action is 123.4.

The Vikings have used play-action more than the Raiders have so far this season. Part of that is game situation- both down-and-distance and score/time remaining. You would think that with the Vikings dominant defense, they would continue to be able to run play action at a high rate to maximize Cousins' strength in that area.

The second graphic represents Cousins' passing metrics to each part of the field. The big takeaway is that the lion's share of Cousins' passing yards and attempts are between the numbers. He's also had good success throwing at intermediate depth (10-19 yards) this season. That's another important element of KOC's scheme-targeting the middle of the field. Both Kyler Murray and Carson Wentz have been reluctant to target the middle of the field, which compromises the effectiveness of O'Connell's scheme. One of the staples of O'Connell's scheme is the dagger route combination, which has largely disappeared from run or targeted routes this season. It's also one of the most effective route combinations for Justin Jefferson and Jordan Addison.

Bottom line, having Kirk Cousins at quarterback should open up the entirety of KOC's playbook, but it may take some time for Cousins to re-familiarize himself with the playbook and get on the same page again with receivers. Still, I don't anticipate Cousins hesitating to get the ball out to open receivers as Murray and Wentz have done this season.

Since the last time Cousins played for the Vikings- three years ago- he had his Achilles tendon repaired and spent most of his time in Atlanta recovering from that. At that point it looked like he was done. His passes lacked velocity and his movement was slower than it had been. Apparently, he couldn't really push off of his foot with the repaired Achilles and that was negatively affecting his game. But after signing with the Raiders, it appears that Cousins has fully recovered from the Achilles injury and no longer has the issues that plagued him in Atlanta. The velocity is back and while Cousins has never been particularly mobile, he is able to move like he used to before the Achilles injury. According to Albert Breer, when the Vikings were looking into trading for Cousins, after, "meshing the scouts' and coaches' analysis, they saw Cousins as still possessing a live arm and deadly accuracy and having returned to the baseline of athleticism he set before tearing his Achilles in 2024, evidenced on tape in the keeper game that's essential to Kubiak's scheme."

I've watched all of Cousins' throws with the Raiders this year, and it is reminiscent of his previous stint with the Vikings. I don't know that his arm strength has declined much- it doesn't look like it- but there is a caveat. He can make all the throws, as you would expect from a starting NFL quarterback, but he needs to step-in to deeper throws for them to have the needed velocity. That may have been the case before as well but it is something that stood out when watching his tape this season. He can't launch balls down field with velocity off his back foot like Jordan Love or Caleb Williams. This is the impact of being his being 38 years old. Additionally, he has not become any more mobile over the last three years, and the scampering of Kyler Murray to pick up first downs here and there is definitely not in Cousins' bag, if that wasn't obvious. He has run for five-yard gains on a couple occasions this season, but that's about it. He is able to maneuver within the pocket effectively and does fine on bootlegs, but he's not going to outrun many pass rushers for very long. He's also not afraid to deliver the ball downfield in the face of oncoming pressure- and pay the price for it. He's also not afraid of throwing into tight windows and does so with regularity.

Cousins has 8 interceptions so far this season, three of which came last weekend against the 49ers. A few of them came on throws left a bit short, a few in tight window throws where ball placement and/or route were not ideal. One his receiver fell down. And the last one came with 4 minutes left in the 49ers game and he was just trying make something happen down multiple scores. But for the most part Cousins' interceptions came while being aggressive and playing on time making throws that Murray and Wentz have generally avoided this season.

Cousins hasn't been sacked much so far this season- just 11 times in 9 games. He's also the only quarterback who has not been sacked on a blitz so far this season. So, he's generally done an excellent job of avoiding the rush and getting the ball out- maybe only one of sacks can be blamed on him. And whereas Murray or Wentz may look to scramble to buy time, Cousins is mainly focused on getting the ball out. He's very much a pocket passer in that respect, with his only move typically being to step up in the pocket to avoid the edge rush.

The Raiders offense has different strengths than the Vikings, and that has influenced Cousins' play. Their offensive line is similar strength as the Vikings, anchored by All-Pro center Tyler Linderbaum. But the strength of their receiver group is their tight ends- Brock Bowers and Michael Mayer- and they run double tight-end sets at the highest rate in the league. Their receivers are Tre Tucker and former Viking Jalen Nailor. The Raiders running game is terrible- last in the league- averaging just 2.9 yards per carry. Ashton Jeanty hasn't done much as a running back, but he is a good receiving back. So, roughly 60% of Cousins' targets with the Raiders were to tight ends or running backs- mainly Jeanty.

Cousins has the third highest PFF passing grade (91.6) when targeting intermediate (10-19 yard) routes this season among starting quarterbacks and has done so at the fifth-highest rate (24.8%). Most of those targets were to tight ends between the numbers. Cousins is likely to target wide receivers more with the Vikings- as he did in 2022-23- as that is the strength of the Vikings receiver group. But the route concepts the Vikings employ will likely have him continue to target intermediate routes over the middle at a fairly high clip, complemented by outside zone runs that can spread the defense out and make linebackers bite on play action.

In addition to having a poor running game, the Raiders also have the last ranked defense in both points and yards allowed. They've allowed at least 30 points in each of their last six games, which has put Cousins under pressure to keep pace. That has affected the game situation and play calling for the Raiders, which pass just over 60% of the time- one of the higher rates in the league.

Still, despite a poor supporting cast when it comes to the running game and defense, Cousins has managed a top ten ranking (7th) across a composite of key quarterback metrics this season. That's leaps and bounds higher than anything the Vikings have had so far this season.

By most metrics, Cousins' performance this season has been pretty close to his season average across five and a half seasons with the Vikings previously, so from an age standpoint there doesn't seem to be an impediment there to his achieving similar results with the Vikings this season compared to what he did with the Raiders and compared to what he did previously in Minnesota. The schemes in Las Vegas and Minnesota are similar- both from the Shanahan tree- and Cousins' spent a year and a half in O'Connell's system already and three and a half seasons with Justin Jefferson, a half season with Jordan Addison and a year and a half with T.J. Hockenson. It's really a question of how fast everything comes back to him. It wouldn't be surprising if there is a ramp up period, but from all accounts he's hit the ground running since he arrived on Tuesday. My guess is there will be hiccups on Sunday as he gets back into the swing of the Vikings offense- and maybe here and there for a few games- but I also expect him to make more throws than we've been seeing from Murray and Wentz this season. Whether that's enough to beat the Packers and 49ers remains to be seen, and once again the Vikings' defense will need to show up and be dominant, but Cousins should be able to allow the Vikings offense to possess the ball and put them in position to score more points than they have so far this season- even with a more limited playbook and kind of re-learning on the field as far as rapport with receivers.

How quickly Cousins can get up to speed with the Vikings offense could mean the difference between a division championship and potential first seed in the playoffs and a wild card spot, assuming the Vikings beat the weaker teams remaining on their schedule. But the next two games are crucial- the Packers and 49ers- in terms of keeping pace within the division and competing for the top seed in the postseason. If the Vikings can win them both, they move to 8-2 and have a much easier schedule the rest of the season and would hold a key tie-breaker against the 49ers. If they lose both games, they fall to 6-4 and if they can regroup from there to gain a wild card spot they will play on the road throughout the playoffs in all likelihood. That's a tough road to make a deep run. But if the Vikings and Cousins ramp up to play their best football in December- and beyond- they will be as formidable a team as there is in the league and not a team any division winner is looking forward to playing.

But a premium is on winning in November- beginning on Sunday- to best position the team for a deep run. Hopefully Cousins- and the rest of the team- is up to the task.

Inside the Y2K Costumes and Sets of Kelly O’Sullivan and Alex Thompson’s ‘Mouse’

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The making of Mouse gave some Arkansas mothers a very good reason to have kept their daughters’ prom dresses. Costume designer Meg Grace put out a call on Facebook; in the week before the school-dance sequence was filmed, a member of her team drove around town collecting gowns, filling a van. Altogether, they gathered some 50 dresses worn to proms between 2000 and 2003. “It felt like a time warp,” Grace says of seeing them on the actors.

That flicker of recognition runs through the funny, affecting film from Ghostlight directors Kelly O’Sullivan and Alex Thompson. Set in 2002 in O’Sullivan’s hometown of North Little Rock, Arkansas, Mouse follows Minnie (Katherine Mallen Kupferer), a teenager accustomed to living in the shadow of her charismatic best friend, Callie (Chloe Coleman). After Callie’s sudden death, Minnie gravitates toward her friend’s poised mother, Helen (Sophie Okonedo), and the inviting home she has long preferred to her own. Their bond grows more complicated, shaped by affection, grief, and that familiar teenage longing to be someone else.

Mouse is particularly attuned to how a teenager’s dissatisfaction can attach itself to almost anything: imperfect skin, house clutter, a mother’s manner of speaking. The clothes and interiors do more than evoke an era; they reveal how Minnie sees herself and the life she wishes were hers.

O’Sullivan wanted the period embedded in her characters’ lives rather than presented as a cartoon version of the early 2000s. That required thinking about how trends actually reached teenagers there. “Arkansas wouldn’t be on the cutting edge of fashion in 2002,” she observes.

Grace, who grew up in Arkansas around the same time, recalls the stores available to her: JCPenney, Sears, Walmart, Big Lots, Kmart. “We might have all wanted to look like what we saw on TV, but what did you actually have access to?” she asks. You might wait until Christmas to ask for a shirt you’d coveted for six months. “That might be the one thing in your closet that’s really stylish, and then you make it work with everything else.”

For research, Grace consulted O’Sullivan’s yearbooks and pressed friends to unearth old photographs, including shots from their first digital cameras. “None of us knew how to put an outfit together,” she says, smiling. The challenge was to create looks that appeared as though a teenager had put them together with limited options and a still-developing sense of style.

Much of the wardrobe came from Arkansas estate sales and secondhand shops. Among the more discerning finds were the cargo capris, a once-ubiquitous garment largely overlooked by today’s Y2K revival. O’Sullivan recognized the look immediately: “With the polo—I was like, I wore this.” And the more dressed-up moments had their own awkwardness. Looking back at school dances, O’Sullivan remembers teenagers transformed by elaborate updos into “40-year-old princesses of a weird country.”

Production designer Linda Lee similarly sought to “create familiarity without creating a caricature.” That meant letting different eras coexist in the interiors. “The calendar might tell you that you’re in the 2000s, but your home doesn’t actually change that quickly,” she points out. Minnie’s house drew more heavily on the 1970s and ’80s, with bold colors and plenty of texture. Helen’s was more understated, incorporating elements from the ’80s and ’90s alongside newer additions.

The contrast extends beyond the decor. Minnie’s mother, Barbara (Tara Mallen, Mallen Kupferer’s real-life mother), is a single parent and veterinarian whose sympathy for hard-luck cases has filled the house with rescue animals. Her generosity leaves little room for domestic polish. Helen’s spacious home, with its freshly cooked dinners, offers Minnie an alluring alternative.

To dress the girls’ bedrooms, Lee cut up old issues of Seventeen and J-14 sourced on eBay, assembling the kind of wall collages teenagers made from what images they had at hand. There were other bedroom essentials to track down too: O’Sullivan remembers Lee announcing, “We have secured the lava lamp.”

The film’s 2002 setting also shapes its portrayal of grief. “I don’t know how to write teenagers now, quite honestly,” O’Sullivan admits. “The way that we grieve has changed so much, culturally, with social media.” The story draws on her own experience of losing a friend in a car accident at the end of her junior year in 2001, though Minnie’s relationship with Helen is fictional.

In the film’s largely offline world, news arrives not immediately and simultaneously but at its own pace, while public, sometimes competitive displays of grief require taking action rather than simply posting a photo carousel. Cell phones are present but hardly universal: A Sidekick makes an appearance, and Callie has a chunky Nokia. (“Indestructible phones,” Lee calls them. “Could play Snake for hours,” O’Sullivan adds.)

For today’s teenage viewers, however, even the film’s most ordinary details can come as a surprise. After seeing Mouse, a young neighbor asked O’Sullivan, “Wait, your mom could pick up another phone in the house and hear your conversation?” She assured him that this was indeed possible—“and,” she says, shaking her head, “the source of a lot of stress.”

FAA grounds domestic flights after reported network outage

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Federal Aviation Administration today grounded all domestic flights in the U.S.

The rare order was issued by the Air Traffic Control System Command Center, or ATCSCC. It’s an FAA facility in Northern Virginia that is responsible for monitoring air traffic and mitigating potential risks. When ATCSCC identifies issues such as airport congestion, it provides local officials with guidance on how to address them.

The ATCSCC sent today’s ground stop order to all 22 regional air traffic control centers in the U.S. The memo explained that the move was “due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.”

U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy elaborated in a post on X that the FAA “issued a nationwide ground stop for all U.S. airports following a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers. FAA technicians are investigating the issue. We will provide immediate updates as we have them.”

The post didn’t provide any details about the communication issue. According to CBS News, the problem affects a “private communications network” that connects components of the U.S. air traffic control system. The outlet cited sources as saying that an update caused the network to reboot, which in turn led it to crash.

On Tuesday, the FAA announced that it was expanding its use of an artificial intelligence system called SMART. The software analyzes weather patterns, flight paths and related data points to generate air traffic scheduling recommendations. The FAA says that the software is designed to reduce delays and fuel consumption.

The agency last issued a national ground stop in January 2023. It was a response to an issue in the FAA’s NOTAM system, which it uses to notify pilots about hazards such as closed runways. The shutdown lasted about two hours and grounded 9,500 flights.

The current shutdown is already much longer, having started at 3:54 p.m. ET. More than 13,000 flights have reportedly been delayed or canceled. Eurocontrol, an organization that helps European Union member states coordinate air traffic, suspended flights to the U.S. at the request of the FAA.

FAA officials said that inbound international flights will be “handled on a case by case basis.” American Airlines, meanwhile, stated that flights already airborne will proceed to their destinations. The airline reportedly expects the disruption to last all day.
