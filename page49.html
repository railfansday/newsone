Apple TV’s Masterpiece Spy Thriller Series Is So Good, It’s Renewed For 4 More Seasons

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Apple TV's spy thriller series has scored an unprecedented win on streaming, with four more seasons confirmed to be on the way, and it just proves how the show is unmatched when it comes to the genre. The espionage genre has become the talk of the town lately, as all eyes turned to one of the biggest movie franchises of all time.

After all, Dune director Denis Villeneuve was charged with casting Daniel Craig's replacement as James Bond for the suave spy's new movie. After a long casting process with many rumors and conflicting reports, The Batman star Robert Pattinson has been officially announced to play James Bond. That reveal comes on the same day that one of Apple TV's best shows released its own exciting future update.

In a competitive streaming market where espionage thrillers have become some of the most popular shows around, Apple TV continues to thrive with a long-running British spy series. The show in question faces competition from hits like Michael Fassbender's The Agency, Gabriel Basso's The Night Agent, Richard Madden's Citadel, John Krasinski's Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan, and more genre successes.

However, only Slow Horses has managed to go the distance with six seasons so far, and all of them being released on an annual schedule. In fact, the first two seasons of Slow Horses arrived on Apple TV in the same year, 2022, showing how the spy thriller differs from its genre competition. Now, Slow Horses just got a massive future update that further sets it apart from other espionage TV shows.

The Slow Horses season 6 finale aired on October 21, but the show's future was already confirmed to extend well beyond its latest season. After all, Apple TV renewed Slow Horses for season 7 in July 2025, while the hit show was confirmed to come back for season 8 more recently, in September, right before Slow Horses season 6 was released on the streaming service. Based on the Mick Herron series of novels following the agents of Slough House, seasons 7 and 8 will be based on the books Bad Actors and Clown Town, respectively, but that is not all.

Slow Horses boasts a near-perfect 97% overall critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes for its six seasons.

Recently, Sid Baker actress Olivia Cooke, who returned to the show in season 6 after a prolonged absence, indicated that Slow Horses might call it a day after season 8. Talking to The Times, Cooke claimed she was shooting the final season of the Apple TV spy thriller series and that the show coming to an end was "what we’ve been told." However, the future could not be brighter for fans of the espionage drama, as Apple TV has officially confirmed that Slow Horses has been renewed for seasons 9 and 10. That said, it means a major change is happening.

So far, all seasons of Slow Horses have had source material to adapt, mostly covering a single book of Herron's fan-favorite series of novels. However, that franchise runs out of books to adapt with the upcoming release of season 8, which should happen in 2028, if the show keeps to its annual release schedule. While Herron has plans to write more books in the franchise, he has a tighter deadline now in place if they are to be adapted, with seasons 9 and 10 likely to begin filming soon, given how Slow Horses season 8 filming is underway two years before its release.

Besides running out of source material to adapt, Slow Horses seasons 9 and 10 could have had to deal with another major change, as the presence of Jack Lowden's River Cartwright was uncertain when the series' renewal was announced. After all, the actor was a finalist for the role of the next James Bond, and if he got the role, that likely meant Slow Horses losing River, a key part of the show throughout all seasons. However, with Robert Pattinson as the new 007, Lowden is expected to remain as Slow Horses works through its four upcoming seasons.

Two university investment portfolios offer a quieter picture of institutional Bitcoin exposure than the usual buying-and-selling headlines. Harvard and Dartmouth reported the same numbers of shares in BlackRock’s Bitcoin ETF at the end of September as they had at the end of June. The positions became more valuable as the fund rose.

The distinction is useful: a larger dollar figure can describe a change in market prices without showing an increase in the number of shares held. These filings, submitted on November 13, also reveal other portfolio choices that put the Bitcoin positions into perspective.

Harvard Management Company’s September filing lists 3,044,612 shares of the iShares Bitcoin Trust ETF, valued at $144.1 million. Dartmouth’s disclosure lists 201,531 shares worth approximately $9.5 million.

The corresponding June disclosures show identical share counts, valued at about $101.4 million and $6.7 million. That works out to an increase of roughly 42% in the reported value of each position. The calculation compares the two filing snapshots; it is not a measure of fresh cash invested during the quarter.

Matching endpoints do not establish that no transactions happened between them. A manager could trade during a quarter and finish with the same holding. The documents establish the reported balances on the two dates.

The difference is easier to see in Harvard’s SPDR Gold Shares position. Its latest filing reports 1,161,000 GLD shares, compared with 58,862 in the June information table. That is nearly twenty times the earlier share count. The September position was worth about $442.2 million.

Those figures support a statement that Harvard’s reported exposure through that gold fund expanded substantially. They do not, by themselves, explain why. Inflation protection, portfolio construction and other motives require evidence beyond a list of securities.

Nor does the gold increase prove that Bitcoin financed it. Establishing such a rotation would require information about cash movements and the wider portfolio. Here, the Bitcoin share count stayed level while the GLD count rose.

Dartmouth also reported 304,803 shares in the Bitwise Solana Staking ETF and 178,148 shares in the Grayscale Ethereum Staking ETF. Those share counts likewise match its June table. Their September values were approximately $4.9 million and $4.5 million respectively.

The fund names describe distinct underlying exposures. Combining their dollar values can show the size of the reported crypto-linked positions, but it does not turn them into one interchangeable investment. Bitcoin, Ether and Solana can respond differently to network developments, and a staking product introduces mechanics that a straightforward Bitcoin trust does not have.

NewsBTC’s recent report on JPMorgan’s crypto-linked securities holdings shows another reason to inspect the actual instrument before interpreting an institutional total.

The SEC’s Form 13F guidance explains the delayed quarterly reporting framework. These disclosures are a record of reportable securities holdings, not a complete picture of each university’s assets or a live trading feed.

That timing matters after November’s sustained Bitcoin ETF withdrawals. A September position can coexist with subsequent market selling, and the latest filings cannot show whether either university changed its exposure after quarter-end.

The evidence is specific but useful: both reported stable Bitcoin ETF share counts through September, while Harvard’s gold holding expanded sharply. The next comparable filing will show whether that pattern survived the market’s later reversal.

Meta is testing Direct, a standalone messaging app for Instagram

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

It’s only available in Turkey and Thailand for now.

Meta is working on a dedicated messaging app for Instagram that could also expand the reach of the app's DMs. The company is starting to test Direct, a new standalone app that brings Instagram's inbox to a separate app, but doesn't require an existing Instagram account to use. Direct will only be available in Thailand and Turkey for now, Meta said in an update.

Direct looks pretty similar to Instagram's inbox at first glance. It has an almost identical layout, with existing chats carrying over from the main app if you log in with your Instagram credentials. It even has some of the same personalization features, like the ability to set custom backgrounds for individual threads and Instagram's status update feature, Notes.

But while Instagram users can log in with their existing accounts, Direct doesn't actually require an Instagram login. People can also sign up for Direct with their email address or phone number, and they'll still be able to message friends in the app. However, it sounds like messaging without a linked Instagram account won't be quite as convenient. Meta notes that users without Instagram credentials will need to manually add contacts to the app via their Instagram username or a QR code in order to start conversations.

Meta says it's also bringing some of Instagram's safety features to Direct. The app won't allow people to start chats with accounts they aren't already connected with via their contacts. And parents will be able to monitor who their teen is chatting with and how much time they spend in the app if they use the company's parental control features. Additionally, Meta says it will "use technology to detect and act on potentially inappropriate interactions with young people," though it's vague about what exactly that will entail.

The test of Direct is an interesting move for Meta. Instagram head Adam Mosseri often notes that messaging is Instagram's most-used feature. But he was also reluctant to give Threads its own inbox because it seemed "redundant" to use multiple apps to reach the same people when Meta briefly considered bringing Instagram's inbox to Threads. He said at the time that mirroring the Instagram inbox could cause "notification routing weirdness" and generally seemed like a less than ideal experience for most people.

The app also raises questions about whether Direct would compete with WhatsApp in the countries where it's rolled out. It's unlikely an Instagram-centric app would replace WhatsApp for many people, but it could eat into that app's engagement if non-Instagram users can use Direct to message friends instead.

That may also be why Direct seems fairly limited for now. Meta describes it as a test that will only be available to people in Turkey and Thailand for the time being. But if the app is a success, it's likely the company will consider expanding it to more places.

Best Isekai Books Like Dungeon Crawler Carl

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Dungeon Crawler Carl by Matt Dinniman is not, technically, an isekai. The genre was popularized in Japan, and in the genre's current form, it centers around an ordinary person who's suddenly transported or reborn into another world. Carl isn't relocated to a different world—the madness comes to his world. But it does have all the other makings of an isekai: an ordinary dude has to go up against RPG-like elements such as monsters, leveling up, loot, questing, and so on.

Of course, there are plenty of litRPG books for fans of DCC, but there are also plenty of isekai that fans of Dinniman's books would enjoy. Lots of manga and anime play off the "adventuring in another world" trope, but we'll take a look at the light novels, specifically, which are breezy, fast-paced and fun to read bite-sized novels that are designed to be portable.

Grab one (or all) of these recommendations, because you never know when you might need some light reading when you're out and about on your own epic adventures... or just commuting to work. Plus, quite a few of these have accompanying anime, so you can continue the journey off the page, too!

In So I'm A Spider, So What? (available for purchase here) a freak accident causes an entire Japanese high school to be obliterated and killed. Most are reincarnated into another world as nobles, adventurers, and other characters. But one girl is reincarnated in a dungeon as the lowest and weakest monster of them all: a tiny spider.

Of course, part of the fun of an isekai is watching the underdog go from nothing to being kind of a big deal in the world, just like in Dungeon Crawler Carl. Over her adventures, the spider grows more and more powerful, makes some very useful allies (and, of course, some dangerous enemies), and becomes an influential figure in this strange new world.

The protagonist of Completionist Chronicles (available for purchase here) is an ordinary Joe, literally. Unlike in a traditional isekai, Joe doesn't get run over by a truck or die to get reborn. Instead, he makes a choice to permanently start over his life inside a game.

As soon as he does, Joe realizes that he has some abilities that make him a wanted man in the world. Forced to practice his forbidden magic in secret, he makes it his mission to learn more about the world. Joe won't settle for just a slight glimpse of the game; he's after that 100% completion achievement. He's going to get every item, skill, ability, and every bit of knowledge that this world has to offer, or die trying.

The Rising Of The Shield Hero (available here) follows Naofumi, just an ordinary nerdy guy who's suddenly summoned to another universe. He learns that he's one of the four legendary heroes who are destined to save the world from destruction. Unfortunately, he's the weakest and least impressive of them all: the Shield Hero.

Without a powerful weapon, Naofumi has to rely on his brains, strategies, and loyal allies who he meets along the way. Together, they fight monsters, unravel political plots, and find a way to rise above his weak-seeming shield and do his part to save this fantasy world he's found himself in.

In Dungeon Crawler Carl, Carl's survival depends largely on his use of, essentially, a hidden tool. That's also the case for Solo Leveling (available for purchase here). This series is also technically not an isekai, since Jinwoo Sung isn't transported to another world permanently. Instead, mysterious Gates suddenly appear in his world, which connect to other worlds full of dangerous monsters.

In Jinwoo's world, some people "awaken" to become fighters while others stay as weak non-fighters. Jinwoo is one of the latter. The thing is, once you awaken, you're assigned a rank, and you stay at that rank forever. One day, Jinwoo finds that he can do something no one else can: he can level up.

This changes everything. As the only person in the world who can level up and increase his strength, Jinwoo grows in power by fighting through dungeons. As he does, he uncovers the secrets of these Gates and what's happening in his world.

The idea behind Log Horizon (available for purchase here) is based around an MMORPG—a massively multiplayer online role-playing game. One day, 30,000 players wake up to find themselves trapped inside their beloved multiplayer video game, Elder Tales. The game becomes much more serious when they realize that they might be stuck in this digital world forever, with no way to escape.

A group of adventurers bands together to find a way back to their home world. They travel around the fantasy world, defeat monsters, and search for clues about what's happening. And soon, they discover something even more alarming: They respawn after death, but lose a little more of their memories each time. The fight to survive and return home begins now!

With two such similar genres as litRPG and isekai, there's bound to be some overlap. He Who Fights with Monsters (available for purchase here) sits right at that intersection between the Western litRPG and Japanese isekai.

Jason is just your run-of-the-mill office supplies middle manager until he's transported to another world. On his very first day, he has to fend against monsters, wizards, cultists, and cannibals. Like Carl, Jason has to use his wits and some special powers to stay alive in this wild world. The only problem is that he's a pretty decent guy, but all his powers are evil.

In DCC, Carl's cat Princess Donut captures the spotlight. The young cat girl in Reincarnated as as Sword (available for purchase here) is also at the center of the story, but as the title suggests, she's not the main character here.

Instead, the protagonist is an office worker who's reincarnated in the fantast world as a sentient sword. He remembers his old life, but not his name, so he becomes the girl's Teacher in both name and status. They work together to achieve both their goals: She wants to become stronger, and he wants to know why he's in this world. They learn more about themselves, too, and become an unstoppable team.

In That Time I Got Reincarnated As A Slime (available for purchase here), a guy stuck at a dead-end job meets his untimely end at the hands of a robber. As he's dying, he unknowingly makes a pact with a strange system that resurrects him in another world... as a slime, of course.

Like the spider from So I'm a Spider, So What?, the little slime doesn't stay weak for very long. He uses his special connection with the aforementioned system to acquire some extremely useful skills, then heads out on his adventure.

As the slime meets new characters, he uses his strategic way of thinking to forge some extremely powerful alliances. Just like Dungeon Crawler Carl, the slime's story revolves around a fight for survival and acquiring power. And just like DCC, the slime gets more and more involved in the overall lore and politics of the world, sending him on more epic adventures each time.

SpaceX Just Brought a Starship Back From Orbit and Caught It. Here's the Part of the Business That Gains the Most.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With the ship caught but the booster lost, why could Starlink gain the most?

SpaceX (SPCX -1.26%) got half its rocket home. On Saturday, Nov. 14, about 22.5 hours after Starship took off from Starbase, Texas, the launch tower's arms closed around the rocket's upper stage (the part SpaceX calls the ship). It was the first time SpaceX has caught a ship.

The lower half didn't make it. A day earlier, the Super Heavy booster's guidance system canceled its catch and steered it out over the Gulf of Mexico, where its flight termination system destroyed it after a landing burn.

Before it came home, the ship delivered 26 of Starlink's newer V3 satellites. And I think Starlink is the part of SpaceX that gains the most from a ship it can catch. After all, it uses a big chunk of SpaceX's launch capacity, and it makes most of the company's operating profit.

The growth stock finished near $139 on Friday, Nov. 13, the last session before Saturday's landing, about 38% under its post-IPO high of $225.64.

SpaceX has caught and reflown Super Heavy boosters before. The ship was the half it had never caught.

On this flight, though, the booster's boostback burn (which turns it back toward the launch site) stopped early after a low-fuel alarm, according to SpaceX, which is still looking into the root cause. So SpaceX still hasn't caught both halves of one rocket on the same flight.

Last month, I argued that the stock wouldn't get full credit for Starship until SpaceX could reuse the whole rocket. Saturday's catch covers half of that. And no caught ship has flown a second time yet. But CEO Elon Musk said on Sunday that this one could probably fly again after a few weeks of refurbishment.

SpaceX records no revenue for launching its own Starlink satellites. Instead, the launch cost is added to the satellites' value on its balance sheet and expensed over time as depreciation in the connectivity segment.

And it launches its own payloads often: 21 of its 33 Falcon launches in the third quarter were internal missions, and every Starship launch so far has been too.

Showing what this build-out costs, connectivity depreciation and amortization climbed 57% year over year to $986 million in the third quarter. This was up 22% from the second quarter, the same rate as the segment's revenue. In other words, the cost of the network is growing as fast as the sales it supports.

Starlink's already very profitable, of course. Connectivity operating income almost doubled year over year to around $2.4 billion. The segment's operating margin kept growing too, hitting around 45%, up from 38% a year earlier and 39% in the second quarter. CFO Bret Johnsen said the quarter had a one-time benefit, mostly from tariff refunds, worth about 3 percentage points of the segment's gross margin. Even without it, the operating margin widened.

This makes connectivity around 36% of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue but about 85% of the operating income from its three segments. Notably, the superintelligence segment, SpaceX's artificial intelligence (AI) business, pulled in about half of all revenue but earned just $431 million, held down by about $1.2 billion in stock-based compensation linked to acquisitions.

In short, Starlink is the profit engine. A cheaper launch might cut the cost of every satellite added to its books, and the depreciation charged against that profit for years to come.

"I think we'll need a few hundred V3 satellites for people to really feel the impact," SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said on the company's third-quarter earnings call in October. She thinks SpaceX can get there by the middle of next year.

Starship has delivered 52 V3 satellites on its two orbital flights, 26 apiece, so every 100 more takes about four flights. If each flight used up a new ship, every load of satellites would carry the cost of a whole upper stage. A ship that's caught and flown again spreads that cost over many launches.

Friday's close puts SpaceX at around $1.9 trillion. Using annualized third-quarter revenue, the stock trades at around 32 times sales. At that valuation, I think investors are already paying for a large share of the savings a reusable Starship could deliver.

Saturday's catch got SpaceX closer to those savings. But they won't show in Starlink's depreciation until caught ships fly again, and the booster still needs to come home on the same flight as the ship. I'd want to see both before paying this much for the shares.
