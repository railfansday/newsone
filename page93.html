Uranus and Neptune’s strange magnetic fields may be getting stranger

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

New simulations suggest Uranus and Neptune’s magnetic fields could shift dramatically within decades, driven by conditions deep inside the planets.

Earth’s magnetic field is fairly simple. It has one strong north pole and one strong south pole, both lined up close to the axis the planet spins on, and that’s why a compass points north.

The magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune are far messier.

Both planets’ magnetic fields are strongly tilted relative to their rotation axes.

A new study suggests the reason lies deep inside both planets, and that their fields may look quite different today from the last time a spacecraft measured them.

The study focuses on how well material deep inside the planets conducts electricity. In the computer models, conductivity increased with depth.

With that one change, the simulated fields matched the broad features measured during Voyager 2’s flybys in the 1980s.

The work was led by Chi Yan, a research associate at the Jackson School of Geosciences at The University of Texas at Austin.

Yan’s study is built on simulations, not new measurements. Each planet’s magnetic field has been measured up close only once.

NASA’s Voyager 2 flew past Uranus in 1986 and past Neptune in 1989. It’s still the only spacecraft to have visited either planet.

Earth’s magnetic field is tilted about 11 degrees from the axis it spins around. Voyager 2 found that Uranus’ field is tilted 59 degrees and Neptune’s is tilted 47 degrees.

On every other planet with a known magnetic field, the simple north-south pattern accounts for 74% to 98% of the magnetic energy at the surface.

For Uranus and Neptune, the figure is only 12%. The rest of their magnetic energy comes from smaller, more complicated patterns.

A planet makes a magnetic field when fluid that can carry electric current moves around deep inside it. Scientists call this process a dynamo.

Earth’s outer core is liquid iron, surrounded by a mostly solid layer of rock. As the molten iron moves, shaped by Earth’s spin, it produces a field with strong north and south poles.

Uranus and Neptune are about four times wider than Earth.

Scientists think they are made mostly of hydrogen, helium, water, ammonia, methane and rocky material, though the exact makeup is still unknown.

They’re called ice giants because water, ammonia and methane are classified as ices in planetary science.

Yan said the planets’ large size and chemistry could explain the tilted fields. Under enough pressure, water molecules can split into charged pieces called ions, which let electricity flow.

“Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, pressures and temperatures become extremely high,” said Yan.

“Under these conditions, which can cause water molecules to break apart into ions, the material may become increasingly electrically conductive with depth.”

The researchers simulated fluid moving inside an ice giant and tracked how the flow, the heat and the magnetic field changed together.

The team tested several interior layouts, with thicker and thinner layers of moving fluid.

In some runs, the fluid conducted electricity equally well at every depth. Those runs made strong, upright fields much like Earth’s, and the poles never flipped.

When the fluid conducted better with depth, the result changed in every layout the team tried. The fields became messy, with several poles, and they flipped direction at irregular times.

Near the top of the moving layer, where the fluid conducted less well, magnetic forces had less control over the flow. The motion there broke into smaller, more vigorous swirls.

The runs that looked most like Uranus and Neptune had a fairly thin layer of moving fluid. The authors wrote that the effect doesn’t need finely tuned conditions.

That flexibility matters because the two planets differ sharply in heat.

Uranus gives off little heat beyond the sunlight it absorbs. Neptune gives off nearly twice the energy it receives from the Sun.

In two runs, the simulated fields closely resembled those measured at Uranus and Neptune, then looked clearly different 32 simulated years later.

More than 30 years have passed since Voyager 2’s visits.

“Earth’s magnetic tilt drifts by only a degree or so over 50 years,” said Yan.

“Our models show the ice giants can behave very differently. In some stretches the tilt swings by tens of degrees within decades. In others it shifts only slightly over the same span.”

In one run that resembled Uranus, the tilt moved 84 degrees over 40 years. In another, it moved 13 degrees. Fast and slow change both showed up in runs resembling Uranus.

A 13-degree shift would fit what telescopes have seen at Uranus. Its auroras have appeared in roughly the same places as in 1986, give or take 10 to 20 degrees.

The slow periods didn’t last, though. In the 13-degree run, the field soon started changing faster.

Auroras are one way to check. They form when charged particles from the Sun interact with gases high in a planet’s atmosphere, and where they show up can hint at whether the planet’s magnetic tilt has moved.

Some recent telescope studies, including one that estimated Uranus’ rotation rate by tracking its auroras, assumed its magnetic field had remained unchanged since 1986.

At Neptune, the southern aurora appears to have moved about 30 degrees in latitude between 1989 and 2023.

Researchers aren’t sure yet whether that move reflects a change in the magnetic field.

They are now studying sharp infrared images of both planets’ auroras from the James Webb Space Telescope.

Tom Stallard, a professor at Northumbria University, led that observing campaign and reviewed the new paper for the journal.

Both planets also have an ionosphere, an electrically charged layer high in the atmosphere.

“We are busy working on what’s contained within these observations, but the shape and structure of the aurora, as well as other weird patterns in the ionosphere promises to provide us with a way of understanding how the magnetic fields have changed, an important stepping stone,” said Stallard.

Yan’s group plans to combine the Webb aurora images, Voyager 2’s magnetic readings and the simulations to estimate how the two fields are arranged today.

Uranus’ magnetic field could also turn out to change slowly over a long period.

If it does, the models suggest the planet’s deep interior carries electricity about 10 times better than standard estimates.

That could mean the inside of Uranus is hotter than scientists thought. Or it could mean the interior holds materials that carry electricity more easily.

Understanding the interiors properly will take new missions back to the ice giants, the researchers wrote.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Physics.

Bigger armies will not stop climate change, and may make the world less safe

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Military emissions stay out of national climate tallies, and two BMJ authors say bigger defense budgets may leave people less safe, not more.

Many governments are raising their military budgets, and climate change is now one of the reasons they give for doing so. The thinking is that regions turning dangerously hot, wet, or dry will grow unstable and may go to war, so a bigger military is a precaution.

Two articles in a medical journal’s climate issue say that logic has it backwards.

War and the daily running of armies are themselves a large and mostly uncounted source of the gases heating the planet, their authors wrote. Spending more on them may leave people less safe, not more.

The articles are opinion pieces, not data studies, and they appeared in The BMJ on October 21. Neta Crawford, a professor of international relations at the University of St Andrews, is the author of the first. The journal commissioned it and didn’t send it for outside peer review.

Mohammed Abba-Aji of Washington University in St. Louis and Nason Maani of the University of Edinburgh are the authors of the second article.

In an interview with Earth.com, Abba-Aji described the comparison behind that article. “What struck me most was the contrast,” he said. The contrast is between what the world spends on weapons and what it gives away as aid.

Security officials describe climate change as a “threat multiplier,” Crawford wrote. The phrase means a warming climate could stress societies until they fight. The U.S. military, the U.K. Ministry of Defence, NATO, and the U.N. Security Council have all used it.

Crawford reversed the claim: war, she wrote, is a climate change multiplier.

The steadiest source of military emissions is the ordinary running of a military, from powering bases to the fuel burned in training and in moving troops. U.S. and NATO military doctrine stresses long-range air strikes, which produce more emissions than defensive doctrines do.

Everyday military emissions don’t appear in national greenhouse gas inventories. Crawford traced the gap to the Kyoto Protocol talks of the 1990s, when the U.S. Department of Defense pushed for a military exemption.

The department was the country’s largest petroleum user and feared being required to cut its emissions. The Pentagon is still the largest user, she wrote, and its annual emissions are larger than those of most countries.

Arms makers add to the total. BAE Systems, the biggest defense company in the U.K., reported about 377,000 tons (341,686 metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent for 2025 from its own operations and purchased energy.

Fighting raises the numbers fast. Crawford cited an accounting group’s estimate for the first four years of the war in Ukraine. Military operations alone produced about 126 million tons (114 million metric tons) of emissions in the form of carbon dioxide equivalent.

Destroyed forests and airliners rerouted around the war zone added more, and rebuilding will add more again. Those three sources come to roughly 190 million tons (170 million metric tons).

Strikes on oil and gas sites are worse still. The Nord Stream pipelines under the Baltic Sea were sabotaged in September 2022, and an estimated 513,000 tons (465,000 metric tons) of methane escaped, a 2025 study published in Nature found.

Crawford put that at about 15 million tons (13.85 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent, three times what Albania emitted during the whole of 2024.

Ukrainian drones hit Russian oil facilities this September, Crawford wrote, and strikes hit oil and gas infrastructure in the Middle East during the war with Iran this year.

The fuel shortages and price jumps were tracked. In contrast, the greenhouse gases released have gone essentially uncounted.

The second article is about the money. World military spending reached $2,887 billion in 2025, Abba-Aji told Earth.com. This is close to 2.5 percent of everything the world’s economies produced.

Official development assistance, the aid that richer governments send to poorer ones, fell to $174 billion the same year, the largest one-year drop on record.

Abba-Aji and Maani describe a cycle. Climate stress can make conflict more likely, through competition over resources or by making it easier for armed groups to recruit.

“Conflict in turn justifies more military spending, and that spending draws money away from the measures that could break the cycle,” Abba-Aji said.

Wealthier governments may stay out of the fighting, the two wrote, and still help create the conditions for it. They supply the weapons that keep a conflict going while cutting aid to the countries affected.

Crawford also objects to the idea that a hotter world leads to war on its own. The link between climate change and armed conflict is neither strong nor necessary, she wrote, citing a 2017 study that re-examined the Syrian civil war.

Governments can support their populations instead, she wrote, and neighboring governments can negotiate over shared resources. Those are political choices, not unavoidable consequences of climate change.

Climate change is already killing people without any war at all. In some regions, heatwaves, wildfires, storms, and floods made worse by warming now kill more people a year than armed conflict does, Crawford wrote.

By the Met Office‘s estimate, more than 1,100 deaths in England and Wales during the May and June heatwaves of 2026 were due to climate change.

The threat-multiplier frame has also changed where militaries operate. For years, Crawford wrote, armed forces have worried that a melting Arctic Ocean will open shipping lanes and a contest for minerals.

The U.S., NATO, Russia, and China have all increased their Arctic operations, at the same time as the region’s climate has become less stable and those operations harder to run.

Some armed forces present themselves as green, Crawford wrote. The U.S. Navy publicized a “great green fleet” in 2016, and the U.S. Army promoted its investments in green technology until mention of climate change was all but banned under the Trump administration.

Moving bases off coal does cut emissions, she wrote. But even forces that use fuel-efficient vehicles, batteries, solar power, and microgrids remain major emitters.

When spending grows, so do training, operations, and purchasing, and the emissions grow with them.

“Substantial emission reductions will occur only when armed forces reduce their overall size and footprint and transition to lower emitting doctrines, including those that do not require long range aircraft missions,” Crawford wrote.

Asked by Earth.com what governments should do first, Abba-Aji said they should “weigh the trade-offs between military and social spending for their health and climate consequences and set aside a share of security funding for climate adaptation, especially in the most vulnerable regions.”

Crawford’s own answer is to spend more on health, the environment, and the energy transition rather than take money from them.

“While some form of defense is required, the focus should shift to how to ensure effective defense at lower emission levels and reduced costs,” she wrote.

The size of the source that both articles describe is still unknown. Day-to-day military emissions are left out of national greenhouse gas inventories, and the gases released by this year’s strikes on oil and gas facilities haven’t been added up either.

Counting them would be the first step toward knowing its magnitude.

The full study was published in the journal The BMJ.

Pedestal AI raises $4.7 million to agentify the supply chain

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Delivery supply chains are the lifeblood of contemporary businesses — and a new startup is betting that purpose-built AI agents can make them run a lot more smoothly.

On Tuesday, Pedestal AI emerged from stealth with $4.7 million in seed funding. The company’s goal is to use agentic AI to make supply chains faster and more reliable — a plan that’s already brought them together with Fortune 500 companies and some of the largest shipping firms in the world.

The seed round was led by Eclipse, with participation from RRE, Converge VC, Timescale Ventures, and VitalStage. Pedestal says the funding will be used to expand its product and engineering time, grow the marketing organization, and support additional deployments.

In practice, much of Pedestal’s work is about removing friction from the shipping process — with agentic technology used as a means to that end. In one case, the company reduced missed deliveries for a major furniture company by 20% by deploying agents to take on some of the work that drivers were supposed to do. The agentic system they built would call customers 30 minutes before a delivery and collect any special instructions; if the call didn’t go through, a text would be sent from a separate system. Along the way, the deployment collected a wealth of data about friction points in the process, which could be used to optimize the system even further.

CEO Raj Aggarwal says contemporary AI systems are particularly useful for dealing with that data. “The challenge is that the data is messy and it’s constantly changing,” Aggarwal told TechCrunch. “It’s disparate because it sits in so many legacy systems. That’s what makes it hard.”

Supply chain AI has become a particularly hot space in recent months as alumni from autonomous driving teams look to launch their own startups. The supply chain startup Atomic, launched by a team of Tesla alumni, came out of stealth in 2025 with an emphasis on streamlining inventory. Another venture called Loop was launched by Uber alums to focus on predictive analytics.

As Aggarwal sees it, deliveries are particularly well-suited to training reinforcement learning systems. Because of their high volumes and easily measured outcomes, it’s easy to fine-tune contemporary AI systems toward what’s working and away from what isn’t.

Perhaps most importantly, it’s the kind of problem that can have an immediate effect on a client’s bottom line, making it easy to justify the expense.

“If you can get it right,” he told TechCrunch, “you can have really big impact.”

The ability to simply plug in at a fast-charging station and start charging within seconds was once a luxury once reserved for Tesla drivers at Superchargers. However, with the advent of Plug & Charge, this seamless operation has come to many other brands, including General Motors. GM announced Plug & Charge support back in 2022, starting with EVGo. Now, Plug & Charge is all in one space via GM's Energy Pass App, with Ionna and ChargePoint added to the Plug & Charge list in 2026. Today, it confirmed support for the largest CCS fast-charging network in the country: Electrify America.

The update comes to CCS-equipped General Motors EVs, with NACS-equipped EVs to follow in early 2027.

To use Plug & Charge with Electrify America (or any other charging network that has a Plug & Charge agreement with GM), the driver must be registered with GM’s Energy Pass in the MyChevrolet, MyGMC, or MyCadillac app. The Energy Pass links your EV with a default payment type, allowing charging stations to start the charging event and bill the driver directly without navigating any other app. The driver doesn’t have to do anything but plug in; there’s no need to use a third-party app or fiddle around with a frustrating interface on the charger itself.

The function works with Ionna, EVGo, and ChargePoint, and now Electrify America. The Energy Pass section of the apps not only controls payment, but serves as a one-stop-shop by showing past charging history and letting you locate and navigate to charging stations. Tesla Supercharging Plug & Charge was announced in summer 2026, but it has not quite yet come to the Energy Pass app yet. It should arrive in 2027.

In order to get more people behind the wheel of EVs, industry leaders have stressed that the ease and quality of charging stops must improve. This goes beyond just making sure there are enough charging stations available and making sure they’re in working order. From start to finish, the charging experience should be easy and painless. Saving time and making things as seamless as possible should help make the charging experience nearly frictionless, which is a good thing for EV adoption.

Importantly, the addition of Electrify America brings 6,200 chargers that are capable of speeds of up to 350 kW. Some of GM’s largest EVs, like the Chevrolet Silverado EV or Cadillac Escalade IQ, advertise their charging times when connected these ultra-fast charger.

Participation in GM Energy Pass is optional. If the driver doesn’t want to use it, they are free to use the existing charging station apps to charge the vehicle. But it should be easier to use Energy Pass, at least at Electrify America, EVGo, and Ionna stations.

After days of political fallout from former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, President Donald Trump is defending commerce secretary Howard Lutnick.

Last week, Lutnick came under fire from treasury secretary Scott Bessent for not aggressively imposing export restrictions on advanced AI chips to China.

But Trump’s defense of Lutnick, sources tell WIRED, reflects the president's own view that allowing the sale of some advanced AI chips to China can keep China reliant on US technology and simultaneously boost US tech stocks.

Lutnick “was doing that because if you didn’t delay it, if you didn’t do something, you could have a blowup with China. We’re having a very good relationship with China right now,” Trump said ahead of a multiday trip to Asia and a meeting on Thursday with Chinese leader Xi Jinping.

Trump convened an Oval Office meeting on November 5 to discuss the political fallout from Huang’s affair with Alice Huang, the former Megaspeed executive whom some in the US government believe was recruited to spy for China, according to two sources briefed on the discussion.

Megaspeed received advanced AI chips manufactured by Nvidia through several layers of companies that started with a subsidiary of a blacklisted Chinese server conglomerate called Inspur, The New York Times has reported.

In the meeting, the sources say, Bessent confronted Lutnick, asking why Megaspeed was not added to the entity list faster, given that secretary of state Marco Rubio and US trade representative Jamieson Greer, among others, had already agreed it was the correct move.

Lutnick responded that he would defer to Trump and would add Megaspeed to the entity list if that was what the president wanted, the sources say.

But Trump settled on a third approach: for Lutnick to work on introducing a new interim trade rule to stop Chinese AI labs from using advanced AI chips remotely by leasing their compute power from data centers in Southeast Asia.

In a statement to WIRED, a White House spokesperson said the new rule would “crack down on the smuggling of advanced US SI chips” and “help ensure that America’s most advanced chips go first to US SI innovators, not Chinese shell companies.” (Superintelligence, or SI, is the administration’s term for artificial intelligence.)

A senior White House official added that Trump has been adamant that Chinese firms cannot acquire the most advanced Nvidia chips, known as Blackwells and Rubins. Those are different from the H200-type chips, built on previous-generation Hopper architecture, that Megaspeed was acquiring.

Lutnick’s position appears to have been based on a series of considerations, the sources say. For one, Megaspeed has already had the chips for months, and blacklisting the company would be akin to playing whack-a-mole unless the administration dealt with the underlying issue of Chinese firms getting remote access to US chips.

Meanwhile, Lutnick has moved cautiously on any new export restrictions—essentially waiting to get explicit approval from Trump—after he faced heavy criticism in 2025 for expanding restrictions against firms with Chinese ties, the sources said.

In September 2025, the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security started aggressively using the so-called affiliates rule, which automatically extends trade restrictions to any company that is 50 percent owned by a company already on the US entity list or the military end-user list.

But the move prompted immediate retaliation by China, which suspended exports of critical rare earth metals to the US and, the sources said, prompted a wave of internal criticism accusing the Commerce Department of having sparked a trade war.

After the Busan summit at the end of October 2025, where Trump and Chinese president Xi Jinping agreed to a trade truce, the White House instructed Commerce to halt the use of the affiliates rule and clear new export controls in advance, the sources said.

The suspension of the affiliates rule—which came almost as soon as it was implemented—may have had a knock-on effect on companies like Megaspeed, one person familiar with deliberations said.

Inspur, the Chinese server conglomerate connected to Megaspeed, operated a subsidiary called Aivres, which, as a California-based company, could legally buy restricted Nvidia chips without triggering direct export bans and ship them internationally to Southeast Asia.

In total, Aivres transferred billions of dollars’ worth of restricted Nvidia chips, including the powerful H200 chips, to Megaspeed’s subsidiaries.

Instead of shipping the physical chips into China, Megaspeed hosted servers at data centers in Malaysia and Indonesia—then rented out compute power to major Chinese companies. A spokesperson for Aivres said in a statement that the company complies with all US export controls.

Google's core updates arrive with a start time and a scope. Everything else you've read about them came from somewhere else.

The routine is familiar by now. Google posts a sentence. It has a name, a start time, and a note that the rollout may take up to two weeks. Within the hour, somebody has published a “what we know so far.” By the evening, there’s a list of winners and losers. Before the week is out, with the rollout still running, there’s a recovery guide in seven steps.

I’ve watched this for most of 20 years. The only part that has changed is the sentence. It used to be longer.

When Google launched what the industry later called Panda, in February 2011, they wrote a post about it. The post said what the change was for: sites that add little, copy from others, or just aren’t very useful. It said how big it was: 11.8% of queries. You could argue with the result, and plenty did, but there was a stated target to argue with.

Here is the entry for the November 2026 core update, which started on Nov. 10. It was released. It applies globally and in all languages. It may take up to two weeks. That’s the lot: three facts, none of them about your site.

Google has been open about why. In a Q&A on Search updates, it said it changes Search thousands of times a year and announces the ones it considers notable, about 10 a year when it last counted in public. Its core updates documentation adds that smaller core updates go out continually and aren’t announced at all. So a named update is a ranking change that Google decided to put on a calendar. The calendar entry tells you when. It tells you nothing about what changed, or about what happens in between.

The announcement shrank to a timestamp, and the commentary expanded to fill the space.

The date does have a use. Google’s own guidance uses it: wait a week after the rollout finishes, then compare that week with the week before it started. The date draws a line through your data so you know where to look. Visibility trackers do a similar job across the market; they show which sites moved.

What neither of them shows is why. Which system changed, and why it moved your site in particular, is known inside Google and nowhere else. A rank tracker and a strong feeling won’t close that gap.

That hasn’t slowed anyone down. An obscure tool vendor published a winners-and-losers study of the November update within a day or two of the launch. It gave the wrong start date and called it the sixth core update of the year. Google’s dashboard lists three. I’d say the analysis was rushed, but that implies there was something to analyze.

The closest thing I know to a core update is the annual performance review.

Anyone who has had a job knows the meeting. You walk in and get a rating that was settled long before the meeting was booked. It reflects the year you had. It also reflects the people you were measured against, because plenty of companies calibrate ratings across a team; you can do the same work as last year and drop a grade because three colleagues did better. Search works the same way. A page is ranked against every other page that could answer the query, so a site can lose ground without changing anything.

A core update is review day. It’s when the rating becomes visible. Nobody sensible leaves that meeting and asks what changed about the meeting.

The comparison stops in one place. After a review you get a manager, written feedback and a chance to ask what went wrong. After a core update you get a dashboard entry. That missing conversation is the entire market for update commentary: an industry of people offering to be the manager who explains your rating, none of whom were in the calibration room.

The strongest objection to all this is that the losses are real, and it’s correct. Companies do lose a large share of their organic revenue overnight, and people lose jobs over it. The practitioners who spend their time on recoveries are dealing with real businesses in real trouble.

There’s also evidence that sometimes the reviewer gets it wrong. In October 2024, Google invited a group of independent publishers hit by the September 2023 helpful content update to its campus. According to people who were in the room, they were told their content wasn’t the problem, and that they shouldn’t count on getting their traffic back. That is an awkward fact for anyone, me included, who says most affected sites had it coming.

Look at what it does to the recovery guide, though. If the content wasn’t the issue, “seven steps for improving your content” were never going to work. The publishers with the best case against Google are also the best evidence that nobody outside it could diagnose them.

Now the less sympathetic group, which in my experience is the bigger one.

Most of the affected sites I’ve been asked to look at over 20 years had the same history. Somebody decided what they could get away with before finding out what the market already offered. They hadn’t researched what customers needed or looked at how good the existing answers were. They picked keywords with volume, wrote about them, and launched.

When that didn’t work, they went looking for a shortcut, and social media had plenty. A thread with a promise that was too good to be true, and was. A “hack” from somebody who discovered SEO in the summer and by autumn had a conference slot and a YouTube channel. In office terms, that’s career advice from the colleague who joined in August and already runs a workshop. They copied it without asking why it would work. Some of these tricks do work for a few months, which is the worst outcome available, because the fall then lands inside an update window and gets blamed on the update.

Then they hired SEO to make it work, as if rankings were a delivery service for businesses that forgot to have a product. I’ve written about the wall that approach runs into; it hasn’t moved.

This is the employee who did the minimum all year and arrives at the review certain that the rating system has changed, and has brought printouts. In fairness to them, they have a problem that needs solving. “The algorithm changed” is something you can say to a board. “We built a site with nothing to say and then paid someone to get it found” tends not to make the deck.

So there’s a buyer for an outside cause, and the update coverage supplies one. It comes with a diagnosis nobody outside Google could have made and a retainer that renews before the next update can contradict it. The seller gets a client, and the client gets a story. The site stays exactly as useful as it was, which was the problem.

This trade feels legitimate to the people in it because of how the industry thinks about quality.

Take E-E-A-T. Google coined it for its quality raters, and Google’s own documentation says it isn’t a specific ranking factor. The industry read that and sold E-E-A-T audits anyway, with scores for a thing Google says it has no score for. Somewhere there is an author bio that was added to fix a business model.

The traits themselves are older than the initialism. Peter Morville’s user experience honeycomb had “credible,” “useful,” and “valuable” in it in 2004, as facets of a product, with no ranking attached. The industry needed Google to hand it an initialism before it noticed that people prefer things they can trust.

“JavaScript SEO” has the same shape: it’s progressive enhancement, which web developers were practicing before anyone gave it a conference track. I made that case in Stop Learning SEO and won’t repeat it.

If quality is a list of items to tick, then a core update is a new edition of the list, and you need someone to tell you what’s on it. There is never a shortage of volunteers. Back in the office, that’s working to the KPIs. The person who works to the KPIs has to know the moment they change. The person who is good at the job mostly finds out on review day that they still are.

So people who hold quality as a checklist need each update explained; the announcement explains nothing, and so somebody makes one up.

The first question a business could ask after an update is “What did this update change for my site?” Answering it would take the list of systems that changed, how much each one counts, and what every competing page did in the same fortnight. Nobody outside Google has those. You can still pay for an answer. It’ll arrive as a confident PDF with a traffic-light table, and it will tell you to improve content quality, show more expertise, and prune the thin pages.

Now read the PDF from the update before. It says the same thing. So will the one after the next update, because the advice was never derived from the update. If the remedy is the same whatever changed, the change taught nobody anything.

The second question is “what do the people I want as customers need that they can’t already get?” It’s a worse question to be asked. It has no villain and no deadline; it can’t be handed to a thread, and the answer may be that the business has nothing to add yet. It’s also slower and more expensive to answer than a PDF. But it can be answered by people you can find and talk to, and it could have been answered before the site launched.

The fair objection to the second question is Google. Google is in the business of taking in what other people know and serving it back as its own answer, and AI Overviews and AI Mode have made that the main event. The part of the web where people found you through a search result is being eaten, and Google is the one holding the fork. I’ve covered what that did to publishers’ traffic. So why polish anything for a channel that’s shrinking?

Because the second question was never about the channel. A site is a product, and what people need from it doesn’t change when fewer of them arrive through Google. What does change is how much of what you know you should hand over for free. I’ve argued that publishing everything is a terrible business strategy, and I still think so. Holding back the method is sensible. Shipping a site that’s a bare outline of what you’re supposed to be good at is a different decision, and it fails the customer long before it fails a ranking system.

The line between those two is thin, and it’s meant to be. It sits in a different place for every business, and no update, checklist, or thread will tell you where yours is. A search engine certainly won’t; it looks after its own interests, as it always has. The only people who know how much is enough are the ones you’re trying to sell to, which is one more reason to go and ask them.

In the office, it’s the gap between “what did the calibration committee change this year?” and “what did I do this year?” Only one of those is yours to find out, and it’s the one that doesn’t come with somebody else to blame.

Google will post another sentence soon enough. The recovery guide for it already exists. It’s the one from last time.
