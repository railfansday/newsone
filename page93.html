South Korea checks exam glasses after a top scorer admits AI cheating

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Officials are weighing Wi-Fi shutdowns and phone handovers to stop AI cheating at Thursday’s exam. The tax exam’s top scorer admitted using a hidden camera, and police are investigating.

A building on Yonsei University’s campus in Seoul.

South Korea will inspect every pair of glasses worn at Thursday’s college entrance exam, Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told lawmakers on Monday, according to Reuters. The AI cheating checks follow a confession by the top scorer on the national tax accountant exam. He said he cheated with a hidden camera and a generative AI tool.

More than 500,000 students sit the exam, known as the Suneung, on Thursday. At the same hearing, Choi rejected calls from some lawmakers to postpone it. A delay would cause “even a larger disruption” for students, he said. Kim Tong-hyung reported his remarks for the Associated Press.

Each of the 1,300 test sites will get at least one specially trained supervisor to spot AI smart glasses, AP reported. Officials are also considering two more steps, the prime minister’s office said. Students could have to hand over smart devices, and public Wi-Fi at test sites could go off.

Proctors have new guidance on hidden cameras. They will watch for unusual movements around sleeves, collars and buttons. The education and science ministries plan to announce more measures on Tuesday. These could include limiting phone signals at test sites, although South Korean law bans jamming electronic signals.

Some tools are off the table. Officials plan only limited use of metal and radio detectors. Thermal cameras are unlikely because of privacy concerns. Supervisors also cannot search a student’s belongings without consent.

“At the government-wide level, we would also need to give further consideration to measures that will make it easier to identify (AI) smart devices,” said Song Geun-hyeon, a senior Education Ministry official.

Officials are debating whether to make manufacturers build such devices to flash or beep while in use, Song said.

The scandal broke on Sunday. Han Won-seok, 34, posted a video on his lawyer’s YouTube channel, his face covered by a mask. He said he photographed the questions with a microcamera and sent the images to an AI tool. He then read the answers on a smartphone, Bloomberg reported.

“I cheated using electronic devices and generative AI. My passing the exam with the top score was not the result of fair effort,” Han said, as quoted by The Guardian.

Han ranked first in both rounds of this year’s exam. He scored 397.5 out of 400 in the April round and 663 out of 800 in the July essay round, AP reported. He had earlier denied claims online that he wore AI smart glasses. He said he also used the same method on a separate accountancy exam.

Han sent a written confession to Seoul police through his lawyer. Police said they had assigned the case to investigators, and Han said he would cooperate. The finance ministry will move to suspend his qualification, the Korea Herald reported. Under Korean law, candidates who cheat cannot retake the test for five years.

The presidential office called the case a “grave matter”, according to Bloomberg. Its chief of staff for policy called an emergency meeting on Sunday to prepare countermeasures before Thursday, The Guardian reported.

AP reported earlier cases this year. Test-takers wore AI smart glasses on an English test in June and on some university essay exams in October. Norway plans a ban on AI glasses in schools. Some US universities have moved back to in-person exams.

In England, the university regulator published a report on AI on 11 November. It drew on talks with more than 300 students and staff. The report warned that AI-detection tools are unreliable, Gizmodo reported. It said universities may need supervised exams in place of take-home essays.

Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jiff is a datetime library for Rust that encourages you to jump into the pit of success. Jiff automatically handles many common problems related to daylight saving time by putting time zone handling front and center into the API. Jiff has already seen widespread adoption in the Rust ecosystem.

Jiff is pronounced like “gif” with a soft “g,” as in “gem.”

This blog announces the Jiff 1.0 release by giving a tour of its API.

Target audience: Rust programmers who need convenient datetime handling.

Jiff is already used in production in many projects: uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and even as the basis for a Temporal implementation in AWS LLRT. It is time to call it 1.0.

Unless some significant defect is found in Jiff’s API, my plan is to commit to Jiff 1.0 indefinitely. This is important because the intent is for Jiff to be an ecosystem building block that appears in the public API of other libraries. This makes Jiff an attractive public dependency, which means semver incompatible releases are likely to cause significant pain in the ecosystem. They will not be made lightly.

To see a list of breaking changes between Jiff 0.2 and Jiff 1.0, please consult the CHANGELOG.

In programming, a datetime library’s core functionality rests at the intersection of a computer system’s concept of time and a human’s concept of time. The prevailing notion of system time is Unix time. A human’s concept of time is far more complex, but the most popular such model is the Gregorian calendar coupled with either a 12-hour or 24-hour clock. At the core of every datetime library rests some routine for converting between these concepts.

A datetime library typically provides much more than this. Indeed, datetime handling has historically been very difficult. My own belief is that the difficulty comes from poor modeling of the domain. Instead of encapsulating the complexity inside a reusable bundle of code, many datetime libraries pass some or all of the complexity on to its users. Jiff instead tries hard to encapsulate the complexity and make it difficult for users to do something wrong. (Jiff is not the first to do this!)

Whenever I needed datetime handling in Rust, I always got confused. Should I use chrono or time? How do I get my local time from a timestamp? How do I use my system’s time zone database? How do I do calendar arithmetic?

Before I started working on Jiff, this confusion got annoying enough for me to consider writing my own crate. I felt like the APIs of both chrono and time could be improved upon. I personally found the domain quite interesting and so set out to build something better.

Why not improve on an existing solution, like chrono or time, instead of adding yet another choice into the ecosystem? Why Your Body Is Begging You to Run More Trails Instead of Roads

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

(Photo: Cavan Images / Brent Doscher / Getty)

Running a marathon beats up your body. Your leg muscles will be damaged on a cellular level, which will translate into soreness and loss of strength that will persist for a week or more. Now imagine how much worse it would be if you were running one of those crazy mountain marathons with rough terrain and steep climbs and descents. It would be awful… right?

Maybe not, according to a new study in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise led by Mélanie Metra and Guillaume Millet of France’s Université Jean Monnet Saint-Étienne. Millet, who once finished third at the absurdly difficult 200-mile Tor des Géants mountain ultra, is one of the world’s leading trail running researchers. I’ve previously covered his research on the differences between trail and road runners, how to run uphill, how to succeed at Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc, and other trail-centric topics.

This time, Millet and his colleagues explore the question of whether road or trail running beats you up more. It’s not a perfect study (for reasons we’ll come back to), but it has some very interesting—and somewhat surprising—insights.

The researchers asked runners to undertake one of the following three challenges: a simulated road marathon of roughly 26 miles on a treadmill; a simulated trail marathon on a treadmill; or an actual trail marathon on a trail outside the lab.

They took a whole bunch of measures of “neuromuscular fatigue” before and after the marathon, then again one day, two days, and seven days later. These involved various measures of leg strength, maximal jumps, questionnaires about subjective pain, and blood tests to assess markers of muscle damage. The objective was to determine whether the fatigue and recovery profile would differ after the various types of running.

To make the three challenges roughly equivalent, they used the following rule of thumb: every 100 meters of uphill climbing is equivalent to an extra kilometer of flat running. With that in mind, the two trail marathons involved a total distance of 28K (just over 17 miles) with 1,400 meters (4,600 feet) of vertical ascent and descent, for a total “equivalent effort” of roughly 42K, which is the standard (flat) marathon distance.

They recruited a total of 46 runners, of whom 16 were primarily road runners and were assigned to the simulated road marathon. The rest were primarily trail runners, and they were divided equally between the two trail marathon groups. The fitness, age, and training characteristics of the three groups were fairly well matched. Everyone was instructed to run the marathon as fast as possible.

The overall finding was that the road marathon beat the runners up far more than either of the two trail marathons.

Here, for example, is what the loss of knee extensor (i.e. quadriceps) strength looked like in the post-marathon period, with the road group in red, the treadmill trail group in light blue, and the outdoor trail group in green:

The road marathoners lost more strength, and were still showing a deficit two days later. There’s a similar pattern, albeit less pronounced, in the subjective ratings of soreness and pain. (Calf muscles, on the other hand, didn’t show much difference between the groups.)

The blood tests also showed a major difference in muscle damage. Here are the results for one of the standard proxies of muscle damage, creatine kinase:

The simplest interpretation is that road running causes more muscle damage than trail running.

This could be because road running is more repetitive: every stride is the same, so the same muscle fibers are being activated over and over until they get damaged. Trail running has more variety, both in the “technicity” of the terrain and in the shifts between flats, uphills, and downhills. The lack of differences between the simulated and real trail groups in the new study implies that technicity doesn’t really matter (since the treadmill has a very regular surface while the outdoor trail has an irregular one), so it’s the uphills and downhills that matter.

Another possibility is that it’s not just the variability in muscle activation—it’s also that all those hills force you to slow down, and in some cases even walk, so the slower pace reduces the load on your muscles.

Both of these factors may help explain one of the big riddles in the results, which is why all the downhills in the trail runs didn’t cause more damage. After all, marathoners complain that the slight downhills in the opening miles of Boston wreak havoc on their legs. But Boston’s smooth and gradual descent lets runners lock into a fast (and damaging) pace, while the irregular ups and downs of the trail marathon lead to a slower pace and more variable stride.

There are also some possible explanations that have less to do with the inherent differences between road and trail and more to do with the study design. One is that the groups weren’t random: the road runners had trained primarily on roads, and the trail runners primarily on trails. Perhaps it’s training on trails that makes your legs more resistant to muscle damage, rather than racing on trails being less damaging. Or perhaps there are other general differences between people who choose road versus trail running. The only way to check that possibility would be to randomize people to different groups.

The more serious problem, in my view, is that the overall average completion time for the road marathon was 3:54, compared to just 3:02 in the simulated trail group and 2:59 in the outdoor trail group. This suggests that “100 meters of climbing is equivalent to an extra kilometer of flat running” is an overly generous conversion factor. It seems very likely to me that I’d be more sore after a four-hour run than after a three-hour run, regardless of what the terrain was like.

To be fair, it’s not automatically true that longer runs cause more soreness than shorter ones. Pace matters, too. Millet himself has previously found that leg muscles are less fatigued after 200 miles at the Tor des Géants than after shorter races, because you simply can’t sustain a hard-enough-to-be-damaging pace for that long. But the difference in durations between the trail and road groups raises questions about the conclusions.

One reason I’m tempted (for better or worse) to wave away those questions is that the results fit with my own intuitions and experiences. I’ve never been as sore as I was after a road marathon; I’ve often been pleasantly surprised at how quickly my legs bounce back after a long ramble in the forest or mountains. We can’t yet call it a scientifically proven fact, but call it a strong hunch.

Lawmakers Unveil Bipartisan Bill to Give NICU Parents More Time Off From Work

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

— Momentum is building for similar policies at companies and state governments

A bipartisan group of U.S. lawmakers unveiled a bill Monday that would significantly expand unpaid leave for working parents with newborns in intensive care, as momentum builds for similar policies at companies and state governments.

The bill, introduced by Rep. Brittany Pettersen (D-Colo.), aims to address a long overlooked plight facing many parents with newborns in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), who often exhaust much of their parental leave before their baby comes home from the hospital. Many mothers and fathers end up working through their newborns' NICU stays to save their time off. The proposed legislation would amend the 1993 Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) to offer up to 12 weeks of dedicated NICU leave to eligible workers, on top of 12 weeks of parental leave already available.

The campaign for NICU leave has gained momentum since Colorado and Illinois became the first states to implement such policies earlier this year. On Tuesday, Walmart, the country's largest private employer, will begin offering a maximum of 12 extra weeks of NICU leave to its full-time salaried and hourly employees. The retail giant joins several U.S. companies that have adopted NICU leave in recent years, including Morgan Stanley, Pinterest, and organic baby formula company Bobbie.

The U.S. has no federal law mandating paid family leave but FMLA entitles eligible parents -- those who have worked at least 12 months for a private employer with 50 or more employees -- to job-protected unpaid leave to care for a newborn.

While Republicans have generally opposed a federal mandate for paid leave, Pettersen said she is optimistic that the amendment to the FMLA will garner bipartisan support.

"This isn't about party, it's about life experiences," Pettersen said in an interview with the Associated Press. "Where we want to go is paid family and medical leave, and we will continue to fight for it. This is a step forward building off the current policy that has bipartisan support."

Her co-sponsors were two Republicans, Rep. Mike Lawler of New York and Rep. Ryan Mackenzie of Pennsylvania, and a Democrat, Rep. Morgan McGarvey of Kentucky. Mackenzie and McGarvey have both advocated for other policies benefiting NICU babies and shared their own stories as fathers with newborns in intensive care.

"For parents with a newborn in the NICU, the last thing they should have to worry about is whether they'll have enough time off work to be there for their child," Mackenzie said in a statement.

Lawler added in a statement that "every working parent deserves the chance to be there for their child, especially when that child is facing a serious medical challenge."

The bill, known as the "Stand By NICU Babies and Families Act," could face a long road. It must be re-introduced after the new Congress is seated in January, and Pettersen will have to search for new Republican sponsors. Lawler and Mackenzie both lost their re-election bids in the midterm elections that gave Democrats control of both chambers of Congress.

Pettersen said she introduced the bill pro-forma to put a stake in the ground as her newly empowered party readies its priorities for the next Congress.

The NICU leave bills passed in Colorado and Illinois illustrate the partisan divide over paid leave mandates. Colorado's paid leave passed mostly along party lines, while a shorter, unpaid leave adopted in Illinois had overwhelming bipartisan support.

In both states, however, the policies were championed by legislators with their own experiences as NICU parents. Nearly one out of 10 babies born in the U.S. are admitted to a NICU, according to the most recent figures from the CDC.

"This issue leave for NICU parents seems to be something that people intuitively get, including people on the right and left and people in between," said Inimai Chettiar, president of A Better Balance, a nonprofit that is advocating for state and federal NICU leave policies.

A Better Balance and other advocates say parental presence in the NICU is important because skin-to-skin contact helps with milk production and improves health outcomes, while time off is critical for women of premature babies who are at higher risk of postpartum depression. A Better Balance said most parents wouldn't need the full 12 weeks of extra leave since the majority of NICU stays are much shorter, but the longer cap will help families facing more critical health situations.
