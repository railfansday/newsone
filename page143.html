Man United must move heaven and earth to bring Cesc Fabregas to Old Trafford as their next head coach

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Matt Law | 04 Nov 2026 21:56, Last updated: 04 Nov 2026 22:03

Como put in one of the most extraordinary performances in Champions League history on Wednesday, with the Italian side recording a 9-1 win over Lens.

Anastasios Douvikas struck a hat-trick, while Nico Paz, Lucas Da Cunha, Marc Oliver Kempf, Mattia Liberali, Martin Baturina and Matteo Papaccioli were also on the scoresheet in France.

Como beat Manchester United 4-1 in their previous game in the Champions League, and Wednesday's success has left them fourth in the overall table, picking up nine points from four matches.

The Blue and Whites have netted 19 times in the league stage of the 2026-27 Champions League, only the holders Paris Saint-Germain (20) have more.

Only two teams have scored eight or more goals in a single Champions League fixture over the last 10 seasons, with Como now on an elite list with Bayern Munich - 8-2 vs. Barcelona in 2020 and then 9-2 vs. Dinamo Zagreb in 2024.

Meanwhile, Como are the first team to score nine in an away European Cup match since Feyenoord did so against Olympiakos Nicosia in September 1971.

The team's fourth-placed finish in the 2025-26 Serie A campaign saw them qualify for the Champions League, and they have already shown that competing at this level will not be an issue.

I Biancoblu have some incredible players - notably the excellent Paz - but so much praise has to go to head coach Cesc Fabregas, who is already making serious waves in the management game.

Fabregas, who will turn 40 next May, has won 46, drawn 26 and lost 25 of his 97 matches in charge of Como since being appointed permanent head coach in July 2024.

Como are fourth in the Serie A table this season on 23 points, only five points behind the leaders Lazio, and a challenge for the title cannot be ruled out.

Fabregas has a contract at Como until the summer of 2028, but Man United should make an attempt to appoint the Spaniard as their new head coach next year.

Whether that means keeping Michael Carrick and enduring some more pain is a different question entirely; there is also the option of bringing in an interim head coach for the rest of the season, should the club ultimately decide to move away from Carrick.

Fabregas' Arsenal and Chelsea connections would make a move to Man United a fascinating one, but he knows the Premier League and also knows what it takes to win it, doing so twice with Chelsea.

The Spaniard was a wonderfully gifted midfielder and has shown during his early management career that he can put together a team capable of playing incredibly exciting football.

Como were head and shoulders above the Red Devils in their recent European clash.

Man United must look to the future with their next appointment, and Fabregas has the potential to become one of the leading coaches in world football in the coming years.

Certinia brings AI into professional services workflows

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Certinia Inc. is bringing AI into the software firms use to sell projects, assign staff, deliver work and bill clients.

AI’s ability to instantaneously draw from a deep well of information and provide guidance in practically any field of expertise has changed the paradigm for professional services. For Certinia Inc., whose business facilitates 7.1 billion professional services operations per month, AI represents a significant opportunity for its clients.

“I think the only industry that’s probably been more disrupted by AI than the technology industry has probably been the services industry,” Robert Cesafsky, chief operating officer of Certinia, told theCUBE. “Whether you are a software company, a hardware company, a tax and accounting firm [or] management consulting … all of these industries are being directly targeted or impacted by AI. Whatever your strategy is, you need to be able to have the right tools. You need to be able to have control of that services business, and that’s what Certinia provides.”

This feature is part of SiliconANGLE Media’s exploration of the architectural shifts powering continuous, production-grade AI. Be sure to check out theCUBE’s coverage of the Certinia at Dreamforce event. (* Disclosure below.)

Certinia launched in 2009 as FinancialForce.com Inc., a cloud-based accounting and enterprise resource planning provider built natively on the Salesforce Inc. platform. It rebranded in 2023 as it broadened from accounting and ERP to managing services businesses.

Certinia’s Salesforce-native platform connects a wide range of processes spanning sales, project delivery, financial management and customer success. The company’s approach helps clients transform cost centers into profit centers by improving visibility into customer activities, optimizing resource deployment and maintaining connectivity throughout the customer lifecycle.

“We can take away all the administrative tasks and outsource that to AI in order to help them deliver faster time outcomes, but it also helps them deliver a lower cost to serve that project,” said Mimi Spier, chief marketing officer of Certinia, in an interview with theCUBE. “We can identify expansion opportunities, [and] at-risk customers where we need to optimize renewals. Everything we’re doing is ultimately affecting the bottom line.”

Certinia’s long-time relationship with Salesforce is a key element in the company’s overall strategy. Certinia is featured under the AI agents and tools category for Salesforce’s AgentExchange and has provided AI-powered sales and delivery for Salesforce’s consulting partner network.

“We have a deep partnership with Salesforce,” said Raju Malhotra, chief product and technology officer of Certinia. “I spend personally a lot of time with the Salesforce executives on business and engineering, and we collaborate. We bring our customer use cases, they tell us about what’s coming up from a capability perspective and we work very well together. They’re led by a founder [chief executive officer] who’s very much in tune with the market, and they want to stay ahead.”

Certinia has sought to stay ahead in its own right by introducing Veda, an enterprise-grade intelligent operations engine that functions as the company’s suite of AI specialist agents and intelligent actions. The solution delivers rules-bound, trusted and ROI-focused autonomous workflows, combining Certinia’s years of institutional memory with advanced AI reasoning. Certinia’s goal with Veda is to provide customers with enough flexibility so that agents integrated into existing business workflows can drive immediate and measurable value.

“We’ve infused Veda throughout the experience for users to be more productive,” Malhotra said. “We have package agents, which are Veda agents. We launched Veda with 10 packages. Now we have 24, and we will finish the year with more than 40 package agents. Those agents are very much persona based, and they help our customers.”

Veda is not simply a layer of generic automation; it represents a key shift in how AI is being used in the enterprise. Certinia’s focus on serving the professional services market motivated the company to ground its AI offering in operational reality, where context, coordination and control matter as much as speed, according to Scott Hebner, principal AI analyst at theCUBE Research.

“Certinia’s recent announcement of Veda is more profound than it may appear on the surface,” he said. “Veda is notable not simply because it introduces a new suite of AI agents and intelligent workflow-based actions, but because it offers a glimpse into what the future of enterprise AI is likely to look like: AI that works alongside humans in consequential workflows, helps people make better decisions and earns trust by being grounded in business context, governance and real operational outcomes.”

Since the launch of Veda in April, Certinia has taken several actions to enhance its AI capabilities. In July, the firm acquired Moonnox, an AI-native services delivery platform purpose-built for the professional services sector.

This acquisition added a contextual layer to Veda through knowledge graphs, persistent project memory and services-specific agents. The combined platform can now connect a new client request with approved scope, staffing requirements and financial impact, then recommend governed actions for professional review, according to Hebner.

“Certinia provides the deep, structured foundation of professional services, customer success and financial management; Moonnox brings the missing half: capturing the unstructured reality — the emails, chats, and calls — where delivery actually happens,” he explained. “Together, they fuse everything a services business knows with everything their teams do, moving Veda beyond orchestration toward context-aware, AI-native services delivery.”

The context graph can also draw on structured records such as timecard entries and utilization figures. Moonnox helps bring that information into the graph for client-facing teams managing projects, according to Cesafsky.

“That’s been where Moonnox has really showed up for us over the last couple of months,” he said. “It’s those capabilities to help the client-facing services [and] delivery teams run and operate their projects. [Moonnox] is effectively an intake machine for a lot of that structured data to bring that into the context graph as well.”

In September, Certinia unveiled its System of Action for services businesses. The offering significantly expanded the Veda AI suite with new agents and more than doubled the Veda Intelligent Actions library from 64 to 135.

Certinia’s System of Action uses a three-tier architecture to bring AI agents into services workflows. The first tier brings together structured records and unstructured project data. The second draws on more than 17 years of Certinia’s experience in services to inform decisions. The third lets agents act within existing workflows.

“We want to accelerate the delivery for our customers,” Malhotra said. “Our customers are solving some specific problems in the professional services world. We don’t want to just give you a project plan. If you choose to execute that project plan with some agents, we’ll give you that. If you want to really think about agentic capabilities to help you in making money, in expanding your time [and] in terms of delivery, we will help you with that.”

For professional services firms, adopting AI agents means deciding which parts of project delivery to delegate to them.

“We view professional services as a leading indicator of where enterprise AI is heading because it combines knowledge-intensive work, dynamic context, collaborative decisions, measurable economics and human accountability,” Hebner said. “If Certinia executes well, Veda could become an important blueprint for translating digital labor into economic outcomes.”

The 10 Best Whodunit Movies in Film History

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There are very few genres that reward an attentive audience quite like a traditional whodunit. From the moment a body is discovered, or a will is read, or a dinner party takes a sinister turn, the contract between filmmaker and viewer is clear: someone here is lying, everyone has a motive, and the truth is usually hiding in plain sight. Deeply rooted in the golden age of detective fiction, these movies have given the genre a life of its own, and the best ones, such as The Thin Man, Murder on the Orient Express, and Clue, offer audiencesmore than a cryptic puzzle.

The greatest whodunits are exercises in precision, balancing a cast of colorful suspects, a tight-knit setting, and a reveal that feels both shocking and inevitable. Some are faithful to the traditions of a drawing-room mystery while others bend and break the rules entirely, but the one thing that unites them all is the commitment to the thrill of answering one question: who did it? From shadowy black-and-white classics like Otto Preminger's iconic noir Laura to sleek modern reinventions such as Knives Out starring Daniel Craig, these are the ten best whodunit movies in film history!

René Clair's adaptation of the world's best-selling mystery novel, And Then There Were None, written by the Queen of Crime Agatha Christie, undoubtedly earns its place among the greatest whodunits by doing what the genre does best: trapping its characters, and its audience, in a puzzle with no way out. The film follows eight strangers who receive an invitation from an anonymous host to a home located on a remote island, where they are picked off one by one, following the grim pattern of a nursery rhyme.

And Then There Were None endures because of its flawless sense of escalating dread. Clair balances genuine suspense with sly, darkly comic touches and lets the tone shift between menace and wit without ever losing control. The ensemble cast, including Barry Fitzgerald, Walter Huston, and Louis Hayward, gives each suspect a distinct personality, making every accusation feel plausible, and every death lands with weight. The movie also pioneered the "closed-circle" setting structure, which countless other mysteries would later borrow, ultimately proving why And Then There Were None is an influential cornerstone of the traditional whodunit.

Robert Altman's Gosford Park is an Oscar-winning love letter to Jean Renoir's classic French film, The Rules of the Game, and features an outstanding ensemble cast of notable stars as well as a witty, cleverly constructed whodunit with real dramatic bite. The movie centers around several British aristocrats and their servants who travel to a countryside estate for the weekend, where their plans of relaxation and fun turn into a hunt for a killer after their gracious host is found murdered.

Altman’s roaming camera and layered soundscape make you feel like an eavesdropper, catching fragments of gossip from both social classes that matter more than they first appear, making Gosford Park one of the best upstairs-downstairs movies. The ensemble cast, including Maggie Smith, Helen Mirren, Michael Gambon, and Clive Owen, delivers uniformly superb performances, while Stephen Fry’s bumbling Inspector Thompson gently parodies the genre’s detective tradition. Part Agatha Christie homage, part social satire, Gosford Park respects the whodunit’s pleasures while expanding what the form can say, making it an intriguing puzzle with real emotional and intellectual weight.

John Guillermin’s Oscar-winning classic Death on the Nile is a glittering, sun-soaked whodunit that proves Agatha Christie’s formula thrives with the right cast and setting. Aboard a luxurious steamer cruising Egypt, the famous Belgian detective, Hercule Poirot (Peter Ustinov), must solve the shocking murder of an heiress, Linnet Doyle (Lois Chiles), who is shot dead during her honeymoon, and nearly everyone aboard had reason to want her gone, most notably the jilted Jacqueline de Bellefort (Mia Farrow), who has been stalking the newlyweds.

Ustinov brings warmth and wry humor to Christie's famous sleuth, while Anthony Shaffer’s screenplay preserves the novel’s ingenious plotting. The solution depends on precise timing, misdirection, and a deception that few viewers spot, yet it feels fair on a second viewing. The suspects are a showcase of acting royalty, including David Niven, Bette Davis, George Kennedy, and Angela Lansbury, who each deliver showstopping performances. Between its collection of familiar faces and its alluring murder mystery shrouded in scandal, Death on the Nile is a murderously clever and gorgeously mounted whodunit that remains a benchmark for the all-star mystery, and is a must-see for any fan of the genre.

The Last of Sheila is a perfect whodunit written by Anthony Perkins and Stephen Sondheim​​​​​, who famously staged elaborate scavenger hunts and murder games for their Hollywood friends, turning their real-life pastime into a razor-sharp mystery. A year after his wife was killed in a hit-and-run, a Hollywood producer, Clinton Green (James Coburn), invites six friends to spend the week doing an elaborate scavenger hunt while aboard his yacht. As each guest receives a card bearing a secret, one by one the secrets are uncovered, and the clues begin to point toward real guilt.

What truly sets The Last of Sheila apart from other classic whodunits is its intricate plotting and the script’s insider wit about Hollywood phoniness, which adds bite. Herbert Ross directs with glossy, sun-drenched menace, and the cast is a delicious collection of acid-tongued egos including Raquel Welch, James Mason, Dyan Cannon, and Ian McShane, who all engage in a bitchy, quotable banter that makes the film as funny as it is twisty. Decades before Clue and Knives Out, The Last of Sheila stood as the prime example of how a whodunit could be both a brilliant game and a savage comedy, deeming it to be a significant contribution to the genre.

Rian Johnson's hit Knives Out undeniably revitalized the whodunit for a new generation of fans and did so by honoring the rules of the genre while gleefully bending them. Daniel Craig stars as a master detective, Benoit Blanc, who investigates the recent death of a wealthy crime novelist, Harlan Thrombey (Christopher Plummer), who is found dead after celebrating his 85th birthday. As Blanc sits down with Thrombey's extensive family of eccentric relatives, each of them proves to be a potential suspect with something to hide or something to gain.

Rather than withholding the truth until the final scene, Johnson reveals the central secret early and then builds suspense from a different question: will the guilty party be caught? That inversion keeps the story fresh while still delivering the satisfying twists that fans expect, and nearly every clue pays off on rewatch. The entire cast is irresistible, notably Ana de Armas, who anchors the film with real heart as the kind-hearted nurse Marta, while Jamie Lee Curtis, Chris Evans, Toni Collette, and Michael Shannon make the Thrombey clan deliciously awful. Funny, stylish, and genuinely clever, Knives Out stands proudly beside Christie’s classics while feeling entirely modern and original.

Instead of borrowing from the pages of great writers, director and screenwriter Jonathan Lynn took the popular board game, Clue, and, with no established story, turned it into one of the most gleefully inventive whodunit mysteries ever made. Set during a stormy night in 1954, six blackmailed guests arrive at a New England mansion for a dinner party hosted by the mysterious Mr. Boddy. When their questionable host turns up dead, the butler, Wadsworth (Tim Curry), leads them on a frantic search through the house as the body count keeps climbing.

The film’s boldest stroke is its trio of endings, which parody the genre’s tradition of the grand explanation while delivering a satisfying solution. Each version recasts earlier scenes, and the screenplay’s tight plotting makes the whole madcap structure feel fair. The comedic ensemble is a dream as Curry’s wired, fast-talking Wadsworth establishes the chaos, while Madeline Kahn, Eileen Brennan, Martin Mull, Michael McKean, Christopher Lloyd, and Lesley Ann Warren all land unforgettable moments. Despite being an initial flop at the box office, Clue has since become a beloved cult classic celebrated for treating the whodunit as both a genuine puzzle and a wild, farcical romp.

Otto Preminger’s noir classic Laura is a haunting whodunit that begins as a traditional murder mystery and gradually turns into a fascinating character study of obsession, control, and deception. Dana Andrews stars as a homicide detective, Mark McPherson, investigating the recent murder of an advertising executive, Laura Hunt (Gene Tierney), who was brutally gunned down in her home. As McPherson talks to Hunt's friends and associates, he finds himself falling hopelessly in love with the beautiful victim.

Laura is widely known for its mid-way plot twist, which upends everything and reframes the second half of the mystery with an unexpected cleverness, ultimately setting it apart from other classic whodunits. The suspects in Laura are a wonderfully poisonous lot, notably Clifton Webb, whose performance as the caddy but elegant columnist and Laura’s mentor, Waldo Lydecker, earned him an Academy Award nomination for Best Supporting Actor. Joseph LaShelle's Oscar-winning cinematography immerses audiences in an almost beautiful nightmare, while David Raksin sets the tone of a dreamlike atmosphere with his sweeping score, which has since become one of the most recognizable film melodies ever written.

W. S. Van Dyke’s pre-Code comedy mystery The Thin Man is the film that ultimately proved a whodunit could be as much about the detectives as the crime itself, making it a landmark entry in the film genre. Adapted from Dashiell Hammett’s novel, the movie follows William Powell as a retired private eye, Nick Charles, who is dragged back into sleuthing when an eccentric inventor, Clyde Wynant (Edward Ellis), vanishes, and his former secretary is found murdered. While Charles would rather be drinking, his wife, Nora (Myrna Loy), is thrilled by the prospect of a real case.

The mystery itself in The Thin Man is deliciously tangled, but Nick and Nora Charles are the film's secret weapon. Powell and Loy, who starred in thirteen movies together, have effortless chemistry, trading quips over cocktails with a playful affection that was revolutionary for its time. Their banter not only makes the film endlessly rewatchable, but it also set the template for the witty, happily married sleuthing couple who have been imitated ever since. Albert Hackett and Frances Goodrich's screenplay keeps the clues moving at a breezy pace, while the finale, a dinner party where Nick gathers everyone to expose the killer, is a classic quality of the quintessential whodunit. Smart, sophisticated, and consistently funny, The Thin Man spawned a slew of hit sequels and, even decades later, remains an undisputed landmark of the genre.

Billy Wilder’s Oscar-nominated classic Witness for the Prosecution is a courtroom whodunit adapted from Agatha Christie’s play of the same name that builds up to one of the most celebrated twist endings in film history. The movie follows ailing barrister Sir Wilfrid Robarts (Charles Laughton), who takes on the defense of Leonard Vole (Tyrone Power), accused of murdering a wealthy widow who had named him in her will. The case looks grim until Vole’s German wife, Christine (Marlene Dietrich), is called to testify against him, turning a seemingly open-and-shut case into a labyrinth of a mystery.

The film’s brilliance lies in how it turns the courtroom into a stage for misdirection. Every cross-examination, every shift in testimony, feels like a clue, and Wilder’s script, co-written with Harry Kurnitz, keeps the audience second-guessing who is lying and why. The final reveal is so audacious that the film’s closing credits famously ask viewers not to spoil it. Laughton is magnificent as the cantankerous Sir Wilfrid, whose sparring with his nurse between scenes provides delicious comic relief. Power is slippery and charming as Vole, while Dietrich is mesmerizing, delivering a performance of cool, calculated ambiguity that steals the entire show.

Sidney Lumet's adaptation of Agatha Christie's famous novel, Murder on the Orient Express, is the gold standard of the classic whodunit, and ends with a twisted reveal that remains shocking and morally provocative decades later. Stranded by a snowdrift in 1935, the luxurious train becomes a sealed room where the notorious Belgian detective, Hercule Poirot, played by Albert Finney, must unravel the stabbing of an unpopular businessman, Edward Rachett (Richard Widmark), whose past hides a terrible secret.

The film’s greatest asset is its dazzling ensemble. Ingrid Bergman won an Oscar for her small but unforgettable turn, while Lauren Bacall, Sean Connery, Vanessa Redgrave, and Anthony Perkins fill every compartment with star power. Finney’s fastidious, theatrical Poirot, buried under heavy makeup and a fussy accent, is a delight and earned him an Oscar nomination, making him the only actor to receive a nomination as Christie's infamous detective. Entertaining, handsomely crafted, and endlessly rewatchable, Murder on the Orient Express is a landmark murder mystery that set the template for every star-studded whodunit that followed, essentially solidifying its place at the top of the list.

Arista’s record quarter shows the network has become central to AI

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

The artificial intelligence buildout is well underway, with much of the focus on silicon and compute hardware. The network is treated as plumbing, something you connect after the important decisions have been made. Arista Networks Inc.’s strong third-quarter results signaled greater awareness of the network’s critical role, and Arista is well-positioned to benefit from this secular trend.

Arista reported third-quarter revenue of $3.66 billion, up 58.5% year over year and 20.5% sequentially. That is the fastest growth in the company’s history as a public company and well ahead of its $3.3 billion guide. Adjusted, non-GAAP earnings per share were $1.20, up from 77 cents a year ago. Gross margin was 64%, operating margin was 49.1%, and cash totaled $14.4 billion with no debt.

Management raised full-year 2026 guidance for the fourth time this year to about $13.3 billion, representing 48% growth. Fourth-quarter revenue is expected to come in between $3.9 billion and $4 billion. Arista also gave a first look at 2027, calling for 38% to 40% growth to about $18.5 billion. Within that, AI networking is expected to double from $3.5 billion to about $7 billion, and campus is expected to grow 60% to about $2 billion. The shares rose about 12% in after-hours trading, moderating to a 5% rise today.

Though investors will always gravitate toward the numbers as proof points, the commentary by Chief Executive Jayshree Ullal (pictured) was equally important. “We believe the industry is shifting from a compute-centric vertical stack to a holistic network-centric approach,” she said.

I agree, and the reason is economics. In large clusters, the network often determines how productive the AI factory is. When packets drop or a link fails, expensive accelerators sit idle, and inference makes the problem worse. Ullal described the move from “highly centralized training compute to disaggregated and distributed inference,” with prefill, decode, storage and KV cache spread across resources. “A well-designed network is becoming a foundational cornerstone for optimal token efficiency,” she said.

Buyers are getting it. Boards and chief financial officers now measure AI by cost per token and accelerator utilization, both of which depend on the network. A 5% gain in GPU utilization across a multigigawatt build is worth far more than the switches that delivered it. Once you understand that math, the network shifts from a commodity to a performance decision.

This awareness benefits every networking vendor, but it disproportionately benefits Arista for three reasons.

The first is accelerator diversity. Three years ago, Arista talked about four or five AI customers running 50,000 to 100,000 Nvidia Corp. graphics processing units, often on InfiniBand. Today, it has more than 100 Etherlink customers and expects more than 10 gigawatts of deployment across 10 or more types of accelerators by the end of 2027.

“In 2026, we are in a super cycle of AI,” Ullal said. “What a stark inflection in just three years.” Nvidia is a formidable networking competitor, and its Spectrum-X Ethernet has won over hyperscalers such as Meta Platforms Inc. and Oracle Corp. But Nvidia’s networking is optimized around its own full stack, and as customers bring Advanced Micro Devices Inc., Microsoft Corp.’s Maia, Meta MTIA, Qualcomm Corp., and Cerebras  Inc. silicon into production, many will prefer a network supplier that isn’t also selling a competing accelerator.

Every new accelerator expands the share of AI networking open to an independent Ethernet vendor, and Arista leads that category. Co-design work with Cerebras and Maia means Arista is now involved in system design rather than added afterward.

The second is scale-across. Power limits are forcing AI capacity to be spread across buildings and regions, and connecting those sites requires deep buffers, large routing tables and very high reliability. Ullal said scale-across accounted for about 30% of AI demand this year and should rise in 2027, with the 7800 platform as the anchor. “There’s just no room for error when you’re connecting millions of XPUs,” she said. This is where Arista’s routing and EOS software experience matter most.

The third is scale-up. Arista showcased rack-scale Ethernet designs at OCP, built on the Ethernet for Scale-Up Networking or ESUN specification, with trials in 2027 and production in 2028. Ullal compared it to the InfiniBand transition: Arista expects to move from “staring at proprietary UALink or NVLink” to “of course, it’s going to be ESUN.” If that happens, Arista’s market expands inside the rack, a segment it has never served.

Security strengthens the argument. Chief Technology Officer Ken Duda said Arista is working with Anthropic PBC, Google LLC and OpenAI Group PBC to integrate AI-driven vulnerability discovery into its software pipeline, including through Project Glasswing. As AI finds flaws faster, an operating system with process isolation and hitless patching becomes an advantage.

Ullal drew the contrast sharply: “Everybody else is dealing with a game of Whac-A-Mole where they’ve got thousands of vulnerabilities and no AVA can possibly keep track of it.” President and Chief Operating Officer Todd Nightingale said this “Glasswing and Mythos effect” is a major reason campus is growing so far ahead of the market.

The stock had fallen more than 20% in the month before the report, according to Chartmill, and analysts raised several concerns.

Deferred revenue. Total deferred revenue was flat at about $6.8 billion, which some interpret as a sign of slowing demand. CFO Chantelle Breithaupt said the balance had “the largest churn in and out” yet stayed flat while the company delivered 58.5% growth, and that the year-end balance will be higher than last year. Ullal said parsing it that way turns demand into “a physics problem,” adding, “We’re just in the best position we’ve been in a long, long time in my 18-year career at Arista.”

Spending and margins. Research and development jumped to $408.5 million from $278.1 million. Duda attributed the increase to hyperscaler prototypes and liquid cooling, and Breithaupt kept the long-term R&D range at 9% to 11% of revenue. Gross margin dipped from 65.2% a year ago, but Ullal held the long-term range at 62% to 64%, including for scale-up products.

Supply and visibility. Purchase commitments rose to $11.4 billion, and inventory to $3.2 billion. Multiyear agreements now cover silicon, circuit boards and memory. Regarding visibility, Ullal said that although Arista has two quarters of order visibility, “we have two years of work behind it.”

For several years, the debate over Arista has centered on whether Ethernet could displace InfiniBand. While InfiniBand will continue to play a key role in AI buildouts, Ethernet has much broader applicability, and its growth will outpace InfiniBand.

The new question is how much of the AI system the network will define, and this quarter suggests the answer is more than most expected. As accelerators multiply, inference spreads and AI-driven threats grow, value moves toward the layer that connects and protects everything. That is Arista’s layer.

Sebastien Naji of William Blair expressed a similar view in his earnings note. “As networking becomes a more important lever for improving performance and token output in AI data centers, Arista is well positioned to capture a growing share of spending across the most demanding AI workloads,” he wrote. “Combined with continued share gains at enterprises and neoclouds, we believe Arista’s expanding addressable market and competitive positioning support further upside in the coming quarters. We continue to view Arista as a core AI infrastructure holding and reiterate our Outperform rating.”

The biggest risk to Arista isn’t deferred revenue or market demand; it’s whether it can keep executing as crisply as it has as it gets bigger and demand accelerates But execution is something the company has excelled at under Jayshree Ullal.

An Expert Guide to Nail Health, According to Professionals

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Here’s exactly how to keep them strong and healthy

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

As manicure trends evolve with new lacquers, changing shapes, and viral techniques, beauty lovers often compromise nail health for aesthetics. While many nail formulas have improved over the years to prioritize strength, uniformity, and finish, experts say incremental habits—from lifestyle shifts to resisting the urge to peel off chipped polish—can ultimately play the biggest role in keeping your nails in excellent condition.

“The future of manicures isn’t just about the latest color trends or how long your gel polish lasts, but how well we care for the nails underneath,” Krisztina van der Boom, founder of DryBy, previously told Harper’s Bazaar. Hand, foot, and nail expert Margaret Dabbs agrees, urging nail enthusiasts to “Adopt a dedicated nail care routine and stick to it.” But what does an effective routine actually look like? Along with keeping your nails clean and hydrated with an emollient moisturizer, like Caudalíe’s Vinotherapist Hand and Nail Cream, professionals suggest keeping a cuticle oil, such as Dior’s Huile Abricot, in your bag for quick touch-ups.

“Cuticle oil is the one product I would never skip,” says Peach Bling founder Sandra Wei. “Look for a formula with jojoba oil, which has small enough molecules to absorb into the nail and surrounding skin.” Wei encourages her clients to apply it daily, ideally before bed, and to treat it as a small ritual. “A rich hand cream and gloves for dishwashing and cleaning also make a real difference,” continues Wei, “since water and harsh detergents are among the most common causes of dry, weak nails.” Maintaining nail and cuticle hydration is also key for mature nails, as they tend to grow more slowly and hold less moisture as we age, which can contribute to brittleness and vertical ridges.

Professionals have debated whether it’s safe to trim your cuticles, since cuticles guard nail beds against bacteria and infection; that's why they often advise pushing them back instead of removing them entirely. “As long as you're not cutting any live skin and damaging your cuticles, gently pushing them back and buffing away excess skin will actually help improve the health and the appearance of your cuticles and will help make your manicure last as long as possible while looking salon-quality,” says Olivia Van Iderstine, vice president of education and manicure trend expert at Olive & June. Prep your fingertips with cuticle oil or a soak in warm, soapy water before a manicure, then use a disposable orangewood stick or stainless steel pusher, such as JinSoon’s HyperCare Cuticle Pusher + Reducer, to improve polish cohesion and create an elongated nail look.

Consistent, gentle filing can also keep nails strong, regardless of your shape preference, because a smooth surface guards against unwanted splits, rips, and tears that can cause long-term damage. While there’s nothing wrong with a budget-friendly emery board, professionals say investing in the right tools provides the right resistance without compromising your nails' integrity. Dabbs is a fan of crystal nail files because they can help prevent micro-splits that lead to peeling and breakage, though the best results depend on not overfiling.

And if you usually opt for gel polish manicures, they don’t have to be a deal breaker for nail health. Several options, from builder gel to Japanese gel and more, provide longevity, durability, and style without weakening natural nails.

“Japanese gel systems are known for their precision and for their focus on preserving the natural nail,” continues Wei. “The prep is minimal and gentle, so we avoid heavy buffing of the nail plate. The gel itself is applied in fine, structured layers that are flexible and sit close to the natural nail.” Peach Bling prioritizes formulas that are free of HEMA and TPO, two ingredients commonly linked to gel allergies and sensitivities. “The result is a manicure that looks refined, wears beautifully, and treats the nail underneath with care,” explains the founder. Bazaar editors love builder gel, as it works like Japanese gel, supporting natural nails with a rounded apex to reinforce their sturdiness.

Experts advise keeping a gel set on for no longer than four weeks, as regrowth changes the nail's balance, ultimately causing unwanted stress and making breaks more likely. Once gel polish begins to lift or crack, it’s often a sign that removal is necessary—and that process can often be more damaging than simply wearing a gel formula in the first place. “What protects nail integrity is how the gel is applied and, even more importantly, how it is removed,” adds Wei. “With careful, gentle removal, there is no need to take a ‘break’ between appointments.”

Five Pieces of Jewelry Calder Made for His Family Head to Bonhams With $300 K. Estimate

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Alex Calder is perhaps best known for inventing the mobile—a kinetic sculpture in which abstract shapes are gently moved by natural air currents—but the late, great American sculptor was also a talented jeweler.

In fact, Calder, known to many as “Sandy,” produced approximately 1,800 unique, handcrafted pieces of wearable art throughout his life, according to the Met, which held the first museum exhibition dedicated to his jewelry in 2008. He famously created custom jewelry for his closest family and friends—five pieces of which will go under the gavel at Bonhams this fall. Related Articles In Postwar Rural France, Alexander Calder Found Respite from Pressures of Fame and a Chance to Dream Big How Collectors Jon and Kim Shirley Stepped In to Restore the Senate Building's Famed Calder

Calder made jewelry as bold and dynamic as his sculpture, constantly experimenting with materials and forms. His avant-garde creations, often in brass, copper, and silver, became popular with 20th-century high society, artists, and celebrities, including socialite Peggy Guggenheim, painter Georgia O’Keeffe, and actress Anjelica Huston, to name but a few.

Calder’s jewelry continues to resonate with high-profile names, too: Just this past September, reality TV star-turned-model Kendall Jenner wore a pair of his hand-hammered silver earrings at the Venice Film Festival. (They originally belonged to the wife of renowned graphic designer Ivan Chermayeff, according to Louisa Guinness, the London-based gallerist who gave them to Jenner.)

The five pieces heading to auction were each made as a gift for a woman in Calder’s family: his sister, his mother, and his niece. They are entirely bespoke, made for that particular person. Because Calder refused every offer to mass-produce his jewelry, any piece that comes to market is a rare find. This quintet has remained in private hands for nearly 70 years, making it rarer still.

The auction star is a necklace Calder made for his sister Margaret Calder Hayes. “Peggy” was a close confidante, a lifelong muse, and a recipient of his early works. Though it was executed circa 1940, the silver piece showcases hand-wrought curves that feel decidedly modern. It is expected to hammer down for between $100,000 and $150,000.

Also on offer are sculptural silver earrings (estimate: $40,000–$60,000) that Calder gifted Peggy, which look very similar to the pair Jenner wore at Venice. Rounding out the five are two silver brooches spelling out the initials of Calder’s mother (Nanette) and niece (Eva), and a spiral-shaped silver ring for his niece.

Bonhams has valued the collection at nearly $300,000. It will be on view at the auction house’s new N.Y.C. flagship from November 7, before crossing the block in the Post-War & Contemporary Art sale on November 19.

Hong Kong artists create a poetic moment in Venice

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kingsley Ng’s installation Laundry Nocturne (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

For Fermata: Hong Kong in Venice, the Hong Kong-born artists Kingsley Ng and Angel Hui are training visitors’ attention on the quiet moments and subtle details connecting both cities. The exhibition, marking the 25th anniversary of Hong Kong’s first participation in the Venice Biennale, is curated by the Hong Kong Museum of Art (HKMoA) and continues until 22 November. It is housed in a historic Venetian residential building in the floating city’s Campo della Tana neighbourhood, a setting that underlines the meditative, lived-in quality to the works.

“What we hope visitors take away from the exhibition is a moment of reflection,” says Dr Maria Mok, the director of the HKMoA. “Through Kingsley and Angel’s attention to everyday details, we hope visitors feel invited to slow down and become more attentive to their surroundings, even within the hustle and intensity of urban life.”

One of Kingsley Ng’s installations, for instance, Laundry Nocturne (2026), consists of a projection and soundscape that evoke the play of light across laundry hung between buildings. It was inspired by the artist’s experience visiting Venice, where billowing laundry on clotheslines is still common, though for Ng it also evoked memories of Hong Kong.

Kingsley Ng in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“The laundry becomes a point of entry into a wider soundscape of nighttime labour: recordings from a wastewater treatment plant, a newspaper-printing facility and a postal sorting centre evoke the work that continues while much of the city sleeps,” Ng says. “These modest, often overlooked processes that keep a city alive do not only connect Venice and Hong Kong. They are like a low hum in almost every city, to be picked up by an ear that doesn’t only listen to loud grandeur.”

For her installations, Angel Hui shines a spotlight on humble features of Hong Kong’s everyday life, including goldfish in plastic bags and a neighbour’s ornate but faded window frame. In her magical rendering, glowing plastic bags embroidered with goldfish motifs are suspended overhead, while a handwrought iron window made by Hui and traditional metalsmiths rotates slowly in the gallery.

“In Hong Kong, a goldfish bag is an everyday object; in Venice, it seemed almost precious—delicate and exposed,” Hui says. “The experience reminded me that a work does not need to be grand to hold meaning. Sometimes, its poetry lies in inviting us to look more attentively, and to meet the familiar with tenderness.”

Angel Hui’s installation I Would Like to Open a Window for You (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

Both artists, through their site-specific installations, encourage visitors to mark a pause while heightening their attention to details of light, sound, colour and texture. This invitation to contemplation is echoed in the exhibition’s title, Fermata, which is a type of musical symbol instructing a performer to hold a note or a pause for an extra moment, and the term for the places where Venetian vaporettos stop to let off and pick up passengers. For Hui, this title and its dual meanings resonate across the exhibition’s disparate locales.

“The difference in pace is pronounced: Hong Kong often presses forward, while Venice seems to make room for pause,” she says. “This is why Fermata—a moment of stopping, of stillness—felt so resonant. In both cities, the ordinary endures, and in that endurance, everyday objects acquire a quiet radiance.”

The exhibition’s title and installations also complement the theme of this year’s Venice Biennale, In Minor Keys, set by the late curator Koyo Kouoh.

Angel Hui in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“I come to see the works as experiences to return to and live alongside,” Ng says. “For me, that is one meaning of In Minor Keys: making time for the ordinary and immediate—the air, light and weather around us—and becoming more attentive to our presence within them.”

The artists’ ability to turn familiar objects and moments into tableaux where time pauses and senses are heightened is key to the exhibition’s power. It allows their installations to resonate across localities, from the Venetian Lagoon to Hong Kong’s cityscape.

“The artworks merge into the Venetian neighbourhood, from laundry hanging across nearby alleys and above our courtyard exhibition space to the view of the neighbourhood outside Kingsley’s Laundry Nocturne gallery, to wrought-iron windows that echo Angel’s work,” Dr Mok says. “All these details are blurring the boundary between the exhibition and its surroundings. They also show how small moments of daily life can quietly synchronise across different cities and audiences.”
