Emily Carr and E.J. Hughes lead the way at Heffel's $10m autumn auctions

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

E.J. Hughes, Wharves at Chemainus, 2000 Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

The Heffel Fine Art Auction House held its marquee autumn evening sales on Wednesday (18 November) in Toronto, attracting a packed house of bidders and spectators to the city’s swank Yorkville district and generating C$14.4m ($10m) in sales, works by Canadian masters taking top dollar. As things got underway, a Heffel representative noted that any US tariffs were “the responsibility of the buyer”, a reflection of these often-testy times.

“At a time of strong national pride, it is especially meaningful to see collectors celebrating Canada’s artistic achievements and our distinct national culture,” David Heffel, the auction house’s president, said at the end of the evening.

Heffel's evening auctions in Toronto on 18 November Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

The back-to-back sales (41 lots of post-war and contemporary art followed by 47 works of Canadian, Impressionist and modern art) got off to a flying start when a watercolour by the Vancouver-born artist E.J. Hughes hammered for C$475,000 ($331,700)—nearly 12 times its high estimate. Titled Wharves at Chemainus (2000) and depicting the titular logging community on Vancouver Island, the painting is modest in size at 20in by 24in; with fees, the price came to C$571,250 ($398,900).

Hughes had made waves at Heffel’s auctions last spring, when his Coastal Boats Near Sidney, BC realised a whoppingC$5.7m ($4.1m), including fees. Several more Hughes works were up for sale Wednesday night including two that significantly outperformer their estimates: The Hope River at Chilliwack, BC (1961), which took in C$253,250 ($176,800) with fees; and The Freighter “World Ares” at Crofton (1980), which hauled in C$229,250 ($160,000) including fees.

Emily Carr, Alert Bay (with Welcome Figure), around 1912 Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

The second sale’s cover lot, Emily Carr’s luminous canvas Alert Bay (with Welcome Figure) (around 1912), brought C$1.3m ($922,000) including fees, comfortably within its estimate. It was the only seven-figure result of the night. Some of the proceeds from its sale will benefit the Audain Art Museum in Whistler, British Columbia, where it was shown in the context of a major traveling exhibition of Carr’s work in 2019-20.

A work on paper by the Carr, Music in the Trees (around 1935), topped its high estimate to achieve an impressive C$421,250 ($294,100) with fees. In all, six works by the Victoria-born artist were sold at Heffel on Wednesday night, fetching a combined, fee-inclusive total of C$2.5m ($1.7m).

Jean Paul Riopelle, Herbeuse, 1967 Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

A handful of works by Jean Paul Riopelle were on offer during the post-war and contemporary portion of the night, generating around C$1.3m ($907,800) in sales. His large-scale, wintry abstraction Herbeuse (1967),proved particularly appealing. Swift bidding pushed it to around double its high estimate. With fees, the price came to C$571,250 ($398,900). It was shown in the Carnegie International the year Riopelle painted it, and later in Paris, where the Montréal-born artist, a master of the palette knife, spent decades. The work with the highest estimate in the post-war and contemporary sale, Riopelle’s Brumaire (1953), was expected to bring between C$1.5m and C$2m, but failed to sell.

Among the night’s other big-ticket items was Paul-Émile Borduas’s oil painting Empâtement (1957), from the artist’s black-and-white period—and punctuated with splashes of red. It realised C$841,250 with fees ($587,400), easily topping its high estimate of C$500,000. It had been acquired directly from the artist by London’s Arthur Tooth & Sons gallery in 1957 and, more recently, was in the collection of Québecois lyricist and songwriter Luc Plamondon.

Paul-Émile Borduas, Empâtement, 1957 Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

J.E.H. MacDonald’s oil sketch Magnetawan River Rapids (1912), a preparatory work for a major canvas that is in the collection of the Art Gallery of Hamilton, achieved C$451,250 with fees ($315,000), tripling its high estimate of C$150,000.

Arguably the surprise of the night was Ivan Eyre (1935-2022), who was long a professor at the University of Manitoba and is best known for his prairie landscapes. His intensely autumnal acrylic-on-canvas landscape Golden Rod (1981) realised C$391,250 with fees ($273,200), more than tripling its high estimate of C$120,000 while setting a new auction record for the artist. That record was smashed in turn just one lot later, when Eyre’s Yellow Wood (around 1995) realised C$481,250 with fees ($336,000), five times its high estimate of C$90,000.

Ivan Eyre, Golden Rod, 1981 Courtesy Heffel Fine Art Auction House

The beloved William Kurelek, who was raised on a farm near Winnipeg, also made a splash.His snowy landscape painting I Knew How the Clouds Arise (1969), which was featured on the cover of the catalogue for the artist’s first retrospective in 1970, realised C$313,250 including fees ($218,700), just over its high estimate, while the evocative campfire scene Wiener Roast on My Father’s Farm (1974), exceeded expectations to sell for C$265,250 with fees ($185,200).

The Altruists True Story: 5 Biggest Questions After The Netflix Show’s Ending

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Altruists unpacks a chaotic partnership between Sam Bankman-Fried and Caroline Ellison, but the story doesn't end after the credits roll on episode 8. Based on the true story of the rise and fall of Sam Bankman-Fried's business ventures, his foray into crypto exchanges, and his tumultuous relationship with his colleague and on-again, off-again, polyamorous partner, Caroline Ellison.

Over the course of eight episodes, the series moves at pace through years of these characters' exploits, with the founding of FTX and Alameda Research being two core parts of the wider narrative, and ultimately a major part of the downfall of people like SBF. However, the series does leave some things unanswered, with regard to other characters, continued relationships, and outcomes for the customers whose money was lost by SBF in his high-risk approach to business.

Thankfully, due to the very public nature of these events and the individuals involved, there are updates and details about many of the burning questions that audiences may have once the limited series wraps up. With that in mind, these are some of the biggest things left unanswered in The Altruists.

Along with the reckless approach to business displayed in The Altruists, one of the most pivotal plots of the entire narrative revolves around the complex relationship between Sam Bankman-Fried and Caroline Ellison. As shown on screen, it appears that neither one was able to effectively communicate their true feelings often.

This is reflected in real life with reports about the coupling, and Bankman-Fried's own biography, Going Infinite: The Rise and Fall of a New Tycoon, providing even more context about how SBF sent Ellison a list of pros and cons for why they should continue sleeping together.

Ellison provided further accounts talking about how SBF would be extremely hot and cold, engaging in romantic relations in one moment and then keeping his distance for long periods. Now, with SBF currently serving a sentence in prison, and Ellison recently being released in January 2026, it appears that the two are not engaged in any sort of active relationship.

One of the other central characters from The Altruists was CZ, or Changpeng Zhao, a Chinese-Canadian billionaire who was painted as a criminal and money launderer in the series. In real life, CZ did end up being sentenced to 4 months in prison after pleading guilty to a handful of financial crimes, and he served out that time, before eventually receiving a presidential pardon from Donald Trump.

During his brief stint in prison, CZ was actually serving time in an adjacent facility to the one where SBF is still enrolled, Lompoc II in California. At present, it appears that CZ is not the public face or central figure in any major organizations, but he remains an active investor and majority shareholder in companies like Binance.

The Altruists spends the majority of its runtime following the impressive rise of Alameda Research, and in turn the FTX trading platform, before both came crashing down in the wake of revelations about the conduct of both SBF and Caroline Ellison and how money was being mismanaged.

The series ends with Bankman-Fried about to be captured by the FBI, and both of his businesses in free-fall. Today, Alameda Research and FTX remain defunct businesses, with both closing their doors and wrapping up operations after filing for bankruptcy in 2022. With the severe damage to public images due to SBF's misconduct and the sentencing, neither company had any legs left to stand on.

At the height of Sam Bankman-Fried's success and popularity, he was ranked as the 41st wealthiest person in the Forbes 400 list. At the time, his wealth was estimated to be somewhere north of $30 billion. But, when SBF was sentenced, part of his punishment was to forfeit 11 billion dollars towards repairing damages, and his companies were liquidated without his seeing any returns from those ventures.

That means that today, SBF's total net worth is close to zero. Interestingly, Caroline Ellison was also forced to pay $11 billion in repairs, though her prison sentence was considerably lighter due to her cooperation. Ultimately, with a few decades of time still to serve, it appears that there is a long way to go before SBF could ever dream of being a billionaire again.

Finally, the entire contention that led to SBF being imprisoned and his businesses falling apart, the $8 billion reallocation of customer funds to shore up Alameda Research, was not addressed in The Altruists' ending. While Bankman-Fried did end up finding the money thanks to a lucky break in the markets with an investment in Anthropic, the bankruptcy and the trials resulted in delays for customers recovering their money.

As of October 2024, the repayments have officially been approved, meaning that customers will receive their money back in full, in accordance with where their balance stood in November 2022, when FTX collapsed. For any customer with less than $50,000 due, they will be repaid between 118% and 119% of that value, though this has led to some frustration from those who believe they should receive more.

In some ways, that makes sense, because crypto markets have continued to thrive in the wake of the FTX's fall, but due to how funds were mismanaged, it seems somewhat miraculous that there is enough money to repay everyone who had investments tied up with the platform in the first place.

Ultimately, The Altruists did a solid job of sticking to the facts of the story, and managed to pack a lot of detail and truth into an eight-episode limited series. Outside of the more personal details of what was said in a room, or the ideas and motivations shared and expressed between individuals, The Altruists manages to deliver a compelling and concise true story that cuts to the heart of one of the biggest financial scandals in modern history.

By Prosper Junior Bakiny – Nov 19, 2026 at 6:45PM EST

Last year, Johnson & Johnson (JNJ -2.04%) acquired a smaller biotech, Halda Therapeutics, in a cash transaction valued at $3.05 billion. The key asset from that transaction was JNJ-6143, an investigational, early stage candidate for prostate cancer. It will take time to determine whether this acquisition was worth it for Johnson & Johnson, but recent clinical developments suggest that the $3.05 billion the pharmaceutical giant spent may have been well spent. Let me explain.

JNJ-6143 is part of an experimental class of cancer medicines called Regulated Induced Proximity Targeting Chimera, which use proteins found in cancer cells to disrupt certain functions those cells need to survive. This approach could lead to much stronger efficacy than existing standards of care for certain cancers. Johnson & Johnson's JNJ-6143 showed the potential of these therapies in a Phase 1 clinical trial, the results of which it presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology 2026 Congress, held in Madrid in October.

The medicine was tested in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. Notably, these patients had received a median of four prior therapies, so we aren't dealing with an easy-to-treat cancer. Yet, JNJ-6143 performed extremely well. It led to an overall response rate (the percentage of patients who respond to treatment) of 75% among patients with measurable disease. This data is highly encouraging. Of course, this was only an early stage study. There is a long road ahead before JNJ-6143 earns approval, that is, if it performs well in future clinical studies.

However, this once again highlights the strength of Johnson & Johnson's pipeline. The company has successfully expanded its lineup of approved drugs in recent years, with newer launches such as Imaavy, which treats generalized myasthenia gravis, and Icotyde, a therapy for plaque psoriasis.

These newer launches could help the company overcome headwinds, such as government-led drug price negotiations and patent cliffs, that it will face over the medium term. Over the next five years, Johnson & Johnson should make significant progress across its pipeline, including with JNJ-6143. Johnson & Johnson's innovative abilities are one of the key reasons the stock is attractive.

And it's worth noting that the company's innovative medicines division could perform even better over the long run, given that it has spun off its slower-growing consumer health division and can focus more on its higher-growth pharmaceutical business. Johnson & Johnson's medtech unit adds even more depth and diversification to the business.

Finally, Johnson & Johnson remains one of the best dividend stocks on the market. PASADENA, Calif. – NASA's Voyager mission team cheered and wiped away tears here on Thursday (Nov. 19), celebrating a big milestone — Voyager 1 just reached one light-day from Earth, something no other spacecraft has ever done. The excitement persisted despite a communication anomaly that cropped up on the probe, which the team remains confident is temporary.

Voyager team members gathered for a press conference about the mission at the Von Karman auditorium here at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) — the first such Voyager event since 1989, when Voyager 1's twin, Voyager 2, flew past Neptune. The gathering served to celebrate Voyager 1's distance milestone: At 5:16 a.m. EST (1016 GMT) on Wednesday (Nov. 18), the probe reached one light-day from Earth — the distance a photon travels in one day, which is about 16 billion miles (26 billion kilometers). That means it now takes a full day for a signal to travel from Earth to Voyager 1, the farthest human-made object out in space.

"I remember in 1977 I was standing at the Cape watching Voyager launch, and I never thought I would be here today, 49 years later, to celebrate Voyager's one light-day. What an incredible milestone," Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker said during Thursday's event.

While this day and this week have been chock-full of excitement over the distance milestone, the Voyager mission team has been hard at work, as usual, keeping the two nearly 50-year-old spacecraft alive and functioning.

Unfortunately, as JPL spokesperson Raquel Villanueva shared at this celebration, on Saturday (Nov. 14), Voyager 1 stopped transmitting science and engineering data. The team has remained in contact with Voyager 1 while investigating the issue, and they are confident that they'll be able to solve it soon.

"We listen to each spacecraft every day," Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd told Space.com at the event. Regarding Voyager 1, "we have an anomaly that is not something unseen before," Dodd said. "We do have a very strong carrier signal. We currently don't have a frame sync, so we're not getting the science or engineering data currently."

"But if you recall," Dodd added, "one light-day is very far from us. [It takes] 24 hours to get the signal to the spacecraft, 24 hours for the signal to come down, so it takes two days to diagnose anything on the spacecraft … So we can't do anything fast, and the data rates are very low."

According to Dodd, this anomaly isn't a major cause for long-term concern. Rather, it's likely just part of the natural aging process for spacecraft.

"These spacecraft are old," she said. "All of us old people have our aches and pains, and you know, sometimes it takes longer to stretch out your muscles and remember things, and so we are in a realm where we're going to see more anomalies with Voyager just due to the distance and the age."
