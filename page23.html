Brandon Ingram expected to make season debut for Clippers on Monday vs. Rockets

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Brandon Ingram is expected to make his 2026-27 season debut for the Los Angeles Clippers on Monday against the Houston Rockets, according to ESPN's Shams Charania.

Before opening training camp, the Clippers announced that Ingram had been diagnosed with a partially torn Achilles tendon in his right leg. The partial tear was discovered while the two-time All-Star underwent surgery to remove a bone spur from his right heel in May.

That heel injury caused Ingram to miss Games 6 and 7 of the Toronto Raptors' first-round playoff defeat to the Cleveland Cavaliers.

Ingram was traded by Toronto to the Clippers as part of a package including Gradey Dick, unprotected 2031 and 2033 first-round selections, a 2027 first-round swap and second-round picks in 2030 and 2033 in exchange for Kawhi Leonard.

The deal was originally agreed upon in late June and expected to become official on July 6 when the NBA's moratorium on transactions ended. However, the trade was put on hold amid the NBA's investigation into the Clippers allegedly evading the league's salary cap while signing Leonard to a three-year, $103 million free-agent contract in July 2019.

Both the Clippers and Raptors agreed that the trade wouldn't be completed until the league's probe was completed. The deal finally went through in mid-September, approximately two weeks before NBA teams opened their training camps.

The Clippers knew about Ingram's Achilles tear — repaired in the same procedure that removed his bone spur — before agreeing to the trade, according to interim president of basketball operations Trent Redden. Redden told reporters that Ingram would require about six fewer months of rehab with a partial tear and expected he would play for the Clippers this season.

Ingram averaged 21.5 points, 5.6 rebounds and 3.7 assists for the Raptors last season after being acquired from the New Orleans Pelicans in early 2025. Shortly after joining Toronto, he signed a three-year, $120 million contract extension.

During his 10 NBA seasons, Ingram has averaged 19.8 points, 5.2 rebounds and 4.2 assists while shooting 37% on 3-pointers. He was the Los Angeles Lakers' 2016 first-round pick (No. 2 overall) out of Duke. Ingram played three seasons for the Lakers before being traded to New Orleans as part of a three-team deal that sent Anthony Davis to L.A.

Without Ingram, the Clippers are off to a 4-10 start that currently has them as the third-worst team in the Western Conference. The team has also been without Bradley Beal (knee) and Jordan Miller (shoulder) during the opening weeks of the season.

Officials Are Hunting for the Lake Tahoe Tree Killer

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A person remains at large after poisoning at least 28 mature pines in three separate incidents over four years (Photo: Rachid Dahnoun/Getty Images)

Authorities in Nevada are looking for a person who has been poisoning trees on the shores of Lake Tahoe. Since 2022, at least 28 pines have been harmed or destroyed, totaling $300,000 in damages, authorities said in a release.

Now, the Washoe County Sheriff’s Office (WCSO) and other local organizations are offering a $147,000 bounty to catch the culprit.

“We take these crimes very seriously and will utilize every available resource to identify and apprehend the person or persons responsible,” Sheriff Darin Balaam said in a statement. “The destruction of mature trees in the Tahoe Basin and the environmental damage caused by these deliberate acts are unacceptable.

In an email to Outside, WCSO spokesperson Rachel Piccinini said the agency is continuing to investigate the crimes, which could result in felony charges.

“Our office has received numerous tips from the community, and we continue to encourage anyone with information that may assist in this investigation to contact our Detective Division,” Piccinini said.

Outside requested further information about whether the incidents pose a threat to people or pets recreating in the area. We will update this article if we receive a response.

The tree poisonings have occurred at Burnt Cedar Beach, a lakefront recreation complex in Incline Village, Nevada, along Lake Tahoe’s north shore. It’s also about a ten-minute drive from the community-owned Diamond Peak Ski Resort. Roughly 9,000 people live in Incline Village, but the area is a tourism hotspot in both winter and summer.

A person remains at large after poisoning at least 28 mature pines in three separate incidents over four years. Authorities say they believe the same person damaged all the trees. The suspect harmed the first six in 2022 and another six in 2024.

Most recently, on July 24, 2026, deputies responded to Burnt Cedar Beach after reports of a strong odor near the base of a large pine tree. The smell matched previous tree poisonings at the property. On August 2, deputies found another tree with a substance around the base; at the time, a trail camera captured a bright light near the area at 2 A.M.

At least 16 trees were damaged in 2026 as a result.

In some instances, someone poured the toxin directly over the tree’s base. In others, someone drilled holes near the roots and poured the substance in them.

Authorities say they have yet to identify the contaminant or determine why someone targeted the trees. They have collected soil samples and submitted them for testing. They hope to identify the substance used and determine whether the perpetrator used the same one in each incident.

WCSO asks anyone with information to call (775) 328-3001 and reference case number 26-4225.

Cult Classic Spy Series Is Getting A Second Remake In 59 Years

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The British cult classic spy series The Prisoner will soon be remade for the second time.

The Prisoner debuted in 1967 with Patrick McGoohan playing an intelligence agent known as Number Six. He gets kidnapped and taken to an unusual location called The Village, where no one is called by their real names. Always monitored and under threat, the agent tries to figure out a way to escape and refuses to accept his new normal.

It's been almost 60 years since The Prisoner ended, but now a remake is on the horizon. THR reports that Amazon Studios is currently developing a new and modern twist on the short-lived series. Susan Montford, Don Murphy, Philippe Maigret and Lucienne Papon have signed on as executive producers, but a showrunner has not been selected yet.

Maigret and Paon work at ITV, while Montford and Murphy have their own production company called Angry Films. Murphy has produced every single live-action Transformers movie, as well as the animated flick Transformers One. He also worked with Montford on Real Steel, Shoot 'Em Up, While She Was Out and Vampire Academy.

There have been attempts in the past to create a movie based on The Prisoner, with big names like Christopher Nolan, Ridley Scott and Simon West all interested at various points, but nothing ever materialized. Now Amazon Studios is taking a stab at a new TV show.

ITV only aired 17 episodes of the original Prisoner back in 1967 and 1968, but it did receive a nomination for Best Dramatic Presentation at the Hugo Awards. McGoohan's co-stars included Angelo Muscat, Peter Swanwick, Denis Shaw and Fenella Fielding.

Reviews were overwhelmingly positive. The Prisoner, considered one of the greatest sci-fi fantasy TV shows of all time, currently holds a perfect score among critics on Rotten Tomatoes and a 93% with audiences. That strong reception wasn't enough to convince the network to renew the series, though.

It took 41 years after the end of The Prisoner for any remake to finally make it to air. The six-episode 2009 miniseries had a solid cast that included Ian McKellen, Jim Caviezel, Ruth Wilson, Jamie Campbell Bower, Hayley Atwell and Lennie James. McKellen and cinematographer Florian Hoffmeister even scored Emmy nominations.

Unfortunately, reviews weren't kind to the show, which has a very low 17% critic score and 41% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes.

Now a second remake is on the way from Amazon Studios. As it's still very early in the development process, no actors have been cast, and a production schedule isn't known at this time, though executives at the studio reportedly consider The Prisoner a priority.

Why The Flying Wing Never Became A Commercial Airliner

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jack Northrop envisioned a sleek, tailless future when he sent the YB-49 aloft on October 21, 1947, stretching a 172-foot (52.4 meters) wing across the desert sky without a conventional fuselage or vertical stabilizer. Promising lower drag and greater efficiency than traditional layouts, the experimental jet bomber nevertheless battled stability challenges before the second prototype crashed on June 5, 1948, killing all five crew members aboard and leading to the program winding down between 1949 and 1950.

Despite the allure of an all-wing silhouette, commercial aviation stuck with cylindrical fuselages and tailed configurations that offered predictable handling, straightforward cabin pressurization, and familiar airport compatibility. Now that digital flight controls and blended-wing concepts are bringing tailless aerodynamics back into the spotlight, what technical, operational, and commercial hurdles still stand between a flying wing concept and a viable passenger airliner?

Converted from two piston-engined YB-35 airframes, the YB-49 stretched to a 172-foot (52.4-meter) wingspan and measured 53 feet, 1 inch (16.2 meters) in length, trading propeller drives for eight Allison J35 turbojets buried inside the wing, according to The Little Aviation Museum. Removing a conventional fuselage and tail assembly allowed almost the entire planform to contribute to lift with reduced parasite drag, helping the prototype reach roughly 493 mph (793 km/h). At the same time, early turbojet fuel burn and internal volume tradeoffs tempered its range and payload flexibility relative to piston-powered designs.

Flying without a tall vertical tail meant the YB-49 had less natural stability in yaw and had delicate pitch and roll-yaw coupling, including dutch roll, during turbulent air, maneuvers, and simulated bombing runs. As noted by MiGFlug, four small vertical fins were fitted to aid directional damping, though in the late 1940s crews relied on mechanical linkages and human coordination rather than high-speed digital flight control computers to manage continuous stability corrections.

After its maiden flight on October 21, 1947, the program flew only two converted airframes before the second prototype crashed on June 5, 1948, claiming the lives of all five crew members on board. Production orders were canceled in 1949 as Air Force leadership shifted toward competing bomber programs, and formal flight testing concluded in March 1950 before the surviving airframes were scrapped.

For an aerodynamically unstable tailless wing to become a dependable aircraft and actually work, it requires replacing purely mechanical linkages with high-speed digital fly-by-wire flight-control computers. Motion sensors can continuously measure aircraft attitude and rate changes, feeding real-time data to flight computers that command rapid, micro-adjusted deflections across trailing-edge control surfaces. That closed-loop automation damps pitch, yaw, and dutch roll oscillations far faster and more consistently than manual pilot inputs alone could achieve in earlier eras.

Digital flight control design made the Northrop B-2 Spirit possible by allowing a pure flying wing to achieve low-observable radar signatures without needing a conventional vertical tail. Instead of using a large fin that would compromise stealth, the B-2 uses computer-coordinated elevons and split drag rudders to generate controlled yawing moments and maintain crisp handling across varying weights and flight regimes. Computing redundancy and sensor voting became foundational to keeping a naturally neutral or unstable wing profile predictable from takeoff through landing.

Decades later, the Northrop B-21 Raider extends that digital control lineage into a modern low-observable bomber program built on newer avionics and computing architectures. The B-2 and B-21 demonstrate that computerized flight controls conquered the core stability and control challenges of tailless military aircraft, but solving stability in a combat airframe did not automatically translate into an airline cabin, and how to make it a reality.

Flying wing bombers nearly entered service during WWII and soon after, however, they would become reality until the B-2 in the 1990s.

The pure flying wing has not necessarily been revived, but JetZero is advancing the Z4 as a blended wing body (BWB) concept designed to carry roughly 230 to 250 passengers by smoothly merging a wide, lifting center body into outer wings. The program has gained traction through a $235 million US Air Force award supporting full-scale demonstrator development alongside plans to establish manufacturing operations in North Carolina. With a full-scale demonstrator targeted to fly in late 2027, the roadmap aims for commercial entry into service in the early 2030s if technical and regulatory hurdles are cleared on schedule.

Commercial interest in the Z4 is very real, and carriers such as Japan Airlines and United Airlines have made agreements to support the development of the aircraft. For airlines evaluating next-generation single-aisle and midsize replacements, a BWB promises lower drag and reduced fuel burn per seat compared with conventional tube-and-wing layouts. Making those conditional fleet commitments become firm deliveries, however, depends on proving that the airframe can meet strict safety, manufacturing, and operational standards at scale.

Propulsion integration sits near the center of that verification effort, including a November 2026 report from Leeham News noting that the FAA is funding a study into top-mounted engine airflow for JetZero's design. Placing engines above the aft airframe can help shield noise, but maintaining clean airflow and stable engine operation across flight conditions remains a critical engineering focus ahead of commercial certification.

A cylindrical tube naturally resists internal cabin pressure by distributing hoop stress evenly around a circular cross-section, whereas a wide, flattened wing-body hull faces far higher bending and pressure-loading demands. A non-cylindrical cabin shell tends to bow outward when pressurized at cruise altitude, so to compensate, designers need to add structural reinforcement, internal bulkheads, or load-bearing frames that add mass and complexity.

Emergency evacuation rules further add to cabin design complexity, particularly the regulatory requirement to evacuate a full aircraft within 90 seconds using half of the available exits, covered in Simple Flying analysis. Unlike a narrow single- or twin-aisle tube where passengers move linearly toward well-marked doors, a wide blended-wing cabin needs carefully engineered pathways, clear signage, and balanced exit placement, so travelers seated deep inside the layout can reach exits without bottlenecks. Cabin tiering across premium and economy zones also needs to align with aisle geometry and exit capacity rather than treating interior floor space as an open hall.

On the ground, widebody wingspans around 213 feet (65 meters) must fit within existing terminal gate envelopes, taxiway clearances, and apron stand markings without forcing costly airport rebuilds. Even when a wide airframe clears wingspan limits at a standard widebody gate, its broader center section and altered fuselage contour can complicate jetbridge docking reach, de-icing access, and ground support vehicle positioning. Smooth ramp operations really depend on proving that turnaround tasks load and unload cleanly alongside conventional airliners.

Several companies, including Airbus, JetZero, and Boeing, are actively developing prototypes.

Within a wide, open cabin, the sensory rhythm of a flight changes compared with walking down a narrow, low-ceilinged aisle. Designers can shape the atmosphere through layered ambient lighting, tactile fabrics, and warmer material palettes that soften the industrial feel of long-haul travel and create distinct zones for rest, work, or socializing. When the interior feels less like a corridor and more like a curated living space, passenger stress often eases.

Sightlines and motion perception play a major role in whether that spaciousness feels inviting or disorienting. Travelers seated further outboard from the centerline can notice more pronounced vertical movement when the aircraft banks or hits chop, making smooth cabin damping and calm visual anchors especially valuable. At the same time, passengers placed in central rows away from outer walls miss natural views of the horizon, requiring thoughtful lighting cues or digital view panels to keep the cabin feeling open and grounded.

For airlines, an unconventional cabin layout offers a rare canvas for brand differentiation in a crowded market. Carriers can build an identity around quiet acoustics, signature color schemes, and tailored service rituals that signal innovation without feeling gimmicky. If passengers connect emotionally with the warmth and poise of the space, the cabin itself becomes part of the airline's promise, not something that needs to be explained to customers.

Pure flying wings remained absent from passenger service because winning aerodynamic efficiency on paper is only half the battle when weighed against structural pressure loads, emergency evacuation rules, airport compatibility, and airline economics. Digital flight controls solved the stability hurdles that haunted the YB-49 and paved the way for stealth bombers, but turning a wide, wing-shaped cabin into a profitable, certified airliner is no easy task.

Any manufacturer brave enough to try and make something like this has to solve practical constraints that circular fuselages and conventional tails handle with far less friction. Whether a blended wing body can actually fill that divide comes down to proving real-world reliability, passenger acceptance, and unit economics without turning every airport gate or cabin layout into a costly exception.

The next major checkpoints arrive with full-scale demonstrator flight testing targeted for late 2027 and potential commercial entry into service in the early 2030s. If JetZero or other ventures like Nautilus can validate aerodynamic performance, propulsion integration, and regulatory certification along that timeline, blended-wing designs could finally move from aviation curiosity to active airline fleets.

Sam’s first review was 2020’s Battletoads reboot, and he’s since reviewed games in The Legend of Zelda, Super Mario, and Pokemon franchises.

Chances are, if you’ve played a video game, you’ve heard the voice of Roger Craig Smith. He’s the video game voice of Sonic the Hedgehog, plays Chris Redfield in the Resident Evil series, and portrays Ezio Auditore da Firenze in Assassin’s Creed.

His 20-year career has seen him appear in almost 200 video games, playing original characters, dubbing international ones, and voicing many of the medium’s greatest stories.

His latest role comes as the English dub of Phantom Blade Zero’s protagonist, Soul. Following the overnight success of the Game of the Year contender, we sat down with Craig Smith to discuss it, including how it came to be last minute, as well as, of course, some chat about Sonic.

Jia Xiongkai is the voice of Phantom Blade Zero’s Soul in its native Chinese. For the longest time, Xiongkai’s English counterpart was Jay Makensuk; however, at the last minute, Craig Smith stepped into the character’s shoes. I had to find out why.

“It was, I guess, late into the game. But that's also fairly common for what this effectively was, which was a localization,” he tells me. “The game would have essentially been done before this happened. It's funny, I guess it's maybe a little unorthodox for a video game, but not so much for a dubbing project.”

How much did he know about replacing Makensuk, though?

“The elephant in the room is the fact that the early dev examples released featured another actor. I don't know the circumstances, but obviously someone else was in the role before that,” he says. “I honestly don’t know why it was, this stuff just happens in the business. I’ve had it go against me in the same way. You’re doing something, and all of a sudden, somebody decides they want to change course.”

“I’ve done vertical slices where I’ve thought that I was going to be the final choice, and it was like ‘maybe not, maybe this was just to help obtain the financing they wanted for the game’. I’ve had things taken away from me at the last minute; I’ve had things fall into my lap, but I never celebrate until the check clears the bank and the project is released.”

Despite playing the English version of Soul, Craig Smith feels it’s equally as important, if not more, to play through the game in its native language, even if that means not hearing his voice.

“If you want to understand what the tone was intended to be and to understand and even broaden your horizons culturally, to kind of hear cadences and delivery styles and the way that protagonists are approached in different cultures, you would want to experience it in that way first,” he says. “I say that as somebody who's been lucky enough to be hired to be the English dubbing voice for tons of games.”

Phantom Blade Zero is rooted in Chinese culture and folklore; does this make voicing a character like Soul more difficult?

“It’s really like threading a needle,” he tells me. “You need to understand the source material and the performance style while trying to blend in more of an English-speaking style of dialogue. You have to try and make sure it works a little bit better for the English-speaking ear.”

“It’s a fascinating part of what we do, trying to make sure all markets are covered, and that people get to experience things in the best way possible for them”

Dubbing is a big part of what Craig Smith does. He’s the English voice of Daigo Dojima in the Yakuza series, and has appeared in Naruto, Bleach, and Aggretsuko. How different is dubbing a character from being the original voice?

“It’s very different. You’re not creating the character, it’s more of an homage,” he says. “You're trying to make sure that you're not stepping on the toes of anything. I don't know that you necessarily get to make it your own. You're trying to give reverence to an original performer and yet also do something in a style that makes sense for maybe localizing”.

I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.

Craig Smith mentioned earlier that projects often come about last minute. It reminded me of a conversation I had 18 months ago with Alix Wilton Regan, Aliona Baranova, Rasmus Hardiker, and Pepe Balderrama about Lies of P. Two of the actors preferred to get their scripts in advance to prepare their lines, while the other two preferred to dive straight into them in the booth—which did Craig Smith prefer, the ones with more time to prepare or the last-minute projects?

“Both have their pluses and minuses, I suppose. There can be an energetic element to the latter approach of just jumping in and going for it. And certain projects really lend themselves to that,” he says. “Now that I'm 20-plus years in the business, I very often ask for stuff in advance. I love to get stuff because I want to come in with some choices. I want to understand some things. It depends on the project. It depends on the character. But I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.”

With Craig Smith’s role in Phantom Blade Zero very much falling into the “dive straight into the booth” camp, was there anything he saw that made him think that the game would be as much of a runaway success that it is?

“I couldn’t believe it. It surprises me that anything gets an audience these days with every device vying for our time, so it’s amazing, but with this game, it wasn’t necessarily a surprise,” he tells me. “It’s right in line with how much work the development team put in. It’s aesthetically beautiful, the fight mechanics are incredible, and there is so much to chew on with the story. Maybe I’m a little surprised, but I’m just so thrilled, I really am.”

Perhaps Craig Smith’s most notable role is the video game version of Sonic the Hedgehog. He’s played the character for 16 years, first appearing in Sonic Colors and Sonic Free Riders back in 2010. For many, he is the Blue Blur, at least until Ben Schwartz stepped into his big red shoes for the live-action silver-screen adaptation.

Smith has always been super complimentary about Schwartz’s version of the character, but he’s never explicitly said whether he was sad that he didn’t get the role, so I had to ask.

“What do you think? I mean, what do you think? I mean, like, no, I don't feel like being a part of some of the biggest movies on Earth,” he says chuckling. “It’s like asking me if I’d come back as Ezio in Assassin’s Creed. What do you think?”

“I mean, as actors we're inherently envious and jealous and, you know, we want to do it all. We want so bad for you to like us, you know. So, of course, I would have. That's always been my biggest hurt, that I never even got a chance, that it was not even an option to get in and have a meeting or anything. It just never happened. The industry's weird, but I get it. It is what it is. But yes, of course, I would have killed for a chance to get in there. I get I might not be right for you, but having the opportunity is the first and foremost thing for me.”

He couldn’t talk about the Sonic Movie version of the character without heaping praise on Schwartz, though.

“I've stated so many times, I'm like, you couldn't ask for a more wonderful human being and a more talented, creative genius in Ben Schwartz to be in the role, doing such a great job with it and instantly understanding the fan base. There’s so much attention on this franchise again, and a big part of that is the success of the films. It’s really reinvigorated the entire brand.”

There is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role.

With my time with Craig Smith running short, I wanted to ask more about his illustrious career. He’s touched many of gaming’s biggest franchises, but there are still a lot that remain free of his voice. Is there any he’s desperate to appear in, or sad that he hasn’t?

“No, not really. And what an unbelievably fortunate position to be in to say that. I look at it like lightning has struck many times in my life. And instead just pinching myself repeatedly and going, ‘can you believe that you woke up on your 30th birthday with essentially no career?’”

“I've reached a certain phase and an age where I just go. I cannot believe this. There is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role, and my attitude always remains the same: I just want to make everybody on the other side of the glass happy.”

This Egg Wash Secret Makes the Prettiest Pie Crusts Every Time

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The secret to a beautifully golden Thanksgiving pie might be the simplest ingredient in your kitchen.

Thanksgiving pies have a way of bringing out the perfectionist in even the most relaxed bakers. Yet you might spend half an hour creating the perfect pie dough design, carefully crimping the edges or cutting little leaves from scraps of pastry, only to pull the finished pie from the oven and discover that your beautiful handiwork has browned unevenly. It’s a small disappointment in the grand scheme of things, but after all that effort, a little indignation is understandable.

An egg wash is supposed to give pastry a lovely golden color and delicate sheen, but getting it to cooperate isn’t always as simple as beating an egg and picking up a pastry brush. Fortunately, there’s an easy trick that makes a noticeable difference, and it comes courtesy of a familiar face in the baking world: Great British Baking Show judge Paul Hollywood.

Paul’s secret is simply to add a pinch of salt to the beaten egg. He lets it sit for about five minutes before brushing it onto pastry dough. In a BBC Food demonstration, Paul uses the technique to glaze scones, but it works just as well for giving pie crusts a beautiful, even finish.

Beat an egg with a small pinch of salt until they’re well combined, then set the mixture aside for a few minutes. Give it another quick stir before brushing a thin, even layer over the pastry. The amount of salt won’t affect the flavor, and there’s no reason to make any other adjustments to your favorite pie recipe.

The difference is subtle but useful. Instead of clinging stubbornly to your pastry brush or collecting in the little crevices of a crimped edge, the egg wash spreads more easily.

If you’ve ever tried brushing a plain beaten egg onto pastry, you’ve probably noticed that it can be surprisingly thick and stringy. That’s because the proteins in the egg give it structure—a nuisance if you need to spread it evenly. Adding salt loosens those proteins, making the egg wash thinner and more fluid after a few minutes. It glides over the pastry more smoothly, leaving a light, even coating rather than stringy puddles.

The egg itself is what gives pastry its golden color and glossy sheen. Salt doesn’t make the crust magically browner; it simply helps the egg wash do its job better. The result is a more consistent finish, particularly around the edges and decorative details where egg wash tends to collect.

A pinch of salt won’t turn a lopsided pie into a masterpiece, but it can help a well-made crust look its best. And after we’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time weaving strips of pastry into a lattice for a Thanksgiving pie, I think we’re entitled to a little help with the finishing touches.

Who is Johannes Zuch supposed to be in GTA 6?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Every tale begins with an unwritten page quietly awaiting the ink of destiny..."

Everyone is a target in Grand Theft Auto 6. Even Jonathan Blow, the puzzle game designer known for The Witness and Order of the Sinking Star, isn’t safe.

Like previous games in the series, GTA 6 features a handful of playable games within the game. (In an interview with Polygon, Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson said that the studio wanted to put some previous games in GTA 6, but the tech made that too challenging.) One of those games is Tenebrae Lux, an arthouse game filled with obtuse puzzles and pretentious monologues. It feels like a parody of The Witness right away, and that’s all but confirmed when the opening credits list its auteur developer as Johannes Zuch. Johannes is close to Jonathan, and Zuch sure sounds like “suck,” doesn’t it? Rockstar even lists Zuch as the sole creator in GTA 6’s final credits, despite the fact that it was made by a small team within the studio.

It’s a hysterical in-joke for those who are familiar with Blow, but it’s also a shining example of why Rockstar Games is so good at what it does. It’s a studio that fully commits to its bits, even when it means creating a whole game to do so.

You’ll first come across Tenebrae Lux early in GTA 6’s story. During a mission in Chapter 1, Cal comes over to Jason’s house to help him redecorate for Lucia’s arrival following her unexpected release from prison. After setting up the TV, Cal insists that the two take a break and play some games on the JoySystem. (Side note: If you’re playing on Xbox, there’s certainly some cognitive dissonance seeing a clear PS5 reference on screen.) He fires up Tenebrae Lux, hyping it up as a real game by a visionary developer. “He would love to work with other people, but he’s just too fucking good at everything,” Cal says of Johannes Zuch, whose name appears in every single opening credit as the game begins, including some comically ostentatious ones that are just outside the realm of believability, like “original idea” and “mise en scène.”

The game itself is a black-and-white first-person puzzle game where you play as a man (voiced by Zuch, of course) who washed up on an island. As you walk around the starting area, listening to Zuch wax poetic in a highfalutin voice, you discover some scattered board game puzzles that require you to move pieces around to wipe other pieces off the board. It’s a pretty direct hit on The Witness’ brand of puzzles, where you have to learn the rules of a format without any tutorials. And it’s also a direct hit on Jonathan Blow’s brand of ostentatious writing; Tenebrae Lux sounds so much like Order of the Sinking Star (which released one month ago) that you’ll think that Blow must have had a Rockstar mole working within his studio, Thekla Inc., during development.

Tenebrae Lux only gets more obnoxious (complimentary) from there when you return to it later by playing it in Jason and Lucia’s home. The next major puzzle throws you into a series of looping, nearly identical rooms. You can only escape through a big-brained puzzle that is so unfathomably obtuse that even a thoroughly written guide would sound like gibberish. It’s completely maddening, as Zuch won’t stop prattling on about the Sisyphean hell he’s trapped in while an old-timey sea shanty plays over and over on a crackling record player.

As the kind of person who earnestly loves this kind of game, I will be the first to admit it: They got my ass real good. Tenebrae Lux is a pitch-perfect parody of a very specific kind of elevated art game; it’s as much a light dunk on the philosophical wandering of The Talos Principle as it is The Witness. Whether it's in good taste for a company as big as Rockstar to dunk on that kind of game is up to debate, but what's impressive is that it’s not a throwaway joke that’s on-screen for 60 seconds. Rockstar sells the gag by turning it into a full game that you can play at any time.

That level of commitment to an out-there idea is everywhere in GTA 6. Quick visual gags turn into entire questlines. Your phone’s news app isn’t just full of goofy headlines, but fully written articles that are hysterical. Magazines are populated with feature articles that expertly ape the tone of your average grocery store glossy. Even your Instagram-like Snapmatic feed is filled with authentic shitposts and even more authentic replies. All of this gives Leonida feel like a real place with a very distinct sense of personality. If you don’t have the time to play through the entirety of GTA 6, you can understand the appeal of Rockstar’s all-in attitude through Tenebrae Lux alone.

And if it sounds like GTA 6 is punching down on indie games, don’t worry: Rockstar takes potshots at itself and other AAA studios in the same breath. When Cal fires up Tenebrae Lux during the mission, he drops a killer meta joke as it loads. “You can see it on the screen man, it’s so fucking raw,” Cal tells Jason in a scene that very closely resembles the famous sequence from 2016’s Uncharted 4 where Nathan Drake and Elena Fisher play a round of Crash Bandicoot together. “None of just sitting around waiting for people to finish talking. Come on, are we watching a movie or playing a game here!?”

“Cool, can we play?” a bored Jason finally says, cutting the chat short. I’m sure some players will feel the same after patching their roof and fixing their stairs in GTA 6’s early missions.
