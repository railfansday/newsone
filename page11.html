‘War’ Creator George Kay on Writing the Celebrity Divorce Drama, That Shocking Season Finale and the Real Couple Who Inspired a Key Scene: ‘It’s Probably an Awful Moment in Someone’s Life’

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

SPOILER ALERT: This article contains major spoilers for “War,” which had its season finale on Nov. 19 on HBO and HBO Max in the U.S., and drops on Nov. 20 on Sky and NOW in the U.K.

Over eight episodes of “War,” actress Carla Duval (Sienna Miller) and tech bro Morgan Henderson (Dominic West) have been squabbling over the couple’s multibillion-dollar fortune as they prepare to part ways for good. The winners, of course, have been the lawyers.

The series centers around two law firms: the modern and fast-paced Taylor & Byrne, founded by Nicholas Taylor (James McArdle) and Serena Byrne (Phoebe Fox), and the stuffy, old-school Cathcarts & Sons, led by St. John Smallwood (Pip Torrens) and Beatrice Ubosi (Nina Sosanya).

If there’s bad blood between Duval and Henderson, relations between the lawyers are worse and bloodier still. Which is perhaps why, in a season finale twist, the divorcing couple decide to negotiate their own settlement amicably, and away from their scheming lawyers.

With Season 1 wrapped up, creator and showrunner George Kay sat down with Variety to talk about some of the real-life inspirations for the show, why he decided on that surprising ending and what we can expect from Season 2, which will see Taylor & Byrne and Cathcarts return with a new case and new clients.

A little bit. Twenty years ago, I had a company that made a documentary series about divorce, and it was quite grim viewing, unsurprisingly. Especially because it was real people, and there was no charisma going on — [“War” director] Ben Taylor wasn’t directing it, basically, and it wasn’t Sienna and Dominic. So I remember having that at the back of my mind, thinking the thing we don’t want here is to make this grim or bleak or anything. And in fact, one of the phrases that we came up with in the writers’ room was that this divorce would be the world’s most expensive couples’ therapy. Even though you don’t physically stay married, you end up closer together.

You want to leave people feeling: “That was good. I liked hanging out with those people.” They were awful a lot of the time, but everyone’s redeemable. And Carla and Morgan don’t despise each other. They just drifted and drifted and drifted.

I wanted to end that story in Season 1, because I wanted him to find his home. And so that was always intended as a season-long story, and really wrapped around that is that thing of “Is it possible to be a good lawyer and a good person?” And I firmly think it is.

We’re not going to extend the espionage thing. But with Johnny, it’s a question of can you ever trust someone who’s done that? Which I think is a relatable thing, whether that’s your relationship or your workplace. If someone’s done something really untrustworthy, can you ever trust them again? So that will linger, and become a big part of Johnny’s story. But the other thing is, we wanted to find a similar character who’s not at the upper echelons of a law firm to really get behind in Season 2. That is Emma Laird, who plays Molly [a junior lawyer at Taylor & Byrne]. We cast Emma, and I remember saying to her, “You’re amazing. Just so you know, please come and do Molly in Season 1, because there’s loads more coming in Season 2.”

Every season, I think it’s worth having someone slightly more junior through which to see the whole thing through a prism of them.

We don’t see her in Season 2. We have guest stars for each season. But we do have a world. So people [in “War”] don’t use X; they use Zephyr. The whole thing keeps kind of bleeding into itself.

That was written with a writer called Toby Finlay, who’s brilliant, and we talked about that a lot in the writers’ room. If you can’t arbitrate on a divorce, you’ll go to litigation, and that’s when it will go public, because it’s not protected anymore. And what’s fun about that is when you’ve got a kind of Morgan Henderson and a Carla Duval, and they are very much in the public eye, that scene triggers the going public of the whole thing. So we were always thinking we want this whole thing to blow up in public on chat shows around “The Milliner,” which is Sienna’s film.

We wanted to put the camera behind the chat show host and the guest, because it’s only when you go on stage, you suddenly get the on-stage eye-view of what it’s like to be on a chat show. We’re so familiar with Graham Norton or Fallon or whatever, seeing it from the front. We felt it was such a private thing to see it from the other side, and then you can hear the lights kind of cracking in the heat, you see the light obfuscating the audience and the pressure of that. She’s such a talented actress, Sienna. She can do this thing of having a public smile, but with private worry in the eyes.

She was really great in terms of dealing with the script and talking to me about, “I don’t think she’d quite say it like that,” and we would sit there together on set and tweak stuff. But never did we talk about the meta thing of Sienna Miller in the public glare. She was treating it as a proper part.

“Notting Hill” plays that much more for laughs with Rhys Ifans in his pants. But actually, I felt that [showed] the press physically bearing down on you.

I really wanted to use it as a way to pressurize Sienna, like that door might physically collapse on her. It was a quicker way into just seeing that the weight of the press attention was right there, so it’s a little nod to “Notting Hill,” just in the sheer kind of [gasp] of opening the door. But it’s not used for laughs.

Who was the guy that chased that woman down the corridor? It was awful.

I think it’s more that sort of stuff happens in hotels, and there’s CCTV in hotels. You’re always looking in thrillers to find ways you could kind of revisit. So it wasn’t trying to plug into any one particular incident.

I wanted to make it a bit different because — full disclosure — I didn’t want Olivia Wilde to think we were just aping her experience. But also, I thought by distancing ourselves from it, we could make it even worse for Carla by putting it right in her biggest night, her biggest moment. This really is the culmination. She’s on a career comeback at that point. She’s been in some less good movies than “The Milliner,” if you can imagine, and so this was a really special night for her. So using that for dramatic effect felt good.

But the thing that I was really inspired by the Olivia Wilde thing was just how professional and upset she was at the same time. Like we’re talking about Sienna’s ability to [show] the pain it’s caused, and then you see how she’s painting on a smile quickly to get through it. And I really spotted that in Olivia Wilde’s way of dealing with that in that moment, which I thought that just reflected very well on Olivia Wilde. So I was happy to be inspired by that because it’s probably an awful moment in someone’s life, and you’ve got to be choose the right way to kind of be inspired.

There were lots of fun names. The script editor came up with a brilliant list of films that are sort of mid-level Euro, kind of awards worthy, like “The Red Violinist of Marseille” and things like that, or you know, “The Harpist of Madrid.” But in the end, it was just “The Milliner.” We had this story that she was undercover in occupied Europe with a hat.

The thing we look for in the divorce [case] is to analyze the relationships of all the lawyers. And with the libel case, it’s all about reputation. How our reputations are affecting us at work and what our work reputation means to us in life is a really cool thing to have exposed by a libel trial. So it’s about playing those two things off against each other, and bringing in a few new characters and a few new bits of casting to make it believable that what we see as predominantly family lawyers can also have other people in their departments who have libel experience.

Nick Mohammed really comes through in Season 2, which was again part of our pledge to him. And there’s a new hiring at Cathcarts, who starts to — not cause problems, but definitely shakes up the dynamic.

Tonally, it’s adjacent but it’s not exactly the same. And we want to have different aspects. It’s a different album.

I’m actually looking the other way. I’m looking for the thing that affects people and then looking for the legal equivalent. It’s the relationship stories that we’ve seen in movies or in our own lives that we really relate to on an emotional level, and then quickly scouring law to say, “Well, where is that felt?” So it’s less with Walliams or Russell Brand. Like, I’m interested in how the justice system will treat those people, but I’m not using that for inspiration for drama because I don’t know how it feels for anyone involved because I don’t know it. So we’re trying to start with the feeling.

“Lauren’s taste, passion and leadership have been integral to Hello Sunshine’s film and television success,” said Witherspoon, founder of Hello Sunshine. “I’m so proud of what we accomplished together and grateful for her partnership and the lasting foundation she built around telling powerful, complex female-driven stories that resonate.” Related Stories Business Reese Witherspoon's Hello Sunshine Breaks Away From Candle Media Business From 'F1' to 'Slow Horses' to 'Call My Agent!': Mediawan Executives Take Madrid Spotlight

“Building Hello Sunshine alongside Reese and our incredible team has been the privilege of a lifetime,” added Neustadter. “I’m deeply proud of all the shows and movies we’ve made, the doors we’ve opened for new voices, and the ways we have put women in the spotlight both in front of and behind the camera. I am grateful for every moment of it, and I will always be cheering for Hello Sunshine.”

Neustadter has served as president of film and television at Hello Sunshine since she joined the company in 2017. Over her tenure, she executive produced a number of TV series, including The Morning Show, Big Little Lies, Little Fires Everywhere, The Last Thing He Told Me, Truth Be Told and Surface — also spearheading adaptations of Reese’s Book Club picks Tiny Beautiful Things and Daisy Jones & The Six. Her departure comes during a period of transition for Hello Sunshine. In October, Witherspoon teamed up with Mediawan to buy the company back from Candle Media. In partnering with the growing French media company, Hello Sunshine joins a sprawling portfolio of entertainment brands that includes Chernin Entertainment and Plan B Entertainment. But the shift has also meant changes at the company. Hello Sunshine’s sale was followed by layoffs its direct-to-consumer division.

Prior to her near-decade at Hello Sunshine, Neustadter worked at old Fox — where she was senior vice president of programming at the broadcast network, overseeing such series as The Mindy Project and Brooklyn Nine-Nine. She joined the network after a tenure at 20th Century Fox, where she worked on such films as Parental Guidance and Witherspoon vehicle This Means War. While at 20th, she was included The Hollywood Reporter’s Next Gen list — the magazine’s annual assessment of the 35 fastest rising entertainment executives under the age of 35.

Trump Says His Private Pilot Used To Use Foreign Air Traffic Control Because The American System Is So Bad

Donald Trump says his private pilot used another country’s air traffic control because the American system was so bad. He wouldn’t identify the country, as though his pilot had discovered a better provider for approaches into Teterboro and didn’t want everyone else signing up. Pilots don’t get that choice. And this isn’t just an ambiguous reference to flying overseas. Trump has made this claim before and described landing in New York, Florida and Tennessee. At his Friday press conference, Trump said his “pilot would use the air traffic control from a totally different country.” This was supposedly before his first term, 11 or 12 years ago. Then he declared, “I know a lot about air traffic control.” Watch Trump describe his pilot using another country’s air traffic control — Aaron Rupar (@atrupar), November 20, 2026 Air traffic control assigns clearances and separates aircraft sharing the same airspace. The controller responsible for your flight changes as you move between sectors and facilities. Your pilot can request a different route or altitude. They can reject an unsafe instruction. They can’t decide to use a different country’s air traffic control service because they like it better. And there’s a clear division of responsibilities. Pilots fly the aircraft and request changes to clearances when needed. Controllers coordinate the traffic. Could There Be A Real Story Behind This? I’ve tried to come up with something plausible, to create the most generous interpretation of these remarks. The best I can do is that Trump is confusing navigation equipment with air traffic control. Perhaps his pilot told him about a preferred navigation system, approach procedure or a piece of equipment he liked from overseas, and that became a story about choosing another country’s controllers. There are also real circumstances where an American flight uses foreign air traffic services: Crossing the North Atlantic. A flight to Scotland could pass through airspace handled by Nav Canada’s Gander operation and Britain’s Shanwick operation. Taking a route through Canada. A pilot could request a route that avoids congested U.S. sectors and spends more time in Canadian airspace (but it doesn’t give Canadian controllers responsibility for a landing in Tennessee). Flying in U.S. airspace assigned to Canadian controllers. “American airspace means American controllers” isn’t always true. Under a 1963 agreement, the United States delegates control of certain portions of its airspace within 50 nautical miles of the Canadian border. But the government assigned that responsibility, the pilot doesn’t choose their preferred controller. None of this would reconcile his comments with what he claimed on May 8, 2025. So, if I’m landing in New York or Florida or Tennessee, they’re using a system from another country. He repeated the story on June 27, 2025, too. So this is a recurring explanation of how he thinks flying works. Google rolls out new search homepage with AI chat history and no search button

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google search is making it even easier to access AI tools on the search page.

Google’s flagship search experience is evolving into an AI experience with stunning speed. Even time-tested elements of search are getting AI makeovers, and the latest to get this treatment is the search homepage on Google.com. A redesign that’s been in testing for the last few weeks is now official, bringing easier access to AI chats and dropping the dedicated search button entirely.

Google search VP Robby Stein confirmed on X that Google is deploying the new desktop search page after a successful test. The page will now have a few more buttons under the search bar, all connected to AI features. There’s an image generation button, one for analyzing files, and one for brainstorming with Gemini. The “I’m Feeling Lucky” button remains, but the Google Search button that used to sit next to it is gone.

The search button has obviously been a part of Google’s search page forever. However, it wasn’t in a very convenient location in the modern interface. When you begin typing a query, the autocomplete suggestions cover that button. You have to click out of the text field to access the button, which is an extra step compared to simply mashing the Enter key to run your search.

The other big change brings your recent AI conversations to the forefront. If you’re logged into Google, the search homepage will have an expandable sidebar on the left with your recent AI Mode search threads. This sidebar will also be accessible on the results page. According to Stein, the sidebar made a “big difference” in how people made progress on their web searches, calling it a “simpler way to pick up where you left off.”

It’s easy to feel frustrated by changes like this if you don’t like Google’s new AI approach to search. But at the same time, the loss of functionality is minimal. Google probably has usage data that shows that particular search button was rarely used. Most people probably just hit Enter or click an autocomplete suggestion to go to the results.

This change says more about Google’s priorities and the future of search. AI features get prominent buttons, and your AI conversations now have a whole sidebar UI. Meanwhile, traditional search is increasingly de-emphasized. There may come a day when AI Mode takes over completely, forcing you to manually select the old-fashioned version of search. It does feel like Google is inching in that direction.

Mauricio Pochettino must fix a club stuck in a constant cycle of failure. The new Man United boss has the personality but the squad is a mish-mash and where he sits as a coach is a great unknown, writes IAN LADYMAN

📅 2026-11-20  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 16:51 EST, 20 November 2026 | Updated: 17:07 EST, 20 November 2026

In a soft-focus Sky TV interview that followed Mauricio Pochettino’s opening press conference on Friday, the Manchester United chief executive Omar Berrada revealed there had been a ‘buzz’ about the club since the arrival of their new manager.

Whether he was speaking metaphorically or referring to the sound of drones stealing Pochettino’s training secrets from above only he will know.

As is usually the case these days, much about the modern United remains unclear.

But what we do know after welcoming the eighth full-time manager since the days of Sir Alex Ferguson is that once again England’s biggest football club is relying on a personality lift to drag them back to their feet.

This is what happens when you get things wrong for so long, when you buy the wrong players and install executives who appear to be learning on the job. You end up hiring and firing managers, waiting for the one who finally arrives with the magic dust, the capacity to somehow create something greater than the sum of the rather wonky parts.

Listening to Pochettino here – a talented coach and a good person – was to occasionally think back to words spoken in the weeks of their arrivals on this territory by predecessors such as Ruben Amorim and Erik ten Hag.

For they too were asked about such intangibles as culture and mood and philosophy. They too were asked about more prosaic football matters such as match fitness.

It’s a conversational cycle that tells a story of recurring and systematic football failure.

Mauricio Pochettino faced reporters in his first press conference as Manchester United boss on Friday

A drone captured footage of Pochettino's first training session on Thursday

Amorim – back on a grim, foggy day exactly two years ago – was asked about Marcus Rashford and whether he had been right to spend time off in New York days before the new United manager walked through the door.

Here, Pochettino could justifiably have been asked about another forward Bryan Mbeumo spending the wee hours of Thursday morning playing the newly released Grand Theft Auto video game when he probably should have been asleep.

This is the United that Pochettino is now being asked to fix, a club where names and faces change but problems remain the same.

The Berrada interview was actually more enlightening than Pochettino’s carefully managed introduction to the wider media.

Certainly it was interesting to listen to his assertion that nine defensive players in one squad was enough. In football, as United are discovering yet again, it’s not necessarily the quantity that matters but the quality.

One point did resonate rather more deeply, though, and that was the one Berrada – once of Manchester City – made about United’s players needing a manager capable of dealing with the scrutiny of the club and who could shield some of his players from all that comes with it.

The recently-departed Michael Carrick did look rather lost on the touchline in the final tumultuous weeks of his time as manager. He didn’t look an awful lot more comfortable in front of the TV cameras either.

Bryan Mbeumo posted this picture after the midnight release of Grand Theft Auto VI

In the dressing room? Well, he lost the loyalty of his captain Bruno Fernandes towards the end and that is never a good sign.

Pochettino will do rather better at all that. The former Chelsea, PSG and Tottenham manager has a personality and a charisma that could fill an aircraft hangar. His players will arrive at Anfield for his opening game on Sunday feeling better about themselves than they have for some time.

United have hired some confidence and some authority and some experience at a time when they desperately need it, even if Berrada’s claim that they had to call the USA Federation last week to check on his release clause suggested this is an appointment made rather on the hoof.

It is, however, the impact that Pochettino can bring over time on a jumbled squad put together for six different managers over an incredible 12 years that will define him. Here, the 54-year-old undoubtedly faces challenges.

Talking this week to someone associated with Tottenham about Pochettino’s progressive seasons there between 2014 and 2019, I was reminded of the quality that the Argentinian was given to work with.

Pochettino's Spurs side harnessed the attacking power of stars including Harry Kane and Heung-min Son

The French international goalkeeper, Hugo Lloris, the Belgian central defensive partnership of Toby Alderweireld and Jan Vertonghen, England international defenders Kyle Walker, Danny Rose and Kieran Trippier and, further forward, the burgeoning attacking talents of Dele Alli, Christian Eriksen, Heung-min Son and Harry Kane.

It was a prototype Premier League football team, both in talent, age, fitness and physicality. That was why, in 2016, it almost won it.

Pochettino does not have this at Old Trafford. He doesn’t have anything like it. He has some talented players. The fact he inherits a team in the bottom three is down to Carrick’s inadequacies.

But this United squad is a mish-mash and it serves a purpose to ask how many would get in that old Spurs team we talk about.

Fernandes? Maybe. Mbeumo? Probably. That is about it and that, frankly, is where United are right now.

Pochettino has long wanted this job. It’s not a posting of convenience for him. Like others before him – men like Jose Mourinho and Louis van Gaal – he has long since coveted the cachet, the stature and the power of this remarkable football institution.

Mourinho and Van Gaal had better players than Pochettino is being handed and they both still failed, largely because they were shown to be coaches already over the top of that hill that both had worked so desperately hard to climb.

Mbeumo is one of the few United players who might have got into Pochettino's Spurs side

Where does Pochettino sit in terms of his own personal and professional growth? That is such a hard question to answer and will be utterly fundamental to how he travels in the weeks and months to come.

Sitting here fresh from the training ground and strangely seeming to question whether the glass of water in front of him was fit to drink, Pochettino declared himself to be in his coaching prime.

The proof or otherwise of that will only be revealed out on the grass. For all that he can plead valid mitigation, Pochettino was not hugely impressive in Paris or at Stamford Bridge. Before he starts to talk about dysfunctional football clubs, he may wish to take a look at his new surroundings.

Liverpool away feels like something of a free hit. Sporting in Lisbon on Wednesday less so and Brentford at home next Saturday definitely not. And so it begins. A run of 12 games in six and a half weeks that may well see Pochettino crawling into January begging for fresh legs and new players.

The new manager said here that he wishes to be across transfers and little wonder. When he looks at the mistakes made in that regard by a succession of United regimes and executives, he may indeed question what it is they put in the water in the north-west.

United fans will perhaps appreciate that one of the ‘higher ups’ at Old Trafford has finally put his head above the parapet, even if they question some of what Berrada said. That’s been overdue.

In terms of their new manager, they can be forgiven for keeping a lid on any excitement. Many of them have simply been wearied by the cycle.
