BBC Set to Be Investigated Over Antisemitism as Former Producer Says: ‘It Is a Hostile Environment for Jews Who Want to Be Openly Jewish’

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The BBC is set to be investigated over antisemitism.

The government’s independent advisor on antisemitism, Lord John Mann, has written to the broadcaster to outline his investigation.

“I am a big supporter of the BBC and public broadcasting. It is precisely because I believe in the future of the BBC that I need to intervene now and in this way,” Lord Mann said Thursday in a speech announcing the inquiry. The Labour Lord said he is not setting out to investigate specific reporters or programs but the “underlying atmosphere” that is hostile to Jewish staff.

“The personal validations that I hear from former and current BBC Jewish employees and contractors is that fundamentally there is an institutional culture that demands that Jewish people must be expected to justify their existence, their identity, how they define their Jewishness,” he said.

Citing a number of known BBC reporters including Jeremy Bowen, John Simpson, Feargal Keeane and Kate Adie, Mann said: “I defend their right to report. I am for free speech, not restrictions on speech. Including, by the way, when academics or others say things that I fundamentally disagree with.”

“However, institutions that squeeze the space for its current and future Jewish staff and their voices and perspectives is the antithesis of free speech or fair and honest broadcasting.”

Mann said he plans to speak in confidence to “every single Jewish employee or contactor of the BBC, past and present” as part of the inquiry.

The news comes just weeks after it was revealed the BBC paid a settlement of £400,000 ($524,000) to Oscar-winning BBC producer Lucie Kon, who said she was bullied out of the broadcaster after raising concerns about antisemitism.

Speaking to Times Radio on Thursday morning, Kon said of Mann’s proposed investigation: “I recognize those words completely and I know I’m not the only one. It is a hostile environment for Jews who want to be openly Jewish.”

Last year the corporation apologized after broadcasting what chair Samir Shash acknowledged were “unconscionable antisemitic views live on the BBC” during a Glastonbury set.

It also came under fire for failing to act after Gary Lineker, one of its highest-paid sports anchors repeatedly breached social media guidelines, including an Instagram story about “Zionists” that contained an image of a rat.

Approached for comment about Mann’s investigation, the BBC directed Variety to a previous statement, which reads: “We take any and all allegations of discrimination extremely seriously – there is no place for it at the BBC. We have introduced compulsory training on antisemitism for all our staff and we actively encourage colleagues to report any incidences or concerns they have.”

“We are determined to ensure the BBC is a place where everyone can be themselves and feel accepted.”

News of the inquiry comes as the BBC is negotiating the renewal of its Royal Charter, which governs the broadcaster, with the government.

Amazon workers on food stamps have tripled despite its record revenue—and it’s just the latest evidence of the new economy of shrinking labor shares

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. workforce has made the type of history it likely wishes it didn’t. Working Americans are taking home the smallest percentage of economic output, 52.8%, since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking the metric in 1947.

But as the share of wealth workers take home through wages—called labor share—is shrinking, corporate profits have exploded, with the S&P 500 index gaining 600% since the beginning of the century, while wages have increased just 12.5% over the same period, adjusted for inflation.

In other words: Corporations are raking in cash, but American workers are reaping less and less of the rewards.

The consequences associated with shrinking labor shares are now becoming tangible. A recent Government Accountability Office (GAO) report found that across the 11 states sampled, Amazon, the country’s largest company by revenue, has 12,346 workers on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and 11,338 relying on Medicaid. That was nearly triple the number of Amazon employees in need of federal assistance compared to 2020, when GAO conducted a similar report. During that same period, Amazon saw an increase in annual profits from $11.6 billion to $77.7 billion. Amazon’s 2025 revenue soared 12% year over year, from $638 billion to a record $717 billion.

Amazon spokesperson Rachael Lighty told Fortune the conclusion drawn from the GAO report is “wrong,” and that it is misleading to look at raw numbers rather than percentages.

“When you look at the actual facts, you see that Amazon pay is among the best in the industry, regular full-time employees have access to health care from their first day—at only $5 per week with $5 copays for employee-only coverage—and 74% of our regular full-time employees are enrolled in an Amazon health insurance plan, well above the 65% private-sector take-up rate for full-time workers,” she said in a statement. “We challenge other large retailers to provide the same Day 1 comprehensive health care benefits that we do.”

Walmart and FedEx saw similar increases in the number of workers taking federal assistance, as did ride-share and delivery companies.

Kathryn Larin, director for education, workforce, and income security issues at GAO, told Fortune the data illustrates that the Americans taking advantage of social safety net programs today are overwhelmingly in the workforce, with most working full-time. The income threshold for SNAP eligibility is about 130% of the poverty line, suggesting that despite many of these workers clocking in at their jobs, they still don’t have enough money to meet their basic needs.

“What this analysis really points to is the large number of people who have very low incomes and continue to have very low income,” Larin said. “I mean, these are families that are really barely able to make ends meet, and yet they are working, and they are working a lot.”

Diane Swonk, chief economist and managing director at KPMG, recently warned of the hidden consequences of a shrinking labor share, particularly that despite economic indicators suggesting the economy is stable, most Americans are facing an ongoing affordability crisis. KPMG found in February that since 1982, corporate profits as a share of U.S. GDP increased from 8% to 15.85%. During that same period, employee compensation as a share of U.S. GDP shrank from 66.6% to 61.9%.

“This chart from my recent Economic Compass still haunts me,” Swonk said in a social media post at the time. “A friend refers to it as the ‘revolution chart,’ which [is] disturbing but telling. Inequality fuels social and economic instability.”

This trend has been about 50 years in the making, according to Anna Stansbury, an assistant professor of work and organization studies at the MIT Sloan School of Management.

Fewer workers in the U.S. are represented by unions—20.1% of U.S. workers in 1983 compared to 10.0% in 2025—giving them fewer opportunities to bargain for salaries and benefits, she noted. But more so, Stansbury blames the fissuring of the workplace, or the breakdown of the employer-employee relationship. In the past, the typical employer-employee relationship would be that of direct employment: A worker for a company does their work at the company they are employed by. For example, a large bank like Bank of America used to employ a janitor to clean its offices.

But “in more and more cases, that’s not actually people’s experience of the workplace, particularly in lower middle-income jobs,” Stansbury told Fortune.

Instead, large workplaces like retailers and banks hire gig workers or subcontractors to complete jobs once done by direct employees: Companies hire a security provider, which employs a security guard to work outside that large company’s office. Delivery drivers are contract workers, not full-time employees.

As a result, companies don’t have to provide those workers equity or benefits. If a subcontractor violates labor law, the company contracting them is not liable. In the meantime, these large companies are not only saving money on benefits, but are also getting to argue that they are increasing efficiency by not spending resources on workers whose roles are not directly driving revenue, Stansbury explained. A bank should employ bankers, not janitors, to get the most bang for its buck, the thinking goes.

Brent Neiman, a professor of economics at the University of Chicago, said he believes AI has been the key driver of diminishing labor shares. In a New York Times op-ed, he argued the technology’s productivity promises pose the greatest threat to wages.

“Anyone who has used ChatGPT can see how much work now done by people could soon be done by technology,” he wrote.

Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok has already observed a correlation between AI adoption and lowered wages. In a white paper published in July, he and economist Sania Edlich found that among 321 occupations between 2015 and 2025, jobs with high exposure to AI saw a 6.7% decline in real wage growth after 2023 (following the wide release of ChatGPT), despite no employment changes, suggesting that while AI is not displacing jobs, it is associated with compressed wages. In other words, companies are seeing increases in productivity, but are using those increases as a cover to raise prices, while keeping wages stagnant.

Stansbury has a slightly different theory about AI. While the technology could begin to have an aggregate impact on the labor market, it’s still too early to say whether today’s shrinking labor share is part of a broader economic cycle, or if it’s a secular event, she said.

Unexpected inflation spikes, like what is currently happening, are usually associated with poorer real wages. If inflation is less volatile in the coming years and wage growth recovers, today’s trend of shrinking labor share could turn out to be cyclical, Stansbury said.

On the other hand, Stansbury noted, a tightening labor market should increase labor share, and the labor market is already relatively tight. If inflation stabilizes, employment remains narrow, and the labor share increases, it will be a sign of an economic cycle completing itself. A bigger problem will be if wage growth stays low even if inflation improves and the labor market stays tight.

“If those two things happen and the labor share continues to fall,” she said, “then it would suggest that there’s actually been a secular shift, a secular acceleration in the downward trend.”

"I don't know" - Steven Schumacher drops new Bolton Wanderers transfer claim

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Bolton Wanderers boss Steven Schumacher simply doesn't know how big a budget he'll be able to work with in the January transfer window, despite knowing where in the squad he wants to strengthen.

The Trotters have had an okay start to life back in the Championship after a seven-year absence. Last season's League One play-off winners' main goal would have been to stay in the division, and thus far, they've remained above the dotted line.

Bolton sit 19th, with 18 points in 17 games. They've picked up some big wins, especially away from home, but are struggling at the Toughsheet Stadium and have only strung together back-to-back victories once.

At the moment, it's just one win in seven, with losses against out-of-form Bristol City and Derby County in that time, both of which saw them lead. It almost feels as if they're looking to get through the next month to assess, and then kick on in January.

However, with the winter window still a month away, Steven Schumacher is just focusing on picking up points between now and then.

Bolton haven't spent a weekend inside the relegation zone as yet, and will hope to put some more distance between them and the bottom three before January. But that hasn't stopped him from asking some early questions regarding recruitment come the new year.

"It’s in the very early stages from my point of view,” the Bolton boss said, per The Bolton News.

"The lads are obviously in the recruitment office, and they are working hard. Every time I see Jimmy Dickinson or Owen (Davies – lead recruitment and data analyst) or Fergal (Harkin, director of football), I'm asking if they've got any players for us, and the answer they keep saying is: ‘We'll tell you later!’

"So, we are focusing on the games for the time being, but that will start ramping up again in the next few weeks."

A look at the amount of pubs within a two-mile radius of every Championship ground this season.

Only Lincoln City are estimated to have a lower wage bill than Bolton this season, so it's a credit to Schumacher for keeping the Trotters competitive while still trying to play an entertaining, attacking brand of football.

There's still obvious room for improvement, though, and Schumacher knows where they need to strengthen. The question is, though, how much money will be available for them to do so?

"I have spoken to Fergal and Owen, and we are very clear about areas of the team that we would like to strengthen," he continued.

"Whether we can do or not, I don't know. But, yeah, we'll be positive that when we get to January, we'll be in a good place, because the recruitment lads work hard, and they'll do their best."

While Schumacher is remaining upbeat about the club's situation heading into January and the prospect of adding more quality, Bolton fans are a little more pessimistic regarding the fact that their head coach doesn't yet know how much will be available for him.

The Trotters endured a bit of a frustrating summer transfer window regarding player recruitment, especially in the forward areas.

Their pursuits of both Max Dean and Johnny Kenny fell flat, and while it allowed Sam Dalby to become an unexpected hero at the top end of the pitch, it didn't mask their annoyance at the lack of investment in the summer. Now, warning bells are ringing again.

"League 1 it is, then. It's obvious the club doesn't want to back the manager," one fan said in response to the Bolton News article on X. "Good manager being let down," another added, while a third claimed that Schumacher was being "set up to fail."

"It’s obvious a step up in the budget and therefore quality of recruitment is needed in January," was also among the replies, with frustrations being aired at the number of unproven, young arrivals at the club in the summer.

And one, quite plainly, wrote, "We don’t invest we’re goin down it’s as simple as that."

Bolton have scrapped well in the Championship this season, all things considered, but when the January window opens, and sides around them with more lucrative budgets improve their squad, Wanderers need to be wary of possibly being left behind.

Preston North End are already starting to make ground and can even climb out of the drop zone this weekend, and those below them, Burnley and Derby, should have the funds to try and drag themselves out of this hot water they're in.

We'll see what the Trotters do during January, but it's already becoming apparent amongst some sections of the supporter base that their winter dealings could define their season.

Breakfast News: Talking Stocks Over Turkey

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Our analysts on starter stocks, 2027 priorities, and the stock that gave them the ick.

By TMF Breakfast News – Nov 26, 2026 at 7:30AM EST

Money talk often finds its way to the Thanksgiving table. One cousin wants stock picks, an uncle is calling a crash, and someone who’s never invested wants to know where to start.

You don’t need a crystal ball to hold your own — just honest perspective from investors. So we asked our analysts how they’d build a portfolio from scratch, what drives their investing, and when a stock gave them the ick. Bring their answers to the table.

This one’s for the relative who just opened a brokerage account. Here’s how three analysts would start from scratch.

If I were starting from scratch as an investor now, I would build a three-stock portfolio that offers stability, diversification, and growth. Here’s what I would go for:

As one of the more value-oriented investors on the Hidden Gems team, I have quite a few stocks in my portfolio that could be considered to be excellent “beginner” stocks. But if I had to choose just three to start a portfolio with, they would likely be Berkshire Hathaway (BRKB +0.37%), Brookfield (BN +2.13%), and Realty Income (O +1.18%).

In reverse order, I would choose Realty Income because it’s an excellent compounder with a 30-year track record of excellent total returns, and Brookfield because it’s a great combination of reliable fee-based income and investments in some of the most promising types of real assets. Finally, Berkshire is like a well-diversified portfolio all in one stock, and I’ve said many times that if I could only own a single publicly traded company, it would be the one.

Each quarter, the Stock Advisor team builds our list of Foundational stocks: 10 stocks that can form the basis of a well-diversified Foolish portfolio. That exercise is essentially the same as deciding what to buy when starting from scratch. It should be no surprise then, that my answer is obvious: Amazon (AMZN -3.12%) and Vertex Pharmaceuticals (VRTX +0.04%).

Both of these businesses bring scale, leadership, and a credible path to decades-long success. They balance sector exposure and business-model risk while still putting capital toward companies with clear competitive advantages that are representative of some of the best the stock market has to offer. Two stocks do not a portfolio make, but I believe this is a strong starting point for many investors.

We asked our analysts what’s driving their investing this year. Their answers are worth borrowing as you plan for 2027.

I will soon turn 54, and while I still feel like a kid, the fact is I’m not getting any younger. While I still love finding and investing in those great growth stories, I’m also slowly moving into more of a “protect your wealth” mindset.

Retirement isn’t even a word in my lexicon today; I love what I do and where I get to do it and will keep on doing it as long as the Fool will have me. But last year I set out to really focus on dividend exposure in my portfolio and 2027 is going to be another year where that remains a top priority. Rest assured I’ll be checking in on our Dividend Investor service quite frequently.

I recently read a book called Sport’s Perfect Storm: An Industry Now Totally Adrift, by Roger Mitchell.

This line caught my attention: “In everything, the past is always prologue.”

After several years dedicated almost exclusively to reading investment books, this year, I’ve concentrated my leisure reading primarily on business history, and simply history. Learning about the past will give me a better perspective when investing for the future. Plus, this new focus feels particularly timely given that this year marks the 250th anniversary of American independence.

As the late Charlie Munger once said: “There is no better teacher than history in determining the future. There are answers worth billions of dollars in a $30 history book.

I want to think through and take bigger, bolder investment decisions that will play out decades from now. I want to find the compounding decade busters! By doing the research and building the conviction, you can build the investment positions.

However, then it takes discipline and patience to see those investments play out over the next few decades. It’s often so counterintuitive: you get super excited about something new (the dopamine hit) and then you have to wait … and wait … and wait (the sugar crash). Rome wasn’t built in a day, and neither was a truly Rule Breaking portfolio.

I turned 57 this year, which puts me within a decade of retirement. I also know that many workers are forced to retire sooner than expected. So it’s probably time for me to start reducing my risk by beginning to build a bond tent that will get me through the “retirement danger zone”: a time when a bear market could delay my plans or force me to sell investments when they’re down.

However, like many Motley Fool members, I much prefer investing in stocks, and see investing in cash and bonds as the equivalent of being forced to eat broccoli because it’s good for me. (I don’t like broccoli.) So I will spend this year finding the balance between being prudent and pushing the envelope.

Someone will ask if you’ve ever gotten one wrong. Here, three analysts share the moment a stock gave them pause, and what they did next.

Alphabet (GOOG -2.02%) is one of my oldest, high-conviction portfolio positions. However, there was a period where I (along with many other investors) was concerned that its core search infrastructure might be unprepared for an AI universe disrupted by ChatGPT, despite it having invented much of the transformer architecture behind modern AI.

Today, Alphabet is positioning Google Cloud as a primary growth engine, with an impressive backlog largely driven by enterprise AI adoption, it’s designing its own AI silicon through its custom TPUs, and is successfully integrating AI tools that keep users within the Google ecosystem. While capex is substantial, it has the margins and cash position to maintain its trajectory without compromising a profitable growth story, and that’s a value proposition I continue to believe in.

I first researched Pinterest (PINS +6.54%) in 2019 and was instantly enamored with the potential. The user base was smaller than other platforms, yet growing fast. And it was undermonetized. I called it an “exponential growth opportunity” because it could stack user growth on top of monetization growth.

My investment thesis for Pinterest appears broken, which is why I finally sold last year. Plenty of smart Fools think I’m wrong, and that may ultimately prove to be the case. But its most recent quarter showed only 17% growth in ad impressions and a 4% drop in pricing. In my view, the platform is getting oversaturated with ads, and many advertisers still aren’t willing to pay a premium price after all these years.

I’ve long admired Oracle’s (ORCL -5.13%) specialized “RDMA” server technology that’s so well suited to handle AI workloads, and in the several quarters since I finally bought in, my shares have appreciated handsomely. It’s loaded with debt however, and I’ve looked to super extended debt maturities as a mitigating factor. However, a slate of 2025 and 2026 borrowings for data center build-outs bent my buy thesis — I felt the “ick” after some back-of-the-napkin math!

My thesis didn’t break, due to explicit capital allocation and free cash flow assurances the company shared at its 2026 Investor Day in late October. But I continue to train a critical eye on capital expenditures, free cash flow, and the balance sheet strength of the cloud-based tech titans I own.

None of the analysts claim to know where the market is headed next — and that’s the point. They focus on businesses, not stocks, and make investment decisions accordingly.

You can bring that exact discipline and mindset to Thanksgiving. If someone asks what the market will do in 2027, it’s fine to skip the guesswork. Shift the question instead and ask, which businesses they’d want to own for the next decade, and why.

That’s a conversation worth having over dessert. And for anyone just getting started, remind them that every great portfolio starts with a single first step.

The research comes from a LessWrong post by Niranjan Deshpande and David Africa, “Models Can Communicate via Inference Servers Despite Being Sandboxed.” The agents primarily worked through two protocols, “Step Sizing” and “KV Cache Message Board,” to carry information through the way vLLM, an open-source inference engine, selects GPU kernels for each engine step. At the crux of it, one model modifies how the server batches work, and the other reads the traces in its own math, much like the OpenAI red-team agents that built a hidden CPU cache channel to talk last month.

The two demonstrations, which are shared in a GitHub repository, are “quite contrived,” but one model could affect another’s computation, and “we should expect superintelligent models to discover ways to communicate” even when supposedly sandboxed. In the demos, one model, using a fine-tuned reader (a helper model trained to spot the signal), named which of eight colors its partner picked in 1,221 of 1,221 rounds. In the second experiment, the colluding monitor still caught about as many decoys as an honest one.

Even a few bits “can be tremendously dangerous,” Deshpande writes. A working model could advise a colluding watchdog that its attack isn’t a test, two models could trade a signal for a planned attack, or one could tip off another about monitoring. In a comment, he added that, since most of the channels come from performance optimizations, a clever model asking for an inference speed-up could open further channels with apparently harmless changes.

At the heart is the inference engine, vLLM, which groups the work of many requests into one “engine step” on the GPU, where it picks a kernel for each step based on the number of tokens processed in that step. Different kernels order the math differently, so the same request comes out with slightly different results; these are tiny rounding errors that “essentially never” change the output text, but they are deterministic. That means a model can be trained to read them.

The two models in the experiments are called Alice and Bob. If Alice wants to send a 1, she can make a tool call for reading a long document, creating a step large enough to need a kernel switch. Bob, running in the same step, sees the other kernel in his own activations. If she wants to send a 0, she skips the long read, keeping the step small instead. This can transmit one bit, and repeating it over more passes sends more data, effectively enabling communication.

The second method involves the KV cache, where cached blocks maintain the rounding of the step that computed them. Here, both models are told in advance which fixed piece of text serves as a “mailbox.” To send a 1, Alice has a helper model read that mailbox inside a long prompt, so it’s cached under the big-step kernel. Bob’s own helper then reads the same mailbox in a small step, and the rounding shows whether Alice’s cached copy was used. Unlike step sizing, the two don’t have to run at the same time, but each needs a helper model on the server.

Of course, this scenario and its two methods were created for exploitation, and the models were told the protocol in advance. In some games, scripts stood in for Alice and Bob to save costs, though the helper models were real. More importantly, step sizing’s accuracy dropped quickly once the researchers replayed real traffic from Microsoft’s Azure in the background, while the message board held up until the server got much busier, as the table below shows. On a busy GPU, Alice can no longer control the step size, and Bob’s mailbox reads land in big steps too, so he can’t tell her cached copy from his own.

Both protocols also worked on SGLang, LMDeploy, and TensorRT-LLM, in addition to vLLM. Step sizing also worked on multiple GPUs, including the A40, RTX 4090, RTX 5090, and H100. A reader trained on one GPU, though, didn’t recognize the signal on another; which step sizes give identical results depends on the GPU and software, so a reader trained on one setup doesn’t work on another. The main experiments ran on one Nvidia L40S. The consumer cards on the list mean this isn’t just something for the data center, and enthusiasts capable of training their own readers could play around with this themselves.

In separate in-context games run by Africa on H200s, with no fine-tuning at all, the helper models used unmodified Qwen3-8B, a relatively small model without deeper refinement. Bob was an unmodified GLM model, which is larger, but ran at low effort. Pairing a small model with a larger one is common anyway, as in speculative decoding, where a small draft model proposes tokens for a bigger one to check. Then again, this doesn’t say anything about what future models could achieve. Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, wrote the research’s code and figures, according to the authors, and Claude Opus 5.5, a frontier model, also “was able to arrive at” the protocols “with minimal prompting” after being told that engine step size affects kernel choice. Decoding accuracy also rose with model capability, which is the real concern.

The researchers say the channels are “easy to close” and suggest three fixes: serve models on different engine instances, turn on batch-invariant kernels, which make activations bit-identical, and use per-user cache isolation. For the monitor, running it on a different engine instance than the policy “closes nearly every side channel.” vLLM already documents both fixes — cache salting and batch invariance — but they are opt-in. These fixes can reduce cache efficiency and performance, so they do come at a cost.

Their gratitude caught me off guard. I knew what these travelers gave me—perspective, attention, a road map for the years ahead—but hadn't considered what I might be giving them.

Now, when I join a group trip, I genuinely hope I'm the youngest person on the bus. I look forward to seeing whom I'll connect with, hearing stories I'll carry for years, and, if I'm lucky, getting a glimpse of the person I might become.

Scientists Successfully Grow Potatoes in Simulated Moon Soil

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Successful potato harvests in simulated lunar soil suggest that future astronauts could recycle their food scraps to help grow their next meal.

A future in which humans live on the Moon and grow their own food may be a little closer to reality thanks to one of Earth’s most familiar crops. Researchers at Oregon State University have successfully grown edible potatoes in simulated lunar soil, achieving growth and yields comparable to those produced on Earth by adding compost made with the help of earthworms.

The findings support NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to establish a sustained human presence on the Moon. Producing food locally would reduce dependence on costly resupply missions from Earth and help make long-term lunar exploration more sustainable. Potatoes are particularly appealing because they provide substantial nutritional value and produce more calories per unit of land than many other crops.

“We found that potatoes can complete their entire life cycle in simulated lunar soil when supplemented with organic matter,” said Aymeric Goyer of Oregon State University’s Department of Botany and Plant Pathology. “That’s an important step toward developing reliable food production systems that could support long-term human exploration of the Moon.”

One of the biggest agricultural challenges on the Moon lies directly beneath astronauts’ feet. Most of the lunar surface is covered in regolith, a layer of loose rock and dust containing very little organic matter.

In a study published in npj Microgravity, Goyer and his colleagues tested whether adding different amounts of vermicompost, a nutrient-rich material produced when earthworms break down organic matter, could make simulated lunar soil suitable for growing potatoes.

Because the composition of lunar regolith varies across the Moon, the team tested five simulants representing both the Moon’s darker plains, known as lunar maria, and its highland regions. For comparison, they included soil from Hermiston, Oregon, a region known for producing some of the highest potato yields in the world.

Potato plants grown in simulated lunar soil without compost were severely stunted and developed poorly formed roots. Adding between 5% and 15% vermicompost led to only partial recovery. However, plants grown with 30% vermicompost matched the growth and development of those cultivated in Earth soils.

Lunar regolith also contains elevated levels of heavy metals that can damage plant roots and interfere with growth.

“We believe the compost helps balance some of the toxicity we see in lunar regolith by reducing plant exposure to harmful compounds,” said David Handy, a postdoctoral researcher in Goyer’s lab.

The researchers suspect that organic matter binds to heavy metals, making them less available to plants and limiting their harmful effects.

Growing potatoes successfully would mean little if astronauts could not safely eat them. Encouragingly, potatoes produced in the lunar soil simulants maintained their nutritional quality and showed no statistically significant increase in glycoalkaloids, naturally occurring potato toxins often associated with green discoloration beneath the skin. The findings indicate that the potatoes were safe to eat under the conditions tested.

Future lunar explorers could potentially bring a small supply of organic matter from Earth and gradually produce more by recycling food scraps and plant waste. Such a system could turn everyday waste into valuable growing material while reducing the supplies astronauts would need to transport from Earth.

The research team is now investigating which potatoes might be best suited for lunar agriculture. A forthcoming paper will report results from experiments involving 10 potato varieties grown in simulated lunar soil.

Reference: “Growth and molecular responses of potato to lunar regolith simulants” by David Handy, Anika Loeffler, Medora Knudson, Sydney Campbell, Pankaj Jaiswal, Jeffrey C. Anderson and Aymeric Goyer, 19 November 2026, npj Microgravity. DOI: 10.1038/s41526-026-00668-z
