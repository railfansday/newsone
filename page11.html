Liverpool are reportedly keeping a close watch on Aston Villa goalkeeper Zion Suzuki as they begin planning for life beyond Alisson Becker.

The Japan international has impressed since moving to Villa Park in the summer and is already attracting attention from several of Europe’s biggest clubs.

Liverpool’s interest is particularly significant because Alisson is in the final year of his contract, leaving the Reds with an important decision to make over one of the most influential players of their recent era.

According to TEAMtalk, Liverpool have checked on Suzuki while assessing their long-term goalkeeping options.

The 24-year-old joined Aston Villa for around £30million in the summer and has quickly impressed with his shot-stopping, distribution and ability to play as a modern sweeper-keeper. Real Madrid, Paris Saint-Germain, Arsenal, Manchester City and Tottenham are also monitoring him.

Villa, however, are under no pressure to sell. Suzuki signed a five-year contract and is considered a major part of their future, with TEAMtalk suggesting he could eventually command a world-record fee for a goalkeeper.

A CaughtOffside report also confirms Liverpool’s interest and notes that Suzuki is being viewed as a potential long-term successor to Alisson.

Liverpool would be wise to explore their options now rather than wait until Alisson actually leaves.

Manager Andoni Iraola admitted this week that he does not know whether the Brazilian will sign another contract, although discussions are taking place behind the scenes.

Alisson’s current deal expires next summer after Liverpool activated a one-year extension earlier in 2026.

Liverpool already have Giorgi Mamardashvili and highly-rated youngster Lucca Brughmans in their plans, but TEAMtalk reports that Mamardashvili is not currently viewed as Alisson’s definitive long-term replacement.

That makes Suzuki an understandable target. He is young, already Premier League-tested and possesses the distribution required in Iraola’s system.

The difficulty will be price. Villa have little reason to sell, and Liverpool could face competition from several elite clubs.

Still, if Alisson does depart in 2027, Suzuki looks like exactly the type of goalkeeper Liverpool should be considering.

Ripple's Schwartz Speaks Out About His Influence Over XRP Price

📅 2026-11-08  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

David Schwartz, former chief technology officer of Ripple and one of the original XRP Ledger architects, has responded to claims that he is partially to blame for XRP's poor performance.

In a recent interaction on the X social media platform, David Schwartz was challenged about his possible impact on the price of the Ripple-linked cryptocurrency.

"Don't run from this. You can honestly say you don't have anything to do with it? It's not unfounded anger," the user wrote.

Although David Schwartz does not entirely deny that he can have an impact on the price of XRP, he is clear that it is minimal.

"Can I honestly say that I have nothing to do with the price of XRP? I guess probably not nothing. But very little," David Schwartz responded.

The response from Schwartz comes in the wake of mounting anger within the XRP community about the token's long-running price slump.

Indeed, XRP has seen roughly a 50% decline in value over the past year, per data from CoinMarketCap. During this period, the token has seen several temporary spikes, but the overall trend has been downward.

Of course, XRP is not the only token experiencing such a dramatic drop in value.

Stellar (XLM), another prominent cryptocurrency that seeks to enable payments, has seen a very similar price trend over the same time period.

The interaction came on the back of a long post in which Schwartz lamented the rise in hostile replies he receives on X.

Schwartz, a Ripple veteran, disclosed that some users are actually following him into other discussions and then proceeding to either label him a fraud or criticize him for his work on XRP.

The Ripple veteran noted that he has now made the decision to block such users, as irrelevant crypto-related posts are disrupting his discussions with other users.

Nonetheless, he stressed that the decision affects both critics and fans. Even if someone makes a positive comment about XRP but in an unsuitable place, they might also get blocked.

He also emphasized that he has no issue with honest criticism or accusations that make sense and come with good arguments.

He is mostly annoyed with users who keep hijacking unrelated discussions to vent their frustration.

In fact, Schwartz even offered to unblock users who commit to not doing so in the future.

Schwartz stepped away from his day-to-day duties at Ripple at the end of 2025. He had worked at the firm for over a decade. However, he has not completely left Ripple.

Earlier this month, Schwartz clarified that he's still on Ripple's board of directors and holds the honorary title of CTO Emeritus.

Furthermore, he disclosed that he still meets with his old team twice a month.

Jeremie Frimpong’s Liverpool future has suddenly become uncertain, with the Netherlands international reportedly open to a move to Saudi Arabia just 18 months after arriving at Anfield.

The 25-year-old joined Liverpool from Bayer Leverkusen for around £29.5million in 2025, but injuries and competition at right-back have prevented him from becoming an undisputed starter.

That has now led to surprising speculation that his stay on Merseyside could be cut short.

According to WinWin, as reported by GOAL, intermediaries have offered Frimpong to Saudi Pro League giants Al-Hilal ahead of the January transfer window.

The report claims Frimpong would be open to discussing a move to Saudi Arabia, potentially giving him the opportunity to start again after an inconsistent spell in England.

However, there is currently no suggestion that Liverpool and Al-Hilal have entered formal negotiations.

Frimpong is receptive to a January departure, with intermediaries approaching Al-Hilal on his behalf. Liverpool could look for another right-back if he leaves, particularly with Frimpong struggling to consistently establish himself.

The biggest twist is that Al-Hilal may not actually want Frimpong.

A source close to the Saudi club told Kooora that Frimpong is not currently among their transfer priorities, with manager Simone Inzaghi instead focused on strengthening at centre-back and in midfield.

That makes this situation worth treating cautiously. Being offered to a club is very different from that club actively pursuing a transfer.

Liverpool also have little reason to force an exit. Frimpong remains contracted until 2030 and still offers elite pace and attacking quality when fit. His recent injury problems have disrupted his rhythm, but selling after only 18 months could prove premature.

There is clearly a debate to be had over whether Frimpong fits Liverpool’s long-term defensive plans.

However, unless Al-Hilal or another club make a significant offer, giving him more time to prove himself appears the more sensible approach.

Tripadvisor: 3 Key Takeaways for Long-Term Investors

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tripadvisor is betting its marketing dollars on Viator, leaving legacy SEO-dependent channels to shrink as their traffic slows.

On its third-quarter 2026 earnings call, held on Nov. 6, 2026, Tripadvisor (TRIP -5.15%) announced a strategic pivot toward its experiences marketplace while shrinking its legacy hotel business. The operational shift reallocates key engineering talent to fuel faster-growing segments. Investors need to evaluate whether cost reductions in legacy lines can protect cash flow as paid search engine optimization traffic slows.

Three key takeaways from management's call stand out.

Tripadvisor's trimming 425 positions in its legacy hotels and other (H&O) segment to pare back fixed costs. Moving 150 of those engineering and data analytics roles to its experiences division helps fund growth where traveler demand's expanding.

Selling restaurant reservation platform TheFork gives management $685 million in net available cash to strengthen its balance sheet. Repaying long-term debt reduces interest expense and gives the company financial flexibility during ongoing strategic changes.

Persistent search engine optimization (SEO) challenges across legacy channels prompted management to stop splitting product development across separate sites. By concentrating spending on the online travel agency (OTA) Viator, the company aims to improve customer acquisition cost (CAC) and customer lifetime value (LTV) metrics.

Artificial intelligence integrations present a fresh distribution avenue across major platform partners, and management isn't hiding its satisfaction with early conversion rates. Tripadvisor relies on its user-generated content, supplier relationships, and brand trust to remain relevant as travel research shifts toward conversational tools.

Strategic execution focuses on debt reduction, cost discipline, and single-flywheel marketing investments to set a solid operational baseline for 2027.

I've always been a little bit confused about the launch of Camera Control on the iPhone 16. It's one of those features that generally feels pretty redundant, especially since there are already better ways to quick launch the camera app that Apple hasn't bothered with yet. Well the good news is that the standalone Camera Control button could be on its way out, or at least it might be according to one leaker.

The leaker Instant Digital posted about this on their Weibo account, noting that the Camera Control and Action Buttons could be merged as soon as next year's iPhone 20. The new button will apparently launch the camera with a single tap, while press-and-hold will fire up whatever Action Button setting you have mapped. There's no mention of touch sensitivity, or any of the other Camera Control features that are currently available.

Instant Digital clarifies that this means the Action Button will be rolled into Camera Control, rather than the other way around. This presumably means the button will remain on the bottom right side of the phones, in the spot Camera Control currently occupies. That said, whichever way this might happen, I am all for it, because I've never liked Camera Control.

I've been using iPhones on and off for years, since before the Camera Control button was added to the iPhone 16 series two years ago. Apple marketed this button as a way to instantly access the camera with a single click and adjust camera features with your finger — rather than the touchscreen controls.

Apple didn't come up with the idea of a dedicated camera button, those have existed on smartphones for years, but mostly fell out of favor. Instead phones came up with better ways to access the camera with the existing button layout — including the well known "double tap" gesture on an Android phone's power button.

Admittedly Camera Control was meant for more. It was a way to access Visual Intelligence, until it got rolled into the camera app as "Siri Mode," and to adjust various different camera levels without having to poke around on the screen.

Though, in my experience with recent iPhones, that was never particularly appealing. On-screen controls always felt faster and simpler, especially since I could adjust things like zoom without taking my hands off the phone itself.

It's really not that difficult to do, and I've never felt the need to actually utilize the Camera Control button to the fullest extent of its capabilities. The older ways were easier and simpler to do, and didn't require much in the way of thinking first. Plus, the volume keys were (and still are) a valid way to activate the shutter for photos and video.

I've never fully understood why Apple felt the need to add that extra button to the side of the iPhone, when better alternatives were already being used. Especially after all the rumors that suggested Apple was trying to eliminate buttons altogether.

I'll admit, in the same way that I do not care for the Camera Control button, there are plenty of people who do. In fact some of them have already taken to social media sites like Reddit to discuss their own feelings about both the Action Button and Camera Control — many of them lamenting the fact that the current iterations may be lost.

I get it to an extent, but it still feels as though iPhones are getting a little too cluttered for my liking, especially on the smaller models. That's assuming you ignore all those rumors about a possible third button on the iPhone 17, which didn't come to pass because it would have been a step too far.

The features that are on offer aren't necessarily bad, but there are better ways to implement them. Combining Camera Control and the Action Button together is one good way of doing it. After all, the Action Button is triggered by a long press, while the Camera Control opens your camera with a single or double press. There's no reason why the two can't live in harmony as a single dual-purpose button.

Of course turning the Camera Control button into an Action Button will mean Siri Mode needs a new shortcut. At the moment, pressing and holding the Camera button launches the camera app in Apple's AI camera mode, just like how it launched Visual Intelligence before the release of iOS 27.

Likewise you won't be able to use the Action Button while the camera is open, since that action is already reserved for recording video — and nobody wants to get rid of that. I'm guessing that the emphasis on the claim that the Action Button will become part of Camera Control means the latter's touch-sensitive controls will stick around for people that actually use them.

Sure, it will take some getting used to, regardless of how old your current iPhone might be, but I think that it will end up being the more efficient option in the long run. I just hope they pick a more sensible spot, because the bottom right side of an iPhone doesn't really scream "Action" to me.

The one thing that has to be made very clear is that this is an unverified rumor. Instant Digital doesn't have a bad reputation, but that doesn't mean they have a 100% track record, and we're still pretty early in the iPhone 20 development cycle. So, in other words, there's no guarantee that Apple will actually make this change with the iPhone 20.

Especially when you remember all the other Camera Control rumors that never came true, and the fact the iPhone 20 is also rumored to swap out buttons for haptic sensors. If every phone rumor we heard turned out to be true, these devices would be a complete mess.

Still, I would quite like to see this happen. The Action Button is useful, and I don't want to see that go away, but there are plenty of people who have grown to like the Camera Control button too — even if I don't really care for it myself. If you're going to keep both, you might as well merge them into a single, easy to use button.

At the very least it's going to look and feel neater than plastering the iPhone with more buttons than a keyboard.

ANALYSIS | How Miro Muheim and Albert Grønbæk were catalysts for Hamburg’s 3-1 win over Borussia Mönchengladbach

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Hamburg finally won again. On a cold Friday night at the Volksparkstadion, they beat Borussia Mönchengladbach 3–1. These three vital points, coming just before the international break, put them on 12 points, three points adrift of Werder Bremen, who occupy a European spot.

HSV also put in an impressive performance throughout the game. Some might argue that the result was influenced by the fact that Gladbach played with 10 men from the 49th minute onwards.

This did indeed impact the game. However, some important moments in the game may have been evident from the outset.

Kou Itakura’s red card actually seems predictable from the very beginning. The reason is simple. He repeatedly struggled when Albert Grønbæk tried to pass him on the right. Whether it was his own decision or that of the coaching staff, Itakura’s tendency to play as a close defender was only causing him trouble.

The problem is that Itakura is not a fast defender, yet he was playing on the side where HSV were targeting their attack. Unlike Philipp Sander, who played in the centre and used his close proximity and constant pressing to Patson Daka to good effect, Itakura is playing in the right space where his decision to press is not necessarily important.

This was especially the case when they couldn’t find a way to build up play with short passes, as HSV tended to send long passes directly forward, with Daka usually becoming the target. Constant pressing became the right approach.

With such an aggressive approach, it was only a matter of time before Itakura received his second yellow card after getting his first in the first half. He was sent off after just 49 minutes.

Grønbæk later finished the game with a spectacular goal from outside the box, showing not only his shooting ability, but also his ability to contribute to many phases of the game.

Scientists figured out how to use engineered bacteria to dissolve rocks faster and capture carbon dioxide

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Engineered ocean bacteria kept rust off olivine sand and sped up rock weathering, a slow process that pulls carbon dioxide into seawater.

As rock slowly wears away, it releases compounds that trap carbon dioxide and take it out of the atmosphere. The trouble is speed. Left alone, the process takes thousands of years, partly because iron in the rock turns to rust and coats the surface.

Now, a team led by Harvard researchers has engineered a common ocean bacterium to keep that rust from building up. In tanks of real harbor water, the microbes made crushed rock dissolve about three times faster than plain seawater did.

That’s a big jump for a process that has looked too slow to matter. So far the researchers have tested it only in lab reactors and a few glass tanks, not in the open ocean.

Neil Dalvie, a synthetic biology fellow in systems biology at Harvard Medical School, led the study with colleagues at the Wyss Institute and the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability.

Dalvie told Earth.com he wanted to highlight the interdisciplinary nature of the work.

“We learned about alkalinity and mineral measurements from geochemists in the rock weathering industry,” he said. “Very few people have both the skillsets for molecular biology, microbial engineering, and mineral processing.”

“Some people describe rock weathering as a silver bullet for climate change,” said Dalvie. “The problem is that it’s too slow.”

The team worked with olivine, a common mineral, in the form of sand. When olivine dissolves in seawater, it gives off magnesium, silicon and iron.

The magnesium is the useful part. It makes seawater more alkaline, and alkaline seawater takes up carbon dioxide from the air. This is held as bicarbonate, a form of carbon dissolved in water.

Because magnesium stays in the ocean for a very long time, the authors count that carbon as stored for good.

Iron is the problem. It doesn’t stay dissolved. It forms a layer of rust on the rock, and that layer slows any further dissolving.

The rust is one reason that natural rock weathering takes so long. Spreading crushed rock on farm fields or coastlines helps, but the authors note that the carbon captured that way has been hard to measure.

Many microbes already have a way to deal with rust. When they run low on iron, they release molecules that grab iron and make it dissolve in water. Biologists call them siderophores.

Once a microbe has enough iron, though, it stops making these molecules. And a microbe doesn’t need much.

The team tested this with Alteromonas macleodii, a marine bacterium that makes its own siderophore. The researchers ran 16 small lab reactors with different amounts of olivine sand and different flow rates.

The bacteria made large amounts of the molecule only when there was little rock and the water moved fast.

With more rock, the bacteria had iron to spare and quit. The authors concluded that the bacterium’s natural settings rule out steady siderophore production in a tank big enough for industrial use.

So the team rewired the bacterium. They replaced the genetic switch that turns siderophore production on only when iron runs short with one that keeps it on all the time.

Dalvie said the change itself was fairly easy. Showing that it worked was the hard part.

“The bacteria keep growing, the cells keep making siderophores, the rock keeps dissolving – it’s very hard to measure all these things at the same time,” Dalvie said.

In the small reactors, the engineered strain produced more than 100 times as much of the siderophore as the natural strain. Olivine dissolved about 2.6 times faster with the engineered cells than in reactors with no cells at all, while the natural strain made no clear difference.

Then came a bigger test. The researchers placed more than 8.8 pounds (4 kilograms) of construction-grade olivine sand in the bottom of 3-gallon (11-liter) glass fish tanks.

Unfiltered seawater from Boston Harbor flowed through each tank at about 1.6 quarts (1.5 liters) a day. Some tanks got a slow drip of nutrients, and two of those also got a fresh dose of the engineered bacteria once a week.

In those two tanks, magnesium came off the rock 3.1 times faster. The water also became more alkaline, which the team calculated as about 0.018 ounce (0.50 grams) of carbon dioxide removed from the air each day.

That’s about half the weight of a paper clip. The amount is small, but it was measured directly in the water rather than estimated from a model.

In earlier small reactors fed raw seawater, the engineered strain held steady for about a week before its numbers fell. That’s why the pilot tanks got a fresh dose weekly.

The strain’s staying power surprised the team. “We were actually surprised that our originally engineered strains were able to survive and perform for over a week in unfiltered seawater,” Dalvie said.

“We think that biofilms may help protect the bacteria from growth competition and viruses in the seawater.”

Bacteria have to eat, and making their food releases carbon dioxide too. So the team ran a life-cycle analysis, an accounting of the emissions from building and running a full-size version.

Sugar failed on both counts. Making it emits too much, and in the lab, feeding it to the bacteria left the water less alkaline, likely because of acidic byproducts. The researchers switched to acetate, a simple food that some companies now make from carbon dioxide itself.

Ordinary bacteria failed the accounting too. Feeding wild microbes enough to keep them hungry for iron would release far more carbon dioxide than the faster weathering captured.

With engineered bacteria and a carbon-neutral acetate feed, the model predicted 74 percent more net carbon removal than a tank of rock and seawater alone. The researchers assumed the 2.6-fold speedup from the lab would hold at that scale.

“We’ve found a way that it could work and, maybe more importantly, now we have a model to calculate what it would look like to implement this at scale,” he said.

Four of the authors, Dalvie among them, have filed a patent application on strains of bacteria engineered for siderophore production that keep rust off rock.

The pilot tanks didn’t stay fast. After about three weeks of feeding, dissolving and carbon uptake dropped in every tank.

Slimy films of microbes had built up on the glass and on top of the sand, especially in the tanks that got the engineered bacteria.

The films appeared to keep the water from mixing, and the researchers suspect they blocked the rock. A thin crust had also formed, and most of the dissolving probably happened in the top 0.4 inch (1 centimeter) of sand.

The authors suggest that breaking up that top layer from time to time could keep the rock working, but they haven’t tested it. Dalvie and his colleagues say the next steps are larger experiments, better nutrient mixes and more efficient bacteria.

Dalvie said a real coastline or mine would need “some sort of enclosure or tank” to make enough of the iron-grabbing molecules. Such a tank “would resemble existing wastewater processes,” he said.

The bigger hurdle, he said, is the food for the bacteria. “Green feedstocks like this need to be available at large scales,” Dalvie said, referring to the acetate made from carbon dioxide and electricity.

The study did not analyze the economics of capturing carbon dioxide. “The price of carbon needs to be high enough to justify a new biochemical process,” he stressed.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Biotechnology.
