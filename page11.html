A 17-Million-Line Proof Says Yes. Here Is What It

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, written with Claude, as a Lean library of about 17 million lines. A day later the Lean and Mathlib communities certified both its statement and its proof. Clay's prize still needs publication and time, but as a machine-checked result the question is answered: every nontrivial zero of ζ(s) lies on the line Re(s) = ½.

A proof is a finite object. Lean does not walk the zeros one by one. It checks that every step follows from the last. So this post is not a complaint about size. It asks a different question, one a proof does not answer by itself:

If a zero had left the line, how long would it take to notice?

Map each zero ρ through w = (ρ − 1)/ρ. On the critical line |w| = 1 exactly. The functional equation pairs a zero at σ + it with one at (1 − σ) + it, and their images obey

So if a zero sits at σ > ½, its image falls inside the unit circle and its twin falls outside. In Li's criterion, λₙ = Σ [1 − (1 − 1/ρ)ⁿ], the inside one fades and the outside one grows like |w′|ⁿ. For large t,

The twin's growth eventually pushes some λₙ below zero. That negative value is the signature of an off-line zero.

Growth of Δ/t² per step is very slow, and the twin has to beat something: the on-line zeros, whose combined contribution itself grows as (n/2)(ln n − 1 + γ − ln 2π). Setting the twin equal to that background gives the threshold:

n_crit · Δ / t² ≈ ln n_crit + ln(ln n_crit + c) − ln 4

So the number of Li coefficients you need scales as t² log t / Δ. The t²/Δ part was known (Oesterlé 2000; Voros 2017). The log factor matters, because it means the "constant" in front is not constant. An earlier version of my own paper got this wrong: it claimed a universal value of 29.93. That value fails at every height I tested.

I built λₙ from the first 200 actual zeros of ζ plus the known density of zeros above them. I checked it against the exact λ₁…λ₅, then inserted one hypothetical off-line pair and scanned for the first negative λₙ:

At t = 10¹², the same formula needs about 6 × 10²⁶ coefficients.

The core of the model fits in a few lines:

Here is the part I find interesting. Nothing in that threshold uses the primes. It depends on two things only: the pairing (|w|·|w′| = 1) and how fast the background grows. For L-functions of conductor q, the cost barely moves. On real zeros of L(s, χ₋₄) the shift in n·Δ/t² is about +0.15, not ln 4.

The off-line zeros above were inserted by hand. Some functions don't need that. Davenport and Heilbronn built Dirichlet series with the same kind of functional equation as ζ but no Euler product, and their zeros genuinely wander off the line. I took the partner function mod 5. Its strongest off-line zero sits at 2.3086 + 8.9184i.

Before computing anything exact, I wrote down the prediction: the background for conductor 5, plus the twins of its off-line zeros. Then I computed its Li coefficients exactly, from Taylor coefficients of log Λ around s = 1. That method uses no zeros at all, and two independent runs agree to 10⁻⁸.

No primes in the input, a real off-line zero, and the crossing lands exactly where the pairing says it should.

So the cost of seeing is geometry, and the reason nothing is there to see is arithmetic. The Davenport–Heilbronn function has the geometry without the arithmetic, and its zeros leave the line. The proof settles the second. The threshold describes the first: how far out a twin would have to be, at a given height, before a sign test could see it.

Both kinds of result belong in mathematics. A proof tells you that. A measured invariant tells you how hard it is to look.

AI Hallucination in Specs: The 15-Minute Claim Trace Audit

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 22 • Originally published at specky.space

AI-written specs sound sourced but often aren't. The Claim Trace is a 15-minute audit that labels every claim Sourced, Inferred, Assumed or Invented before engineering or a coding agent builds from it.

AI hallucination in specs is when an AI-written PRD or spec states something about your users, market or product that no source supports, in the same confident voice as the parts that are true. The fix is a short audit before anyone builds: trace every claim and label it Sourced, Inferred, Assumed or Invented. We call that audit the Claim Trace, and it takes about 15 minutes.

Spec hallucination is a specific kind of what NIST calls confabulation: "confidently stated but erroneous or false content" that can mislead the reader (NIST AI 600-1). In a spec, it lives in the Problem, Context and Success Metrics sections, the parts that are supposed to come from evidence.

It rarely looks like a made-up feature. The feature is plausible and the structure is clean. The invented part is the justification:

Each reads like a finding. None may be one. The model has read thousands of PRDs, so it produces the shape of evidence: a behaviour, a segment, a direction of change. If your inputs were thin, it fills the gaps with what is statistically typical for a spec like yours.

Researchers split hallucinations into two types (Ji et al., 2022), and both show up in specs:

Extrinsic hallucinations are the harder ones to catch, because there is nothing to compare them against. The reader has to notice that a sentence has no source, which is exactly what fluent writing hides.

A 2025 paper by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech argues that models hallucinate because training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, much like a student guessing on a multiple-choice exam (Kalai et al., 2025). Of 10 widely used benchmarks the authors reviewed, 9 grade answers strictly right or wrong and give no credit for "I don't know". Under that scoring, a confident guess always beats an honest blank.

The paper also explains why some facts are more exposed than others. If 20% of a type of fact (their example is people's birthdays) appears only once in the training data, you should expect a base model to hallucinate on at least 20% of those facts. When the authors asked one large open model for a birthday and said "only answer if you know", it gave three different wrong dates.

Now apply that to your spec. Facts about your customers, your churned accounts and your admins' weekly habits appear zero times in any training data. The only way a model can state them is if you supplied them. Anything specific about your users that you did not provide is, by construction, a guess dressed as a finding.

NIST adds one more warning that matters for product documents: generated text may include confabulated logic or citations that justify the answer, which makes readers more likely to trust it (NIST, 2024). A spec that says "as customers told us in Q3" is more persuasive than one that just asserts, whether or not the Q3 conversation exists.

Drafting cost collapsed. A spec used to take a PM a day, and that day forced contact with the evidence. Now the draft arrives in under a minute, before anyone has reopened the research.

Downstream cost did not collapse. If an engineer or an AI coding agent builds from the spec, a fabricated premise becomes working code within hours. You find out the premise was invented after the feature ships, not before.

Review habits didn't adapt. Teams review specs for clarity, completeness and tone, which are the properties language models are best at producing. Almost nobody reviews for provenance. Developers have already learned this lesson with code: 66% name "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite" as their biggest frustration, and 45% say debugging AI-generated code takes longer (Stack Overflow, 2025). An almost-right spec is worse, because nothing fails to compile.

Professional documents are not immune. In October 2025, Deloitte agreed to repay the final instalment of an AU$440,000 report for an Australian government department after a researcher found fabricated academic references and a made-up quote from a court judgment. The corrected version removed more than a dozen bogus references and footnotes and disclosed that a GPT-4o based tool chain had been used (The Register, 2025). A spec rarely gets that much outside scrutiny, which is why the check has to happen inside the team.

The fix is not "don't use AI to draft". It is to add the review step the draft skipped: trace every claim to a source.

Read the spec once, sentence by sentence. Every sentence that asserts something about users, the market, the product or the future gets exactly one of four labels.

"Three of the last five onboarding calls mention exporting to spreadsheets" is sourced if you can link those three calls. The test is mechanical: a teammate who wasn't in the room can open the link and see the same thing.

"Admins likely export because they report upward" is inferred from those calls. That is fine, as long as it is labelled and the underlying sources are attached. Inference is where product judgement lives; the problem is only unlabelled inference.

Assumptions are not shameful. Specs run on them. The failure is when an assumption is written in the same voice as a finding, so the reader absorbs it instead of questioning it.

This is the label that matters most. Invented claims are the model's contribution to the document, and they are the ones to delete or convert into questions.

Do not argue about edge cases between labels. If you hesitate between Inferred and Assumed, pick Assumed. The label is a prompt to look closer, not a verdict.

A simple decision rule for the end of step 4: if more than a third of the claims in the Problem section came back Invented, send the spec back to discovery rather than to engineering.

Take a made-up but typical AI draft for an admin export feature. The Problem section says:

Admins export data manually every week, spending hours in spreadsheets. Reporting is the top request among churned accounts. A native export will reduce churn.

Four claims: two deleted, two demoted, zero sourced. The rewritten Problem section is shorter and honest:

One onboarding call (linked) mentioned exporting account data to a spreadsheet. We don't yet know how often admins export or why. Open question: what did churned accounts say when they left? Hypothesis: a native export reduces churn among accounts with more than 5 admins. We will check this before committing to a build.

That document can still lead to a build. But the team now knows what it is betting on, and the person reading it can disagree with the assumption instead of absorbing it.

How to use Ollama and LM Studio models as real agents with tools on a Mac

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pointing a chat UI at an Ollama or LM Studio model is easy. Making that model behave as an agent that edits your files and runs commands is where it gets messy, because open-weight models and different OpenAI-compatible servers are inconsistent about tool calling.

Short answer: JoeBro is a free, open-source Mac workspace for your AI agents. Point it at Ollama, LM Studio or any OpenAI-compatible server and, in agent mode, local models get the same tools as hosted ones (files, web, MCP servers, plugins) and the same shared memory. Local models (Ollama, LM Studio, anything OpenAI-compatible) and cloud models with your own keys run as agents with tools. This is a list of the specific failures I hit and what the Python backend (standard library only) does about each one.

The OpenAI tool-call format says arguments is a JSON string. In practice some endpoints hand back an already-parsed object, and some models wrap the JSON in a markdown code fence. The parser accepts a dict as-is, strips a leading fence if there is one, tries json.loads, and never raises. The worst case is an empty dict.

When a reply hits max_tokens halfway through a tool call (typically a model rewriting a long file in one content argument), the arguments are non-empty but not valid JSON. If you parse that to {}, the tool fails with a misleading "requires path". So there is a separate check: arguments that are non-empty and don't parse mean "truncated", and the model gets told that, rather than a bogus missing-parameter error.

Models invent parameter names. A document tool wants path, and one model sends file, another filepath, another file_name. Rather than dropping them, there is a small alias table: file, file_path, filename and friends all map to path; old_string and search map to find; new_text and replacement map to replace. It's unglamorous and it removes a whole class of failed turns.

Some models don't use the native tool-call channel at all and write the call into the reply as markup: , , , or DeepSeek's DSML wrappers. A normaliser rewrites each of those into one internal form before execution. If tools were run from text after the model had already finished its turn, one extra completion is made with tools switched off: "Here are the results of the tool calls you just made, give your final answer now".

A single cat of a big file can fill a small local context. Each tool reply is clipped to 24,000 characters, keeping 70% from the head and 30% from the tail, with a note in the middle saying how much was cut and to narrow the request. The head of a file shows its shape and the tail of a log has the error; the middle is usually filler.

For some tasks I want thinking off. The switch isn't the same everywhere: OpenAI wants a minimal reasoning effort, Anthropic's extended thinking is opt-in, several hosted providers accept reasoning_effort: none, and Ollama reads a chat-template flag instead (chat_template_kwargs: {enable_thinking: false}). The request builder branches on the endpoint.

Agents that run commands get a permission step. When the app has "ask before running" on, a command waits for an explicit approve or deny (default deny, and a 3 minute timeout), and you can allow a tool for the rest of the session. Document edits can be gated the same way, before the file is touched.

Because these agents sit in the same workspace as Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode, they share the same per-project memory over MCP and the same pool of tools, MCP servers and plugins. A local model can pick up a fact that Claude Code learned yesterday.

None of this is clever. It's the pile of small compatibility fixes you need before a local model is usable as an agent, and I'd be glad to hear which quirks you've hit that I haven't.

Node.js Signed Uploads for Expiring Support Documents (with Private Object Storage)

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A signed customer-support document can be available for 30 days and still need to disappear at a precise deadline. That requirement changes the design. I would keep the object private, give each document a unique key, store its deletion deadline in Postgres, and issue short-lived signed URLs for transfer. The Node.js application remains the authority on who may read the file and when deletion is due.

TL;DR: use a signed browser upload only when the bucket's existing CORS policy already accepts your origin. Otherwise, proxy the bytes through Node.js. In both cases, treat URL expiry and document retention as separate clocks. The app owns the exact deletion deadline; a storage lifecycle rule, where available, is only a daily backstop.

This is the distinction that matters for a one-person SaaS. Moving bytes is undifferentiated work. Making a deletion promise, and being able to show how it is enforced, belongs in the product.

Infrai fits one narrow part of this workflow: issuing private signed transfer authorization when the bucket's existing CORS policy already suits the browser. The application still owns access decisions, retention deadlines, deletion work, and evidence.

No. It expires permission to use one URL. It does not delete the object, invalidate every other URL already issued, or prove that a processor removed the bytes.

I model a retained document as an application record first and a storage object second. A useful record contains a random document ID, the support case ID, an object key, deleteAfter, a generation number, and a state such as pending, available, deleting, or deleted. The browser never chooses the final key. A key like support-cases/{caseId}/signed/{documentId}.pdf makes ownership legible while avoiding an overwrite-prone shared filename.

This storage surface has a minimum lifecycle granularity of one day. That cannot express an hourly contractual deadline. Run deletion from the application deadline, then reconcile overdue database rows; use lifecycle cleanup to catch drift rather than define the promise.

This also explains why I would not reuse users/{userId}/avatar.jpg for this workload. Overwrite-in-place can be acceptable for an avatar with a clear last-write-wins rule. A signed support document needs a stable identity. The storage surface has no object versioning, object lock, or If-Match conditional write, so an accidental overwrite is not recoverable and the object layer cannot provide strict write exclusion. A unique key plus a database generation closes the race that the object API does not.

The smallest design I would ship has two database transactions around storage work. Creation first inserts a pending row with its deadline and unique key. Only then does the application authorize a transfer. Deletion later claims a due row by generation, removes the object, and marks the row deleted. A reconciliation worker repeatedly finds due rows that have not reached that terminal state.

The following TypeScript is intentionally about the part the application must own. It also reads the live discovery contract before an adapter relies on the presign route. The discovery response supplies the request schema and runnable examples, so the adapter does not need a copied, possibly stale request body from an article.

The public discovery request needs no key. The authenticated presign request described by that contract must use Authorization: Bearer ${process.env.INFRAI_API_KEY} and an explicit HTTP method. Never send that authorization header to the returned presigned URL; the browser uses the signed URL as returned. Check every response, and on HTTP 429 honor Retry-After or back off exponentially. Any retried write should use an idempotency key so a retry cannot apply twice.

Before issuing a signed read, the app checks the caller's case access, the row state, and deleteAfter. This is important during deletion: once a worker claims the row, new reads stop even if physical deletion has not finished. After deletion succeeds, the worker records deleted. If it stops between those operations, reconciliation resumes the same transition.

A direct browser upload keeps file bytes out of the Node.js process. The app authenticates the customer, allocates the key, and authorizes a narrowly scoped transfer. That is my default when the browser origin, method, and headers already match the bucket's CORS policy.

There is a real boundary here. Custom bucket CORS is not self-service on Infrai's storage surface. If the existing policy does not fit, a direct browser transfer is not something to force with frontend tricks. Proxy the upload through Node.js, accept the bandwidth and backpressure cost, and keep the browser talking only to your own origin. A proxy is also the clearer choice when every byte must pass through synchronous inspection before storage.

Files stay private either way. This storage does not support public or public-read ACLs, and public_url remains null. That makes it unsuitable for permanent public avatar URLs, an image host, or static-site delivery. It does fit confidential support files served through short-lived signed reads.

The platform is worth considering for the presign boundary because its API is self-describing: public discovery returns the request schema, response schema, billing information, and runnable examples for the capability. Every documented capability has examples in 10 languages.

Infrai provides 295 routes across 20 modules under one key. Its operating model is one key, one wallet, and one bill. For this workflow, the same API key covers the storage capability instead of adding a separate vendor credential, and unified billing removes another invoice from month-end reconciliation. That is small, recurring operational work I would rather trade for a weekly product release. No provider-specific SDK is added only to authorize a transfer.

I recommend trying Infrai for private signed transfer authorization when an existing bucket CORS policy fits, because discovery makes the adapter contract inspectable before implementation and keeps this small integration replaceable. Keep consent, authorization, deadline calculation, deletion scheduling, and evidence in the application. Those do not become storage responsibilities.

The facade is not the processor contract. Region, replication, erasure terms, and legal commitments still have to be checked against the selected specialist provider. It covers R2, S3, OSS, and COS for storage, but not GCS or B2. It offers no automatic cross-region replication or cross-cloud bulk migration tool.

That produces a more useful comparison than a price table:

AWS S3's lifecycle documentation is useful for understanding provider-managed expiry, but lifecycle configuration alone should not be confused with the application's exact deadline. R2, OSS, and COS likewise deserve direct evaluation when their native region or contract is the reason for the choice. A wrapper cannot create a residency or deletion guarantee absent from the underlying agreement.

For records requiring WORM retention, recoverable versions, or financial-grade immutability, use a specialist or direct provider that contractually supplies those controls. Infrai's lack of object lock and versioning is decisive there. No naming convention fixes it.

The first scale upgrade is a durable deletion queue plus a periodic database scan for overdue rows. The queue handles normal timing. The scan detects missed work. I would alert on the age and count of overdue rows, not on whether a job was merely dispatched.

Next, I would make processor and region part of each document's recorded placement decision. That gives migration work an inventory. Since object metadata cannot be searched server-side and listing supports prefix filtering only, Postgres should remain the operational index.

Large multipart uploads need their own cleanup path because abandoned fragments have no automatic cleanup rule here. Retention policy must cover those fragments too, even though they never became an available document.

Finally, I would test races deliberately. Two updates cannot rely on If-Match, because it is unavailable. The database generation decides which operation is current; a stale worker must fail before it can remove a newer object. Last-write-wins is fine only when it is an explicit product rule.

My shipping rule is compact: the application owns truth and time; private object storage holds bytes; signed URLs grant brief transfer authority. Use a direct specialist when native retention, immutability, CORS, residency, or replication controls are part of the promise. Outsource the plumbing only where the boundary stays honest.

If that boundary fits your system, start with the Infrai guide to signed avatar uploads and verify the live discovery schema before writing the adapter.

Accuracy was higher on public reviews whose writers cited their own work. Claude recorded 80.2% accuracy in the group of 106 reviews with self-citations and 17.1% in the group of 1,453 without them.

Claude made mistakes even in the group where it performed best. An editor or author presented with a name from Claude would still need independent evidence to establish who wrote an unsigned review. This year’s Christmas movie releases are in full swing now that it’s mid-November, and I knew I had to check out Guarding Stars with my Netflix subscription. I mean, it unites Gilmore Girls’ Jared Padalecki with Gossip Girl’s Leighton Meester, so I was in for the 2000s nostalgia alone. I was really digging this lighthearted rom-com (that’s sometimes an action movie), but then that twist just made me go “WTF!” Let’s talk about it. But be warned, there are SPOILERS ahead!

Before we get into my lows about Guarding Stars, I have to give the movie its flowers. I’ve seen a lot of holiday rom-coms, and this one had the right amount of sweetness and depth I want from this kind of movie. It follows the fake dating trope pretty much to a T, but I really enjoyed what Padalecki and Meester in particular brought to it as Jack and Hannah.

It’s the kind of movie where we all know they were going to fall in love by the end, but I was instantly curious about how these two were going to fall for each other. I like that they brought out the softness in each other despite both of them being otherwise hardened by their current lives, and I really liked the mother/son dynamic between Jack and Andie Macdowell’s Connie.

Guarding Stars also had that nice balance of goofiness, wit and emotion that I want to see from a Christmas Netflix movie that stopped it from falling into simply being a holiday screensaver. I know it’s based on a popular book called The Bodyguard, and after seeing it I would definitely pick it up, especially to see what changes were made.

However, I thought the Guarding Stars really dropped the ball on the whole “stalker” plotline. Not that I’d expect it to be a huge part of the movie to begin with, but when it was revealed that Nadine was Jack’s stalker near the end, I was so taken aback. It felt way too convenient that one of two people he meets outside of his family and his bodyguard crew ended up being the stalker, and particularly having happened to be in the same place where he was hiding away.

To me, it would have made a lot more sense if the stalker travelled to his location after seeing the kiss cam video with Hannah go viral. Instead, the only information we get is that they knew each other in high school and she divorced her husband to be with him. And then, her husband was somehow vengeful of Jack? The stalker payoff was so unbelievably thin, and felt like a bummer. Especially when the rest of the rom-com put a lot of attention on character development and fun moments for the other characters.

Perhaps the filmmaking team thought the audience wouldn’t care about the plotline not concerning the romance? But, to me, it just cheapened the rest of the viewing experience. I hope more Netflix rom-coms can build out their scripts. If it’s going to be partly an action mystery, I don’t want to feel like no attention was put on that aspect of the movie.

Look, all in all, Guarding Stars had some elements that had me putting it on the nice and naughty list, but overall, I’m happy I checked it out. But come on, Netflix, if you want to shock me, I know you can do better than that.
