IBM plans to bring Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to enterprise customers

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mythos would hunt for unknown flaws in customers' code, and developers would approve every fix.

IBM’s office in Dubai Internet City, Dubai, 3 November 2026

IBM plans to add Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to Concert Protect so customers can scan their own code for unknown flaws, test which are exploitable and get fixes for developers to approve. It is aiming for early 2027. Mythos itself remains limited to vetted organisations.

IBM plans to build Anthropic’s Claude Mythos into its Concert Protect security software, the company said on Monday. Mythos is so good at finding security flaws that only vetted groups can use it. IBM wants to offer the feature in early 2027.

Mythos would scan code that customers pick for flaws no one knew about, including ones that normal scanners miss. Concert Protect would then check whether hackers could really use each flaw in that customer’s systems.

IBM and Red Hat tools would draft fixes, which developers must review and approve before they go in. IBM cautioned that its plans could change. It is not betting on one lab: in June it joined OpenAI’s cyber programme for a similar service.

“The bottleneck is no longer finding vulnerabilities. It is determining which can actually be exploited and fixing them before attackers do,” IBM wrote.

That gap is real. Project Glasswing, Anthropic’s restricted Mythos programme, flagged more than 10,000 possible serious flaws in its first month, but only 97 had been patched by then.

IBM says its past Mythos work, through Glasswing, was internal research. This is its first step toward selling it to customers. That fits Anthropic’s plan to put Mythos inside partners’ products, where users get findings and fixes rather than the model itself.

Access to Mythos is tightly controlled. Anthropic briefly switched it off in June to comply with US export controls, and the White House now reportedly decides who joins its partner list. Anthropic released Mythos 5.2 on Monday, still only for vetted organisations.

11 Classic Italian Dishes That Belong On Your Thanksgiving Table

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Italian food is inherently celebratory. The cuisine embodies togetherness, featuring giant loaves of semolina bread, big salads, and overflowing family-style platters that snake their way around the table until there's a little bit of everything on everyone's plate. And while we didn't all necessarily grow up eating meatballs, gnocchi, and lasagna, these meals have slowly become a fundamental part of the American cooking canon. Italian food isn't necessarily easy to prepare, but its familiarity and simple elegance make the dishes feel accessible, even if they're difficult to master.

Italian food is also so well suited for the holiday table because of its adaptability. Regardless of your culinary background or self-identified food heritage, most classic Italian dishes can be reconfigured to fit any cuisine or occasion, including Thanksgiving. When you think about it, the two are a natural fit. Italian food is hearty, cozy, and comforting, with plenty of creamy carbohydrates and hearty main courses. The same goes for your typical Thanksgiving fare, allowing this list to feature recipe ideas that blend the two. Think pumpkin-stuffed ravioli, turkey lasagna, and risotto topped with roasted butternut squash. These interesting mashups will undoubtedly bring some extra excitement (and cheese) to your upcoming holiday menu.

A whole roast turkey is one of the most divisive holiday dishes. Done correctly, it's succulent, juicy, and full of bold poultry flavor; prepared improperly, you're left with pounds of bland, boring meat. But you don't need to cook a 10-pound bird to get turkey on the Thanksgiving table. These slow-cooker Italian turkey and zucchini meatballs use classic Italian cooking methods and ingredients to bring Thanksgiving flair to the holiday table. Meatballs are typically viewed as casual fare, but with the right cooking techniques and seasonings, they can be just as delicious as anything.

This recipe calls for a mixture of ground turkey meat and shredded zucchini. Why? The zucchini helps the meatballs retain moisture and adds some savory vegetal notes to the meat. These two ingredients are paired with a supporting cast of garlic, egg, breadcrumbs, and Pecorino Romano cheese to round out the flavor and give the meatballs a firmer texture. More importantly, the slow cooking keeps the meatballs submerged in a homemade tomato sauce, trapping moisture and keeping that notoriously dry turkey meat nice and luscious. This technique not only makes perfectly prepared meatballs, but also lets you tend to Grandma and Cousin Peter without worrying about something else overcooking.

This rich turkey tomato Bolognese is another creative way to add turkey to your Thanksgiving feast without cooking, carving, and roasting a massive piece of poultry. Bolognese is essentially a slow-simmered Italian sauce made with tomatoes and ground meat. As it cooks, the ingredients meld together, creating a thick, stew-like consistency that's greater than the sum of its parts. Although it is typically made with ground beef or pork, you can easily substitute turkey without losing any of the dish's heartiness or texture. The aromatics give it a sweet undertone, and the white wine helps deglaze the pan, capturing those brown bits of concentrated flavor.

But what sets this recipe apart is another ingredient synonymous with an authentic Bolognese yet largely omitted in jarred varieties: milk. Dairy may seem like a strange addition, but it gives the sauce a smooth, rich texture and helps tenderize the meat. Equally important to building your Bolognese sauce is pairing it with the correct pasta. Spaghetti is the most common but not the best option (at least according to Ina Garten). Instead, choose something flatter with more surface area, like tagliatelle or pappardelle. If you're feeling tubular, go with a shape with a wider diameter, like rigatoni, so the sauce can get trapped inside.

Sausage has rightfully earned its place on the Thanksgiving table, so much so that you can find it in everything from stuffing to cornbread casseroles to white gravy. But depending on which style you choose, the meat can easily overpower or oversalt a dish. When preparing this zucchini involtini, it's best to use an uncooked, Italian-style sausage. Unlike those popular, pre-cooked, vacuum-sealed chicken sausages, a quality Italian sausage comes raw and in a natural casing; otherwise, you can't sufficiently break up, brown, and mix the meat with other ingredients.

This appetizer requires some preparation, but the final result is well worth the effort. The most laborious task is slicing the zucchini into long, thin planks and piping them full of the sausage and ricotta filling. After you prepare the rolls, the rest of the dish comes together quickly. Just top each one with shredded cheese, and bake them together in a thin layer of sauce. The chew of the sausage paired with the sweet tomato sauce and creamy ricotta makes for an unforgettable appetizer that tastes just as impressive as it looks. If you don't want to make your tomato sauce from scratch, you can easily substitute your favorite jarred marinara.

Agnolotti started in the Piedmont region of northern Italy as a humble, pinched pasta that farmers would make to use up leftover bits of meat. Unlike ravioli, which are perfectly square, agnolotti are thin and rectangular, more like a standard bedroom pillow rather than a throw. This agnolotti di zucca is the perfect seasonal pasta, especially if you're in the mood to make fresh dough.

And while many Thanksgiving centerpieces feature a cornucopia of squash and gourds, this recipe actually includes them in the dish, specifically in the agnolotti filling, which is made with a mixture of ricotta cheese and roasted delicata squash. If you don't want to deal with chopping and de-seeding, a pumpkin purée will also work, but the flavor may fall short without those natural caramel notes you get from freshly roasted squash. The fresh pasta is an excellent foil, the dough chewy and toothsome as it cradles the delicate filling. After the agnolotti are stuffed and boiled, they're finished in a pan with butter and sage for a bite that's all things autumnal. Obviously, this dish isn't exactly light fare, so serve it in place of your more classic holiday carbohydrates.

Speaking of ravioli, you can't have a list of classic Italian dishes without mentioning this instantly recognizable stuffed pasta. Moving beyond squash, this homemade ravioli recipe focuses on the other orange gourd: pumpkin. But we're not talking about the canned stuff; this dish gets its unique flavor from freshly roasted sugar pumpkin. Does it require more prep work? Yes. You have to remove the seeds and stringy pulp, roast the pumpkin, and scoop out the flesh, but these extra steps add a bright earthiness you just can't recreate with canned pumpkin.

Unlike most pasta fillings, which are salty, savory, and creamy, this recipe goes in another direction, pairing pulverized almonds with ground nutmeg, ricotta cheese, and, of course, roasted pumpkin. The finished ravioli are coated in a traditional butter-sage sauce, furthering that autumn feel. Again, these require homemade dough, but it's fairly forgiving if you roll it out to the proper thickness — slightly less than 1 millimeter thick. Cut the dough in circles, and fold over the filling to make pretty little half moons, a nice, festive homage to the fall harvest.

The Thanksgiving table is usually covered with the classics: stuffing, cornbread, turkey, and sweet potato casserole. But this predictability can easily veer into monotony. Change is difficult, but it can be a good thing, especially for your palate. Reinventing your holiday menu isn't so much about eliminating as it is about balancing. Holiday food is notoriously rich and sweet; adding a dish that's salty, spicy, and earthy can help bring some relief.

These spicy sausage stuffed mushrooms deliver all of the above. A stuffed mushroom is a festive appetizer, but if you use super-sized portobellos, they quickly become worthy of a main course. The stuffing is a mixture of spicy sausage, breadcrumbs, egg, and grated cheese. If you want to sacrifice a mushroom (or stems), you can dice it up and add it in there too. Once the filling is sautéed, all you need to do is clean and stuff your mushrooms, then bake until everything is cooked through. Compared to making and filling your own fresh pasta dough, this appetizer is pretty easy to throw together. You can also prepare these ahead of time and just bake them off when ready. The sausage's pepperiness, the crunch of the breadcrumbs, and the mushrooms' earthy, umami notes make this a definite crowd-pleaser.

Risotto is cultural fusion at its finest, blending northern Italian cooking traditions with the culinary influence of Middle Eastern traders and travelers. It's a simple dish that requires only a modest collection of ingredients. But don't be fooled by its understated appearance — risotto takes a lot of skill and patience to execute successfully. If you've ever tasted a quality risotto, you'll know its texture is what makes it so elegant and luxurious. As the Arborio rice cooks, the grains release their natural starches, creating a smooth, velvety texture without any cream.

This butternut squash and sage risotto follows the same basic principles, but its flavor profile leans more toward seasonal fall ingredients and isn't quite what you would find at a classic Italian restaurant. The butternut squash is diced into cubes, tossed with honey and olive oil, roasted, and served on top of the finished dish. But more important than the garnish is the cooking of the risotto itself. The Arborio rice should be lightly sautéed with oil and aromatics until opaque before receiving its first ladle of broth. From there, you're going one cup at a time, waiting for the grains to absorb the broth or stock before adding the next round of liquid. The risotto is ready when it naturally spreads across the plate.

Turkey is the iconic Thanksgiving centerpiece, but what really gets the whole family gathered around the table is the variety of smooth and scoopable side dishes. Nobody is surprised by mashed potatoes, macaroni and cheese, and corn pudding, but if you want something with more Italian flair and a nuttier, toastier finish, consider adding polenta to your menu.

Polenta is essentially a cornmeal porridge that's mounted with butter. It's rich and filling and probably the reason Uncle Bob will fall asleep before dessert. This polenta pasticciata (also known as "messed up" polenta) is like a mix of scalloped potatoes and a cheesy casserole. To make the dish, you cook, cool, and refrigerate the polenta in a baking dish until set. You then slice the polenta into half-inch-thick slices and stack them like a lasagna, adding alternating layers of polenta and cheese. This recipe also uses a trifle of fromage, including Gruyère, Gorgonzola dolce (an Italian blue cheese), and rich, creamy mascarpone. Finally, you bake the whole thing until golden brown and bubbly. Be forewarned: You may never be able to go back to eating an ordinary casserole ever again.

You can skip the turkey; you can ixnay the green bean casserole; you can even pass on the sweet potatoes, but it's hard to call it a Thanksgiving feast without stuffing. I never understood why we relegate this savory bread pudding to just one day of the year, as it's easy to put together, uniquely herby, and a great way to salvage that stale loaf. Given these characteristics, you can take your stuffing in hundreds of directions. But since this article focuses on classic Italian dishes, we're sticking with that cadre of ingredients.

At first glance, this sausage and mushroom stuffing seems relatively ordinary; it doesn't use a scoop of gochujang or soak its bread in a dashi broth. But if you look closer, you'll see it's special in its simplicity. It all starts with the bread, which is a nice, tart sourdough. Next is the spicy, porky, and piquant Italian sausage. Finally, it's the broth. Sure, you can use chicken stock, but this recipe calls for pouring your vermouth-infused broth back into the pan with the cooked fennel, onion, and celery to infuse it with that deep, naturally sweet vegetable flavor. Right before baking, you quickly cook your mushrooms and add them to the bread mixture with a sprinkling of fresh herbs. No superfluous additions here; each ingredient has a place and a purpose, which is why this makes for such a terrific stuffing.

It's hard to pinpoint exactly when macaroni and cheese made its way to the Thanksgiving table, but this classic comfort food is now somewhat of a holiday fixture. And while it's hard to find fault with any bubbling crock of cheesy noodles, if you want to deviate from this French-inspired dish and go Italian, make this turkey and spinach gnocchi Alfredo bake.

Rarely does one recipe have it all, but this one comes pretty close. The ingredient list could almost be healthy — if you closed your eyes before pouring in the cup of heavy cream. But it's not all richness upon richness. The foundation of this dish is diced — not ground — turkey breast, gnocchi, spinach, and cheese. Still, you'll find it surprisingly balanced, thanks to the turkey's meatiness and slightly gamey flavor. The sauce is made with turkey or chicken stock thickened with roux and finished with cream. After dousing the gnocchi in the sauce, topping with mozzarella, and baking in the oven, you'll have a show-stopping side dish that will likely upstage anything else on the table.

No matter the occasion — dinner party, sweet 16 celebration, competitive evening of Catan — a baking dish filled to the brim with bubbling waves of saucy pasta sheets and melted cheese always turns heads. Lasagna is that dish, always here to nourish and satisfy. A big tray can feed an army (of at least six) comfortably, all without the effort of preparing individual burgers, cutlets, steaks, or filets.

Traditional lasagna, or at least the one most of us know today, is an import from Southern Italian immigrants, made primarily with a simple meat ragu, ricotta cheese, and mozzarella (and pasta sheets, of course). This spinach turkey lasagna isn't much different, the main change being substituting ground turkey for ground beef. The ground turkey adds some extra chew and texture to the somewhat mushy lasagna. Using a leaner meat also helps cut down on the dish's overall richness and allows other flavors, like the spinach and marinara sauce, to shine. But most importantly, it's the fact that it's turkey that makes this minimal-fuss main course such a cool and tasty culinary crossover.

‘One of the highest-stress occupations’: Why $148,000 median pay can’t fix the air traffic controller shortage

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. has about 6% fewer air traffic controllers than it did a decade ago, even as the number of flights relying on the air traffic control system has grown 10%, according to a January analysis from the U.S. Government Accountability Office. The shortage isn’t confined to American airspace.

Countries from Australia to Qatar now compete for the same small pool of certified controllers, recruiting across borders with work visas and pay pegged to experience. Aviation training company CAE projects that 71,000 new air traffic controllers will be needed worldwide by 2034, or roughly 7,000 a year.

The U.S. can’t join that bidding war. Every FAA controller must be an American citizen, leaving the agency dependent on a domestic pipeline that turns away the vast majority of applicants.

For airlines, that pipeline sets the pace of the whole system. Delta CEO Ed Bastian told Fortune in April that the biggest opportunity for AI in aviation is in air traffic control, not inside the plane, because it would “do more in terms of helping our customers have quicker travel, more efficient travel.”

The GAO traces much of the decline to disruptions in training. Government shutdowns in 2013 and in 2018 and 2019 froze hiring and training, and the COVID-19 pandemic halted training for four months, then held it at reduced capacity for nearly two years. High attrition from 2019 through 2024 made it harder to catch up.

The 2025 shutdown made things worse: by November of that year, the FAA said nearly half of major air traffic control facilities were short-staffed as controllers worked without pay.

The deeper problem may be the job itself. “It’s one of the highest stress occupations out there,” says Tiffany Bisbey, an assistant professor of industrial/organizational psychology at George Washington University. When controllers show up in academic research, she says, it’s usually as the subjects of studies on stress.

The federal government says as much. The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes the work environment this way: “Their work can be stressful because maximum concentration is required at all times. Night, weekend, and rotating shifts are common.”

Money is not what keeps people away. The median American air traffic controller earned $148,080 a year, or $71.19 an hour, as of May 2025, according to the BLS, and the top 10% earned more than $215,610.

The bar to apply is also lower than many people assume. The FAA requires a bachelor’s degree, one year of progressively responsible work experience, or a combination of the two, and new hires then spend more than a year training on the job. When the FAA pitched the job to Gen Z video gamers this spring, it advertised pay of at least $155,000 after three years.

Other countries are short too, and some are filling the gap by hiring controllers trained elsewhere. In September, Airservices Australia posted a call for experienced international controllers, offering a work visa with eligibility to transition to permanent residency and promising “the perfect balance between work and leisure.”

Australia needs the help. That same month, unplanned absences left eight of Sydney Airport’s 49 controllers unavailable and delayed roughly a third of inbound flights one morning.

The competition cuts both ways. Qatar has been recruiting Airservices Australia controllers, Canada’s NAV CANADA used an outside firm to recruit eight foreign controllers, and South Africa lost 28 controllers in eight months, Reuters reported this month. The country that trains a controller usually bears the full cost of that training, even if the controller later leaves, a spokesperson for the industry group CANSO told the news service.

The FAA’s qualification rules narrow the pool well before training begins. Applicants must be U.S. citizens under 31 and pass pre-employment tests, a medical exam, and a security investigation. “Less than 10% of all applicants meet these requirements and are accepted into the training program,” the FAA says.

Even fewer finish. About 2% of applicants qualify for and complete the full training process, the GAO found.

The agency is hiring faster than it has in years. The FAA hired 2,235 controller trainees in fiscal 2026, the most in its history, and its 2026-2028 workforce plan puts the certified workforce at about 11,000 as of April, against a target of 12,563.

That target is new. In May, the FAA lowered its staffing goal by about 2,000 controllers, citing forecast demand and changes to scheduling and training. The National Air Traffic Controllers Association said it was not involved in drafting the plan.

Bisbey sees no safe shortcut. Fast-tracking applicants or compressing training, she says, is not a viable fix, and she suspects the people who designed controller training “probably thought they needed to do even more, not less.” As she puts it: “It’s such a high-risk organization, and the training is so arduous for a reason.”

The FAA has experimented at the margins, including a 2024 pilot program that let graduates of two Oklahoma schools skip additional FAA Academy training and go straight to facility training. But certifying a controller still takes years, and until the pipeline catches up, travelers should expect more delays.

Bisbey thinks most will take them in stride, since few would trade a delay for a less safe flight. “As annoying as it is, it’s not going to make people stop traveling.”

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is facing an internal investigation after admitting to an affair with a Chinese businesswoman who U.S. intelligence agencies believe is a Chinese spy.

While the company has not yet named specific investigators, that probe is being helmed by the board with help from outside counsel, according to Nvidia. Lawyer Stephen Neal is the board's lead independent director and head of corporate governance, likely making him a key figure in the investigation.

In a statement, Nvidia's board said it has "no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised," though it will examine all evidence. China has dismissed the espionage claims. Huang, one of the most powerful businessmen in the artificial intelligence boom, said he would "cooperate fully" with the process.

Yet the probe has sparked uncertainty around Huang's future at the chipmaker and raised concerns of broader ripple effects to companies in the tech sector that are exposed to the AI build-out.

In a Monday note, Citi analysts wrote that one of the key issues for investors moving forward will be "potential governance implications" and "increased regulatory scrutiny" as the company navigates the investigation.

Nvidia's board has 10 independent members, including a former president of Taco Bell, a former Eli Lilly executive and a former vice chairman from Goldman Sachs. Neal, who joined Nvidia's board of directors in 2019, is a chairman emeritus and senior counsel of the law firm Cooley LLP, where he also served as CEO from 2001 to 2008.

Neal did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

It won't be his first corporate governance probe. According to Cooley, Neal has litigated cases across securities, bankruptcies, antitrust, takeovers and more. And he's conducted several investigations for multiple boards of directors, including at General Motors, where he advised the company in disputes with Ross Perot.

He also guided Walter Hewlett and the Hewlett Trust to stop a merger between Hewlett-Packard Co. and Compaq Computer Corp. and helped PacifiCare Health Systems with transactional matters.

According to the firm, Neal has conducted several investigations for committees of directors and independent directors, though most of those client names are confidential. He's also handled investigations with the Securities and Exchange Commission for both corporations and individuals.

Cooley has a long history with Nvidia, too. The law firm said it incorporated the company in 1993 and has served as its principal outside corporate and litigation counsel for roughly three decades. Cooley handled Nvidia's initial public offering in 1999.

Investors will be closely watching the investigation, and its emphasis on independence, as the process unfolds, according to Brayden King, a professor of management and organizations at Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Management.

"You definitely want somebody leading it who people can look at and say, 'OK, we believe this is a report that we can fully believe and trust that the outcome is the right outcome,'" King told CNBC. "That's what investors are looking for. They just don't like the instability that comes with all of this."

King said the Nvidia investigation lies at a unique intersection of Wall Street and Washington, D.C., where the company is likely navigating its next steps to try to appease both groups.

Because Nvidia's reputation is highly correlated with Huang's reputation, King said, conducting a transparent investigation will be key to regaining and retaining shareholders' trust. Not having an immediate CEO succession plan in place is only impeding that confidence, he added.

"I think the board realizes that they're doing an independent investigation in part because they really need to understand to what extent there are real problems here, and they also know that if they don't do an independent investigation, then there will be lingering concerns about whether their key stakeholders can trust the company," King said.

If the investigation shows that national security was indeed put at risk, King said, the company will need to take "very strong measures" to restore its reputation.

"That will basically come down to having to put structures in place in corporate governance to assure that this can't happen again," King said. "The independent review is going to have to be truly independent, and it's going to have to be candid enough that people believe that we can restore some normalcy around how the company operates."

It's also likely influencing how other tech companies, particularly those in the AI space, are thinking about their own corporate governance structures and how they might navigate increased scrutiny on their leaders, he added.

Marcel Kahan, a professor of law at New York University, said he's not surprised that Nvidia has been potentially exposed to industrial espionage.

Because the company operates in such a high-visibility and crucial sector, Kahan said companies like Nvidia have to be careful in their operations and whom they trust with their information. The investigation could mean the company may consider adding additional governance committees to ensure this doesn't happen again.

The revelations about its CEO will also likely mean more specialized training from Nvidia and its law firms to ensure trade secrets are kept close.

He also said the company is likely prioritizing an in-depth investigation over transparency to investors, focusing on recruiting "the best of the best" to assist with the probe.

"Nvidia is one of the most valuable companies on Wall Street," Kahan told CNBC. "I think, given all the attention that's being paid to it, this is one of the instances where I would not be that worried about anything being done in an underhanded way."

Still, although Nvidia is a major company with massive influence on the market, the controversy may not end up making too large of a dent in corporate governance structures, according to Kathryn Harrigan, a professor of business leadership at Columbia Business School.

Harrigan said because Wall Street and Silicon Valley have seen similar controversies play out before, this scandal may simply be following the script of its predecessors.

"Is it going to change the basic structure of the AI build-out? Probably not," she told CNBC. "You sometimes wait years to get on their schedule if you have an order for chips. It's not like you can magically switch from one vendor to another."

Scientists Discover a Hidden Weakness in Prostate Cancer That Could Transform Treatment

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A newly identified vulnerability in a cancer-driving protein could pave the way for a different approach to treating prostate cancer.

Researchers at the University of Michigan have identified a potential way to target a protein that helps drive prostate cancer but has long been considered beyond the reach of drugs. Their experimental compound, PBITE-1, interfered with the protein’s cancer-promoting activity in laboratory models, offering a possible starting point for more targeted treatments.

The protein, known as ERG, becomes abnormally active when two genes, TMPRSS2 and ERG, fuse together. This genetic rearrangement occurs in roughly half of prostate cancer cases among patients of European ancestry and promotes tumor cell growth and migration.

For years, ERG has been considered “undruggable” because it appeared to lack the molecular pockets where medications typically attach. In the new study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, researchers discovered an accessible pocket in a region called the PNT domain, which helps regulate ERG’s interactions with other proteins.

After screening more than 1,600 compounds, the team identified a promising molecule and refined it into PBITE-1. In experiments involving prostate cancer cell lines and laboratory models of human and mouse prostate tissue, the compound inhibited ERG activity, triggered cancer cell death, and reduced the ability of cancer cells to invade surrounding tissue.

“Although we were the first to identify the gene fusion as a driver of prostate cancer, it was challenging to find a drug target for ERG,” said Arul Chinnaiyan, M.D., Ph.D., S.P. Hicks Professor of Pathology and member of the Rogel Cancer Center.

Most existing prostate cancer therapies focus on androgen receptors, proteins that respond to hormones such as testosterone. These receptors can activate the TMPRSS2-ERG fusion, helping fuel tumor growth. Drugs that block androgen signaling can slow the disease, but tumors often develop resistance, and treatment can cause side effects that affect quality of life.

Targeting ERG directly could provide an alternative approach for patients whose tumors carry the gene fusion, rather than relying exclusively on treatments that interfere with hormone signaling.

“This work shows that instead of treating all prostate cancer as one disease, we have the potential to personalize therapy for subtypes of prostate cancer.”

PBITE-1 remains an experimental research tool, and its effects have not been established in patients. Further research will be needed to determine whether compounds targeting ERG can be developed into safe and effective medicines.

“Although it is unlikely that PBITE-1 is ready to become a drug any time soon, this study shows that ERG is a druggable target,” Chinnaiyan said.

“It is exciting that we now have a path forward to target ERG in prostate cancer.”

Since her breakout role as Eleven, Millie Bobby Brown has become one of Netflix’s go-to contract stars, and it’s easy to see why.
