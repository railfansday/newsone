Kraken parent Payward gains market share as slower trading weighs on earnings

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Payward’s adjusted EBITDA fell 66% year over year to $60 million, even as its share of global spot trading nearly doubled and fee-based services expanded.

Kraken’s parent company Payward nearly doubled its share of global crypto spot trading to 6.5% in September from 3.3% a year earlier, even as weaker trading activity weighed on its earnings.

The market-share gain means Payward captured a larger portion of spot trading relative to competitors, rather than an increase in trading activity across its platforms. The company handled $353 billion in transactions in the third quarter, down 30% from a year earlier, according to financial results released Friday.

The two figures measure different things. The market share tracks its portion of global spot trading across its platforms in September, compared with a year earlier, while Payward’s quarterly transaction volume includes activity across multiple products, including spot, margin, equities, payments and certain derivatives.

Adjusted EBITDA fell to $60 million from the $178.6 million reported a year earlier. Total platform margin fell 12% to $487 million. Payward introduced that measure this quarter to replace adjusted revenue, adding a deduction for staking rewards paid to customers.

Payward has spent the past two years expanding beyond its core cryptocurrency exchange business through acquisitions in derivatives, proprietary trading, tokenized assets and payments. The strategy aims to diversify revenue beyond transaction fees, which fluctuate with market activity.

Its purchases include futures broker NinjaTrader and proprietary trading firm Breakout in 2025, followed by the acquisition of US-regulated derivatives platform Bitnomial in May 2026. The company also expanded into stablecoin payments with its purchase of Reap in July.

Those businesses contributed to a broader mix of trading and service-based activity in the third quarter. The number of derivatives contracts on which Payward charges a per-contract fee rose 84% to 66 million. Margin from subscriptions, trader evaluations and other paid services rose 59% to $125 million.

Breakout, which charges traders for evaluations that can qualify them for funded trading accounts, was included for the full quarter after joining in September 2025. The July purchase of Reap added a stablecoin payments and card-issuing business. The company did not quantify either acquisition’s contribution to the quarter.

Results improved from the second quarter despite remaining below year-earlier levels. Total transaction volume rose 22%, while total platform margin increased 12% to $487 million from $436 million.

The improvement was not driven by trading alone. Margin earned from assets customers hold on Payward’s platforms, including staking-related services, rose 26% to $128 million. That accounted for roughly half of the $51 million quarterly increase in total platform margin.

Adjusted EBITDA climbed to $60 million from $23 million, while its margin relative to total platform margin rose to 12% from 5%. Compared with a year earlier, however, adjusted EBITDA was down 66%, underscoring the pressure on profitability despite a growing contribution from services less directly tied to trading activity. Asset- and access-based services accounted for 52% of total platform margin, up from 46% a year earlier.

That shift could matter for an eventual public listing. Payward confidentially filed for a US IPO in November 2025 but paused its plans in March as market conditions weakened. By September, the earliest potential listing window under consideration was the second quarter of 2027.

Genetic Switch Helped Evolve the Large Primate Brain

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers discovered a primate-specific DNA regulatory switch that governs the production of specialized brain-building stem cells called basal radial glia (bRG). The study shows that evolution expanded primate cerebral cortex complexity by altering how the shared DACH1 gene is regulated, boosting bRG progenitor production without needing to invent entirely new genes.

Source: National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University

What genetic innovations enabled the human cerebral cortex, the wrinkled outer sheet of neural tissue responsible for language, abstract reasoning, perception, and memory, to grow vastly larger and more intricate than the brains of most other mammals?

For decades, evolutionary neurobiologists have hunted for uniquely human genes that either expand the starting pool of neural progenitor cells or prolong the developmental window during which those cells divide.

Now, a collaborative team of researchers in Taiwan and Japan has uncovered a third evolutionary mechanism: rather than inventing new genes from scratch, evolution rewrote the regulatory instructions controlling an ancient, shared gene to alter which types of brain-building cells are manufactured.

Led by investigators at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (NYCU) and Academia Sinica in Taiwan, alongside collaborators at Kyoto University in Japan, the study pinpoints a small noncoding DNA element that drives the production of basal radial glia (bRG), a specialized class of neural stem cells essential for cortical expansion.

To conceptualize how embryonic brain development evolved, the researchers compare the developing cortex to a biological factory staffed by neural stem cells. Evolution can scale up output by enlarging the workforce or extending factory operating hours, or it can modify the factory’s operating manual to produce a specialized mix of workers.

In developing primate brains, basal radial glia (bRGs) serve as high-capacity cortical builders. Abundant in humans and non-human primates yet remarkably scarce in rodents, bRGs undergo repeated rounds of self-renewing division within the outer subventricular zone, dramatically amplifying the neuronal output needed to fold and expand the cerebral cortex. They also generate upper-layer projection neurons that form long-range connections bridging distant cortical regions and hemispheres.

“What interested us was that the gene already exists in both humans and mice. The important difference may not be the gene itself, but the instructions controlling how it is used during brain development. That gives us another way to understand how evolution could produce different kinds of brains,” explained Professor Pei-Shan Hou at the Institute of Anatomy and Cell Biology, NYCU, Taiwan.

The research team focused on DACH1 (Dachshund Family Transcription Factor 1), a developmental gene conserved across mice, monkeys, and humans. Comparative genomic analysis revealed a compact regulatory DNA sequence acting as an enhancer switch for DACH1; while conserved across primates, this regulatory motif differs in non-primate vertebrates.

To determine how this evolutionary edit shapes cortical assembly, the investigators combined cross-species stem cell engineering with live-tissue neuroimaging:

Further molecular dissection showed that DACH1 drives this cell-fate shift by suppressing an opposing signaling pathway that otherwise restricts neural stem cell diversification, ultimately shifting the balance of newborn neurons toward subtypes that wire the brain’s two hemispheres together.

While the authors emphasize that brain evolution and human intelligence emerge from complex, multi-gene networks rather than a single regulatory switch, the findings demonstrate how subtle tweaks in noncoding DNA can profoundly reshape cellular architecture.

By illuminating how regulatory DNA coordinates progenitor identity and developmental timing, the work provides both a clearer framework for understanding primate brain evolution and improved benchmarks for engineering high-fidelity human cortical organoids to model neurodevelopmental disorders.

How did evolution help make the human brain larger without creating new genes?

Researchers discovered that evolution modified a small regulatory stretch of noncoding DNA, acting like a genetic switch, that controls an existing gene called DACH1. By altering when and how strongly DACH1 is used during development, primates produce far more cortex-building stem cells than mice, even though both species share the DACH1 gene.

What are basal radial glia (bRG), and why are they important?

Basal radial glia are specialized neural stem cells that are abundant in developing primate brains but rare in mice. They divide repeatedly to generate huge numbers of neurons for the cerebral cortex—the brain’s outer layer responsible for memory, language, and complex thought—as well as nerve cells that connect the brain’s two hemispheres.

How did scientists prove this DNA switch controls brain cell production?

When researchers deleted the primate regulatory DNA region from lab-grown human and rhesus monkey brain organoids, the organoids produced significantly fewer basal radial glia cells. Conversely, inserting the human DNA switch into developing mouse brains increased the production of bRG-like cells.

An LHX2-DACH1 regulatory innovation drives basal progenitor expansion in the primate neocortex

A defining feature of the primate neocortex is an expanded cortical progenitor pool, especially the basal radial glia (bRG), contributing to complex intracortical circuitry underlying higher cognition.

Here, a comparative transcriptomic analysis of developing human and mouse neocortical cells identified DACH1 as a transcription factor enriched in human neurogenic RGs.

Using both primate ESCs and mouse models, we found an LHX2-DACH1-miR-215-5p program regulating bRG production. DACH1 loss in human cortical progenitors impaired bRG production and shifted neuronal output toward corticospinal neurons, whereas Dach1 depletion in mice had no effect.

Conversely, ectopic DACH1 expression in the rodent neocortex induced bRG formation and promoted callosal neuron production. Further, we found that DACH1 is regulated by LHX2 via a primate-evolved enhancer. Introducing this enhancer into mice promoted bRG generation, while its removal in primates impaired bRG production.

Mechanistically, DACH1 promotes bRG generation by suppressing miR-215-5p–mediated down-regulation of centromere- and centrosome-related genes. These findings reveal a mechanism by which cortical progenitor expansion shapes the primate cortical connectome for cognitive evolution.

The issues were related to communications and radar issues, according to an internal message from Alaska Airlines, which was seen by CNBC.

A ground stop halts flights at their departure point.

"The FAA issued a nationwide ground stop for all U.S. airports following communication issues impacting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers," the FAA said.

The agency said in a statement that "FAA technicians are investigating the issue."

Inbound international flights will be managed on a case by case basis, the FAA said.

How The Developers Of '007 First Light' Nailed The Feeling Of Being James Bond Through Its Cars And Vehicles

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As a massive fan of the James Bond franchise, my main hope going into "007 First Light" wasn't that it would have characters and plot points I recognized or have all the right bits of fan service — I just wanted the game to evoke the same emotions that the Bond movies do, to really feel like I was being Bond when playing. Luckily the game delivered those feelings in spades. IO Interactive, best known for the "Hitman" franchise, created one of the best pieces of James Bond media ever, and a big part of "First Light's" success is down to the vehicles you get to drive as Bond and how they're woven into the main story.

To learn more about the game's development, and the creation of the new DLC missions that feature the Aston Martin Valhalla, I hopped on a call with Rasmus Poulsen, the game's art director, and Dominic Vega, audio director and game director of live content/missions. "We knew from the get-go that we wanted as wide a range of driving experiences as we could manage to squeeze in the game, and of course that means everything from the unexpected to the hoped for," said Poulsen, and man did they deliver.

SPOILER ALERT: While I won't be talking about major plot or character spoilers in this story, I do spoil all of the various vehicular scenes and moments, so if you haven't played or finished the game yet, bookmark this page and come back to it later.

The first excellent car moment comes in one of the very first missions, when Bond is going through initial training to join the reborn 00 program. At a facility in Malta, Bond gets thrown the keys to a 2006 Aston Martin DBS, the same car seen in "Casino Royale" and "Quantum of Solace." It's described as "a classic" by one of Bond's compatriots, who adds it's "seen some real-life action too back in her day. Still a gorgeous drive." She's not lying — the DBS is totally banged up, covered in duct tape and zip ties.

Bond proceeds to race around a course, smashing through boxes and jumping down staircases. Despite how realistic the cars look and sound the handling is definitely on the arcade side of things, but it's also very fun. You're encouraged to do handbrake turns and always keep the throttle pinned, and enough actual skill and good driving is needed to do well in this mission and subsequent ones.

It's not a very long sequence, with Bond quickly moving on to other parts of training, but it's a great way to get us car people excited at the beginning of the game. This particular choice of car this early on is a great nod to our outgoing Bond on the silver screen, too. Bond movies have always had car chases, but it wasn't until the Daniel Craig films that the chases really leveled up, becoming grittier and more realistic and more exciting, sometimes the most blockbuster-y set pieces in the entire movie.

A few missions later, Bond and his fellow 00 trainees have to infiltrate a chess tournament at the Grand Carpathian Hotel in Slovakia, and they drive up in a 2007 Jaguar XJ, with Bond dressed as a chauffeur. This is where the game really nails the Bond feeling, with the regular cars and seemingly mundane scenes, and it was this aspect of putting cars in the game that Poulsen and Vega enjoyed the most. "The Bond franchise is not only about the low end and the high end, it's also about the sleeper cars. It's about getting the car people to go, 'I see you,' and it's for us to go, 'we see you too.' The middle of the pack there is really exciting to me," Poulsen said.

"Each of the Bond movies, the best ones, are a cohort of bands. Think of the curation of the cast of vehicles in the movie," Vega added. "We're making an 18-hour experience, we want to make sure that the cast of vehicles gets the nod [from fans]. It gets the 'ah OK, I see what you're doing there, oh you're doing a twist here, oh it's 'Living Daylights.'" The development team was in contact with Aston Martin and Jaguar Land Rover from the very beginning, and they knew they wanted to include a big variety of cars. "There's partnerships that you have to do with Bond, right, it's not Bond without certain vehicles in the mix, but then you still always want to surprise, you want to 'and' the expectation. The player should absolutely have their fantasy met, 'and'. I think it's really nice when people in the car world notice that," he said.

In the Slovakia scene, the player calmly drives the XJ down a winding mountain road toward the hotel. You don't drive fast, or take any shortcuts, or go off the set path; it's all about setting a scene. While driving the XJ, Bond and his crew are talking about the mission and giving you more backstory, like in a transitional movie scene. Instead of making something like this a cutscene, "First Light" lets you act it out. It's like the vehicular equivalent of a "walk and talk," if you know your movie-biz slang. You can take your time on the drive, which is especially nice now that a photo mode has been added to the game. (Everything you see in this story was captured by yours truly.) With how much of the game is straight-up action, it's really nice to have these moments of levity in between that add more depth to the characters.

Poulsen says the development team visited the JLR headquarters in Gaydon, getting tours of the heritage collection and its restoration facility, to figure out what vehicles they might want to use in the game. There was everything from classics to prototypes and rarities like the XJ220, plus things that aren't even out yet. "We walked around discussing, 'hmm, we need something that's not what gets the front page, what is it going to be?' We looked at the XJ of that particular year; it has a story of old money rather than flash, it's not so old that it's vintage, it's just in-between," Poulsen said. They pointed it out to Jaguar, saying "we want that one," and the company obliged. It is a perfect spec, too, one of the later facelifts in green over cream with those lovely five-spoke wheels.

Another part of the game sees Bond and his mentor heading to Mauritania, with the level starting off in a kitted-out Land Rover Defender. You get to drive the SUV across the gorgeous landscape, even doing some light rock crawling, before coming upon a city built inside of a ship graveyard that's run by the pirate king Bawma, played brilliantly by Lenny Kravitz. It's again used as a conversation piece, setting up the scenes to come. Bond gets back in the Defender for another exploring sequence a little later, and after a series of events including a lot more fighting, he gets rescued with the help of a great Series II pickup.

It's not all mundane drives, though. Later in the mission that starts with the XJ, after chaos ensues at the hotel, Bond has to chase after someone who's just hopped into a second-gen Jaguar XKR. He does so by breaking into and hotwiring a Bahama Yellow 1970 Aston Martin DBS, the same model used in "On Her Majesty's Secret Service" (and the same color as the DBS from "The Persuaders"). There's a great moment of Bond cheekily flirting with a mysterious woman who hops into the car with him, and you're off.

Again, it's a pre-set route, but there are a bunch of fun shortcuts like swerving into a muddy field to avoid logging trucks, jumping over canals and driving through a farmer's market. You have to avoid other traffic and contend with other obstacles, and it can actually be a little tough at times. In one climactic moment, Bond drives the car onto and over a transport truck, using it as a ramp to get huge air. Who among us hasn't dreamt of doing that hundreds of times? Adding to the fun is a great damage model, with the DBS getting really beat up by the end of it even if you try and drive safely.

The car chase ends, but Bond is immediately thrust into a firefight at an airfield, which, of course, leads to him dodging explosions to hitch a ride on an escaping cargo plane. To do so, he takes control over one of those airport stair trucks, and jumps onto the wing of the plane. Then he fights a bunch of bad guys on the wing as the plane takes off, and eventually it leads to him freefalling from the plane, avoiding debris while trying to find a parachute — and you're in control pretty much the whole time. It's awesome, and really embodies that unique Bond feeling.

Like the "Hitman" games, "First Light" is all about using your environment to your advantage, and there's a lot of moments where a vehicle is part of that environment. In parts of the game where more baddies are arriving in cars, you can shoot at them, causing them to leak gas or oil and become easier to explode. Some vehicles can be hacked, with Bond able to set off car alarms or remotely activate gadgets like smoke screens. "Plenty of movies you see a car gadget that you never get to see [used in] the movie, so that was a little bit of a head nod there," Vega said.

The two best vehicular sequences in the game don't feature cars at all, though. Or at least not ones you're driving, since you do get to smash up plenty of others. At one point in the game, Bond escapes a gala by commandeering a garbage truck and wreaking havoc through the streets of London, and then later on he takes control of a giant mining truck and drives it through a quarry in Vietnam to, again, save his friends and escape the bad guys. While both sequences are small parts of much longer missions, they're absolute blasts to play through, some of my favorite moments of the game for sure, and they feel totally Bond. Just as you might realize, "oh hell yeah, I think Bond is gonna get in that tank" while watching one of the films, I was sitting on my couch thinking, "oh hell yeah, am I about to drive that thing?"

Vega said the two sequences came about in very different ways, with the mine truck being more organic. "The level's getting put together, and somebody puts a prop in, and then I think there is that incentive in game development, and especially in Bond, where you just want to say 'yes' to the player. Like you're rolling downhill, and someone throws something into the snowball, and instead of rejecting it you keep going." You see a bunch of these mining trucks earlier on in the mission, the game teasing what's about to come without you even knowing it yet. "The mining truck there being part of establishing the space, the place, and then ever so slightly you go, 'guys, well, let's go,' because you embody the mind of the character," Poulsen said of the scene being thought up on the fly.

"[That's the thing of First Light] being a catered experience. It's not open world, it's not everything, it's not all the vehicles; it's a specific subset of vehicles. That means it's interesting for us, that we can lure you forward with this truck and all these things, and have you go 'ah, yeah,' you got what you wanted. Rather than have a plethora of vehicles in front of you and you go 'OK, cool, which one?' No, the one we chose," said Poulsen. "You get what you wanted, but in a way that you wholly couldn't imagine or didn't imagine — unless you're a Bond fan, of course, in which case maybe you do imagine it."

The garbage truck, meanwhile, was a much longer road to get to. Vega talked about how resourcefulness is one of Bond's key character traits, one that differentiates him from other secret agent characters, and that's baked into the level designs from jump. Another one of Bond's best qualities is how he'll smash straight through an obstacle and keep on going. Which he does in this sequence quite a lot. "It was always in the script that he's going to get into this bin lorry in this really unexpected fashion. I think Craig embodied that, I think Brosnan had a bit of that, I think Dalton had a bit of that — put two dots on a map and draw a straight line, that's how James Bond got there, and that is a really cool moment of expression," Vega said.

What makes the garbage truck escape especially cathartic is how the mission starts: with an upset Bond sitting in traffic in a Range Rover SV, having a conversation with his boss. You can't even side-swipe traffic, with the game displaying a parking sensor warning on the screen and pulling the car back into the lane. "It's such a Londoner inside commentary there. He's frustrated, he's been told to stand down. We stick you in a powerful fantasy vehicle like the Land Rover and you can't open it up, you're just running errands. It's putting you in that headspace," said Vega, "so when we finally put you behind the wheel [of the garbage truck] an hour and a half later it's just like go go go go through the wall!"

One thing the game doesn't let Bond drive is what would traditionally be the hero car. In fact, the game rubs it in your face a bit. In a mission fairly early on in the game, you visit Q in his lab and have a conversation in front of a covered-up car that's being worked on by robots. The car is clearly the Aston Martin Valhalla to any slightly trained eye, and Q refuses to let Bond drive it even when it gets finished. He's not even a 00 yet, of course he's not getting to drive Q's souped-up supercar, and it's being built for a different agent anyway. "It kind of puts you in the shoes of Bond," said Vega, "who's like 'I wish you'd give me this on a mission!' That's always Q and Bond's relationship, it's a bit of restraint from Q where he gives you the gadgets you need, he's not giving you the keys to the castle."

Well, Bond will be Bond, so in one of the game's final missions, there's a scene where Bond gets those proverbial castle keys. To escape a siege on Q's lab he climbs into the Valhalla, fires up its machine guns and rockets, and does donuts around the room, killing bad guys and blowing basically everything up. You do actually get to control the car for this scene, but it's pretty brief, leading right into a cutscene that has a few comedic moments involving the car and its built-in voice assistant. It's a satisfying moment, since for hours after the Aston Martin is first seen, you've probably been thinking "man, I really hope we get to do something with the Valhalla."

The team began working on the game a little before the Valhalla was production-ready, receiving the CAD files from Aston so they could get started on it and figure out how to Bond-ify the car. "One of the artistic pillars for the project is that dichotomy between downplayed credibility and outlandish adventurous over-the-top-ness. We need to combine those two things," said Poulsen. Since the game uses a third-person camera view where you're looking forward from behind the character, and thus behind any vehicle, they decided against putting weapons in the headlights or front end of the car, where you wouldn't see them. The Valhalla has such a short, low front end there wasn't much to work with anyway. "We went with a design that would utilize the body and the shapes in an interesting way, and it made sense to put all the weaponry on the back," Poulsen said. "The principal concept artist did a bunch of designs with the CAD model, where we were experimenting with opening up the panels like a beetle or folding them in and out."

When they sent their ideas back to Aston, explaining the why and how behind their designs, the Aston team simply gave them the thumbs up. "Rather than being like 'no actually, you have a radiator there, and this and there's pipes and the thing and the valve,' they didn't give us a hard time because I think they could see that we had tried to some degree respect the negative space and interior space and all that stuff in the car," Poulsen said. The final design features the Valhalla's rear quarter panels opening out horizontally at an angle to reveal a giant auto-aiming machine gun mounted on an arm on each side. "We got that wonderful interplay and a really playful yet rather credible sense of weapons folding out. I'm sure it wouldn't necessarily be doable like this, but [Aston] didn't have a hard time with us wanting to do that, so it was a very smooth relationship."

That couldn't be it for the Valhalla, so the "First Light" team has released a few "Valhalla Protocol" time trial tactical simulation missions as DLC that let you really wring out the Valhalla on a newly designed course, either with or without weapons and obstacles engaged. When you're using the machine guns and rockets to take down obstacles, drones and other vehicles, it's definitely more outlandish than Bond movies usually are, but it fits right in with past Bond video games and reminds me of other excellent titles like "Spy Hunter." Even in strictly time trial mode it's pretty tough to achieve one of the best time targets, since you've still got to contend with all sorts of things on the road, like ramps that you obviously will want to jump off.

"At IO, we believe that shipping the game is the first step. We tell you the story we want to tell, but there's so much more that comes after that. We knew long, long ago that the best possible moment for the Valhalla was the climax of the techo-wizard experience. You've just done all this awesome stuff in Q lab, you see everything that Q built for you, it was always going to be racing around in donuts because you don't want to end that on a chase," Vega said. How Many Crew Members Does It Take To Fly A KC-135 Stratotanker?

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

First flown in 1956, the Boeing KC-135 Stratotanker has spent decades refueling US military aircraft in flight. Despite its age, it remains a major part of the United States Air Force's tanker fleet, supporting fighters, bombers, and other aircraft on missions around the world. But how many people does it actually take to operate an aircraft responsible for such an important job? The answer raises some interesting questions about how the Stratotanker was designed and how its crew responsibilities have changed over the years.

The KC-135's crew arrangement is not quite as straightforward as it might seem. Its refueling missions require close coordination between the cockpit crew and the operator responsible for transferring fuel, while other missions can bring additional personnel aboard. Its history also raises questions about why it differs from commercial aircraft of the same era and how its crew compares with that of the newer KC-46 Pegasus. Looking at these differences helps explain how the Stratotanker has continued to serve for so many decades.

For a normal refueling mission, according to the US Air Force's KC-135 fact sheet, the standard crew is three. The KC-135R and KC-135T normally operate with a three-person crew: a pilot, a copilot, and a boom operator. The two pilots occupy the cockpit, while the boom operator works at the rear of the aircraft, controlling the flying boom used to transfer fuel to the receiving aircraft. The National Museum of the US Air Force confirms the same crew number for the KC-135R. The more interesting question is how those three people divide the work.

The workload is substantial. The Air Force fact sheet gives the Stratotanker a maximum takeoff weight of 322,500 pounds (146,285 kg), with up to 200,000 pounds (90,719 kg) of transfer fuel and, depending on configuration, as much as 83,000 pounds (37,648 kg) of cargo. The aircraft carries all of that on a wingspan of 130 feet, 10 inches (39.88 meters), yet the normal crew is still small enough to sit around a kitchen table.

That was not always the case. Early KC-135s carried four people, with a navigator joining the two pilots and boom operator, before advances in navigation and avionics removed that position from the standard crew. The aircraft has nevertheless remained in service for decades. According to Simple Flying, the Air Force still operates roughly 396 Stratotankers across active-duty wings, the Air National Guard and the Air Force Reserve.

More recent Air Force figures cited by the Government Accountability Office put the fleet at 373 aircraft as of early March 2026, before accounting for the losses and damage sustained during Operation Epic Fury against Iran. The Congressional Research Service later listed seven KC-135s as lost or damaged during the conflict. With hundreds of Stratotankers still in the inventory and the basic crew arrangement remaining central to their operation, the obvious next question is what each crew member is actually responsible for.

The three-person crew arrangement makes more sense once the KC-135's refueling system comes into view. Aerial refueling requires two aircraft to maintain close formation while a rigid boom connects them in flight. The Air Force lists the KC-135's maximum speed at 530 miles per hour (853 km/h) at 30,000 feet (9,144 meters), although fuel transfer takes place at speeds appropriate to the aircraft involved. The receiving jet moves into position below and behind the tanker, the boom extends, and the final connection is made from the rear of the Stratotanker.

Each crew member has a specific responsibility. The pilot flies the tanker and maintains a stable flight path, while the copilot shares flying duties, handles communications and checklists, and monitors aircraft systems. At the back, the boom operator, an enlisted airman, controls the boom and guides its nozzle into the receiving aircraft. The National Museum of the US Air Force explains that the operator uses small wing-like control surfaces called ruddervators to steer the boom into position. Working from a prone position and looking through a window, the operator effectively flies the boom itself. The system can transfer nearly all the KC-135's usable fuel, while a drogue attachment can be fitted to support aircraft equipped with refueling probes. The arrangement has proved capable of some extraordinary missions.

The museum's KC-135R earned the Mackay Trophy for a sortie flown on May 31, 1967, after a Navy pilot was shot down in Southeast Asia and a rescue operation ran into fuel difficulties. Two US Navy Douglas KA-3 Skywarriors kept a pair of Vought F-8 Crusaders airborne, but both the tankers and the fighters were running low on fuel. The KC-135 crew began refueling the KA-3s while continuing to pass fuel to the F-8s. In effect, one tanker was refueling another tanker that was simultaneously refueling a fighter. The KC-135 subsequently provided additional fuel to two McDonnell Douglas F-4 Phantom fighters on its way to an alternative landing base.

Interestingly, although the KC-135 could operate with a basic crew of three, the aircraft involved in this award-winning mission carried four. Its crew consisted of Major John H. Casteel, aircraft commander; Captain Richard L. Trail, copilot; Captain Dean L. Hoar, navigator; and Master Sergeant Nathan C. Campbell, boom operator. This historical example illustrates the distinction between the aircraft's earlier four-person arrangement and its modern standard crew. The additional crew member was a navigator, not a flight engineer.

Here’s how the B-52’s crew works and why the United States Air Force wants fewer airmen.

The navigator's presence on earlier KC-135 missions helps explain how the tanker's crew evolved over time, but it also highlights a more fundamental difference between the aircraft and its civilian contemporaries: the absence of a dedicated flight engineer. The tanker was developed alongside the Boeing 707, and many airliners of the period used a third cockpit crewmember to monitor engines and systems. Yet Air Force records for the KC-135 identify pilots, a boom operator and, on earlier versions, a navigator.

The absence of a flight engineer from the standard KC-135 crew does not mean the aircraft never employed one. In a 2017 Air Force profile, Master Sgt. Robert Self was described as the service's last KC-135 flight engineer, following a career that included assignments on specialized NKC-135 and KC-135 aircraft used for testing and support programs. His responsibilities included operating the fuel panel, monitoring aircraft systems and advising the pilots. He also recalled that earlier KC-135 configurations had carried both flight engineers and navigators, positions that gradually disappeared as onboard computers and avionics became more capable.

Two pilots were always intended to occupy the front seats, with the boom operator handling refueling from the rear. Then the avionics changed. As reported by the National Guard in a story about Iowa's 185th, PACER CRAG brought compass, radar, and GPS improvements to the fleet by the early 2000s, while modern GPS removed the need for the navigator on standard missions. GlobalSecurity's overview of the program likewise notes that the commercial off-the-shelf upgrade eliminated the navigator requirement on most missions. The Simple Flying report mentioned earlier adds that digital flight decks and enhanced autopilots have further reduced the workload.

The tradeoff is workload. A base spokesman at Fairchild told the Spokane Journal that, while the upgrades were arriving,it basically meant more responsibility for the pilot and copilot. Navigation functions moved into the avionics, while some tasks previously handled by another crew member became part of the cockpit workload. This distinction is important when comparing the KC-135 with the Boeing KC-46, which was designed from the outset around a very different refueling station and a more modern approach to crew responsibilities.

The easiest way to see the difference is to look at the boom operator. On the KC-135, that person works at the rear of the aircraft and looks outside through a window. On the KC-46 Pegasus, there is no comparable window to look through. According to Air Mobility Command's KC-46A fact sheet, the Pegasus uses a fly-by-wire refueling boom and gives its Air Refueling Operator panoramic displays with wingtip-to-wingtip situational awareness. From that station, the operator controls the boom, centerline drogue, and wing aerial refueling pods.

The screen-based system was intended to offer advantages, but it also created problems that required a major redesign. The same fact sheet says that the Air Force and Boeing agreed in April 2020 on the final Remote Vision System design, known as RVS 2.0, after deficiencies had affected safe and effective refueling. The issue has not disappeared completely: as Simple Flying reported, the Pegasus still has refueling limitations with some US Air Force aircraft, including the FairchildA-10 Warthog, while Boeing works on boom and vision-system fixes. The KC-135's window may look primitive alongside the KC-46's cameras, but it also offers a remarkably direct view of the aircraft being refueled.

The contrast does not mean the KC-135 is about to disappear. According to Simple Flying, the Air Force originally planned to field 179 KC-46s, but that figure has since grown substantially. The current program calls for 263 Pegasus tankers, almost 100 more than the original requirement, as the service looks to replace much of its aging KC-135 fleet. As of early March 2026, the Air Force had 373 KC-135s and 103 KC-46As in its inventory, according to the Government Accountability Office. The Pegasus is the newer design, but the Stratotanker remains a major part of the force and is being kept in service for decades longer than originally intended. That creates an important question about the three-person crew: is it sufficient for every mission the aging tanker is now being asked to perform?

How many crew members does the V-22 Osprey need? The answer depends on the military service, the mission, and the demands of each variant.

The three-person crew is a starting point, not a fixed rule. The Air Force fact sheet notes that some missions require a navigator and that only a limited number of navigator suites are available for those specialized tasks. It is an uncommon arrangement today, but it shows why the published crew number needs a little context: the KC-135's staffing changes with the mission.

Aeromedical evacuation is the clearest example. The KC-135 can carry litter and ambulatory patients on patient support pallets, and the fact sheet says an aeromedical evacuation mission adds a basic medical crew of five: two flight nurses and three medical technicians. That number can change according to the patients being carried. In the basic configuration described by the Air Force, a hospital mission can therefore put eight crew members aboard before the patients themselves are counted.

For aeromedical evacuation, the KC-135's cargo compartment can be fitted with a stanchion litter system, allowing patients to travel on a secured litter rather than ordinary passenger seats. The system must be assembled and attached inside the aircraft by support personnel, while the medical team brings the equipment needed to monitor and care for patients during flight.

Another issue that is harder to solve with crew changes is age. The last KC-135 was delivered in 1965, so even the newest aircraft are now decades old. As reported by ExecutiveBiz, SNC has assembled a team with BAE Systems, Collins Aerospace and GE Aerospace to pursue the Air Force's KC-135 Center Console Refresh, a program aimed at replacing aging fuel-system equipment, flight displays and computing architecture. SNC announced the consortium in November 2026.

BAE would design the fuel management system to reduce pilot workload; Collins, which has supplied KC-135 avionics for more than three decades, would add new displays and computers built on an open architecture; and GE Aerospace would contribute avionics already flying on the KC-46 and Lockheed C-130 Hercules. The proposal also includes autothrottle capability, with performance projected from July 2028 through June 2038 and the award still anticipated. In other words, the same aircraft that learned to do without a navigator decades ago may soon get another set of tools designed to make the remaining crew's jobs easier.

The standard KC-135 Stratotanker crew consists of three people: a pilot, a copilot, and a boom operator. Earlier configurations commonly included a fourth crew member, a navigator, whose position gradually disappeared as navigation systems and avionics improved. Specialized missions can still require additional personnel, while aeromedical evacuation typically adds five medical crew members to the basic crew. Flight engineers also served aboard certain specialized KC-135 aircraft, but they were not part of the standard operational crew. The result is an aircraft that has retained its essential three-person operating arrangement while adapting to decades of technological change.

That makes the boom operator the most distinctive member of the crew. From the rear of the tanker, one person is responsible for the final connection between two aircraft moving in formation, doing it through a window that has been part of the KC-135's design for generations. The KC-46 approaches the same task from the opposite direction, replacing that view with cameras and displays. The basic mission has barely changed; the way the crew carries it out has.

The KC-135's story is not yet finished simply because the KC-46 is entering the force. Looking ahead, Air & Space Forces Magazine reports that the Air Force plans to keep the KC-135 until at least 2050, even as it considers a bridge tanker and a future Next-Generation Air-Refueling System. If the cockpit refresh moves forward, the old tanker could receive a substantially modernized flight deck under the program's 2028 to 2038 timeline. For a design that first flew in the 1950s, the next chapter may be less about adding people and more about giving the same three-person crew better tools for the job.

After playing it over the last month or so, though, I realize the difference between city builder and logistics planner is a lot more distinct than I initially gave it credit for — and I've irreversibly become a logistics sicko.

The first thing you do in Cities: Skylines is build a road, and then you start earmarking plots of land for residential and commercial zoning. The biggest logistics puzzles you end up solving involve road improvements, or more accurately, managing traffic as your city gets bigger. It's a design and aesthetics challenge more than anything, though. In the struggle to make roads big enough to handle the needs of a growing city, keeping things from looking like a dystopian hellscape is more important than optimizing traffic flow. Well, for me it is anyway, but the game's comparatively lax management requirements let personal preference prevail over logistical needs.

Not so with Transport Fever 3. Here, you start with a small town and a burgeoning transport company. The first task is creating a small network of transport lines to support consumer travel and industrial growth. Then you encourage new industry by making more lines that connect resource deposits with processing facilities in, or near, the town. This small-but-important difference captured me instantly. Planning industries in Cities usually takes little foresight. Once you have the money, you just construct the necessary buildings as close to the resource as possible and make sure there's a road that can link them back to the main city for deliveries. Most resource nodes exist on map tiles outside your main metro area anyway, so you can just position any old road how you’d like and go on about your business. Or you can just ignore industry entirely and turn your town into a strip mall paradise.

But the goal in Transport Fever 3 is to grow the existing town, and it won't grow without industry. More people need more food, more places to work, and more ways to get to work, ideally on time and without being crammed on a tiny coach ill-adapted for the purpose. You find places suitable for residential building and industry like in Cities, but it all builds up based on roads and road types, which gives you more control over how everything develops and more challenges to solve. Opting for a country road influences the kind of residential areas that will populate along it and makes it easier to create neighborhood-like areas, for example, as opposed to having homes just pop up inconveniently on a standard road. Country roads aren't suitable for all types of travel, though, so you have to find alternative solutions to ensure the people living there have access to all the goods and transportation methods they need. Multiply those considerations for every new residential and commercial area, imagine designing a functional and aesthetically pleasing infrastructure network to accommodate them, and you've got an idea of how this game works.

There are a slew of other considerations to deal with, too. Terrain gradient determines the type of road you need — and now much it'll cost to maintain — so freight vehicles don't get stuck. Trains are wonderful for moving goods and people, but require special production chains in place to operate, which puts another burden on your local infrastructure. That's true for pretty much every transport improvement. It makes one area easier, but requires a lot more planning and work to keep others from spiraling out of control.

It's all comparatively easy to maintain when settlements are smaller, and in sandbox mode, the game doesn't punish you for keeping things small. Heck, you can have a bunch of mid-sized towns connected via water transport and airports if you want. As long as your business isn't bankrupt, you're winning. But once your town grows into a city with multiple districts, managing everything is a nested stack of challenges that just gets more complex as time passes.

Which, it turns out, is exactly what I want. I frequently find myself speeding up the simulation in Cities until some disaster or other requires my direct intervention. It's very passive once you get to a certain point, where improvements are additive and routine. Make a new district, zone it like the last one — or, how about this for excitement, get a bit wild and invert the zoning swatches — connect the roads, add more power and water, proceed like before. I certainly still enjoy designing a huge city and seeing the results of my planning pay off, but I miss the hands-on requirements of early city building. That hands-off feeling never disappears in Transport Fever 3. It's a never-ending puzzle that captured me and won't let me go, and I couldn't be happier about it.

60 years ago, the Leonid meteor shower unleashed one of the most spectacular meteor storms in recorded history. As the 2026 peak approaches, here's what skywatchers can expect.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

This year marks a rather auspicious anniversary for meteor watchers. Sixty years ago, one of the most stupendous Leonid meteor displays lit up the predawn skies over the central and western U.S. So, what will the 2026 Leonids bring us? And what might they bring in the years to come?

The Leonid meteor shower is one of the most celebrated of the year's annual displays of "shooting stars." The mere mention of the Leonids usually elicits mental images of a sky filled with shooting stars pouring down through the sky like rain. Such meteor storms have indeed occurred with the Leonids, such as in 1833 and 1966 when meteor rates of tens of thousands per hour were observed. In more recent years, most notably 1999, 2001 and 2002, lesser Leonid displays of up to "only" a few thousand meteors per hour took place.

Unfortunately, that's not likely to be the case this year. In fact, comparatively speaking, this is going to be a lean year for the Leonids chiefly because of the expected lack of any significant activity. Although the Leonids are one of the most famous of all the annual meteor displays, this year they're not likely to be very productive. It's probable they are going to be rather weak and there probably will be long stretches when not a single Leonid will be seen. I'll explain why in a moment. First, let's find out about this shower's lineage.

The Leonids are so named because the shower's radiant point, from where the meteors seem to fan out, is located within the constellation of Leo the Lion, from the backward question mark pattern of stars known as "The Sickle." The meteors are caused by the Tempel-Tuttle comet, which sweeps through the inner solar system every 33⅓ years. Each time the comet passes closest to the sun it leaves a "river of rubble" in its wake: a dense trail of dusty debris. A meteor storm becomes possible if the Earth were to score a direct hit on a fresh dust trail ejected by the comet over the past several centuries.

The "lion's share" (no pun intended) of comet dust can be found just ahead and trailing behind Tempel-Tuttle. That comet last swept through the inner solar system in 1998. That's why spectacular meteor showers were seen in 1999, 2001 and 2002, with declining numbers thereafter.

According to the International Meteor Organization's 2026 meteor shower calendar, the Leonids are expected to reach their regular maximum at 23:45 UTC (6:45 p.m. EST) on Nov. 17, with around 15 meteors per hour possible under ideal viewing conditions.

Other forecasts place the peak at slightly different times. Margaret Campbell-Brown and Peter Brown, meteor experts for the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada, predict that the peak of this year's Leonids will occur at 00:00 hours UTC on Nov. 18. Russian meteor expert Mikhail Maslov feels that the peak will come about a third of a day earlier, at 16:00 UTC on Nov. 17.

Several weaker encounters with older debris trails are also predicted between Nov. 17 and 19, although no major meteor storm is expected.

Back in September 2014, comet Tempel-Tuttle was as far from the sun as it can get: 1.84 billion miles (2.96 billion km). The comet has since spent the last dozen years slowly approaching the inner solar system, but it's still 1.11 billion miles (1.79 billion km) from the sun, which is still well beyond the orbit of Saturn.

In contrast, at the point in the comet's orbit where we will be passing by on Tuesday morning, there is nothing save for a scattered few particles; bits and pieces likely loosened from the comet's nucleus a millennium or two ago.

So, this year's Leonids are expected to show only low activity; "maybe" at best 10 to 15 Leonids per hour might be seen, but more likely hourly counts will be less than that. The moon will not pose a problem; it's at first quarter phase, but it will set before 11 p.m. local time Monday evening, leaving the rest of the night dark.

Watching a meteor shower consists of lying back, looking up at the sky... and waiting. Keep in mind that any local light pollution or obstructions like tall trees or buildings will further reduce your chances of making a meteor sighting. Leo does not start coming fully into view until the after-midnight hours, so that would be the best time to concentrate on looking for Leonids. As dawn is about to break at around 5 a.m., the Sickle will have climbed more than two-thirds of the way up from the southeast horizon to the point directly overhead (called the zenith).

Because the Leonids are moving along in their orbit around the sun in a direction opposite to that of Earth, they slam into our atmosphere nearly head-on, resulting in the fastest meteor velocities possible: 45 miles (72 km) per second. Such speeds tend to produce bright meteors, which leave long-lasting streaks or persistent trains in their wake. A mighty Leonid fireball can be quite spectacular, but such outstandingly bright meteors are likely to be very few and very far between this year (if any are seen at all).

It was a different story 60 years ago, in 1966, when anticipation ran high for a potentially heavy display of Leonid meteors. The previous year, comet Tempel-Tuttle swept around the sun and was accompanied later that year by an unexpected surge of Leonid activity, with brilliant meteors darting across the sky at one or two per minute as seen from Hawaii and Australia. The most sanguine prediction for 1966 came from Dr. Kenneth L. Franklin (1923-2007), chief scientist at New York's Hayden Planetarium. Dr. Franklin had carefully examined the orbit of comet Tempel-Tuttle and concluded that " . . . the position of the comet is roughly what it was in 1833. So, a dramatic shower like the one that occurred in that year may yet be in the offing for 1966."

Based on Dr. Franklin's prediction, a "meteor party" was planned for the peak night of the Leonids in Central Park. New Yorkers of all ages were invited to gather on the Great Lawn at midnight to watch for the shooting stars. Ten thousand people turned out, but unfortunately skies were hopelessly cloudy.

But across the central and western US, where skies were mostly clear, Leonids were falling at a rate described by many as "too numerous to count." One observer stationed north of Mission, Texas said that meteors falling in all directions gave the impression of a "gigantic umbrella," appearing to "waterfall" out of the head of Leo. Perhaps the best views were from California and Arizona. At the Table Mountain Observatory, near Wrightwood, California, one resident astronomer commented that he and a colleague " . . . watched a rain of meteors, turn into a hail of meteors and finally a storm of meteors, too numerous to count by 3:50 a.m. Pacific Time. Instinctively we sought to shield our upturned faces from imagined celestial debris." From 6,850-foot Kitt Peak in southern Arizona, thirteen amateur astronomers saw Leonids falling at the staggering rate of 40 per second (144,000 per hour)!

In 2005, French meteor expert Jeremie Vaubaillon published a technical paper with two colleagues in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics that forecast Leonid activity for years up to 2100. Sadly, in the year 2029, Jupiter is expected to throw Comet Tempel-Tuttle off its current path through space, making it all but impossible — at least through the beginning of the 22nd century — to see a repeat of the Great Leonid Storm of 1966. But Vaubaillon expects the next big Leonid outburst in the year 2034, when rates could reach up to 2,000 per hour.

That's just 8 years from now. Mark your calendars.

IndieWire Honors Film Celebration to Fête John Turturro, the ‘Artificial’ Cast, Los Javis, and More

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

IndieWire, the definitive outlet for creative independence in film and TV, announced on Friday, November 13 the lineup for the Winter 2026 edition of our IndieWire Honors event, featuring our largest lineup yet of film honorees. Curated and selected by IndieWire’s editorial team, IndieWire Honors is a celebration of the filmmakers, actors, and artisans key to crafting some of the most exciting cinematic works this year.

The event will take place on Thursday, December 3 in Los Angeles, starting with an intimate cocktail reception and awards ceremony. IndieWire will also begin rolling out exclusive editorial content, including honoree profiles, on Friday, November 27 through the lead-up to the awards night. Throughout the ceremony, into the next day, IndieWire will publish photos and exclusive social content from the event as well. The ceremony’s host will be announced at a later date. “It’s thrilling to be able to recognize the artists behind so many of this year’s most exciting and compelling films,” said Brent Lang, IndieWire’s editor-in-chief. “They dazzled us with their talent, inspired us with their willingness to take risks, and left an indelible mark on cinema. We are excited to celebrate their accomplishments.”

“It’s exciting to celebrate such an eclectic group of film talent for this year’s IndieWire Honors,” said James Israel, IndieWire’s SVP and Publisher. “We are also pleased to continue to celebrate top craft artisans in entertainment.”

The Winter 2026 IndieWire Honors honorees are:

The fact that he is known for being a frequent collaborator of both Spike Lee and the Coen brothers conveys just how expansive an actor Turturro is. The Brooklyn native is quintessentially New York, as most recently proven in Sony Pictures Classics’ acclaimed Sundance premiere “The Only Living Pickpocket in New York,” written and directed by Noah Segan. Yet, a filmmaker can still drop the performer into the corporate dystopia of sci-fi series “Severance,” or have him go on a Southern odyssey in “O Brother, Where Art Thou?,” and he just as well hits it out of the park.

With nearly five decades of film work under his belt, starting with an uncredited role in “Raging Bull,” Turturro manages to be one of the few veteran actors who continues to best himself year after year.

The pair of Madrid-based directors colloquially known as Los Javis are not shy about cluing the audience in on the shoulders upon which they stand on. They cast Penélope Cruz and Lola Dueñas, two muses of auteur Pedro Almodóvar, as the most important women within their epic adaptation of an unfinished play by Federico García Lorca, another one of Spain’s queer artist forefathers. That reverence is balanced by the confidence in their own eye for new talent, weaving together three different timelines led by three relative unknowns, including most notably, indie musician Guitarricadelafuente, who also contributed the poignant original song “La Nieve” to the project.

Made outside the Hollywood system and chosen to represent Spain in the Best International Feature Oscar race, the Netflix release “La Bola Negra” is a film of immense scale. If viewers knew nothing of the director duo before seeing the film, they come away with a crash course in everything they are about. Considering how they won both the Best Director Award at Cannes, and the coveted People’s Choice Award at TIFF, it seems to be an experience most cinephiles enjoy.

“Tallgrass” is the code word that unlocks what makes the creative collaboration between “Paper Tiger” writer-director James Gray and star Adam Driver such a feat. Though the Neon release, which premiered at Cannes, is their first time ever working together, the filmmaker imbues the two-time Oscar nominee with such a specific vision of what it feels like to realize one’s time has gone by. Though the film is another deeply personal work in the oeuvre of the Queens-born auteur, Driver adds a more broad portrait of a figure wrapped up in crime, slowly feeling the overwhelming situation constrict and constrict.

“Josephine” was not made to be an easy watch, but boasts the rare feat of winning both the Grand Jury Prize and Audience Award at the Sundance Film Festival. Written and directed by Araújo, and starring Gemma Chan and Mason Reeves as mother and daughter, the drama is a bold exploration of how little control parents have over their children being confronted by certain horrors in life that there’s no guarantee of avoiding.

An early release for new distributor Sumerian Pictures, “Josephine” has only felt more necessary as the year has gone on, and news stories like the Cornell rape case have come to the forefront. It is a conversation piece about a subject many wish to evade, that puts forth the effort to reach meaningful discourse.

The most bold artistic choice in “Artificial,” the latest film from prolific director Luca Guadagnino, is to not proffer any romance in becoming one of the Promethean figures. The repeated refrain of “Feel the AGI” rings as a benevolent threat by the time the two hours and 20 minutes of the Neon release are over. Garfield, Borisov, Barbaro, and Barinholtz, all four playing key figures in the genesis of OpenAI, play incredibly off of each other as tech titans forcing a smile as they convince their audience artificial technology is inevitable. Join or die. Their joint efforts to invoke a common sense of humanity would be funny if the reality of the situation weren’t so uncanny.

Re-teaming with her “Secret Sunshine” director Lee Chang-dong almost 20 years later, the “Possible Love” star is unmissable as a woman caught in a class divide between her blue collar, out-of-work husband, and the couple that hosts them as a means to interview them for a documentary. It is a role that she said uses “the whole entirety of her body” to push back against the weight of capitalism. The Netflix film chosen to represent South Korea in the Best International Feature category at the 2027 Oscars offers a feast of ideas, with Jeon’s layered turn as Mi-ok alone being enough fodder for many conversations to come.

All sorts of biopics enter theaters each year, but few task their leads with the difficult job of playing someone still actively in the public eye. In Peter Farrelly’s “I Play Rocky,” from Amazon MGM Studios, newcomer Ippolito deftly captures the distinctive look and sound of a young and hungry Sylvester Stallone. But what is more impressive than that form of transformation is how the 27-year-old actor captures the spirit of the unlikely star and scribe of the 1977 Best Picture winner. How some of his best moments come when he himself plays Rocky is a meta element we are still unpacking, but add it to the overwhelming evidence that Ippolito is one to watch.

There is a standout moment in Oscar winner Siân Heder’s “Being Heumann” in which Madeley, as trailblazing disability rights activist Judy Heumann, asks a well-meaning congressman what he means by calling her “inspiring.” “Inspired to do what?,” she says. It is a moment that clarifies that the film about Heumann leading the successful protest to get the government to sign Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act is not standard fare about a woman in a wheelchair overcoming odds, but a film about her refusing to let those odds overcome her.

Madeley embodies a fight against circumstances that applies to more than just the disability community. May casting directors take notice of the limitless potential she offers as part of any type of ensemble.

As the President and CEO of Laika Studios, Knight has spent over 20 years educating audiences on the artform of stop-motion animation. And still, in his latest film “Wildwood,” a fantasy epic taking place in the forests outside Portland, Oregon, Knight directs scenes that seem inexplicable. It is hard enough to animate one bird flying, using that technique, yet the Fathom Entertainment release sees whole flocks fly off toward a war between species. The film’s commentary on parents and children is as heartrending as the craft behind how the film was made is genuinely inspiring to filmmakers of all types.

Talk about legacy. Decades after his father, noted documentarian William Greaves, hosted a party for the surviving members of the Harlem Renaissance, “Once Upon a Time in Harlem” co-directed finished the film, one of the gems of Sundance 2026. To listen to Black luminaries from the ‘20s speak about how their work has endured into the ‘70s, with the dramatic irony of watching the film through a modern lens, is a singular cinematic experience. In a year where there is so much conversation around what pushes the documentary artform forward, the Neon release delivers both necessary context and innovation.

There is humor in having puppetry, one of the world’s oldest artforms, at the center of a conversation around innovation, but come on. Ortiz’s performance as Rocky in “Project Hail Mary,” supported by the work of visual effects team leaders Scanlan, Lambert, and Sarnowska, made us fall in love with a spidery alien rock that has no mouth or face. Puppetry’s forebearers would have still found that to be an insurmountable challenge. Hence, why the artist behind the fan favorite character opposite Ryan Gosling in the Amazon MGM Studios blockbuster deserves their flowers for helping viewers go beyond their earthly imaginations.
