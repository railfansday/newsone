How Aussie Bitcoiners can avoid 2027’s shock CGT hike: Part 1

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From setting up an SMSF or a company, to just leaving the country entirely, here are some strategies Australian Bitcoiners can employ to deal with 2027’s massive CGT hike.

The Australian government’s decision to scrap the 50% capital gains discount for all investments in the May budget came as a big shock for crypto owners. The new scheme effectively doubles the taxable component of a gain, minus a small discount for inflation.

But there are legal ways to minimize the amount of CGT you’ll have to pay — if you start prepping now.

Some Aussie crypto holders are taking drastic measures — like Finder founder and crypto king Fred Schebesta who is relocating to Singapore which offers 0% CGT, stating the tax changes were a “killer blow.”But there are less dramatic methods to reduce your CGT bills including setting up a self managed super fund (SMSF), a company to trade crypto at the company tax rate, or to just cross your fingers and hope the opposition will reverse the changes after the next election.

Since 1999, Aussie asset owners have only paid tax on half of their capital gain. From July 1, 2027 they’ll pay their full income tax rate — which means 30% CGT if your income and gains are between A$45,000 and $135,000, 37% for the portion between $135,000 and $190,000 and 47% above that. (All figures in this story are in Aussie dollars)

Adding insult to injury, low-income earners who aren’t on welfare like students and retirees will pay a minimum 30% CGT rate no matter how little the gain.

The only discount now available accounts for the effect of inflation on your cost basis. So if you buy a house for $1 million in 2027 and sell it for $1.5 million after 10 years, with inflation at 3%, you won’t have to pay tax on the $344,000 or so attributed to inflation.

“The challenge for cryptocurrency is that the CGT changes, indexation really helps slower growing assets not fast ones,” explains Andrea Yuen, Swyftx CEO.

So if you bought Bitcoin for $10,000 and sell it ten years later for $100,000, the inflation discount is only around $3,439. Under the old system you would pay tax on $45,000 of gains. Under the new system you’ll pay tax on $86,561 of gains.

Stockspot analyst Chris Bryki compared how ten different asset classes would fare under the new rules. “Crypto sits at the bottom because almost its entire return comes from capital growth,” he wrote in The Australian newspaper.

Here are a few different strategies to help crypto holders deal with the changes, ranging from little to no effort, to restructuring your entire life.

Any gain you’ve made before July 1 2027 will be taxed under the existing 50% discount scheme. So your best bet is to hope a raging bull market pushes the value of your portfolio as high as possible before that date.You should faithfully record the value of every crypto asset you have that day, as any gain from that value will be taxed at the higher rate.

Ben Simpson, founder of crypto advisory service Collective Shift, says Bitcoin’s performance since mid-year in a challenging macro environment bodes well for further gains before July.

But Simpson (who points out he’s not a financial advisor) adds, “I probably wouldn’t be betting the farm on the fact that we’re gonna have a huge bull run”

Yuen notes that prices aren’t within your control.  “A strategy isn’t a strategy if you’re just sort of hoping,” she says.

Both the official opposition The Coalition, and the insurgent opposition One Nation, have promised to reverse the changes. Crypto voters who feel strongly enough about this single issue, could vote for a change in government.However, the ruling ALP holds 94 out of 150 seats in the House of Representatives and will be difficult to dislodge from office. If the government does change — and the legislation can pass the Senate in time — it’s technically possible the changes could be reversed before anyone has to file taxes from July 1 2028.

But it’s probably more likely that any change to the system would occur after that date. Adrian Forza, director of Crypto Tax Australia, says this would make calculating gains very difficult.

Australian retirement funds are called superannuation funds, and once you are over 60 you can access your investments tax-free — even as they keep growing.Unfortunately none of the retail super funds allow clients to invest in crypto, so if you want to protect your crypto gains using this method, you’d have to set up an SMSF. About a third of Simpson’s high net worth clients invest in crypto via SMSFs.  “Buying Bitcoin and accumulating it there is a super solid idea,” he says, although he cautions against adding many altcoins. “You’re risking your retirement, which is an ultra long-term hold, on short-term altcoins,” he says.

Australian super fund AMP has invested in Bitcoin — but doesn’t allow its clients to do so.

The big drawback with SMSFs — explains the 27-year-old founder — is that younger crypto investors can’t access the money for decades.

Realistically then, it’s a strategy more suited to wealthier investors aged over 50, who have a shorter period to wait.

The government has also made running an SMSF a pain in the backside, with lots of compliance paperwork and audits, as well as a bunch of unavoidable fees that make it prohibitive for smaller balances. Annual costs typically range between $2,000 and $4,000, although online providers like Stake can reduce total annual costs to around $1,330, as long as you only buy Bitcoin or Ether ETFs.

You also have to sell any crypto and pay tax on it before you can rebuy it with your SMSF, and there are annual contribution limits to factor in, although Forza says you should be able to get almost $400,000 in there with a bit of creativity.

Keep an eye out in December for a detailed report on how to set up a crypto SMSF.

A strategy some billionaires employ to avoid paying tax is by borrowing against their assets, as repaying the loan often works out cheaper than paying tax on a sale.

Bitcoiners can now access a range of lenders, including BlockEarner, Veild and Swyftx, that let you employ a similar strategy by borrowing against your BTC as collateral.

“We like to bring that strategy to the masses, not just to billionaires,” laughs Yuen.

But it can be risky if you don’t have a billionaire’s deep pockets. Borrowers face high interest rates, relatively short loan periods (typically 12 months), and risk losing their Bitcoin if the market moves against them. You can’t borrow against the full value either — Swyftx has a loan-to-value ratio around 40%.”If your collateral drops below a certain amount, there will be a margin call,” she says.  “I would say it’s a bet on a price going up. I think you have to have a view that crypto will go up — or at least not go down so that you get margin-called... Otherwise, a crypto-backed loan wouldn’t make sense for you.”

Simpson doesn’t recommend this strategy due to the risks, but says it can pay off for some people. For example if you borrow at 10% interest, and Bitcoin compounds by 33% a year (as it has done in the past) you’d end up much better off than paying CGT.

But he warns it’s a leveraged bet that is best suited to sophisticated investors.

Bitcoin is down 28% over the past year, so anyone borrowing a year ago would have faced margin calls and possible liquidation as well as interest costs.

Borrowing against Bitcoin for a house deposit has become a more popular strategy. (BlockEarner)

Forza cautions that borrowing crypto can also be a CGT event in some circumstances, such as borrowing in DeFi. If you deposit ETH to Aave, it gets automatically swapped to WETH which  is a taxable event, and when you withdraw the ETH it’s another taxable event.

Borrowing from centralized lenders like BlockEarner or Swyftx is currently a grey area, and the ATO has not clarified if it’s a taxable event.”If you’re talking about [borrowing] ten grand fine. But if you’ve got three to five Bitcoin, I wouldn’t risk it because if it goes against you then you’re going to be paying tax.””Vield is the only one in Australia that’s got a private ruling that says their product is not a taxable event. No one else has got one.”Swftxy confirmed it has not got a private ruling and said clients should seek tax advice, while BlockEarner did not respond to a request for comment.

How Leaders Can Turn Cultural Misunderstandings Into Stronger Teams

At my company, our differing backgrounds and perspectives make us stronger, but they can also lead to friction. Here’s how I handle it.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

“I don’t like him. He’s rude.” That was the chatter I was hearing about our newest hire, an IT whiz from Ankara in my native Turkey.

Dismayed as I was to hear this assessment, I wasn’t totally surprised. The person who made it was American; exactly the sort of eternally upbeat, outgoing person the country’s reputation is built upon. Our new hire, on the other hand, was more introverted and subdued — he was polite, but not overly focused on pleasantries.

What was happening here wasn’t a difference in personality as much as culture. I experienced this myself when I first moved to the U.S. — people were so friendly, it was almost unnerving. It took me a while to realize the friendliness wasn’t fake. Nor was it the same thing as closeness. Americans will ask how you’re doing, mean it for the few seconds it takes to respond and move on. In Turkey, you don’t ask unless you have the time to actually hear the answer. It took time for me to understand this and adapt to it.

Jotform has offices across five countries; even within those offices, our teams are incredibly diverse. Our strength comes from our differing backgrounds and perspectives, but they can also lead to moments of friction, like the one with our new hire. As leaders, it’s incumbent on us to build a culture that leverages these differences rather than suffers from them. Here’s how.

Hiring is one of the areas I refuse to delegate completely — it’s just too important. By the time we’ve narrowed the field to a handful of candidates for a position, it’s clear that any one of them has the qualifications needed to do the job well. But when making a final hiring decision, I want to learn more about how they think.

One of the key qualities I always look for? Open-mindedness.

Open-minded people don’t assume their default way of thinking is automatically the right one. When something collides with their perspective, they don’t dismiss it — they want to know more.

To find this, I like to ask candidates to tell me about a time they were proven wrong. Do they get defensive and shift blame, or do they walk me through what changed their mind? The answer tells me whether they’ll bring that same flexibility to a teammate who does things differently than they expect.

There are several reasons that I have my teams work in-office, not the least of which is that remote workers tend to struggle more with isolation and stress than onsite workers, and experience lower overall well-being.

That’s in part because so much is lost when we interact only through screens. Nonverbal communication, like body language and tone of voice, make up the bulk of how we present ourselves to others, much of which get flattened out online. If you recall the feeling of emptiness and exhaustion that often accompanied a pandemic-era online happy hour or team-building exercise, you know what I’m talking about.

At my company, we have what we call Jotformers Week, in which employees from nearly two dozen countries gather together for several days of camaraderie. And because no one likes forced fun, we ask what would make the experience meaningful for participants, ensuring everyone has a say in how the time is spent.

These retreats build a sense of cohesion, filling in the gaps that time and distance tend to create. A tone that seemed cold over Slack gets clarified over dinner. A silence that may have seemed disagreement turns out to be the way someone gathers their thoughts. Our American team quickly found out that our new Turkish IT hire only seems stern; he actually has a quietly wicked sense of humor that regularly has the group in stitches.

Making time for in-person team building is an expense, to be sure. But having your employees feel genuinely connected, even across oceans? That’s priceless.

In the absence of information, people will make it up, the saying goes. Ambiguity tends to breed misunderstandings, which can quickly erode the culture you’ve worked so hard to create.

Don’t leave room for misinterpretation. Write down what your company stands for, and make sure everyone sees it. Reinforce it regularly in visible forums like company-wide newsletters and all-hands calls.

Also, be clear about expectations. At Jotform, I encourage employees to sign off on nights and weekends. When they take PTO, I tell them to delete Slack off their phones and truly take time to refresh and recharge. This way, no one has to spend their family vacation stressing about whether they’re expected to check in — they already have the answer, no interpretation or guessing needed.

Not only does this practice create a sense of psychological safety, it also closes the gap where cultural misunderstanding tends to take root. The rule is just the rule, the same for everyone reading it.

How I learned to love an obnoxious phone grip

Much like a great recipe, it started with a stick of butter.

This wasn’t real butter; it was a friend’s new phone grip, complemented by a phone case with bold stripes. Butter is in vogue now, or at least that’s what a 12-year-old told me a few months ago. This stick of butter was labeled “unsalty” and committed to the bit by including the appropriate tablespoon markings, just like the real thing. I was enamored. “Where did you get that?” I asked. It was a PopSocket-branded combo, and I found it so irresistible I ordered an equally eye-catching grip a few weeks later. After years of carrying buttoned-up, minimalist tech, I finally said, “To hell with it. I’m gonna have some fun.” I have zero regrets, and it seems I’m not alone in this journey.

Over the past few years I’ve been a vocal, occasionally regretful, member of Team No Phone Case. A case adds bulk and collects pocket lint. Why spend $800 on a piece of tech just to cover it up? If I must use a case, I want it to barely exist. This philosophy worked great with my iPhone 13 Mini, which is perfect as-is. Its size means it’s eminently grippable — no PopSocket necessary and it feels much more secure when I hold it. But phones are big now and are only getting bigger.

Since it’s just held on by a magnet, you can take it off whenever you want to present yourself as a serious grownup

An extended period using Google’s hefty Pixel 10 Pro Fold last year convinced me to adopt a magnetic grip just to use the thing more comfortably. At first, I gravitated toward the minimalist options like low-profile ring stands. But then I saw that stick of butter, and something clicked. A chunky grip is easy to hold. It’s fun. And since it’s just held on by a magnet, you can take it off whenever you want to present yourself as a serious grownup. Why not? I put in an order for a PopSocket shaped like a hot dog, and I was so excited when it arrived I accidentally pulled it apart.

This “why not have fun” ethos has clearly been part of Bailey Hikawa’s mission for the better part of a decade. She’s been making unconventional phone grips and cases out of her Los Angeles studio since 2018. Her designs incorporate input from the disabled community, aiming to offer an easier grip for people who hold their phone differently. But they can be appreciated by anyone who’s ever felt their phone slide out of their fingers. They’re also vibrant and bold. “I would like the phone case to feel like a friend or like a new stuffed animal,” she told The Verge in 2019. Earlier this year, Apple collaborated with Hikawa on a chunky, bright MagSafe grip. Her own online shop includes other grips made of flexible, clear urethane with spaghetti noodles or baby shrimp suspended inside. They’re handmade and the very definition of one-of-a-kind.

In more conventional territory, PopSocket has a surprising number of unusual grips and cases on offer. There’s a classic tomato pincushion, the food truck collection where I found my beloved hot dog, and an aptly named “Sugar Rush” collection. I asked PopSocket founder David Barnett whether I was alone in my pursuit of a bold grip. “We’re seeing more consumers embrace color, texture and personality in their phone accessories,” he told me over email. “I think people want their everyday essentials to feel a little less ordinary. Your phone goes everywhere with you, so why not have a little fun with it?”

I think phone makers are partly to blame here, too. Aside from your stray Cosmic Orange and anything Motorola is doing, phones mostly come in shades of Boring and Light Boring. Don’t even get us started on what passes for a purple phone these days. Maybe a radical reaction to this sameness was inevitable. Whatever the reason, you can find an awful lot of punchy, vibrant grips and cases without much effort. There are color-blocked “dopamine” cases all over TikTok and Amazon, viral phone cases that hold a tube of lip gloss, and OhSnap grips in colors called things like “hazmat honey” and “electric teal.”

Me and my ridiculous PopSocket are in good company. Or we will be — I left it in the office last time I was in New York and its absence has been bumming me out ever since. We’ll be reunited soon enough, but I guess if there’s one downside to being able to take a grip off your phone easily, it’s that you can accidentally leave it behind without much effort.

China Unveils Their Plan for a Crewed Moon Landing

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yesterday, on November 3rd, the deputy chief designer of China's crewed space program and chief designer of the lunar exploration program (Wang Xiang) unveiled an official statement detailing its plans for a crewed mission to the Moon. Under the proposed architecture, China will conduct two launches using the Long March 10 rocket, sending the Lanyue lunar lander and the Mengzhou crewed spaceship to the Moon, where they will rendezvous in orbit. Of the three taikonaut crew, two will then transfer from the spaceship to the lander and descend to the surface to conduct science operations.

The plans were outlined in a keynote speech Wang delivered at the 7th Manned Space Symposium, held from Oct. 31st to Nov.1st at Beihang University in Beijing. This year's theme was "Gathering Wisdom for the Galaxy: Digital and Intelligent Empowerment for High-Quality Development of Manned Spaceflight," and saw more than 800 experts and scholars from fields related to crewed spaceflight convene to discuss strategies, technologies, and the hurdles China's space program will face.

These plans echo the preliminary plan statements made at the 9th China (International) Commercial Aerospace Forum in July 2023. As Deputy chief engineer Zhang Hailian of the China Manned Space Engineering (CMSE) office said at the time, two Long March-10 rockets would launch the elements to the Moon separately. Zhang's slideshow also included a rendering of a lander depicted on a mural inside China's Manned Lunar Deep Exploration Project Office.

The CMSE unveiled the lander design to the world in September 2023 and officially named it Lanyue ("embracing the Moon") in early 2024. Since then, development of the Long March 10, Mengzhou spacecraft, and Lanyue lander has proceeded apace and reached several key milestones. This included a tethered test of the lander last year, which successfully simulated descent, guidance, control, and engine shutdown.

The Mengzhou spacecraft also successfully completed a series of zero-height abort and maximum dynamic pressure escape tests in June 2025 while the Long March-10 successfully completed a low-altitude demonstration and validation test earlier this year. These developmental milestones place China on track to complete a crewed lunar mission by 2030.

In an interview with China's state-controlled media company, CCTV, Wang said that their planning approach follows two principles and will consist of using different vehicle variants for different stages. To prepare for rendezvous in lunar orbit, China will use the Tiangong space station in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) to validate the rockets, lander and space capsule. An uncrewed flight mission of the Lanyue lander will also happen before a crew landing is attempted, which will gather critical environmental data on the luanr surface.

In addition, Wang highlighted how in preparation for crewed missions, the mission components will all be tested in an uncrewed state first:

We will use the near-Earth version of the Long March 10 to launch the near-Earth version of the Mengzhou spacecraft to perform space station missions in Low-Earth Orbit, validating the rocket's basic technologies and the critical re-entry and return phases of the spacecraft. Then we will use the lunar version of the Long March 10 to launch the lunar version of the Mengzhou spacecraft to execute the lunar landing mission. This is the validation process for the rocket: near-Earth first, then the Moon.

Artist's recreation of the two Chinese spacecraft that will put 'taikonauts' on the Moon. Credit: CSM

For the near-Earth phase of the plan, the Long March 10A (CZ 10A) will be used, a single stick variant with only two stages and no boosters. For the lunar mission, the standard, three-stage variant with additional boosters will be used, which has a payload capacity of 27 metric tons (~30 U.S. tons). This process is virtually identical to what NASA has been pursuing with its Artemis Program. Initially, NASA hoped to use the Block 2 variant of the Space Launch System (SLS) to deliver crew and payloads to the lunar surface (which has since been cancelled).

China's incremental "near-Earth first, then the Moon" approach is also very similar to the NASA's current launch plans. Before a crewed Orion spacecraft flew four astronauts around the Moon with Artemis II, NASA launched the uncrewed Artemis I capsule on a circumlunar flight to validate all the key systems. China's stated plans for an orbital rendezvous is also similar to NASA's revised plans for the Artemis III mission scheduled for next year, involving the seperate launch and an Orion spacecraft with a yet-to-be-determined Human Landing System (HLS).

"In an uncrewed state, we will validate many key technologies of the entire crewed process as possible, including launch, orbiting the Moon, docking with the Mengzhou spacecraft, landing and staying on the lunar surface, taking off again, and re-docking with the Mengzhou spacecraft," Wang added. "This is to ensure that the crewed flight is safe and reliable." Wang further indicated that the program has established system plans, technical standards and quality requirements under a single development framework.

China's long-term vision includes the creation of an International Lunar Research Station, the construction of which is currently planned to commence in 2031 using the super heavy-lift Long March 9 rockets. Roscosmo's involvement in the project is unclear at this time due to Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine and the way this has led to record lows in terms of annual launches and delays in the super-heavy Yenisei rocket. The details of this base are sitll somewhat vague, with the stated description claiming it will be located on the surface or in orbit.

Archaeologists in Ukraine have found a colorful belt buckle that may have been worn by a nomadic equestrian warrior 1,800 years ago on the fringes of the Roman Empire.

The belt buckle was unearthed by local residents in the Tsetsyn Forest in southeastern Ukraine and given to Antonii Moisei, a professor at Bukovinian State Medical University, according to a translated statement. The roughly square metal buckle, which is about 0.8 inches (2.1 centimeters) on each side, has teal, blue and yellow enamel decorations.

The style of the decoration and the metal construction place the creation of the buckle in the second to third centuries A.D., when this part of Ukraine was in the ancient province of Roman Dacia, according to Moisei.

The column that the Roman emperor Trajan erected in Rome following the empire's triumph in the Dacian Wars.

Under Emperor Trajan, Rome fought the Dacian Wars (A.D. 101-102 and 105-106) against the denizens of the Dacian kingdom and its leader, Decebalus. Roman legions destroyed Dacia and annexed it as a new province. Trajan celebrated his victory in Rome, complete with a famous column that depicts details of the Dacian Wars.

In addition to the buckle, Moisei collected a decorative belt fitting and three small, round metal objects that probably belonged to the same belt. Moisei created a reconstruction of what the belt might have looked like before it was lost in the forest nearly two millennia ago.

But it's unclear who lost the belt. One of Moisei's theories is that the belt belonged to a horse-riding Sarmatian warrior originally from the Black Sea region. The Sarmatians were a group of tribes that lived outside the borders of the Roman Empire, in what are now central Ukraine, southern Russia and the Balkans. While some Sarmatian warriors fought against the incursion of the Roman Empire, others fought with Rome as mercenaries.

The metal belt fragments are part of a Roman provincial craft tradition, according to the statement, and they may have been obtained through trade or as a trophy of war. But Moisei is still studying the artifacts to confirm whether they were Sarmatian. Either way, the military belt provides new evidence of Roman military contact on the outskirts of the empire, and future work could help experts understand how the Romans interacted with the local Dacians during wartime.

Nabrawind’s Crane-Free Wind Towers Face a Supplier Test

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Spain’s Nabrawind has developed a way to install exceptionally tall wind turbine towers without bringing an enormous crawler crane to the site. Its Nabralift system uses a tubular upper tower supported by a three-legged steel structure. Hydraulic equipment raises the assembled turbine while modular sections are inserted below it near ground level. The lifting frame can then be removed and used on another installation. This addresses genuine logistical and construction problems, particularly at remote sites where specialist cranes are expensive to transport and where wind can interrupt conventional lifting operations.

The company has progressed beyond demonstration-scale hardware. In September 2026, UL Solutions issued type certification for Envision’s 7.8 MW turbine on Nabrawind’s 188-meter tower. Fortescue is using 17 of those tower configurations at the 133 MW Nullagine wind project in Western Australia’s Pilbara region, where construction is underway. Nabrawind previously erected a 160-meter prototype in Spain in under two weeks and has supplied smaller commercial towers in Morocco and France.

The engineering benefits are credible. Nabrawind reports using roughly 20% less tower steel than comparable conventional tubular designs and reducing foundation concrete by as much as 80%, depending on the design and site. Those are supplier claims rather than independently established savings across all wind farms. The potential to avoid large cranes, simplify transport and raise turbines in windier conditions could also matter substantially to total installed cost and construction schedules. High hub heights only earn their keep when those costs are justified by the electricity generated over the project’s life.

The current deployment has an unusual commercial structure. Fortescue acquired Nabrawind in September 2025 and is building Nullagine to serve its own mining operations. Fortescue has the capital, internal electricity demand and industrial incentive to support a technology it owns. Successful installation in that context would be important evidence of construction performance, but it would not establish that an unrelated wind developer can finance and maintain the same system under ordinary third-party commercial arrangements.

Nabralift’s proprietary construction approach creates a procurement issue. Conventional tubular towers can be sourced from several qualified manufacturers, and experienced contractors can compete for much of their construction work. A developer selecting Nabralift currently depends on one supplier for the core tower system, specialist lifting knowledge and associated support. Wind turbine original-equipment manufacturers also create supplier dependencies, but concentrating another critical part of the project in a proprietary installation system requires its own contract and risk assessment.

A type certificate provides valuable technical assurance about the certified turbine-and-tower configuration. A lender or insurer still needs enforceable delivery schedules, long-term warranties, repair and maintenance provisions, clarity on replacement parts, and a workable remedy if the supplier’s capacity or commercial priorities change. These questions become more important when a wind farm must operate for 25 years or longer and there is no qualified alternative provider of the same system.

Nabrawind’s next important proof point is an independently developed and financed large wind farm that accepts those risks on commercial terms. Qualified installation partners, credible long-term service arrangements and greater competitive capacity could make its engineering advantages easier for developers to buy. The 188-meter tower has earned technical attention; its broader market depends on financing and procurement confidence catching up with the machinery.
