Sheep grazing in solar fields offers farmers economic, ecological gains

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two new research papers shed light on the growing practice of grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays. One demonstrates that, in the right numbers, sheep grazing increases populations of beneficial insects like bees and lady beetles compared to mowing around solar panels. The other research describes a decision-making support tool to help farmers profitably grow their solar grazing herds.

A team from the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (CALS) found that when two sheep per hectare graze around solar arrays, the abundance of wild bees, honey bees and lady beetles jumped by 38%, compared to a control plot that was mowed. The increase was driven primarily by an 83% increase in honey bees alone, according to the team’s findings in “Low solar-grazing densities support beneficial insects in agrivoltaic systems,” published Oct. 7 in Environmental Research Letters.

“Sheep grazing and beneficial insect habitat are two prominent conversation topics around solar development right now,” said Leana Zoller, the paper’s first author and a postdoctoral associate in natural resources in the Cornell CALS Ashley School of Global Development and the Environment. “But what’s been missing is research that treats them as parts of the same system. When we talk about sustainable solar development, integrating these discussions into a single framework is really helpful to farmers and community decision makers.”

For three years, Zoller and a team of entomologists, ecologists and animal and plant scientists tested various stocking rates – how many sheep per hectare are allowed to graze a certain plot of land – using Cornell’s teaching sheep grazing at the university-owned Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, New York. At four sheep per hectare, they found beneficial insect abundance was similar to the mowed control site, while vegetation management effectiveness was greater than at two sheep per hectare. At eight sheep per hectare, vegetation management was maximized and sheep flock health remained high, but honey bee abundance dropped by 45%.

“We zeroed in on stocking rates because that’s a concrete number that sheep farmers understand and work with every day, and we want this research to be useful,” said Steve Grodsky, paper senior author and assistant professor of natural resources in the Ashley School. “With solar grazing, people, energy, agricultural animals, plants and insects are all part of this ecosystem. We’re trying to understand the connections between those pieces so that when solar developers, municipalities looking at solar development, or farmers are faced with a decision, they can have scientific information about potential trade-offs and synergies.”

Grodsky is also assistant unit leader of the U.S. Geological Survey New York Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. Additional co-authors are sheep farmer and solar grazing consultant Nikola Kochendoerfer, Ph.D. ’20; Antonio DiTommaso, professor in the School of Integrative Plant Science; John Losey, professor of entomology; Scott McArt, Ph.D. ’12, associate professor of entomology; Paige Muñiz, formerly a technician in McArt’s lab; and Todd Ugine, Ph.D. ’04, research associate in Losey’s lab, all in CALS. Sarah Carson, director of Cornell’s Campus Sustainability Office, was key in coordinating site access between researchers and the solar company that manages the Cascadilla solar farm.

Another Cornell research team, from the Cornell SC Johnson College of Business, has developed a tool that helps farmers whose sheep graze in solar arrays to make better decisions about their operation. The team described the tool in “Flock Management for Solar Grazing,” which published Sept. 28 in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

After a chance encounter with Caleb Scott, Ithaca-based sheep farmer and founder of United Agrivoltaics, Vincent Slaugh, assistant professor at the Cornell Peter and Stephanie Nolan School of Hotel Administration and first author of the paper, began thinking through the economics and logistics of solar grazing. He recruited Linwei Xin, associate professor of operations research and information engineering, and Shane Henderson, Charles W. Lake, Jr. Professor in Productivity, both in the Cornell Duffield College of Engineering, to work on the topic.

Together, they developed a spreadsheet-based model that helps sheep farmers to optimize procurement and sales decisions, based on factors like target flock size, costs, anticipated revenues, and expected vegetation in particular solar sites; if a farmer doesn’t have enough sheep to keep the grass down, they’ll have to cut it some other way. It also helps solar developers understand growers’ needs and develop contracts that are more profitable and sustainable for all parties long-term. United Agrivoltaics has already incorporated the tool into its onboarding process.

Slaugh sees opportunity for U.S. shepherds to grow their operations – especially with solar contracts offering a steady source of income and animal feed. Current domestic sheep production for meat and wool doesn’t come close to meeting domestic demand; about half of all lamb and mutton consumed in the U.S. is imported, primarily from New Zealand and Australia.

“If we grow the sheep industry, will the American diet eventually include more lamb? Could that be a side effect of the solar energy revolution? I think the answer is very likely yes,” Slaugh said. “And then when that happens, we’d expect processors and supporting industries to come back, for banks to be willing to give more loans, and for supply chains to follow. All of that grows the economy, and rural economies in particular.”

Kochendoerfer, who grazes 700 ewes on solar installations in New York and Vermont, said that economy is already growing.

“I work with a lot of farms that are now scaling up because of solar,” Kochendoerfer said. “There is a new wave of sheep farmers who are capitalizing on this access to good land. With solar grazing, you get paid to graze as opposed to having to pay to graze. That leads to sustainable, meaningful economic income that these farmers can use to expand, build a barn, buy equipment or hire someone. It’s enabling a lot of folks to build sound, viable agricultural businesses.”

Funding for these research projects came from the Cornell Atkinson Center for Sustainability, the National Institute for Food and Agriculture and the National Science Foundation.

Krisy Gashler is a writer for the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences.

Microsoft fixes Windows activation as admins pick through Exchange outage wreckage

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Another unscheduled patch for IT teams already having quite enough fun with Redmond

Microsoft has released an out-of-band update to fix licensing and activation errors introduced by November's Windows security update.

The fix is available from the Microsoft Update Catalog for Windows Server 2025, 2022, and 2019, Windows 10 22H2 ESU, and Windows 10 Enterprise LTSC 2021 and 2019. Windows 11 23H2 users can obtain it from the Catalog or through Windows Update's Optional updates menu.

The "non-security OOB update" targets affected enterprise devices. Microsoft stated: "Certain Windows licensing and activation operations using the Software Licensing Management Tool (SLMGR) might fail with error 0x80041008 (Invalid parameter).

"This primarily affects organizations using slmgr.vbs commands or automated processes to configure Key Management Service (KMS) activation, install product keys, or manage Windows licensing."

Devices that are already activated will remain activated, Microsoft said.

Breaking the machinery customers use to activate paid-for software is a bad look for a Patch Tuesday release. Administrators may wonder how Microsoft's updates are tested when installing one means waiting for another to repair the damage.

Those same administrators may also have been fielding calls about last week's widespread Exchange Online outage, which Microsoft has partly attributed to a defect it introduced in July.

On top of that, Microsoft told administrators just days ago to consider unsupported Exchange Server 2016 and 2019 "permanently vulnerable." Exchange Online avoids that particular support deadline, but the recent outage was a reminder that handing over the servers doesn't make software defects disappear.

Forget "security by obscurity." Last week's approach was more like "security by unavailability."

Between getting Windows activated and keeping Exchange Online online, Microsoft gave administrators plenty to do. None of it was likely to feature in their original plans for the week.

Why Lucid Stock Tapped the Brakes Today

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The EV industry isn't expecting help from the federal government.

Lucid (LCID -6.21%) began the week on a rough note as shareholders sold the EV stock after several leaders of electric vehicle companies, including Lucid, told CNBC recently that they didn't expect a Democratic-controlled Congress to bring legislative help to the industry.

Lucid stock was already under pressure from disappointing third-quarter results reported last week, and the latest news certainly didn't improve investor sentiment.

Lucid's shares were down 6% today, as of 12:25 p.m. ET.

Investors' hope that a more EV-friendly Congress could help jump-start the stalling electric vehicle industry was quickly dashed when CNBC reported today that the CEOs of Lucid and Rivian aren't expecting Democrats in Congress to boost the industry.

Lucid CEO Silvio Napoli told the financial news site that he's planning for the worst, but hoping for the best. Adding, "Anything positive, we'll take it." And Rivian CEO RJ Scaringe said his company doesn't plan its future around who will be in charge in Congress, adding, "I think we're going to actually have a lot of challenges getting things done."

Lucid shareholders were likely disappointed that EV leadership appears to be quickly shutting down any hope of a more EV-friendly regulatory environment.

Lucid reported its third-quarter financial results last week, with sales of $350 million and a loss of $2.74 per share. Those results missed Wall Street's consensus estimates of $407 million in revenue and a loss of $2.28 per share for the quarter.

The company also said it would expand its credit line by $1.75 billion from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund to provide "additional financial flexibility" to fund operations through Q4 2027.

While the additional money is a help to Lucid, it's also a sign that Lucid continues to burn through its cash. Add to that the fact that EV automakers aren't expecting the federal government to give the industry a jolt through new legislative support, and it becomes clear why Lucid stock is tumbling today.

-Materie prime, sussidiarie, di consumo e merci: 10,4 milioni di euro (6,5 milioni nel 2024/2025);

-Costi per servizi: 30,5 milioni (36 milioni); Costi per godimento di beni di terzi: 8,1 milioni (6,7 milioni);

-Costi per il personale: 172,3 milioni (142,1 milioni), di cui 165,3 milioni per salari e stipendi (135,8 milioni);

-Ammortamenti e svalutazioni: 135 milioni (118,2 milioni);

-Variazione delle rimanenze: -800mila euro (-100mila euro);

-Oneri diversi di gestione: 17,7 milioni (6,2 milioni); Totale: 373,3 milioni (315,5 milioni).

A incidere maggiormente sull’aumento delle spese è stato il costo della squadra, anche in delle virtù operazioni sul mercato effettuate per soddisfare le richieste dell’ex allenatore Antonio Conte. Infatti, la voce salari è cresciuta dai 132,9 milioni dell’esercizio precedente ai 161,9 milioni contabilizzati al 30 giugno 2026. I soli compensi contrattuali dei calciatori hanno raggiunto quota 128,9 milioni di euro, ai quali si aggiungono i premi legati al conseguimento dei risultati sportivi, anch’essi in aumento.

Tuttavia, la crescita del fatturato ha permesso al Napoli di tornare a registrare un risultato economico positivo. La differenza tra il valore ei costi della produzione si è attestata intorno agli 11 milioni di euro, invertendo la tendenza negativa dell’esercizio precedente. Al netto della componente fiscale, il Napoli ha archiviato il bilancio al 30 giugno 2026 con un utile di circa 2,2 milioni di euro. Un risultato in netto miglioramento rispetto alla perdita di 21,4 milioni di euro registrata dodici mesi prima. Il club partenopeo torna quindi in utile dopo un solo anno caratterizzato da un risultato economico negativo, raggiungendo contestualmente il fatturato più elevato della propria storia.

Infine, sul documento contabile ci sono indicazioni significative anche sulla situazione patrimoniale e finanziaria della SSC Napoli. Il patrimonio netto si mantiene ampiamente positivo, attestandosi a circa 192,4 milioni di euro, mentre le disponibilità liquide ammontano a 172,9 milioni.

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
