Windows 7 gets a second wind with modern GPU support.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Windows 7 may have been retired years ago, but that hasn’t stopped enthusiasts from finding ways to bring modern hardware and features to Microsoft’s beloved operating system. A new community project is now giving Windows 7 the ability to support modern Nvidia, AMD, and Intel graphics cards, along with DirectX 12 and Vulkan 1.4 capabilities.

Developed by enthusiasts EAZY BLACK, kryptik, and VisualEhrmanntraut, the project backports WDDM 2.2 from Windows 10. Interestingly, Windows 7 originally launched with WDDM 1.1, while Microsoft introduced WDDM 2.0 with Windows 10 in 2015 and WDDM 2.2 with the Creators Update in 2017. Microsoft also introduced a limited version of DirectX 12 for Windows 7 in 2019; however, that implementation was only meant for developers to port select games to the older operating system.

According to a post by @TheBobPony on X, Nvidia's RTX 40 and RTX 50 series, AMD's Radeon RX 7000 and RX 9000 series, and select Intel Arc graphics cards are supported. Screenshots confirm that the backport enables GPUs to be recognized by Windows 7 as well as third-party apps like GPU-Z. It is also claimed that features such as ray tracing and DLSS also work on compatible hardware. Older GPUs, including Nvidia's RTX 30 series and AMD's Radeon RX 6000 series, may also benefit from the project. That said, actual feature support depends on the graphics card, driver implementation, and application.

For anyone who still prefers Windows 7 or relies on older software and hardware, this project is worth exploring, especially if you intend to extend the usefulness of the operating system. Users can also combine it with the VxKex compatibility layer, allowing newer Windows apps to run on older operating systems. You can grab the project files and modified drivers by heading to the W7Revived website.

There’s a reason why enthusiasts continue to keep Windows 7 alive. Released in 2009 as a successor to Windows Vista, it offered a familiar interface, solid performance, and a straightforward desktop experience. It became one of Microsoft’s most popular operating systems and remained a favorite among PC enthusiasts and everyday users long after the arrival of Windows 10.

In fact, Windows 7 still has some life left in it. We recently tested the aging operating system against Windows 11 on a modern PC running on an AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D, Radeon RX 6950 XT, and 64GB of DDR5 memory. The tests included eight DirectX 11 and two DirectX 9 titles, with Windows 7 delivering better performance in certain scenarios, particularly CPU-bound games, along with stronger 1% lows in some titles.

Crying too hard at the end of The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping to fully grasp those final scenes? It can happen to the best of us. Let’s break down the ending of Sunrise on the Reaping to make sure you didn’t miss anything, including some of the things that Suzanne Collins’ book explains in a tiiiiiny bit more detail.

It should come as no surprise that Haymitch wins the Hunger Games. You should know that going in, even if you haven’t read Sunrise on the Reaping. When we first met the character in The Hunger Games, he was Katniss and Peeta’s mentor because he was District 12’s only living victor.

What we didn’t totally know before the release of this book, and subsequent film, was that Haymitch teamed up with other recent victors like Beetee, Mags, and Wiress (as well as future gamemaker Plutarch Heavensbee) to try and take down the games from within. As we first learned that in Catching Fire, the flaws that Haymitch found in the arena contributed to his victory.

President Snow does not like to be beaten and finds creative ways to get revenge against those who double-cross him. We see that early on with Beetee’s son Ampert, who gets reaped to punish Beetee for how he won his games. We see it later with Peeta and Katniss. And Haymitch is allowed to return home at the expense of his mother, brother, and girlfriend. All three die horrifically as soon as he gets back. As many have said: Haymitch didn’t drink enough.

At the end of the film, we see how President Snow’s revenge against Haymitch’s community causes him to spiral fast. With his family and girlfriend dead, in addition to the PTSD from surviving the Hunger Games, he becomes inconsolable and addicted to sleeping syrup. It does not help that he is the only person living in Victor’s Village; because District 12’s only other victor, Lucy Gray Baird, went missing 40 years prior.

One day, Plutarch comes to visit Haymitch ahead of his victory tour to film an update for the Capitol News, and they have a chat. (In the book, this conversation happens during the tour.) He wants Haymitch to keep working with them to overthrow the Capitol. He refuses. “I am living proof the Capitol always wins,” Haymitch says. “You need someone different from me.”

Plutarch insists that they need someone exactly like him, actually. Even if it takes generations and they don’t get to see the results, they have to keep imagining a different future so that one day it comes true. It doesn’t make them heroes. It doesn’t even make them good people. But they’re trying.

Haymitch drifts away from two of his only friends, Burdock Everdeen and Asterid March. If you couldn’t tell from their names, or didn’t hear what they call their infant daughter, yes! Those are Katniss’ parents. (Also, yes! Burdock Everdeen is a member of the Covey. That’s why he sings one of Lucy Gray’s songs, "The Old Therebefore," at the funeral for District 12’s tributes. One day, he’ll change the course of history by teaching her “The Hanging Tree.”

The main reason that Haymitch cuts himself off is grief and guilt. He wants to be alone with his pain. Losing the people he was closest too makes him feel like he doesn’t deserve to be close to anyone, and maybe shouldn’t for their own protection. Movie protagonists are always thinking that, aren’t they? Like, okay Spider-Man! Whatever you say!

But the book reveals another, even more devastating reason: when baby Katniss grows up and starts wearing her hair in pigtail braids, she looks just like Haymitch’s “sweetheart” and first ally, Louella.

As we know from reading and watching The Hunger Games, the rebellion in Panem did eventually find the right victors to raise an army and end the games for good in District 12’s own Peeta Mellark and Katniss Everdeen. They were able to finally implement the plans that Beetee, Wiress, and the other victors had been testing since the second Quarter Quell, if not before.

Without even knowing it, Peeta and Katniss helped Haymitch keep his promise to Lenore Dove. So, of course, the story has to end with them. We see Haymitch, again played by Woody Harrelson, finally celebrating his birthday with no reapings. In Collin’s novel, Haymitch tells Katniss and Peeta about Lenore Dove in order to add her to a District 12 book of memories they’re putting together. Katniss gifts him goose eggs to hatch and raise. The film, however, takes a simpler approach, and just shows old man Haymitch greeting his former mentees before going to spend some alone time with the geese. He’s finally earned some peace.

Tom Hardy's Crime Thriller Hits 321 Million Minutes Watched on Streaming

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

MobLand has launched a brand-new era for modern gangsters on television. The series follows Harry Da Souza (Tom Hardy), the right-hand man and perpetual fixer for the London-based Harrigan crime family, led by Conrad (Pierce Brosnan), a brutal mob boss who reigns over the drug trade with terrifying violence. Even though Conrad is a frightening figure, his wife, Maeve (Helen Mirren), turns out to be even more ruthless.

Ritchie gives us a new look at London with the Harrigan family.

The Harrigans definitely keep it in the family, with sons Brandon (Daniel Betts) and Kevin (Paddy Considine), as well as Conrad's secret love child, Seraphina (Mandeep Dhillon), all competing to take over the head of the table. The family is constantly descending into dangerous territory, especially when a gang war breaks out in Season 1 between the Harrigan and Stevenson clans. Season 2, which kicked off in September, sees the Harrigans facing even more conflict, both from outside forces and their own internal fractures.

It's clear that MobLand is winning over audiences. The crime drama just hit a milestone of 321 million minutes watched on Paramount+ this year, and consistently lands in the top 10 most-watched streaming originals. Much of MobLand's success has to be attributed to the thrilling writing. The storylines are extremely fast-paced and action-packed, and with the backdrop of a treacherous criminal underworld, there's no shortage of high-stakes situations. MobLand also sets itself apart from other dramas by having morally ambiguous characters that you might find yourself rooting for, even when their actions are pretty abhorrent. Since no character is safe from death, the show is all the more bingeable.

Hardy leads MobLand's cast brilliantly as a man who has a strange set of ethics, making for a completely unique protagonist. Brosnan, in a role that's a far cry from his James Bond days, is harrowing as a man desperately trying to hold onto his power. But Mirren is on a whole other level as the Machiavellian matriarch of the Harrigans. Many of her actions are skin-crawling and cold-hearted, but Mirren ensures that Maeve is always endlessly fascinating to watch.

MobLand's supporting cast is every bit as worthy of mention. Joanne Froggatt plays Harry's aimless wife, Jan, who can't seem to understand how her life has become so entangled with a bunch of criminals. Lara Pulver, as Kevin's wife, Bella, nails the affectations of a bored wife who tries to use her position in the family to her advantage. Both Janet McTeer and Toby Jones have created memorable villains who use their intelligence, rather than brawn, to take down their enemies.

With Season 2 wrapping up this week, viewers who have invested in the show shouldn't worry — MobLand has already been renewed for a third season as well. As a crime thriller that thrives on chaos and danger, MobLand should continue its reign on streaming charts for a long time to come.

Boeing's Most Modern Engines Are Quieter, But Its Tricks Weren't Copied By Other Planemakers For One Main Reason

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One of the most distinctive features of the Boeing 787 Dreamliner is the unusual sawtooth pattern at the back of the engine nacelle. They're called 'chevrons', and their primary purpose is to reduce the aircraft's noise levels. The 787 wasn't only intended to be game-changing for airlines; it was also meant to be a major leap forward in noise reduction and the passenger experience. Boeing also incorporated chevrons into the engine nacelles of the 747-8 (which came out right before the 787) and the 737 MAX.

Boeing patented the chevron design that it implemented into these three aircraft, which is why other manufacturers have not incorporated this exact design. However, the patents for Boeing's chevrons have since expired, and Airbus has publicly rejected integrating chevrons into its contemporary aircraft during this period. Even more interestingly, Boeing has decided not to integrate chevrons into the upcoming 777X, reversing course after initially planning them during early development.

The main reason Boeing designed chevrons for the 787, 747-8, and 737 MAX is noise reduction. They smooth the mixing of hot exhaust air coming out of the engine core with the cooler bypass air flowing around the engine. By blending the air more smoothly, the engine produces less aerodynamic noise, allowing Boeing's newest airliners to more easily comply with increasingly strict noise regulations while providing a more comfortable experience for passengers.

Multiple aircraft types also feature chevrons, but other types incorporate them in the inner nozzle, and they are part of the engine rather than a design choice by the airframe. The 787, 747-8, and 737 MAX are the only types widely used today that feature chevrons on the nacelle (which are not actually a part of the engine itself), and they're one of the most distinctive features on modern Boeing airliners. In addition, the company also originally depicted the upcoming 777X with chevrons in concept renderings.

However, Airbus notably rejected the idea of using chevrons on the A320neo, A350, and A330neo, all of which entered service in the 2010s. In addition, the 777X will not feature chevrons, and there's no indication that either Boeing or Airbus plans to employ them in future aircraft projects. While the chevrons were an innovative new idea in the 2000s and early 2010s, when the 787, 747-8, and 737 MAX were being developed, they have largely been rendered obsolete today.

The vortices generated by the chevrons take energy away from the airflow coming out of the rear of the engine. Because they protrude into the airflow, they also increase drag. As such, it's estimated that chevrons may reduce thrust by up to 0.5% and lead to a slight increase in fuel burn. This is a small amount, but aircraft engineering and design is all about trade-offs to achieve an airframe's desired goals. For Boeing, this was initially worth it to reduce noise levels.

Airbus, however, did not view the slight noise reduction as worth the trade-off in fuel efficiency. Instead, its latest aircraft, like the A350, focus more heavily on acoustic treatments within the nacelle, such as sound-absorbing lining, to reduce noise emissions. Both are valid approaches, and they show how Airbus and Boeing pursue different aircraft designs to achieve largely the same goals: cost-effective, competitive airliners that the market wants. Both companies have made very different choices and have found success doing so.

(737 MAX 7, 737 MAX 8, 737 MAX 9, 737 MAX 10)

While the chevrons do slightly reduce effective thrust and add drag, they can allow Boeing to slightly reduce heavy sound-deadening in the fuselage. This reduces weight, which improves fuel efficiency. As with all aircraft engineering decisions, there are benefits and drawbacks to the chevrons. Boeing decided that the benefits outweighed the negatives and patented its design. Airbus opted to pursue a different direction rather than develop its own chevron design.

The battle between two aviation giants heats up as they strive for the quietest cabin experience.

The 777X does not have chevrons on its engine nacelles, a change made early in the aircraft's development after initial renderings were released with chevrons. Ultimately, operating costs are extremely important to airlines, and the chevrons' direct fuel-burn impact, while minor, is not ideal. The 777X, however, is Boeing's newest aircraft program, which means that it has years of experience from when the 787 first began development, designing technology to minimize the noise impact of its planes.

While Boeing's new approach to keeping noise levels low for the 777X has not been fully disclosed, the most likely explanation is more advanced acoustic treatments inside the engine nacelle, similar to what Airbus has implemented with the A350. Innovations likely make these solutions more effective at reducing noise while potentially weighing less. In turn, this means that Boeing no longer needs to implement chevrons, which work well at eliminating noise but slightly hurt fuel efficiency.

In addition to sound acoustics, the 777X's GE9X engines are truly enormous, with a fan diameter of 134 inches (340 centimeters) and a bypass ratio of 10:1. High bypass engines like these generate significantly less noise than turbofans with lower bypass ratios, as less air proportionally is being fed through the engine core. Until further advancements in chevron design reduce the fuel efficiency impact, it's unlikely that future airlines will incorporate them to reduce noise.

Airbus and Boeing design aircraft that achieve very similar results for airlines, but take very different approaches to get there. There are several main examples, such as how the fuselage is assembled on the 787 and A350. The Dreamliner's fuselage is made up of several 'barrels', which reduces weight and improves fuel efficiency. The A350, however, uses traditional panel construction, which is easier to fabricate and comes with lower maintenance costs. Both are valid approaches.

Airbus has tended to favor smaller passenger windows on its aircraft compared to contemporary Boeing aircraft. Smaller windows save weight, but Boeing bets that larger windows deliver a better passenger experience. Boeing designed the 787 and 777X with raked wingtips, which have an aerodynamic advantage on long-haul routes. The A350 uses blended winglets, which effectively increase the aircraft's wingspan while still allowing it to fit within standard airport gates.

In addition to these minor design changes, you also see differences in how they approach various aircraft projects. The 777X, for example, is a significant overhaul of the legacy 777 airframe, featuring a fuselage stretch, a brand-new carbon-composite wing, new cabin windows, a new interior, new engines, and a new cockpit. The A330neo, also an overhaul of a 1990s-era widebody, only features a new engine, a new winglet design, and a new interior, along with software enhancements.

In the 1960s, a Boeing 707 taking off could easily exceed 110 decibels. Today, a 787 taking off typically produces noise that is under 95 decibels. The decibel scale is logarithmic, meaning the 707 was more than four times as loud as the 787, even though the 787 has a maximum takeoff weight (MTOW) that can be nearly double that of the 707 and engines that are significantly more powerful. Admittedly, the 787 has only two engines instead of four, but it still represents a significant achievement in reducing aircraft noise levels.

Over the years, ICAO has set noise standards for aircraft, with deadlines for when aircraft are expected to comply with the new limitations. Other regulators, such as the FAA, may set their own standards that roughly align with ICAO's. FAA Stage Five noise standards require a cumulative reduction of seven decibels compared to Stage Four standards, which equals a cumulative reduction of 17 decibels compared to the older Stage Three standards.

The FAA's Stage Five standards, which took effect in 2017, only applied to new aircraft types submitted for review after the standards were implemented, and it did not require already-certified types to be in compliance (however, newer types like the 787 were already in compliance anyway). In the past, the FAA did require pre-existing types to meet newer standards, most notably when Stage Three standards took effect. This required manufacturers to develop 'hush kits' for types like the 727 to lower their noise emissions.

Beyond exterior noise, passengers have benefited from noise reductions, since a loud flight can be extremely fatiguing. More advanced acoustic nacelle treatments and innovations such as chevrons have allowed manufacturers to reduce heavy sound-deadening while still providing an overall quieter experience. As innovations in aircraft design continue, airliners will get quieter both outside and inside the cabin, while also becoming even more fuel-efficient.

It's impossible to talk about British murder mysteries without mentioning Broadchurch. Starring a post-Doctor Who David Tennant and a pre-Oscar-winning Olivia Colman, Broadchurch, which premiered on ITV in 2013, centers on the devastating murder of 11-year-old Danny Latimer (Oskar McNamara) in the titular English seaside town. A pillar of the community, local Detective Sergeant Ellie Miller (Colman) is prepared to lead the investigation, only for the job to be handed to the out-of-towner Detective Inspector Alec Hardy (Tennant).

A small-town killing and internal police politics may not sound like anything new, but Broadchurch season 1 took that familiar premise and turned it into something far more compelling, while Tennant and Colman's note-perfect performances elevated the series to masterpiece status. Unfortunately, Broadchurch's sophomore season was an undeniable step down in a few key ways.

Broadchurch season 1 is so spectacular because it takes an already riveting mystery and turns it into a complex, deep, and heartbreaking character study of a community in the throes of grief and how it fractures as a result. Rather than sticking Danny's family members and their fellow Broadchurch residents in neat procedural boxes like suspects, witnesses, etc., the show treats them as people reeling from loss and shows how easily close-knit environments can become pressure cookers when tragedy strikes.

The fact that Ellie's son was best friends with Danny adds another emotionally complicated layer to the story. Meanwhile, the killer reveal at the end of Broadchurch season 1 focuses less on the brilliant detectives who cracked the case, and more on how learning the answers doesn't always make things better, as deep-seated betrayal is a whole different beast than being left in the dark.

The 2010s were an intense time for TV with the advent of streaming, bringing incredibly acclaimed crime dramas to the table throughout the decade.

Unfortunately, the second chapter of the saga devolves into an awkwardly paced courtroom drama that sacrifices season 1's gritty realism for implausible theatrics. On top of that, Broadchurch season 2 introduces a new mystery that's not nearly as captivating as Danny Latimer's murder, making the sophomore installment feel scattered and unsatisfying. While it is mitigated by more excellent performances from Colman and Tennant, as well as supporting cast members like Jodie Whittaker, Charlotte Rampling, and Marianne Jean-Baptiste, season 2 is nowhere close to the perfection that is Broadchurch season 1.

Broadchurch's third and final season also falls short of season 1's magic, but it's a significant step up from season 2. Season 3 loses the unpopular courtroom storyline and shifts back to solely focusing on one case, the sexual assault of Trish Winterman (Julie Hesmondhalgh), while also finding closure for Danny's parents, Beth (Whittaker) and Mark (Andrew Buchan).

As a whole, when a TV show comes out of the gate swinging with a lightning-in-a-bottle first season, it can be incredibly difficult to sustain that momentum in subsequent installments, just as it can at times be impossible to recover from a dreaded sophomore slump. However, Broadchurch's ending came on the heels of a competent story that gave its characters fitting conclusions, righting the ship after the subpar season 2 and ensuring its legacy as one of Britain's best murder mystery shows remained intact.
