Italy squad: Five players to watch under Roberto Mancini this month including Chelsea, Como, AC Milan stars

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Oliver Thomas | 09 Nov 2026 20:30

Roberto Mancini has named his latest Italy squad ahead of two UEFA Nations League fixtures against France and Belgium during the November international break.

A place in the quarter-finals is up for grabs and the Azzurri currently sit second in League A Group 1, three points behind leaders France and only one point above Belgium in third.

Italy will welcome France to San Siro for their penultimate group fixture on Thursday, before travelling to Brussels for a potentially pivotal clash with Belgium on Sunday.

From breakout starlets making waves in Serie A and the Champions League to high-profile Premier League imports looking to cement their status, Mancini’s 32-man squad is a bold blend of elite experience and vibrant, emerging youth.

These two upcoming fixtures present the perfect stage for several Azzurri stars to stake their long-term claims in the senior side, and here, Sports Mole takes a closer look at five players to watch out for during this month’s international break.

AC Milan’s breakout starlet has taken to Serie A by storm after making a prolific start to the 2026-27 campaign, scoring eight goals in 13 appearances across all competitions.

The electrifying 20-year-old midfielder recently made history as the youngest Italian ever to score a hat-trick in a major European competition during a 4-0 Europa League win over Red Bull Salzburg.

Mancini’s decision to award Cisse his first senior call-up reflects a hunger to inject fresh, dynamic energy into the Azzurri attack.

"I have a lot of desire. I want to prove my qualities. I hope to have the chance, and being there is a source of great pride,” Cisse told reporters as he reacted to his maiden Azzurri call-up.

Cisse is certainly one to watch as he bids to translate his impressive club form and clinical tempo-changing ability onto the international stage against elite opposition.

At the age of just 19, attacking midfielder Mattia Liberali has enjoyed a lively start to life under Cesc Fabregas at Como following his €6m summer move from AC Milan.

Once compared to Phil Foden by Milan legend Alessandro Costacurta, Liberali marked his first and only Serie A start of the season with a goal in a 3-1 victory over Fiorentina last month.

The right-sided winger also made a notable impression as a second-half substitute in Como’s record-breaking 9-1 away triumph over Lens in the Champions League, scoring once and providing two assists during a 32-minute cameo.

Liberali’s exceptional technical ability, creativity and eye for a pass have prompted Mancini to fast-track him to the senior set-up, just a month after making his Under-21 debut for the Azzurri.

Having already tasted European success as a Under-17 European Championship winner with Italy, Liberali provides a fascinating creative weapon for the upcoming Nations League double-header.

“Liberali is a very good player with great quality,” Mancini told reporters on Monday. “He has made only a few Serie A appearances, but he did very well at youth level. All the young players here have impressed us and given us positive signs.”

Standing at an imposing 6ft 5in at the age of just 18, Udinese centre-back Matteo Palma has earned his first ever call-up to the senior Italy squad after Gianluca Mancini pulled out due to injury.

Born in Berlin, Palma previously represented Italy at the Under-15s level before playing for Germany's youth ranks up to the Under-18s, but he has now filed a switch back to the Azzurri.

Gifted with immense physical strength, excellent positioning and comfortable progression on the ball, Palma has stood out as a lone bright spark in a struggling Udinese side sitting rock bottom of the Serie A table, recently cementing his place with four consecutive 90-minute outings.

Palma has naturally drawn comparisons to Atalanta BC’s Giorgio Scalvini and Mancini believes that the teenager has the potential to “become a very good defender” for the national team in years to come.

Confronting heavyweights France and Belgium this month will be the ultimate test of his maturity and high tactical intelligence, should he be thrown into Mancini’s team straight away.

After being named the 2025-26 Serie A Defender of the Year on loan at Cagliari, Marco Palestra earned a high-profile £43m move from Atalanta to Chelsea this summer, but his start to life at Stamford Bridge has been disrupted by injury.

The 21-year-old was limited to just 43 minutes of action across Chelsea’s opening eight Premier League games, but he has since started the last two for Xabi Alonso’s side, including a 2-0 home win over Manchester United.

Blessed with an athletic physique and explosive pace, Palestra is comfortable operating as either a traditional full-back or a modern wing-back on either side of the defence, with his versatility being a key trait admired by Mancini.

Palestra made his senior international debut for Italy back in March and although he has only just recovered from injury, Mancini feels that the time is right to recall the youngster this month.

Not only will Palestra rival Michael Kayode for a place on the right side of defence, but he will also provide competition for both Federico Dimarco and Riccardo Calafiori on the left.

The most-capped player in Italy’s current squad is goalkeeper and captain Gianluigi Donnarumma, who is expected to earn his 88th and 89th caps against France and Belgium this month.

Despite the 27-year-old’s world-class status, recent scrutiny over a plethora of high-profile mistakes at club level with Man City has increased the pressure on the shot-stopper to deliver a flawless international break.

Donnarumma has been part of a Citizens side that has conceded 17 goals across their last eight matches in all competitions, while he has failed to keep a clean sheet with Italy in any of his four Nations League appearances.

Improvements between the sticks from Donnarumma will be essential if a transitioning Azzurri side hopes to keep France and Belgium quiet as well as advance to the quarter-finals.

However, failure to deliver two commanding performances will only intensify the debate over whether Donnarumma should be taken out of the firing line, opening the door for hungry rivals at both club and international level.

Best Actress Frontrunner Julianne Moore Nailed ‘The Debut’ by Singing, Styling Her Hair Like Liza Minnelli, and Avoiding Playing ‘Some Suffering Mom’

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“Hello, I’m Julie,” said a radiant Julianne Moore, holding out her hand as she popped out of the elevator at Middleburg, Virginia’s Salamander Resort. Her hair was red, her skin was white, her dress as yellow as gold. A few hours later after a rapturous Middleburg Film Festival reaction to “The Debut” (December 11), we talked in a hotel meeting room. The A24 crowdpleaser has been wowing audiences and critics since Telluride.

Jesse Eisenberg has done it again. His follow-up to “A Real Pain,” which won Kieran Culkin the Supporting Actor Oscar, could score Best Actress for Moore. The musical comedy has earned Moore raves on the festival circuit for her role as a sad suburban homemaker turned anxious aspiring actress in a New Jersey community theater. A sixth Oscar nomination is inevitable, and possibly a second Oscar win after “Still Alice” in 2015. It’s hard to believe she launched her career in 1988 as a regular on soap opera “As the World Turns.” Related Stories Rejected Twice by Sundance, ‘Mouse’ Is One of the Year’s Best Movies. Why Aren’t More People Talking About It? From Los Javis to Lord and Miller, Director Duos Are a High-Risk, High-Reward Prospect in the Oscar Race

Moore is the current Best Actress frontrunner, even though she has won before. Sometimes nabbing an Oscar is a disadvantage in the Oscar race. But not if the performance is undeniable: Katharine Hepburn holds the record with four, while nipping at her heels are three-time winners Meryl Streep, Frances McDormand, Jack Nicholson, and Sean Penn.

Eisenberg wrote a rich character for Moore, knowing what the veteran actress could do with the childlike housewife Mona who, in order to learn how to act, must find herself. The director and actress had worked together on “When You Finish Saving the World” (2022). “[‘The Debut‘] is about this woman who has been chewed up but not spit out,” said Eisenberg, quoting Mona’s signature song. “It’s my point of view through somebody I consider a muse. I was writing something about myself as a seven-year-old in 1990, but I couldn’t picture anybody else except her doing it.”

It helped that Moore had told Eisenberg exactly what she was looking for. “I said I had just done ‘Dear Evan Hansen,’ and I loved singing, and I would love to have another opportunity,” she said. “I told him that I would love to do more comedy, and I didn’t want to play some suffering mom.”

Eisenberg put all of this in the screenplay, “but in the most surprising and inventive way,” she said. “He’s such a specific writer. He’s so incredibly empathetic. He has a compassionate worldview. He understands creativity. All of that stuff went into this wonderful and delightful story. It’s joyful and life-affirming too.”

Moore didn’t know she was playing a seven-year-old version of Eisenberg until he started telling that to the press. “Then he was like, ‘This is my experience of being a kid and going to community theater.’ But I could sense that there was a personal point of view in it. I very much felt with Mona that she was on a subjective journey, and that he was inside of that. With Mona, I always knew he was looking through her.”

“The Debut” is told from her point of view, as she tries to talk the overbearing local community theater director Jerry (Paul Giamatti) into hiring her, an untrained actress. He does it because he has no one else. But first, he must teach her how to act.

Mona dresses like a schoolgirl, with skirts, blouses, cardigan sweaters. “She’s so contained, and the way she learns is so literal,” said Moore, “the way a baby learns, like, you make a face at a baby, the baby makes a face back, but they don’t have intention behind it. But they’re learning things in an exterior way, and then they match it with feeling later on. So everything she’s doing is a childlike way of learning.”

Mona goes from an invisible nonentity in her own home to someone who demands attention. “She’s a kid who has found friends for the first time,” said Eisenberg. “She’s the girl who transferred to the right school at seven years old, and then the first rehearsal happens, and she is told she is terrible, and she has no idea why. And it’s the girl who transferred to the right school and gets an F on the very first test, and then the rest of the semester is panicking, and all she’s seeing is everybody else get A’s, and she still has the F, and she doesn’t know what to do. And then the teacher pulls her aside and says, ‘You either show up, or you are out on your ass.’ And something switches in her, and she makes the decision: ‘I am going to show up.’ And she begins to turn into a monster.”

The theater troupe embraces Mona as she unfolds like a flower. “We’ve been like those theater kids cracking corny jokes and finding community,” said Moore. “It’s all done with so much affection. Jesse looks at that world with not a hint of malice.”

Giamatti’s director relentlessly pushes the newbie through her paces until they have a powerful confrontation on the empty stage where he forces her to break down. Eisenberg reduced the actors’ anxiety by placing the eight-page showstopper in the middle of the shoot: not the beginning, and not the end. On the first day he went over to Moore’s apartment to talk about the movie. He said, “I really don’t want you to worry about that scene. I don’t want you to worry about memorizing that because I know that as an actor, I would I lose my mind. I would just be drilling it all the time, and I wouldn’t be able to concentrate. We’re going to have a teleprompter on that day. So don’t worry about it.”

So Moore worked on it normally, she said, “like I worked on everything else in the movie. So when we got to it, I knew it. I didn’t need a teleprompter. He knew instinctively as a director and an actor that what scares us sometimes is just a volume of language. He didn’t want to freeze me up. And having a partner like Paul who was so awake and aware and generous made it a pleasure. We were enjoying it when we did shoot it.”

Moore and Giamatti had never worked together before. She once introduced herself to him in a bookstore in Montauk. “He was very shy,” she said. “He is the greatest scene partner. I didn’t expect him to be so generous. He’s so funny, and he’s so dear. He’s present, incredibly modest, and as a partner, so available. Paul and I would work on it occasionally. When we were doing that scene, the rhythm was so important in it. You throw something, he hits it right back. It was our sense together of keeping it aloft, keeping it moving, keeping the river flowing. It felt electric with the two of us.”

The big blowout happens early in the story. “In a lot of movies, that kind of set piece would happen later,” said Moore. “Instead, it’s like a discovery place. It’s where she starts to begin to unlock herself emotionally, but she doesn’t learn from it. She comes in the next day, and starts to rehearse. And her belief is that acting is size, that it’s just huge, right? That’s when she starts yelling. She thinks it’s about being loud, which then allows her to go on the next journey with Jerry, where he says, ‘Let’s talk about feeling and reality and intention.’ So she’s slowly putting these pieces together. But that’s the first moment where she attaches something outside of herself to her own emotion.”

The whole shoot was fast, as Eisenberg didn’t have time to muck about and often shot just one take of a scene. “She and Paul Giamatti are so good on take one,” said Eisenberg. “It’s embarrassing. This is a small budget movie. The first two days of our schedule, we filmed in one day. The first two days, which were big ensemble musical numbers, we filmed in one day because we were prepared as a crew, but the actors were perfect on take one.”

Moore didn’t feel rushed. “It doesn’t feel, working with Jesse, like you’re moving too fast,” she said. “Working in independent film, you’re always moving quickly because you need to. You don’t have the time. You don’t have the money. So you want to move through things quickly. But I never felt rushed with him. I never I never felt like I was leaving something, that I didn’t get to complete something.”

The challenge for Moore, as it is for every actor shooting out of order, was making her character changes seamless. Eisenberg started with the theater scenes, then shot her family, and then jumped into Mona’s increasing weirdness. “The hardest thing always is making sure that you’ve pitched things correctly,” Moore said. “You want to make sure that you’ve managed to match it to another scene. So it was always hard to figure out. That’s our job.”

Eisenberg wanted Mona to move from looking quite childlike in the beginning, and swiftly move through her evolving transformations. Moore had fun with the costumes, figuring out the crazy wigs, makeup, and outfits in the second half. “Jesse had written that Mona electrocutes herself and destroys her hair, and that she was going to get her hair cut,” said Moore, “but he hadn’t written in a color change. We were messing around with it and trying to figure out what to do with hair and makeup and wardrobe and we had this idea that by having her dye her hair dark and chop it off, I wanted to homage the great ladies of musical theater, Liza Minnelli and Chita Rivera and Patti LuPone. It’s a wonderful Easter egg for that character.”

Making everything easier for Moore was actor-turned-director Eisenberg, who’d tell her when to ‘raise the stakes’ or ‘go hotter.’ “He’s maybe the most collaborative person I’ve ever worked with my entire life,” said Moore. “He listens to everyone. He asks people for opinions. He’ll ask people for notes, in a way that I would be like, ‘you don’t have to do that. This is your movie. It’s your creation.’ But he does collate opinion in a way. He’s fascinating in his generosity.”

Needless to say, singing her centerpiece song (“This City May Have Chewed Me Up (But It Hasn’t Spit Me Out”) was nervous-making, given that Moore was surrounded by Broadway super-belters like Tony winners Danny Burstein and Bonnie Tillman. “These people have the voices of angels,” said Moore. “When I did sing that song for the first time, and they’re all sitting there watching me, I think they did feel worried for me because they have so much ease. The song that she sings is so beautiful. In the singing of the song, she’s attaching her own emotional experience suddenly to the song. She understands the song in the moment she’s singing that. Somehow she wants more, and she knows that this is an avenue for her to find that. And so it’s a journey back to herself: finding that creativity and connection and community brings her to an essential sense of self.”

If degree of difficulty counts with Oscar voters, Moore may have that advantage.

Now, amid this phase of his career, one of Depp’s most recent producers is getting real about the prospect of his comeback.

In 2023, Depp starred in the French historical drama film Jeanne du Barry, and the film sees him play the role of Louis XV. Nellee Holmes served as one of the executive producers on that film, and she recently spoke to CNN as part of a wide ranging story on Depp. Holmes was quite complimentary of the actor, calling him a “good human being.” In the producer’s estimation, Depp has forged connections with “people who trust him” and still believe in him. As for the quality of the leading man’s work, Holmes had this to say:

You cannot say anything that makes him bad. His work is his weapon.

In terms of Jeanne du Barry, the film generated headlines several years back due to being Depp’s first post-trial movie. Ultimately, the movie debuted at the Cannes Film Festival in May 2023, and Depp received a standing ovation. Despite that praise, Maïwenn would later claim Depp was “difficult” to work with due to creative differences and personal conduct.

Droplets carry most of the risk. The people most likely to catch the flu are those who share air and space with a sick person. Surfaces add a secondary path, one that runs through hands rather than through the air.

Some people infected with influenza never develop symptoms, yet they can still pass the virus to others. An infection like this carries no warning sign. Neither the infected person nor the people nearby know to take precautions.

This differs from the short window of spread just before symptoms appear. A person in that window will soon feel sick. An asymptomatic infection never reaches the sick stage at all. The person can carry and spread the virus without ever learning they had the flu.

Ordinary speech is one way this happens. Flu travels mainly in droplets. Talking releases them just as coughing and sneezing do. A person with no cough and no sneeze can still pass the virus on during a conversation, a meeting or a shared meal.

Asymptomatic spread also slips past the usual signals people use to decide whether to stay home. The CDC's guidance for returning to work or school centers on fever. Someone who never runs a fever has no trigger to stay home under that rule. They can carry on with their normal routine at the office, on public transit and at home with family.

Without symptoms, these infections also have no visible start or finish. Nothing tells the person when they began carrying the virus or when they stopped. That leaves no practical way for them to time their own precautions.

The protective habits that matter here are the ones that do not depend on how anyone feels. An annual flu vaccine lowers the chance of being infected at all. Regular handwashing and good ventilation in shared indoor spaces also work without anyone needing to notice a symptom first.

Masking when ill offers less help in this case. A person with no symptoms has no reason to put on a mask.

In a household where one person already has the flu, those everyday steps also protect relatives who feel fine but may be carrying the virus without knowing it.

Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, sold under the brand name Tamiflu, can shorten a bout of flu. They work best when a patient starts them within 48 hours of the first symptoms.

These drugs act against the influenza virus itself. That sets them apart from over-the-counter remedies that only ease aches, fever or congestion.

The 48-hour window is counted from the onset of symptoms, not from a diagnosis or a doctor's appointment. Someone who wakes up with a fever and body aches has started the clock that morning. Waiting several days to see whether the illness passes on its own uses up the period in which the medicine works best. Once symptoms have run for two full days, that period is over.

Anyone who suspects the flu and wants to consider an antiviral should contact a doctor, nurse or other clinician early in the illness. A call in the first day of symptoms leaves time to decide on treatment inside the window. The clinician can then judge whether an antiviral fits that patient's case.

Shortening an illness is not the same as switching off contagiousness. Oseltamivir can make a flu episode shorter. It does not end a person's ability to spread the virus at a fixed point.

Starting a course of Tamiflu does not mean a patient stops being contagious after a set number of doses or days. That holds whether the drug was started on the first day of symptoms or the second. No antiviral comes with a built-in date when the risk to others ends.

That distinction matters for anyone deciding when to rejoin coworkers, classmates or family. A patient who feels better sooner on an antiviral may still be carrying the virus. The prescription's start date is not a reliable guide to when it is safe to be around other people.

A cough and tiredness can hang on for a week or more after a flu fever has broken. Those leftover symptoms do not necessarily mean a person is still as contagious as they were in the first days of illness.

Recovery from influenza tends to unfold in two stages. The fever, chills and body aches usually fade first. The cough and the low energy often take longer to clear.

That gap can confuse coworkers and family members. Someone still coughing at their desk may look sick to colleagues, even after the period of heaviest viral spread has passed. The cough on its own is a sign of healing airways rather than a reliable gauge of how much virus remains.

The lingering cough reflects the damage the infection does to the airways. Influenza attacks the cells lining the nose, throat and lungs. After the immune system brings the virus under control, that lining still needs time to repair. Irritated airways can stay sensitive in the meantime. A dry, nagging cough is a common result.

Fatigue follows a similar pattern. The immune response that clears influenza also leaves many people drained. Sleep broken up by fever and coughing fits adds to the deficit. Energy often comes back in stages rather than all at once.

Some changes during the recovery phase call for medical attention. A fever or cough that improves and then returns or worsens can signal a complication. Bacterial pneumonia, sinus infections and ear infections are among the complications that can follow the flu.

Influenza can also make chronic conditions such as asthma and heart disease worse. For someone with asthma, a cough that grows harder to control during recovery is a reason to call a clinician.

Difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, and ongoing dizziness or confusion warrant prompt care. Those signs can point to a new problem rather than an ordinary slow recovery.

Without those warning signs, a leftover cough and fatigue generally fade with time as the airways heal and strength returns.

Five habits lower the chance of passing influenza to others during recovery. They are an annual flu vaccine for the people around you, regular handwashing, covering coughs and sneezes, wearing a mask while sick and keeping indoor air moving.

Each habit targets a different link in the chain of transmission. Together they cover the hands, the air and the people a sick person is most likely to be near.

Vaccination is the one step that works before anyone else gets sick. A new flu vaccine is produced each year because the strains in circulation change from season to season. Last season's shot may not match the viruses going around now. Household members and coworkers who are vaccinated are less likely to catch the virus and carry it further. For a family caring for someone with the flu, an up-to-date shot for everyone else adds a practical layer of protection.

Handwashing deals with the virus that ends up on hands after coughing, sneezing or blowing the nose. Soap and water remove it from the skin. An alcohol-based hand sanitizer is a workable substitute when a sink is not nearby. The moments that matter most are after using a tissue, before preparing food and before touching shared items such as remote controls, door handles or phones.

Covering coughs and sneezes keeps droplets out of the air and away from the people nearby. A tissue that goes straight into the trash does the job. The inside of the elbow is the fallback when no tissue is at hand. Coughing into bare hands moves the virus onto everything those hands touch next.

A well-fitting mask adds another barrier. Wearing one while sick reduces the droplets released when talking, coughing or sneezing. It is most useful in shared spaces — a kitchen, a car or the waiting room at a clinic or pharmacy.

Ventilation matters because droplets and smaller particles linger in still indoor air. Opening a window, running an exhaust fan or using an air purifier with a HEPA filter keeps air moving. Moving air dilutes what a sick person breathes out. Small, closed rooms hold the heaviest concentration.

Beyond those five habits, distance inside the home also helps. Where space allows, a sick person can sleep in a separate room and use a separate bathroom. Cups, utensils and towels are best kept to one person until recovery. These steps carry the most weight around people at higher risk of serious flu complications. That group includes infants, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a chronic health condition.
