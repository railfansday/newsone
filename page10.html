Officials Are Hunting for the Lake Tahoe Tree Killer

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A person remains at large after poisoning at least 28 mature pines in three separate incidents over four years (Photo: Rachid Dahnoun/Getty Images)

Authorities in Nevada are looking for a person who has been poisoning trees on the shores of Lake Tahoe. Since 2022, at least 28 pines have been harmed or destroyed, totaling $300,000 in damages, authorities said in a release.

Now, the Washoe County Sheriff’s Office (WCSO) and other local organizations are offering a $147,000 bounty to catch the culprit.

“We take these crimes very seriously and will utilize every available resource to identify and apprehend the person or persons responsible,” Sheriff Darin Balaam said in a statement. “The destruction of mature trees in the Tahoe Basin and the environmental damage caused by these deliberate acts are unacceptable.

In an email to Outside, WCSO spokesperson Rachel Piccinini said the agency is continuing to investigate the crimes, which could result in felony charges.

“Our office has received numerous tips from the community, and we continue to encourage anyone with information that may assist in this investigation to contact our Detective Division,” Piccinini said.

Outside requested further information about whether the incidents pose a threat to people or pets recreating in the area. We will update this article if we receive a response.

The tree poisonings have occurred at Burnt Cedar Beach, a lakefront recreation complex in Incline Village, Nevada, along Lake Tahoe’s north shore. It’s also about a ten-minute drive from the community-owned Diamond Peak Ski Resort. Roughly 9,000 people live in Incline Village, but the area is a tourism hotspot in both winter and summer.

A person remains at large after poisoning at least 28 mature pines in three separate incidents over four years. Authorities say they believe the same person damaged all the trees. The suspect harmed the first six in 2022 and another six in 2024.

Most recently, on July 24, 2026, deputies responded to Burnt Cedar Beach after reports of a strong odor near the base of a large pine tree. The smell matched previous tree poisonings at the property. On August 2, deputies found another tree with a substance around the base; at the time, a trail camera captured a bright light near the area at 2 A.M.

At least 16 trees were damaged in 2026 as a result.

In some instances, someone poured the toxin directly over the tree’s base. In others, someone drilled holes near the roots and poured the substance in them.

Authorities say they have yet to identify the contaminant or determine why someone targeted the trees. They have collected soil samples and submitted them for testing. They hope to identify the substance used and determine whether the perpetrator used the same one in each incident.

WCSO asks anyone with information to call (775) 328-3001 and reference case number 26-4225.

Cult Classic Spy Series Is Getting A Second Remake In 59 Years

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The British cult classic spy series The Prisoner will soon be remade for the second time.

The Prisoner debuted in 1967 with Patrick McGoohan playing an intelligence agent known as Number Six. He gets kidnapped and taken to an unusual location called The Village, where no one is called by their real names. Always monitored and under threat, the agent tries to figure out a way to escape and refuses to accept his new normal.

It's been almost 60 years since The Prisoner ended, but now a remake is on the horizon. THR reports that Amazon Studios is currently developing a new and modern twist on the short-lived series. Susan Montford, Don Murphy, Philippe Maigret and Lucienne Papon have signed on as executive producers, but a showrunner has not been selected yet.

Maigret and Paon work at ITV, while Montford and Murphy have their own production company called Angry Films. Murphy has produced every single live-action Transformers movie, as well as the animated flick Transformers One. He also worked with Montford on Real Steel, Shoot 'Em Up, While She Was Out and Vampire Academy.

There have been attempts in the past to create a movie based on The Prisoner, with big names like Christopher Nolan, Ridley Scott and Simon West all interested at various points, but nothing ever materialized. Now Amazon Studios is taking a stab at a new TV show.

ITV only aired 17 episodes of the original Prisoner back in 1967 and 1968, but it did receive a nomination for Best Dramatic Presentation at the Hugo Awards. McGoohan's co-stars included Angelo Muscat, Peter Swanwick, Denis Shaw and Fenella Fielding.

Reviews were overwhelmingly positive. The Prisoner, considered one of the greatest sci-fi fantasy TV shows of all time, currently holds a perfect score among critics on Rotten Tomatoes and a 93% with audiences. That strong reception wasn't enough to convince the network to renew the series, though.

It took 41 years after the end of The Prisoner for any remake to finally make it to air. The six-episode 2009 miniseries had a solid cast that included Ian McKellen, Jim Caviezel, Ruth Wilson, Jamie Campbell Bower, Hayley Atwell and Lennie James. McKellen and cinematographer Florian Hoffmeister even scored Emmy nominations.

Unfortunately, reviews weren't kind to the show, which has a very low 17% critic score and 41% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes.

Now a second remake is on the way from Amazon Studios. As it's still very early in the development process, no actors have been cast, and a production schedule isn't known at this time, though executives at the studio reportedly consider The Prisoner a priority.

Why The Flying Wing Never Became A Commercial Airliner

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jack Northrop envisioned a sleek, tailless future when he sent the YB-49 aloft on October 21, 1947, stretching a 172-foot (52.4 meters) wing across the desert sky without a conventional fuselage or vertical stabilizer. Promising lower drag and greater efficiency than traditional layouts, the experimental jet bomber nevertheless battled stability challenges before the second prototype crashed on June 5, 1948, killing all five crew members aboard and leading to the program winding down between 1949 and 1950.

Despite the allure of an all-wing silhouette, commercial aviation stuck with cylindrical fuselages and tailed configurations that offered predictable handling, straightforward cabin pressurization, and familiar airport compatibility. Now that digital flight controls and blended-wing concepts are bringing tailless aerodynamics back into the spotlight, what technical, operational, and commercial hurdles still stand between a flying wing concept and a viable passenger airliner?

Converted from two piston-engined YB-35 airframes, the YB-49 stretched to a 172-foot (52.4-meter) wingspan and measured 53 feet, 1 inch (16.2 meters) in length, trading propeller drives for eight Allison J35 turbojets buried inside the wing, according to The Little Aviation Museum. Removing a conventional fuselage and tail assembly allowed almost the entire planform to contribute to lift with reduced parasite drag, helping the prototype reach roughly 493 mph (793 km/h). At the same time, early turbojet fuel burn and internal volume tradeoffs tempered its range and payload flexibility relative to piston-powered designs.

Flying without a tall vertical tail meant the YB-49 had less natural stability in yaw and had delicate pitch and roll-yaw coupling, including dutch roll, during turbulent air, maneuvers, and simulated bombing runs. As noted by MiGFlug, four small vertical fins were fitted to aid directional damping, though in the late 1940s crews relied on mechanical linkages and human coordination rather than high-speed digital flight control computers to manage continuous stability corrections.

After its maiden flight on October 21, 1947, the program flew only two converted airframes before the second prototype crashed on June 5, 1948, claiming the lives of all five crew members on board. Production orders were canceled in 1949 as Air Force leadership shifted toward competing bomber programs, and formal flight testing concluded in March 1950 before the surviving airframes were scrapped.

For an aerodynamically unstable tailless wing to become a dependable aircraft and actually work, it requires replacing purely mechanical linkages with high-speed digital fly-by-wire flight-control computers. Motion sensors can continuously measure aircraft attitude and rate changes, feeding real-time data to flight computers that command rapid, micro-adjusted deflections across trailing-edge control surfaces. That closed-loop automation damps pitch, yaw, and dutch roll oscillations far faster and more consistently than manual pilot inputs alone could achieve in earlier eras.

Digital flight control design made the Northrop B-2 Spirit possible by allowing a pure flying wing to achieve low-observable radar signatures without needing a conventional vertical tail. Instead of using a large fin that would compromise stealth, the B-2 uses computer-coordinated elevons and split drag rudders to generate controlled yawing moments and maintain crisp handling across varying weights and flight regimes. Computing redundancy and sensor voting became foundational to keeping a naturally neutral or unstable wing profile predictable from takeoff through landing.

Decades later, the Northrop B-21 Raider extends that digital control lineage into a modern low-observable bomber program built on newer avionics and computing architectures. The B-2 and B-21 demonstrate that computerized flight controls conquered the core stability and control challenges of tailless military aircraft, but solving stability in a combat airframe did not automatically translate into an airline cabin, and how to make it a reality.

Flying wing bombers nearly entered service during WWII and soon after, however, they would become reality until the B-2 in the 1990s.

The pure flying wing has not necessarily been revived, but JetZero is advancing the Z4 as a blended wing body (BWB) concept designed to carry roughly 230 to 250 passengers by smoothly merging a wide, lifting center body into outer wings. The program has gained traction through a $235 million US Air Force award supporting full-scale demonstrator development alongside plans to establish manufacturing operations in North Carolina. With a full-scale demonstrator targeted to fly in late 2027, the roadmap aims for commercial entry into service in the early 2030s if technical and regulatory hurdles are cleared on schedule.

Commercial interest in the Z4 is very real, and carriers such as Japan Airlines and United Airlines have made agreements to support the development of the aircraft. For airlines evaluating next-generation single-aisle and midsize replacements, a BWB promises lower drag and reduced fuel burn per seat compared with conventional tube-and-wing layouts. Making those conditional fleet commitments become firm deliveries, however, depends on proving that the airframe can meet strict safety, manufacturing, and operational standards at scale.

Propulsion integration sits near the center of that verification effort, including a November 2026 report from Leeham News noting that the FAA is funding a study into top-mounted engine airflow for JetZero's design. Placing engines above the aft airframe can help shield noise, but maintaining clean airflow and stable engine operation across flight conditions remains a critical engineering focus ahead of commercial certification.

A cylindrical tube naturally resists internal cabin pressure by distributing hoop stress evenly around a circular cross-section, whereas a wide, flattened wing-body hull faces far higher bending and pressure-loading demands. A non-cylindrical cabin shell tends to bow outward when pressurized at cruise altitude, so to compensate, designers need to add structural reinforcement, internal bulkheads, or load-bearing frames that add mass and complexity.

Emergency evacuation rules further add to cabin design complexity, particularly the regulatory requirement to evacuate a full aircraft within 90 seconds using half of the available exits, covered in Simple Flying analysis. Unlike a narrow single- or twin-aisle tube where passengers move linearly toward well-marked doors, a wide blended-wing cabin needs carefully engineered pathways, clear signage, and balanced exit placement, so travelers seated deep inside the layout can reach exits without bottlenecks. Cabin tiering across premium and economy zones also needs to align with aisle geometry and exit capacity rather than treating interior floor space as an open hall.

On the ground, widebody wingspans around 213 feet (65 meters) must fit within existing terminal gate envelopes, taxiway clearances, and apron stand markings without forcing costly airport rebuilds. Even when a wide airframe clears wingspan limits at a standard widebody gate, its broader center section and altered fuselage contour can complicate jetbridge docking reach, de-icing access, and ground support vehicle positioning. Smooth ramp operations really depend on proving that turnaround tasks load and unload cleanly alongside conventional airliners.

Several companies, including Airbus, JetZero, and Boeing, are actively developing prototypes.

Within a wide, open cabin, the sensory rhythm of a flight changes compared with walking down a narrow, low-ceilinged aisle. Designers can shape the atmosphere through layered ambient lighting, tactile fabrics, and warmer material palettes that soften the industrial feel of long-haul travel and create distinct zones for rest, work, or socializing. When the interior feels less like a corridor and more like a curated living space, passenger stress often eases.

Sightlines and motion perception play a major role in whether that spaciousness feels inviting or disorienting. Travelers seated further outboard from the centerline can notice more pronounced vertical movement when the aircraft banks or hits chop, making smooth cabin damping and calm visual anchors especially valuable. At the same time, passengers placed in central rows away from outer walls miss natural views of the horizon, requiring thoughtful lighting cues or digital view panels to keep the cabin feeling open and grounded.

For airlines, an unconventional cabin layout offers a rare canvas for brand differentiation in a crowded market. Carriers can build an identity around quiet acoustics, signature color schemes, and tailored service rituals that signal innovation without feeling gimmicky. If passengers connect emotionally with the warmth and poise of the space, the cabin itself becomes part of the airline's promise, not something that needs to be explained to customers.

Pure flying wings remained absent from passenger service because winning aerodynamic efficiency on paper is only half the battle when weighed against structural pressure loads, emergency evacuation rules, airport compatibility, and airline economics. Digital flight controls solved the stability hurdles that haunted the YB-49 and paved the way for stealth bombers, but turning a wide, wing-shaped cabin into a profitable, certified airliner is no easy task.

Any manufacturer brave enough to try and make something like this has to solve practical constraints that circular fuselages and conventional tails handle with far less friction. Whether a blended wing body can actually fill that divide comes down to proving real-world reliability, passenger acceptance, and unit economics without turning every airport gate or cabin layout into a costly exception.

The next major checkpoints arrive with full-scale demonstrator flight testing targeted for late 2027 and potential commercial entry into service in the early 2030s. If JetZero or other ventures like Nautilus can validate aerodynamic performance, propulsion integration, and regulatory certification along that timeline, blended-wing designs could finally move from aviation curiosity to active airline fleets.

This Egg Wash Secret Makes the Prettiest Pie Crusts Every Time

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The secret to a beautifully golden Thanksgiving pie might be the simplest ingredient in your kitchen.

Thanksgiving pies have a way of bringing out the perfectionist in even the most relaxed bakers. Yet you might spend half an hour creating the perfect pie dough design, carefully crimping the edges or cutting little leaves from scraps of pastry, only to pull the finished pie from the oven and discover that your beautiful handiwork has browned unevenly. It’s a small disappointment in the grand scheme of things, but after all that effort, a little indignation is understandable.

An egg wash is supposed to give pastry a lovely golden color and delicate sheen, but getting it to cooperate isn’t always as simple as beating an egg and picking up a pastry brush. Fortunately, there’s an easy trick that makes a noticeable difference, and it comes courtesy of a familiar face in the baking world: Great British Baking Show judge Paul Hollywood.

Paul’s secret is simply to add a pinch of salt to the beaten egg. He lets it sit for about five minutes before brushing it onto pastry dough. In a BBC Food demonstration, Paul uses the technique to glaze scones, but it works just as well for giving pie crusts a beautiful, even finish.

Beat an egg with a small pinch of salt until they’re well combined, then set the mixture aside for a few minutes. Give it another quick stir before brushing a thin, even layer over the pastry. The amount of salt won’t affect the flavor, and there’s no reason to make any other adjustments to your favorite pie recipe.

The difference is subtle but useful. Instead of clinging stubbornly to your pastry brush or collecting in the little crevices of a crimped edge, the egg wash spreads more easily.

If you’ve ever tried brushing a plain beaten egg onto pastry, you’ve probably noticed that it can be surprisingly thick and stringy. That’s because the proteins in the egg give it structure—a nuisance if you need to spread it evenly. Adding salt loosens those proteins, making the egg wash thinner and more fluid after a few minutes. It glides over the pastry more smoothly, leaving a light, even coating rather than stringy puddles.

The egg itself is what gives pastry its golden color and glossy sheen. Salt doesn’t make the crust magically browner; it simply helps the egg wash do its job better. The result is a more consistent finish, particularly around the edges and decorative details where egg wash tends to collect.

A pinch of salt won’t turn a lopsided pie into a masterpiece, but it can help a well-made crust look its best. And after we’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time weaving strips of pastry into a lattice for a Thanksgiving pie, I think we’re entitled to a little help with the finishing touches.

Whether it's in good taste for a company as big as Rockstar to dunk on that kind of game is up to debate, but what's impressive is that it’s not a throwaway joke that’s on-screen for 60 seconds. The 16 Most Expensive Artworks Ever Sold at Auction

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Daniel Cassady, Alex Greenberger November 20, 2026 5:00pm

Auction records make for great headlines, like the 2026 banner sale of Gustav Klimt’s Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer (1914–16), which topped $236.4 million at Sotheby’s. There’s a thrill in watching numbers climb into the stratosphere, each sale another reminder that, in the art market, money has a way of bending reality. But the price of a painting has never been the most interesting thing about it.

Look at the list of the most expensive artworks ever sold at auction, and you’ll see more than just a parade of billionaires with good taste (or at least expensive taste). You’ll see a history of ideas—what mattered to the artists who made them, what obsessed the collectors who chased them, and how we decide, decades or centuries later, which works still hold power.

Yes, Salvator Mundi sold for $450 million, but is it really a Leonardo? Picasso’s Women of Algiers, Pollock’s Number 7A, 1948, and Warhol’s Shot Sage Blue Marilyn all shattered records, with the Pollock work shooting far beyond its $100 million estimate when it came to auction at Christie’s in May 2026 in a sale of works from the S. I. Newhouse collection. But these works’ real value has nothing to do with what someone was willing to pay on a given evening at Christie’s. The best art—the kind that shifts culture, lingers in your mind, and changes how you see the world—exists in a space money can’t touch.

But of course, once a work crosses a threshold, there’s something almost inevitable about the price spiral. With each record-breaking sale, the bar gets set higher, the stakes get sharper, and the frenzy deepens. The game has changed, but in a way, the auction houses are just as much a stage for the spectacle as for the art itself. Every price tag opens the door to another possibility. If one work can sell for $450 million, what’s to stop the next from reaching half a billion—or more? With so many players in the game, all vying for the ultimate cultural trophy, the numbers have a way of multiplying faster than the logic behind them.

And yet, the more those numbers rise, the further they drift from the essential thing that makes art so valuable: the art itself. High prices can make us think we’re in the presence of something transcendent, but it’s the work that holds the power, not the price. So, here’s the list. The numbers are staggering, but don’t get too caught up in them. The real story is in the art itself.

A version of this article was first published on November 19, 2025. It was last updated on November 20, 2026.

Sold for $119.9 million at Sotheby’s New York in May 2012

When it sold for $107 million at Sotheby’s New York, Munch’s pastel The Scream (1895), one of the four versions—two painted and two in pastel—that he made of the same subject, became the most expensive work ever to be sold at a public auction, stealing the trophy from Picasso’s 1932 Nude, Green Leaves and Bust, which sold at Christie’s in 2010 for $106.5 million.

At the time, it was the only version of The Scream still in private hands; its seller was Petter Olsen, a Norwegian businessman whose father, Thomas, was Munch’s friend and patron. The picture, which opened at $40 million, sparked a 12-minute bidding war, with wide-eyed potential buyers both in the salesroom and on the phones. According to the Wall Street Journal, its buyer was investor Leon Black, a trustee at the Museum of Modern Art, where The Scream later went on view.

After the sale Olsen, described by Art in America as “the wild-haired and bespectacled seller,” read a statement that tied the work to climate change and global warming: “The Scream for me shows the horrifying moment when man realizes his impact on nature and the irreversible changes that he has initiated, making the planet increasingly uninhabitable. . . . More death, less life. And very few lifeboats left as we go down.”

Sold for $121 million at Christie’s New York in November 2024

The “crown jewel” of the auction market in 2024 (as Christie’s so humbly put it) was perhaps the finest example of a subject that Magritte revisited more than 25 times throughout his career: an otherworldly scene showing a house on tree-lined street at night. But instead being enveloped in darkness, the sky above the trees is impossibly blue, with big, cottony clouds floating by. This 1954 version of Empire of Light, which sold for $121 million and came from the collection of designer and philanthropist Mica Ertegun, set a new record for Magritte’s work at auction.

After the sale, Christie’s chairman of 20th and 21st century art Alex Rotter said the house that evening had adopted a “masterpiece approach” to this sale. “In a market that is not so easy to maneuver, we thought that if we present the greatest works we can get … these are the best examples,” Rotter said, admitting that the auction had its share of lots that didn’t catch collectors’ attention. But “the works that we put all the emphasis behind really proved us right. They had multiple bidders and showed that a market based on individual taste is on the rise.”

Sold for $137.7 million at Christie’s New York in November 2022

Cézanne’s deliciously rich landscape of the French countryside and his beloved Mont Sainte-Victoire is the second of five works from the Paul Allen sale at Christie’s to make this list. When the hammer fell, Mont Sainte-Victoire (1888–90) doubled Cézanne’s previous auction high of $60.5 million, for a still life from the collection of John Hay Whitney.

Like Cézanne’s best works, Mont Sainte-Victoire breaks aspects of the world—in this case, a French mountain—into shapes shaded with color, as though they were three-dimensional geometrical forms. In that way, Cézanne paved the way for Cubist artists such as Pablo Picasso and George Braque, who would push his innovations even further toward abstraction.

Sold for $139.3 million at Sotheby’s New York in November 2023

Woman with a Watch (1932), one of Picasso’s many paintings of his mistress Marie-Thérèse Walter, became the second most expensive work by the artist ever sold at auction when it fetched $139 million at Sotheby’s in 2023. The canvas, from the collection of late philanthropist Emily Fisher Landau, drew intense bidding before selling to a phone bidder represented by Sotheby’s head of global fine art, Brooke Lampley.

Woman with a Watch had a rarefied provenance, changing hands just a few times since Picasso painted it in 1932. It was acquired by Landau in 1968 from Pace Gallery, which had purchased it from Switzerland’s Galerie Beyeler two years earlier.

Pictures of Walter typically go for top dollar. In 2010, Nude, Green Leaves and Bust—also painted in 1932—broke records at $106.5 million. Still, the most expensive Picasso of all time is a work that does not depict Walter. But more on that in a bit.

Sold for $140.8 million at Poly International Auction Co., Ltd., Beijing in December 2017

Qi Baishi’s Twelve Screens of Landscapes (1925) shattered records in 2017, selling for RMB 931.5 million ($140.8 million) at Beijing Poly Auction and making it the most expensive Chinese artwork ever sold at auction. The bidding war lasted over 20 minutes, with more than 60 bid increments before a Chinese collector secured the 12-panel piece.

Each 70-inch panel captures a sweeping vista from Qi’s travels across China. Originally displayed at his solo exhibition in 1954 and later featured in a memorial show in 1958, the set of panels was kept by Qi’s pupil, Xiuyi Guo, before resurfacing at auction.

A similar version of Twelve Screens of Landscapes, painted around 1932 as a gift to Kuomintang general Wang Zuanxu, resides in the Chongqing Museum. The set sold in 2017 was the only one in private hands at the time, making it a rarity.

Sold for $141.3 million at Christie’s New York in May 2015

Alberto Giacometti’s Pointing Man (1947) became the most expensive sculpture ever sold at auction when it fetched $141.3 million at Christie’s New York in May 2015. The life-size bronze, standing at more than five and a half feet tall, was sold by real estate magnate Sheldon Solow, surpassing Giacometti’s previous auction record of $104.3 million, which was set at Sotheby’s London five years earlier by Walking Man I (1960).

One of six casts made by the artist (not counting an additional artist’s proof), this particular Pointing Man had been held privately for 45 years before the sale. Four of those casts reside in major museums, including the Museum of Modern Art in New York and the Tate in London, which made this auction a prime opportunity for collectors. Similar sculptures by Giacometti depicting skeletal, elongated figures have likewise sold for big sums.

At the time, Christie’s head Pylkkänen called the piece “one of the finest works of art I have had the honor to handle.” Whoever bought the piece clearly agreed about its quality.

Sold for $142.4 million at Christie’s New York in November 2013

Francis Bacon’s Three Studies of Lucian Freud (1969) briefly became the most expensive artwork ever sold at auction when it sold for $142.4 million at Christie’s New York in November 2013. The triptych surpassed the previous record, held by Edvard Munch’s pastel The Scream, which sold for $119.9 million at Sotheby’s in 2012 (and which also appears on this list, above).

Bacon’s three-panel portrait—each part stands six and a half feet tall—depicts Bacon’s longtime friend and artistic rival, Lucian Freud, in a series of contorted poses. The piece had an unusual history: its panels were separated by a dealer in the 1970s and remained apart for 15 years before being reunited by a collector in 1989.

The work was expected to sell for over $85 million, but after a five-minute bidding war, it was secured by New York’s Acquavella Galleries. In a strategic move, Christie’s shifted the Bacon painting to the ninth lot of the evening to allow unsuccessful bidders a chance at other works—an approach that proved successful, contributing to the auction house’s record-breaking $691.6 million total for the night.

Shortly after the sale, the anonymous buyer lent the triptych to the Portland Art Museum for public viewing from December 2013 to March 2014.

Sold for $149 million at Christie’s New York in November 2022

In November 2022, an early 20th-century masterpiece by Georges Seurat, Models (Small Version) (1888), sold for a staggering $149 million at Christie’s New York. The painting, depicting three nude figures in Seurat’s signature pointillist style, was one of the rarest privately-owned works by the artist. It came from the collection of Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen and was expected to surpass $100 million at auction.

The bidding opened at $75 million, drawing competition from four bidders, including specialists representing clients in New York and Asia. After several minutes of heated back-and-forth, the work hammered at $130 million, with Christie’s Asia chairman securing the piece for a final total of $149 million, including fees. The sale set a new auction record for Seurat, shattering his previous high of $34 million, achieved in 2018 for The Harbor at Grandcamp(1885).

The last time Models (Small Version) appeared on the market was in 1970, when it fetched just over $1 million at Christie’s. Prior to Allen’s ownership, it had belonged to the influential collector John Quinn, a champion of modernist artists in the early 20th century.

Sold for $157.2 million at Sotheby’s New York in May 2018

Amedeo Modigliani’s Reclining Nude (on Her Left Side) (1917), one of the artist’s most celebrated nudes, sold for $157.2 million at Sotheby’s New York in 2018. The total price, including the buyer’s premium, followed a hammer bid of $139 million, which fell just short of its undisclosed estimate of $150 million. Despite this, the sale set a record for the most expensive artwork ever sold at Sotheby’s at the time.

The large-scale canvas—depicting a nude woman gazing over her shoulder at the viewer—was the star lot of the auction house’s Impressionist and Modern art sale. Bidding opened at $125 million with auctioneer Helena Newman presiding over the room. The winning bid came from a client on the phone with Simon Shaw, Sotheby’s co-head of Impressionist and Modern art worldwide, after a subdued and measured exchange.

The work belongs to Modigliani’s groundbreaking series of reclining nudes, painted between 1916 and 1919. When first exhibited in 1917 at Parisian dealer Berthe Weill’s gallery, these erotic paintings caused such a scandal that police shut down the show after just two days. At the time, only two drawings were sold.

While the sale price was extraordinary, it did not surpass Modigliani’s auction record, which remains with Reclining Nude (1917–18), sold at Christie’s in 2015 for $170.4 million to Chinese collector Liu Yiqian. Sotheby’s, however, positioned Reclining Nude (on Her Left Side) as a transformative work, with Simon Shaw stating, “There is the nude before Modigliani, and there is the nude after Modigliani.”

Sold for $170.4 million at Christie’s New York in November 2015

Amedeo Modigliani’s Reclining Nude (1917–18) shattered expectations at Christie’s New York in 2015, selling for $170.4 million, more than doubling the artist’s previous auction record of $70.7 million. The work, which carried an on-request estimate of over $100 million, became the second-most expensive painting ever sold at auction at the time.

The sale took place during Christie’s curated evening auction, “The Artist’s Muse,” which aimed to build buzz around 20th-century masterpieces. Despite a mixed night—with several major lots, including works by Willem de Kooning and Lucian Freud, failing to sell—the Modigliani nude electrified the room. Bidding was fierce, ultimately concluding with a Chinese collector securing the work via telephone, as revealed by Pylkkänen after the sale. (The collector was later revealed to be Chinese billionaire businessman Liu Yiqian.)

Reclining Nude is part of Modigliani’s celebrated series of reclining nudes, painted between 1916 and 1919. These works, revolutionary in their sensuality and bold in their approach to form, scandalized Paris when first exhibited at Berthe Weill’s gallery in 1917, leading to the show’s abrupt closure by police. Today, they are considered among the most important contributions to the modern nude tradition.

The sale of Reclining Nude was a high point in an otherwise uneven auction, which totaled $491.4 million—above its $440 million low estimate but falling far short of the $705.8 million brought in by a similar sale earlier that year. While other records were set that night—including for works by Gustave Courbet, Balthus, and Roy Lichtenstein—the Modigliani painting stood out as the undeniable star of the evening, solidifying the artist’s status as one of the most sought-after names in the art market.

Sold for $179.4 million at Christie’s New York in May 2015

Pablo Picasso’s Women of Algiers (Version ‘O’) (1955) set a new auction record in 2015 when it sold for $179.4 million at Christie’s New York, becoming the most expensive painting ever sold at auction at the time. The painting, the final and most intricate piece in Picasso’s 15-work “Women of Algiers” series (1954–55), had an on-request estimate of around $140 million but soared past that figure after deep bidding.

The competition for the masterpiece played out entirely over the phone, with then-Christie’s executives Loic Gouzer and Brett Gorvy representing rival bidders. Pylkkänen, serving as auctioneer, declared “Still many people in the game” as bidding climbed past $140 million. Eventually, Gorvy’s bidder emerged victorious with a hammer price of $160 million, drawing cheers from the audience.

The “Women of Algiers” series was inspired by Eugène Delacroix’s 1834 masterpiece of the same name. Created in response to the Nationalist uprising in Algeria against French colonial rule, the series reflects Picasso’s fascination with both European Orientalist art and the evolving political landscape of the time. The version here is considered the most elaborate and fully realized of the set.

The painting had a notable provenance, originally acquired by New York collectors Victor and Sally Ganz directly from legendary dealer Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler in 1956. It first appeared at auction in 1997, when it sold for $31.9 million—meaning its value increased nearly sixfold in less than two decades.

While Christie’s maintained confidentiality regarding the winning bidder, reports later suggested that the buyer was former Qatari Prime Minister Hamad bin Jassim bin Jaber Al Thani. The sale reinforced Picasso’s dominance in the art market, cementing Women of Algiers (Version ‘O’) as one of the defining auction moments of the decade.

Sold for $181.2 million at Christie’s New York in May 2026

This 11-foot-wide painting by Jackson Pollock came to auction at Christie’s with both a first-class provenance and remarkable historical cachet. Pollock painted the work in 1948, the year after he first devised his drip painting technique, in which the artist flung his paint at the canvas rather than applying it directly using his brush. The technique distanced Pollock’s hand from his painting, leaving the application of his paint to chance.

Before he owned Sotheby’s, businessman A. Alfred Taubman owned the painting, selling it to publishing magnate S. I. Newhouse in 2000. In 2026, nearly a decade after Newhouse’s death, the painting headed to sale at Christie’s with a $100 million estimate, poising it to break Pollock’s record. At the time of the Christie’s auction, the painting hadn’t been seen publicly since 1977.

The work ended up shooting far beyond its estimate and becoming one of the most expensive paintings ever sold. Bidding opened at $80 million, and following 60 bids, the painting hammered at $157 million. With fees, the total rose to $181.2 million.

On November 18, 2026, Vincent van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (1890), a picture of sunlit trees set against oncoming storm clouds, sold for $190.4 million (including fees) at Sotheby’s. The cover lot for the auction house’s Blaquier-Arrieta collection sale, which the house had billed as “legendary,” it set a new auction record for the Dutch painter. The reported buyer was dealer William Acquavella.

The artist’s previous record of $117.2 million was set at Christie’s in November 2022 when Orchard with Cypresses (1888), a landscape from the collection of Microsoft cofounder Paul Allen, sold for $102 million. The new result beat that by $73.2 million, a jump of 62.4 percent in just four years.

That the sale happened at all was a victory for Sotheby’s. Just days before the auction, descendants of Samuel Fischer, the German Jewish publisher who once owned the painting, challenged its sale, arguing that their family had been forced to part with it in Amsterdam in 1936 because of Nazi persecution. Sotheby’s disputed the claim, pointing to archival records showing the painting had sold for 68,000 Swiss francs, around $22,000 at the time, which it considered a fair market price. After several days of talks, the Blaquier-Arrieta family and the Fischer heirs reached an agreement, clearing the painting’s title.

Painted shortly after Van Gogh arrived at Auvers-sur-Oise in May 1890, Blossoming Chestnut Trees is among the 70-plus paintings (and more than 50 drawings) that the artist made there before taking his own life only two months later. It was also one of only eight Auvers landscapes still in private hands, according to research cited by the Art Newspaper.

The electric scene, with its thick brushwork, also hadn’t been seen in decades. It was last exhibited at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum in 1964 before Argentine collectors Carlos Pedro and Nelly Arrieta Blaquier, bought it in 1975—the kind of provenance and scarcity that fuels record-breaking prices like this one.—Daniel Cassady

Sold for $195 million at Christie’s New York in May 2022

Andy Warhol’s Shot Sage Blue Marilyn, one of the most iconic images in modern art, became the most expensive 20th-century artwork ever sold at auction when it fetched $195 million at Christie’s New York in 2022. The silkscreen portrait, based on a publicity still of Marilyn Monroe from the 1953 film Niagara, is part of Warhol’s famous “Shot Marilyn” series—named after a 1964 incident when performance artist Dorothy Podber fired a gun at a stack of Warhol’s canvases.

The work was the star lot of the evening, drawing competition from four bidders, including three on the phone and one in the room. Art dealer Larry Gagosian, bidding in person, ultimately secured the painting with a hammer price of $170 million, well below its $200 million estimate.

The “Shot Marilyn” works are among Warhol’s most coveted pieces, with only five versions in existence. This particular portrait had been in a private collection for five decades, coming from the estate of Swiss dealers Thomas and Doris Ammann. With no financial guarantee, its sale marked a major moment for both the Warhol market and the broader contemporary art market.

In surpassing the $179.4 million record set by Picasso’s Women of Algiers (Version ‘O’) (1955) in 2015, Shot Sage Blue Marilyn reaffirmed Warhol’s place at the pinnacle of modern art valuations. While some experts saw the sale as a watershed moment, others noted that privately brokered transactions for blue-chip artworks have long exceeded the $200 million mark.

Gustav Klimt’s Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer (1914–16) became the second-most expensive artwork ever sold at auction when it achieved $236.4 million at Sotheby’s in November 2025, surpassing Klimt’s previous records and setting a new high for any work of modern art. The full-length portrait, one of only two named Klimt commissions in private hands, was the undisputed centerpiece of the sale of the Leonard A. Lauder Collection.

Bidding opened at $130 million and unfolded over 20 tense minutes, narrowing to two phone bidders represented by Sotheby’s specialists David Galperin and Julian Dawes. Dawes’s bidder ultimately won with a hammer price of $205 million, prompting applause in the salesroom and marking the most expensive lot ever handled by Sotheby’s. The result vaulted past Picasso’s Les Femmes d’Alger (Version ‘O’), which had held the record for a modern artwork at $179.4 million since 2015. Andy Warhol’s Shot Sage Blue Marilyn, meanwhile, made global headlines when it skirted the $200 million mark in 2022.

The painting was commissioned by one of Klimt’s most important patron families, survived wartime confiscation, and was restituted in 1948 before entering Lauder’s collection in the 1980s. Estimated in excess of $150 million, it had been widely expected to challenge the artist’s previous high. Instead, it rewrote the record books—cementing Klimt as one of the few early-modern painters consistently capable of commanding nine-figure prices in today’s market, and potentially opening the door for even more masterpieces to break the $200 million barrier.

Sold for $450.3 million at Christie’s New York in November 2017

In a landmark moment in the art world, Leonardo da Vinci’s Salvator Mundi (c. 1500) set a new record for the most expensive artwork ever sold at auction, fetching an astonishing $450.3 million at Christie’s in New York in November 2017. The sale, which took place in a packed salesroom at Christie’s Rockefeller Center headquarters, was driven by an intense 19-minute bidding war.

Bidding began in earnest at $100 million—which was also the guarantee—and escalated rapidly from there, with cheers and applause breaking out as the painting crossed the $200 million threshold. Pylkkanen, again serving as auctioneer, skillfully kept the crowd engaged as bids climbed incrementally in the final moments. At one point, the bidding reached $350 million, before finally settling at $400 million, with an additional $50 million in fees for the buyer.

The Salvator Mundi’s journey to the auction block was filled with intrigue, including debates over its authenticity and a controversial restoration by conservator Dianne Modestini. Despite these uncertainties, the painting’s remarkable backstory and its connection to Leonardo da Vinci—whether authentic or not—captivated buyers. The work had once sold for just $1,175 in 2005, when it was attributed to a follower of Leonardo, but was later restored and re-attributed to the master himself.

The sale marked a culmination of a carefully orchestrated marketing campaign by Christie’s, which included a global tour and celebrity endorsements, such as Patti Smith and Leonardo DiCaprio. The Salvator Mundi’s new auction price far eclipsed the previous record-holder, Pablo Picasso’s Women of Algiers (Version O) (1955), which had sold for $179 million earlier that year.

This sale marked not only a record-breaking moment for the auction house but also highlighted the increasingly influential role of market value in the art world, where the price tag may overshadow the painting’s historical and artistic significance.

The buyer of Salvator Mundi was revealed a month after to the sale to be Saudi Arabian crown prince Mohammed Bin Salman, with quiet plans for it to eventually go on view in Riyadh.

Why The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping Is The Darkest Movie In The Series

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The following post contains spoilers for "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping."

It's rare for a franchise to get better after 4 or 5 movies, especially when they are prequels that explore questions no one needed answered. It didn't work for "Star Wars," it didn't work for "Harry Potter," and it shouldn't work for "Hunger Games." At best, these prequels just introduced characters that don't matter in the large scheme of things because everything they do or accomplish gets erased in the original story, at worst they try so hard to answer questions no one cares about that it ends up tarnishing the original work.

And yet, when it comes to "Hunger Games," not only has the franchise improved with time, but the two spin-off prequels are arguably better than even the original series. The latest film, "Sunrise on the Reaping" deepens what came before, while introducing new characters that are as engaging as those we know and add to the larger worldbuilding of Panem. Or, as /Film's review puts it, "Beyond the shock value, this story remains human, emotional, and gripping from beginning to end."

Even if this movie doesn't have the compelling romance that was central to "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes," it delivers a harrowing tale of rebellion. What's more, this is somehow the darkest movie in the "Hunger Games" series, and it's not even close. Not just in terms of deaths — though there are some truly brutal deaths in the movie (which are somehow tamer than the book!) — but also in the overall tone the movie explores. This is the "Hunger Games" equivalent of "Andor" or "Rogue One," a bleak tragedy about one man who dared stand up against a tyrannical government and paid for it with everything he had.

The "Hunger Games" movies are full of death and suffering; it's part of the game. But "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes it to another level. Though it takes a long while before the nearly 3-hour runtime gets to the actual games, there's a feeling of hopelessness from the first moment. Early on, a kid gets shot for trying to escape the Reaping. Later, on the day of the opening ceremony, the youngest tribute from District 12, Loella, gets brutally killed in a chariot accident.

But it's not just the fact that there are plenty of deaths, or that they're horrible to watch. Sure, seeing poor Ampert get completely mauled by a swarm of cute forest animals and Maysilee Donner getting gored to death by birds is harrowing. The Capitol doesn't just kill children; it tortures them and pulls them apart and makes their parents watch.

Indeed, more than the deaths themselves, it's the meanness around them that makes this movie stand out. When Loella dies, it isn't a murder done by another tribute trying to get an advantage. Instead, it's an accident caused by Capitol audiences throwing flares at the parade circuit knowing full well it will cause the chariot horses to go berserk, because they want to see the tributes suffer. Compared to every other "Hunger Games" movie, the tributes are not only livestock sent to slaughter, or pawns in a political game. Here, they are subjects of absolute torture, playthings to be slowly toyed with and brutalized before getting discarded for the pleasure of others.

We especially see this in the presence of Ampert, whom we learn is only in the games as a punishment for his father Beetee's rebellious activities. Beetee is forced to mentor his own son and then watch him die.

Throughout "Sunrise on the Reaping," there is a sense of hopelessness and inevitability. We know that, eventually, the Hunger Games end and the Capitol falls — but the characters in the movie don't know that. At every turn, Haymitch and his allies see their every effort get squashed and mocked. Their attempts at leveling the playfield just a little bit are met with overwhelming force. Even before the games begin, Haymitch knows he'll never get to go back home because President Snow has personally seen to it that Haymitch dies for his insubordination during the opening ceremony.

Then we get to the third act, where the movie just becomes a cruel feel-bad experience, as Plutarch Heavensbee convinces Snow to let Haymitch survive to prolong his suffering. Haymitch is forced to watch Maysilee die brutally at the hands of waterbird mutts while he is virtually unscathed. Then, even after he wins the games, Haymitch discovers that the televised games were heavily edited to make him look selfish and cowardly, a traitor to every one of his allies, who won only because he was lucky. As if that wasn't enough, Haymitch has to see his family and his girlfriend die horrible deaths after he goes back home from the death games, in what's without a doubt the most nihilistic ending in the franchise.

It makes sense, however. "Sunrise on the Reaping" takes place at a pivotal moment in the "Hunger Games" timeline, at the height of President Snow's power. We are far removed from the games being almost canceled due to lack of interest like in the previous movie, but also far removed from the simmering rebellion of the original movies. Here, Snow gets away with everything, and he relishes in the cruelty of his games.
