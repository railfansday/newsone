Brazil electoral court fines Meta, sees no deliberate election bias

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The fine came after President Lula's coalition filed a complaint. The court said there was no evidence of intent to influence the election. Meta said it will defend itself against "false allegations."

Brazil's top electoral court has ordered Meta to pay 7.5 million reais ($1.34 million; €1.22 million) for failing to fully comply with judicial orders during last month's presidential election.

However, the judicial body has so far found no evidence that Meta deliberately acted to influence the election outcome.

Ahead of the runoff vote on October 25, leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva's coalition filed a complaint, accusing Meta of "algorithmic manipulation" favoring his far-right rival Flavio Bolsonaro.

Platforms owned by Meta include Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp.

Meta told the court last month that it had implemented measures to guarantee equal treatment for both campaigns.

The Superior Electoral Court (TSE) unanimously ruled on Tuesday to impose the 7.5 million reais fine on the US tech giant.

Meta missed deadlines and lacked transparency in its investigation, the court said.

On the other hand, the court's reporting judge Marco Aurelio Belizze wrote in his ruling that there were no preliminary evidence indicating that any intentional actions were taken to influence the election.

"That ​conclusion would require robust evidence, to be produced through the appropriate legal proceedings," he said.

According to Bellizze, the fine was based on Meta's overall handling of the court orders, including delays, incomplete disclosures, and challenges in verifying some of the information it provided.

In a statement on Wednesday, Meta strongly denied the court's ruling.

"We will continue to present the facts and defend ourselves against false allegations in the ‌ongoing ​legal proceedings," Meta said in a statement on Wednesday.

Lula defeated Bolsonaro in a tight run-off in October 25 with him winning 51.7% of the vote against Bolsonaro's 48.2%.

In his victory speech, Lula urged more regulation of big tech platforms after allegations that Meta interfered in the race.

"We had to overcome foreign interference in the election, coming from outside through digital platforms that we will have to regulate," he said.

From Local to Global: Anshuman Jha & Sarah-Jane Dias On Advocating Animal Rights In ‘Lakadbaggha 2’ & Uniqueness Of Crafting An Indian Action Franchise Rooted In Local Marital Arts

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Deadline’s Local to Global series touched down in Bruges, Belgium, last week where we sat down with actor-director Anshuman Jha and actress Sarah-Jane Dias to discuss their upcoming Indian action title Lakadbaggha 2: The Monkey Business. The film, which had its world premiere at the Razor Reel Flanders Film Festival where it was the runner-up for the Audience Choice Award, is the second instalment of Jha’s franchise and intersects local Indian mythology with indigenous martial arts all while advocating for animal conservation.

The project is produced by First Ray Films and Matta Cinema and follows Arjun Bakshi (Jha) who, weeks before his wedding, is shattered when his fiancée (Dias) disappears while on a mission in North Sulawesi, Indonesia. Her trail leads to a deadly island where an underground fight tournament hides the auction of the last surviving Yaki monkey – forcing Arjun to fight not just for love, but for a life that cannot speak for itself. “I feel that animals are going extinct because humanity is going extinct,” said Jha. “So, the next evolution of heroes or ‘superheroes’ is going to be somebody who’s not more powerful, but somebody who will have a lot more compassion. Being ordinary is the real superpower and that was the root of the birth story of Arjun Bakshi.”

The first instalment of the Hindi-language franchise sees vegan Arjun travel to Kolkata where he turns into a vigilante after discovering an illegal underground animal trade syndicate. The latest film heads to Indonesia, where it puts a spotlight on the Yaki Monkey, whose population has been reduced from 50,000 in1990 to 4,500 today. “This is a real-world problem,” said Jha. “I feel films need to not just entertain but also have a voice, especially for those who are voiceless and this story is in essence about that.”

India has a rich martial arts heritage, particularly through Kalaripayattu, also known as Kalari, which Jha says has rarely been conveyed on screen.

“We are the second biggest film industry in the world, so technically there should be more films featuring this,” he says. “The effort was to bring a very 1980s pulp action genre fan to the screen. I’m a huge Bruce Lee fan. He’s had a huge influence on my life and a lot of who I am, what I believe in and how I live goes in alignment with the principles of martial arts.”

Dias noted the film’s unconventional approach to portraying an action hero: “Quintessentially, when there is fighting in a film, it’s over love or someone wanting to take over the world or they are some dark force, but I have never seen it done to protect animals or the environment, especially in a superhero action film and I think that’s actually really unique.”

Jha worked with master fight choreographer Kecha Khamphakdee, who is behind the iconic fight scenes in Ong-Bak, to design the combat scenes in the latest film. Tony Jaa, the star of Ong-Bak: Muay Thai Warrior makes his Bollywood/Hindi film debut in the film while Dan Chupong, another star from the Ong-Bak world, plays the antagonist in the film.

When pressed about the challenges of Indian films traveling outside its own borders, Jha stressed that he was conscious of this when making the film and hopes it’s a story that Western audiences can embrace because of its universal themes and the fact that it’s an atypical Indian independent film.

“I want this film to travel the world,” says Jha. “I feel like there’s an emotional quotient – EQ – and there’s cinema quotient. I think because we have 1.6 billion people in India, sometimes as filmmakers we get very happy with just what we have. The truth is, there are 5.4 billion people outside of India. So, there’s a huge audience out there and the cinema quotient of audiences outside of India is very different from the inside of India.”

Seattle VC firm Ascend closes $17M fund to back ‘Third Act’ startups in physical AI and hardware

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Todd Bishop on Nov 11, 2026 at 7:15 amNovember 11, 2026 at 7:30 am

Venture firm Ascend has closed a $17 million third fund and landed its first institutional investor, in what founder Kirby Winfield calls the toughest fundraising market for small venture firms in 15 years.

The Seattle-based firm invests at the earliest stage, often writing the first check into a startup. It was the first investor in Overland AI, the maker of autonomous military vehicles, and an early backer of subsea geothermal startup Endurance Energy, among others.

Ascend Fund III will back more physical AI startups building robots, energy systems, defense technology and other ventures that blend software and hardware — part of a larger bet on what the Ascend team likes to call Seattle’s “Third Act.”

Winfield said raising a third fund in this market shows that Ascend has “arrived on the national stage,” along with Seattle’s rise as a hub for this new class of startups. He said the firm’s track record puts it in the same league as the country’s top seed investors.

“We are not just some nice guys in the corner of the country helping out our neighbors,” said Winfield, who works closely with Jen Haller, Ascend partner and chief of staff, and Nate Bek, a principal at the firm.

Who’s backing the fund: The new institutional investor is FirstLook Partners, a firm that invests in smaller venture funds. Josh Porter, FirstLook’s co-founder and managing partner, said the firm, which is “very bullish on the Seattle ecosystem,” looked at several startup markets outside San Francisco and New York and met many investors in Seattle, “but all roads kept leading back to Kirby.”

Porter said Winfield has “lived the founder journey” and has “the operational chops to sit across the table from a founder and empathize with what they’re going through.”

The rest of the fund comes from family offices, founders who have sold companies, and more than a dozen founders of Ascend portfolio companies.

A tough market for small funds: Ascend’s raise comes as smaller venture firms struggle to bring in new money. Their investors, mostly wealthy individuals and family offices, are waiting longer for returns from earlier funds.

Just 211 newer venture firms closed at least one fund through the end of September, down from 927 in all of 2022, according to the PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor. Established firms took a record 88.2% of the money raised by U.S. venture funds in the first nine months of the year, the report found. Porter said smaller firms have to show “a right to exist and a right to win deals.”

Ascend launched its first fund in 2019 and ultimately raised $15 million. Its second fund closed at $25 million in 2023. The firm generally operates in the $15 million to $25 million range and writes the same size checks regardless of fund size, Winfield said.

“We think Kirby knows his place in the larger ecosystem, knows the value he brings to the table, and has sized his fund accordingly,” Porter said.

The firm has already drawn about 60% of the money committed to Fund III. GeekWire first reported that the firm was raising the fund in April 2025, based on filings at the time. Endurance was one of the firm’s first investments from the new fund, in mid-2025.

Ascend’s track record: Winfield started Ascend after two decades in leadership roles at four startups. He described the first fund as a proof of concept that “stumbled into some unicorns,” including Fabric, an e-commerce software company that was valued at $1.5 billion in 2022. Ascend was in its first round.

With its second fund, Ascend moved away from consumer and marketplace investing to focus on enterprise AI and deep tech. Its most successful investments from that fund, according to Winfield, include Overland AI, Sabi, Yoodli and Zocks.

After Ascend’s first checks, its portfolio companies have gone on to raise more than $1.5 billion from investors including Accel, Benchmark, Bessemer, Founders Fund, Khosla and Lightspeed, according to Ascend.

Ascend’s second and third funds rank in the top 10% of funds of $50 million or less, based on current valuations of their portfolio companies, Winfield said, citing figures Ascend shared with its investors at its annual meeting. The firm didn’t disclose specific returns or how much cash it has returned to its investors.

What Ascend looks for: The firm looks for ideas that seem too difficult, too boring, too confusing or too small for big multistage venture firms to evaluate. Ideally, Winfield said, its founders are doing something that makes Series A investors “a little bit nervous.”

That’s why Ascend hasn’t made many more defense tech investments since backing Overland, he said. Valuations for well-known founders in the sector have soared as more investors have piled in.

“The time to invest in defense tech was when nobody was investing in defense tech,” Winfield said.

Porter said Ascend’s focus on Seattle and physical AI is “really the bet we made with Ascend,” adding that Endurance and Overland have the potential to become “generational, category-defining companies.”

Fund III also backs AI software and infrastructure companies, including Seattle-area startup Govstream.ai, which uses AI to speed city permitting.

Seattle’s economic climate: Winfield is a champion of Seattle’s startup scene, known for Ascend’s annual Founders Bash party in the city and his ever-present ☔🔥☔ emojis on social media, symbolizing hot startups in rainy Seattle.

Ascend’s refreshed website and brand, launched with Fund III, nod to the firm’s Seattle roots with its longstanding Seattle Startup Toolkit and a Spotify playlist of Pacific Northwest indie rock and hip-hop.

But Winfield has also been a vocal critic of state and local policies he sees as hostile to startups, including Washington’s new tax on income over $1 million, which takes effect in 2028 and survived a repeal vote last week. He worries the tax could eventually be extended to people earning $150,000 a year, which would make it harder for startups to recruit employees from places like Texas.

“The chilling effect that’s going to have on the ecosystem is real, and it’s happening,” he said.

About 60% of Ascend’s portfolio companies are based in Seattle, according to the firm.

Bek said hardware companies such as Endurance and Overland still have good reasons to be in Seattle, including testing on Lake Union and in Yakima, access to the sea and University of Washington talent. For Overland, he said, that also means proximity to Joint Base Lewis-McChord.

Combined with the region’s history in aerospace, defense and energy, Winfield noted, those advantages make Seattle one of the country’s key hubs for building physical products. He called it “El Segundo Northwest,” a reference to the Los Angeles-area city that has become a hub for defense and hardware startups.

As Winfield notes, Ascend isn’t the only early-stage firm chasing these companies. Pre-seed firms such as Cantos, Ironspring and Silent Ventures are backing similar founders, and some Seattle firms that historically focused on software are becoming more open to investing in hardware.

Ultimately, he said, Seattle still benefits from a long legacy of technical founders, and Ascend is “betting that those folks are going to continue to create companies that are worth hundreds of billions of dollars.”

Contemporary Beadworker Ken Williams Jr Awarded Rakow Commission by Corning Museum of Glass

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Marking the prize’s 40th year, "I Am the Hungry Cat" is the first Rakow Commission awarded for beadwork.

The Corning Museum of Glass selected Northern Arapaho and Cattaraugus Seneca artist Ken Williams Jr as the recipient of the 2026 Rakow Commission. Williams began learning beadwork at an early age on the Cattaraugus Seneca Indian Territory and later refined his technique alongside master beadworkers from his mother’s Northern Arapaho family.

Widely celebrated for his pictorial beadwork, Williams used the Commission as an opportunity to move beyond functional and wearable forms, creating a standalone wall panel. Utilizing 68 different colors from a range of historic to contemporary made beads, the framed composition features a graphic depiction of the artist’s cat, Valentino, complete with thought bubbles depicting his favorite foods as part of his “Yum Series.” In centering imagery of his cat and favorite foods detailed in bold colors and expressive lines, Williams is actually presenting a self-portrait through the work.

Now on view in the Museum’s Contemporary Art + Design Gallery, I Am the Hungry Cat, draws inspiration from Pop Art and a vibrant color palette and bridges ancestral artistic traditions with a playful contemporary sensibility, expanding the possibilities of glass as a medium for narrative art.

The Rakow Commission is one of the most prestigious annual awards for artists working in glass and supports the creation of a new work by encouraging artists to venture into technical or conceptual projects that they would have been unable to explore due to financial limitations. The annual program was endowed by the late Dr. and Mrs. Leonard S. Rakow, who were Fellows, benefactors, and dear friends of the Museum.

In its 40th year, the annual award was first presented in 1986, and recipients ranged from emerging to established artists including Te Rongo Kirkwood, Charisse Pearlina Weston, Leo Tecosky, Preston Singletary, Silvia Levenson, Klaus Moje, and Lino Tagliapietra.

ABA President Barbara Howard says association will continue to prioritize the defense of democracy

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

It’s been a busy few months for Barbara Howard.

As the ABA’s president, Howard has worked to protect voting rights and urged her fellow members to do the same. She vowed to fight assaults on civil rights, which include critical threats facing unaccompanied children and other immigrants. She also spent three weeks in Europe, where she talked with lawyers in London and Copenhagen about the association’s efforts to defend the rule of law.

Howard, principal of Barbara J. Howard Co. in Cincinnati, is serving a one-year term that ends in August. She has a long history of leadership in the association, which includes being a member of the House of Delegates since 1986. She is also a past chair of the House.

In a conversation with the ABA Journal on Monday, Howard talked more about her first tasks as ABA president and what she plans to focus on in the months ahead. Our interview has been edited for length and clarity.

It’s been a few months since you became the ABA’s president, and about a week since the midterm elections. You’ve said that you want ABA members to help protect the right to vote. Why is that important?

I’ve put out two statements, one in September and one in October, and in both of them, I pointed to the role that we as citizens play in our democracy. As many before us have said, with democracy, it’s not one and done. It needs to be actively pursued and defended every day. It’s not the kind of thing where you can just sit back on your laurels and say, “Well, we’ve got our democracy.” And we all realized that. I think that’s what we saw as a country.

Voting is one of the most important responsibilities of an American citizen. It’s how we give our citizens the opportunity to hold our lawmakers and our officials accountable, and it’s how we have our voices heard. So for me, the focus was very much on getting out messages and encouraging lawyers around the country to talk with their fellow citizens, to engage with them, to assure them that our voting systems are very secure and that they should exercise the right to vote.

How have you been involved in those efforts?

Well, I spent Election Day as a poll worker. This was my fifth year. I worked the same polling place as I did last year. So that means on Monday night spending an hour, two hours setting up all the equipment, getting everything ready. Then it was about a 15-hour day, from roughly 5:15 in the morning until 8:15 at night. And we had probably about a 25% increase in the number of people who voted from the year before.

You’ve also expressed a need for the ABA to continue its fight against attacks on civil rights. What has that work looked like so far?

So far this year I’ve really been focused on the elections because we’ve had to be. As we now move past the elections, we need to be sure all the members of Congress are able to take their seats in January.

Another issue right now is immigration. We have seen some very negative developments with respect to the representation of especially unaccompanied minor children, but immigrants in general. ProBAR has had its funds pulled, and the entity with whom it contracts, Acacia, has had its grant terminated. There has been some money generated to allow ProBAR to set up a hotline for unaccompanied minor children to call in to get direction in terms of how they can get legal representation, which is required by Congress. That’s called the DORY hotline. Shelters have refused to give the phone number to minors who have asked for it. That’s a matter that is going to need some immediate attention.

There is also the overall issue with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services saying that rather than award a new grant to Acacia or someone else, it is going to internalize its representation so that the government is both representing the minor children and making the decisions on who stays and goes. That is an apparent, if not actual, conflict of interest. There’s a letter going out to Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. urging him to reassess that whole process. We will continue to monitor that very, very closely.

Turning to the ABA itself, many of its leaders have focused on how to make the association more efficient and serve its members better. What changes do you hope to see moving forward?

The Board of Governors has taken on what we’re calling “the renovation project.” It has become apparent as I’ve sat through several budget cycles and in talking to incoming section, division and forum chairs and staff directors, as well as others in the ABA, that we are too complex. We’ve gotten so complex that it’s made it really hard for new members, especially younger lawyers and potential new members, to navigate the ABA. When they get frustrated, they just give up. Because we’re seeing the nonrenewal rate of first-year lawyers is so much greater than for the rest of the association population, it’s crying out for reform.

We have five teams that are making their phone calls to entities, whether it’s a section, division, forum or a standing or special committee or commission. We are looking at all aspects of the association to see where we can make some changes that hopefully will simplify the structure, eliminate both silos and duplication of effort, and make it easier for people to find their way to their home or homes. We will be having an open forum at the midyear meeting for people to express their thoughts and ideas. We will really start working right after midyear on some proposals for revising our structure.

The first goal of the strategic plan is that the ABA is a home for all lawyers. So this is to help make that happen. The third goal is that we have a strong financial foundation to take us forward into our next 150 years as an association. So this is also to help in that effort.

I can imagine that is a big priority. Are there any others you’d like to mention?

The other one is the middle goal of the strategic plan, which is that we are the voice of the legal profession and defender of the rule of law. I have spoken a lot about the rule of law, both across the country and overseas. Lawyers overseas are very, very focused on what’s happening in the United States. We carry a great weight with our colleagues, and it’s important to them that they know what we’re doing to make sure that our democracy thrives in the face of some real attacks.

I have no preconceived idea that we are on an easy street now that the elections are passed. I think there will be continued efforts to dismantle parts of our democracy, and we will continue to defend it. That will certainly be a priority.

What have you enjoyed about the job and found most fun?

I’ve really enjoyed talking with people and helping create solutions. That is what we as lawyers do best—we solve problems. So helping advance the mission of the ABA and solve problems for either lawyers or entities within the ABA has been very rewarding. And I’ve certainly enjoyed traveling and getting to know both ABA members, and again, our colleagues around the world who are very much interested in the work of the ABA.

You’ve been an ABA member for a long time, and you know a lot of people well. But what is one thing other members might not know about you?

I am a third-generation lawyer. My maternal grandfather was also mayor of the city of Toledo, where I grew up, for 10 years. As a child, it was kind of fun walking down the street with him. People would greet him as Mr. Mayor. And then my paternal grandfather was not only successful in his practice, but he was also appointed as a probate court judge for a while.

Volcanic eruptions leave a lingering chill in the ocean

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Major volcanic eruptions may briefly strengthen ocean cold spells, but their cooling effects vary widely across different regions.

Pinatubo’s eruption was over long before the cooling was. The 1991 eruption cooled the lower atmosphere worldwide by as much as 0.9°F (0.5°C), with effects lasting about two years.

At sea, that chill had an uneven reach. A spell of unusually cold water could grow stronger in one region without doing so in another.

A new study follows those cold spells through six big eruptions. Each eruption preceded an increase in their strength overall, a short-lived reversal of the weakening seen across 175 years.

Marine cold spells are runs of days when the sea surface is unusually cold. An icy sea isn’t necessarily having one; it may just be as cold as it usually is.

The study used the coldest 10% of temperatures for a given place and time of year as its cutoff. The water had to stay that cold for five days or longer, rather than just dip for a day or two.

Jan Kempert, at South Westphalia University of Applied Sciences, worked with Raven Quilestino-Olario, at the University of Cologne’s Institute of Geology and Mineralogy, on the research. Both institutions are in Germany.

“We noticed that while the cooling effects of major volcanic eruptions have been widely studied, relatively little attention has been given to how these volcanic winters affect extreme cold events in the ocean,” Quilestino-Olario said.

Their source was COBE-SST3, a record from the Japan Meteorological Agency. It gives daily estimates of ocean surface temperatures from 1850 to 2024, based mostly on readings taken at sea.

For each cold spell they found, the pair added up how far below normal the water was, day by day. That total became the spell’s score.

The score thus counted the length of the spell as well as the cold. A longer stretch or a deeper chill would push it up.

In 79% of the ocean map squares, however, the scores went down over those 175 years.

Cold spells grew less frequent in about the same share of squares, extending a decline that satellites had already recorded for 1982 to 2020.

The eruption list began with Krakatau in 1883 and Santa Maria in 1902. It continued with Novarupta in 1912, Agung in 1963, El Chichón in 1982, and finally Pinatubo in 1991.

The stratosphere, high above the ground, is where sulfur-rich particles from large eruptions can linger. They cut down the sunlight reaching the surface, sometimes leaving months or years of cooling.

The comparison put cold spells from the 30 years before an eruption on one side and a short period after it on the other.

The later run always started the next year and lasted five years for Krakatau, two for Pinatubo, and one for each of the other four.

There was more than a volcano to reckon with in some of those years. El Chichón, in particular, had some inconvenient company.

The eruption came in 1982. Across 1982 and 1983, the Pacific also experienced one of only six “super” El Niño events since 1850.

Both events could leave a mark on the temperature record, so the researchers had to allow for the overlap.

Kempert and Quilestino-Olario removed the influence of El Niño and La Niña, along with the long-term weakening of cold spells.

After those adjustments, marine cold spells were stronger than expected following all six eruptions.

“Our study shows that although cold extremes in the ocean have generally become less frequent and less intense over the past 175 years, major volcanic eruptions may temporarily interrupt this long-term pattern,” Kempert said.

They also tried choosing the years without looking at eruption dates at all. In 10,000 random draws, each containing six years, they checked for an increase in cold-spell strength.

Just about 2.4% of the draws produced an increase as large as the one after the eruptions. In other words, most random choices of years didn’t produce anything of that size.

The world average was one thing, though. The maps were another.

From 1884 to 1888, about 80% of the ocean squares had stronger cold spells than expected. That was the period following Krakatau; for Pinatubo, the share came to 70%.

For Santa Maria and Agung it was roughly half. El Chichón and Novarupta came in below half, a reminder that an increase in the world average needn’t appear in most places.

Five eruptions had their biggest response in the middle latitudes, the bands between 30 and 60 degrees north or south of the equator. Novarupta broke that pattern.

The only high-latitude eruption in the six, Novarupta also had the weakest effect on the global average. The authors were cautious about that result.

None of these eruptions occurred in an otherwise unchanging ocean. Ocean circulation was still shifting water around, the seasons were changing, and natural swings in climate were still going on.

According to the authors, volcanic cooling and those ongoing changes together could account for the patchy map. Changes in circulation can even bring warmer winters to parts of the far north after an eruption.

Some parts of the ocean have very few nineteenth-century observations. The record is less reliable in places with few historical readings, and in places where sea ice was present.

For Pinatubo, the team ran the analysis again with a satellite-era record from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

The two records agreed in 79% of the map squares about whether cold spells were stronger or weaker than expected.

Another choice affected the answer: the years used to stand for normal temperatures. Comparing each eruption with its previous 30 years gave stronger cold spells after all six.

When the researchers used the same modern reference period for every eruption, fewer eruptions showed an increase.

The count was five with 1961 to 1990 as the comparison, and four with 1981 to 2010.

How much extra cold can be pinned on the volcanoes themselves remains uncertain. The records describe the aftermath, but can’t separate out every cause.

Additional tests removed a North Atlantic climate pattern as well as El Niño.

Several other natural cycles remained, partly because their consistent monthly records don’t reach as far back as 1850.

There’s a further difficulty with El Niño: an eruption may affect it. Remove El Niño from the calculation and some of the volcanic effect may go with it.

The authors call for further checks using other temperature records, measurements beneath the surface, and climate models.

Their own next project will look at marine heatwaves, periods of unusually warm ocean water.

“We hope to use the same long-term ocean temperature dataset to explore how marine heatwaves have changed over the past 175 years, this time placing greater emphasis on the uncertainties associated with historical observations,” Quilestino-Olario said.

The heatwave work could help establish whether eruptions change unusually hot conditions at sea as well as cold ones.

The full study was published in the journal Ocean-Land-Atmosphere Research.
