With a fluffed-up quiff and a supermodel on his arm, here’s a look at Iowa’s next governor. Long before Democrat Rob Sand made political history, he was having a very different kind of moment in the spotlight, posing for Steven Meisel in the pages of Vogue Italia.

The year was 2004, and Sand was among the fresh faces who appeared in “Be My Baby,” an editorial in the February issue, photographed by Meisel and styled by Brana Wolf. Alongside Sand was a cast of rising fashion stars, including a young Caroline Trentini, with whom he posed in a series of moody, black-and-white portraits.

The concept centered on a romanticized vision of 1950s youth culture, abounding with the aforementioned rockabilly hair and lots of leather The original Italian introduction described the mood as a collision of bon ton—good manners and traditional propriety—and transgression.

Womenswear included diaphanous Blumarine slips, Prada silk and tweed, and chiffon dresses by Rossella Tarabini for Anna Molinari.

“Was a fun day!” Sand wrote beneath an Instagram post by Meisel revisiting the shoot.

Sand’s modeling career was no mere college dalliance. While studying political science at Brown University, the six-foot-three Iowan spent time working in Milan and Paris, walking for Prada, cult Japanese label Number (N)ine, and Giuliano Fujiwara. He also appeared in Prada’s spring 2003 menswear show. In fact, his fashion commitments were once so serious that he missed the 2004 Iowa caucuses in his hometown of Decorah because he was working in Milan.

On November 3, Sand defeated Republican Zach Lahn to become the first Democrat elected governor of Iowa in 20 years, following two terms as state auditor. His newly resurfaced modeling portfolio has since become an unlikely internet sensation. Sand himself has joined in on the joke, sharing one of his old Vogue Italia photographs with the caption: “I’m disappointed to learn images of me modeling for Prada are circulating. Tank Dell catches perfect 63-yard touchdown in matchup vs. Chargers, his first score since gruesome 2024 knee injury

For the first time in nearly two years, Tank Dell made his way back into the end zone on Sunday afternoon.

Dell, who missed all of last season recovering from a gruesome knee injury he suffered in 2024, scored a touchdown just before halftime of their matchup with the Los Angeles Chargers on Sunday. It was just Dell's second game back on the field since the injury.

Dell, in the second quarter at SoFi Stadium, easily broke up the right side of the field and hauled in a deep pass from C.J. Stroud. He completed the 63-yard run into the end zone, which put Houston up 17-9 at the time.

The comeback touchdown was about as perfect as it could have possibly been.

Dell went down in a matchup with the Kansas City Chiefs in December 2024 while scoring a 30-yard touchdown. As Dell made the catch, teammate Jared Wayne dove in to try and make the catch too and accidentally crashed into his left knee. Dell ended up being taken off the field on a stretcher.

In the end, Dell tore multiple ligaments, sustained meniscus damage and dislocated his left knee. He missed all of the 2025 campaign, and only made it back onto the field for the first time last week in the Texans' 27-20 overtime loss to the New York Giants.

The injury was just the latest significant one that Dell has faced in his career, too. He fractured his fibula during his rookie season, and he was then shot in the leg as a bystander at a mass shooting in Florida in 2024.

The catch on Sunday was Dell's second of the game, and just his third this season. Houston entered the contest with a 2-5 record, while the Chargers are still the lone winless team in the league.

While Houston's season hasn't gotten off to the best start, Dell has made his way into the end zone once again. His comeback is officially complete.

The 5 Scariest Books of All Time, According to Stephen King

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It’s interesting to find out what scares one person, and compare their fears to those of another person, perhaps naturally, but then when you're looking at someone who is usually the person who causes fear, then finding out what scares them (if anything) is even more intriguing. Enter Stephen King (no, wait, not like that!), who probably doesn’t need much of an introduction, not just at this stage, but really any stage in the last several decades. His earliest published novels came out in the 1970s, and so many of his initial classics belonged wholly or in part to the horror genre.

You'd be missing out on great books like 11/22/63, much of The Dark Tower, and most of Different Seasons if you only read King’s works of horror, but sure, horror is the genre he’s most associated with for honestly fair reasons. He’s also read and absorbed a lot of horror made by other people, as the non-fiction book Danse Macabre (1981) demonstrates, but more recently, he gave his top 5 scariest books to USA Today in an interview. It’s subjective, and it’s also not the same as Stephen King saying they're the five “best” scary books ever written, but it’s worth looking at what makes the arguable master of horror himself feel fear (no one is safe from being affected by a scary story, it seems).

In the past, Stephen King has also been asked about which stories of his he views most favorably, and he made some unexpected choices there, and also, he highlighted something recent at the time he was asked: 2021’s Billy Summers. Maybe he likes doing that, and it is interesting to not just go straight back to the classics and/or the heavy-hitters, so he could've been trying to do something like that again when one of his five picks was the (at the time of writing) very recent Trad Wife. It’s a 2026 novel about very contemporary things, as you might guess from the title, because the idea of a “tradwife” is a more online and modern-day thing, but it’s all about wanting women to be traditional wives, as things might've been in older days that some people choose to romanticize.

Trad Wife has divided people to some extent, if you go over the other reviews out there, but Stephen King is clearly a champion of the book.

That’s probably the gentlest and least provocative way to define it without rattling anyone. So, the book’s about a journalist who wants to document someone who appears to be a “tradwife,” and she does that while on the property she lives on with her family, but then things slowly get strange and more horrific, though potentially not in the ways you're expecting. Trad Wife has divided people to some extent, if you go over the other reviews out there (admittedly there aren’t a ton, seeing as it’s still a fairly new release), but Stephen King is clearly a champion of the book, being confident enough to label it the best horror novel of 2026.

More of an established heavy-hitter now, Stephen King is a big fan of The Silence of the Lambs, which was a sequel to Red Dragon. Now, Red Dragon got a movie adaptation in the form of Manhunter, but the film version of The Silence of the Lambs wasn’t really a sequel to that film, and then there was a prequel to The Silence of the Lambs (1991) that was called Red Dragon about a decade later, but that one wasn’t great. Oh, and you get some Red Dragon in the TV show, Hannibal, though chronologically, that one didn’t have enough seasons to get to The Silence of the Lambs material.

Anyway, point is, you can not read all that much, and you still probably know the deal with this series, to some extent, and you likely have some familiarity with Hannibal Lecter and what a great character he is. The Silence of the Lambs was obviously a great movie, but the source material is also remarkable, and it’s one of those great cases where a high-quality book seamlessly became a high-quality movie (something that cannot be said about every Stephen King novel that’s made its way to the big screen, but at least there'll always be Stand by Me, Misery, and The Shawshank Redemption, among others).

It’s fair that some of Stephen King’s supernatural horror books get a lot of attention, but just as skilled is what he often manages to do when he tackles psychological horror (and sure, when he has the two collide, like in The Shining, that can also be phenomenal). He singles out The Other as a psychological horror book that frightened him to a particularly great extent, and with it being published a few years before King’s first published novel, you can probably assume it had some kind of influence on his own work, or at least some of his subsequent books.

At the center of The Other is a pair of twins, with one of them being very troubled and the other being seemingly anything but, and the overall novel does increasingly unsettling things with that premise. It’s a slow-burn without ever running the risk of feeling too slow, and there is also something worth celebrating about a book that can prove frightening while sounding, on paper, a bit ordinary or even too straightforward. The Other just does a lot of stuff right, and going into the things it does right would run the risk of ruining things. Just trust King and the other people who are fans of this one (it was very popular back when released, but is perhaps a little more obscure nowadays than the other older books King’s highlighted as being the most frightening to him).

Oh, and now this one isn't obscure by any metric. The Exorcist was a big deal as a book, for a little while, and then it was a potentially even bigger deal as a movie, with that movie getting made not long after the book came out. A similar thing happened with The Godfather novel being the book equivalent of a blockbuster, and then the movie was made rather fast, and ultimately became even more culturally significant. With The Exorcist, though, the source material here is very much worth reading even if you’ve seen the movie, since there are ideas it explores – and sights it describes – that prove more unsettling when they're placed in your head, versus seeing/experiencing those things on a screen.

And that’s saying a lot, because the movie is still absolutely surprising and effectively horrifying with what it shows and explores. Guess it’s the case that you’ve got a winning work of horror either way, with The Exorcist (1971) being potentially the definitive demonic possession novel, and The Exorcist (1973) almost definitely being the definitive demonic possession movie. Also, there is a blending of more supernatural (or otherworldly) horror with psychologically troubling stuff in The Exorcist and, once more, the interplay just serves to make both those strands of horror hit even harder.

The Exorcist is singled out in polls on Goodreads as being one of the scariest novels of all time, among those voting, and Lord of the Flies is one you'll often see in such polls, too. And hey, it’s relevant here, because it was one of King’s five picks when he was asked about books that scared him. It’s interesting, because Lord of the Flies isn't outwardly a horror book, probably more definable as a survival story, or maybe like a small-scale dystopian novel without real sci-fi elements, but it’s horrifying without necessarily being full-on horror. And of course it is, because it’s about young boys getting stranded on an island and finding themselves clashing over various things, all in increasingly violent ways.

People tend to read Lord of the Flies when they're younger as well, which has an effect on it being more frightening, like if it marks the first time you're forced to confront some really troubling ideas. Stephen King himself has written some pretty great books that are comparable to Lord of the Flies, like The Mist (technically a novella, with it involving people trapped in a supermarket), and Under the Dome (in which a dome does indeed come down over a very small town, trapping people under it). Also, King’s love for Lord of the Flies is apparent in his fiction beyond looking at the stories of his that seem a little similar premise-wise, since Lord of the Flies itself is an important part of the novella “Low Men in Yellow Coats,” mostly in the way it has a strong impact on that story’s young protagonist.

Godzilla Minus Zero director doesn't believe that a sequel would necessarily benefit the massive franchise.

Godzilla Minus Zero director Takashi Yamazaki has opened up about the challenges of following his Oscar-winning film. He explained why he ultimately abandoned the idea of making a sequel designed specifically to surpass the original. The director initially wanted to create a movie that could replicate or outdo the success of Godzilla Minus One. However, he eventually decided that approaching the project with those goals could compromise the quality of the content.

In an interview with IGN, Yamazaki discussed the pressure surrounding his return to the franchise following the overwhelmingly positive reception of his Academy Award-winning 2023 film. The director said that he thought about creating a more dramatic and explosive movie that would be bigger than its predecessor. However, he eventually scrapped that approach after realizing how bigger isn't always better, as well as how only focusing on commercial success and more awards could sacrifice the effectiveness of the storyline. He added that if he went with his original plans, he would have been doing it for the wrong reasons.

Instead of focusing on making the sequel outperform the first film, Yamazaki decided to concentrate on creating an entertaining experience with a meaningful story. According to the director, he dropped the idea that he needed this sequel to beat Godzilla Minus One. As a result, he was able to give himself and his team more creative freedom. He described the change in plans as an important moment for Godzilla Minus Zero, helping him move forward without worrying about living in the shadow of Godzilla Minus One.

In the beginning I thought, ‘Well, how do I outdo its predecessor and how do we make a movie that's going to win all these awards and it's going to be bigger in scale?' But I think it's very dangerous to create a movie with that type of mindset because you're going to risk diluting what makes the movie great in the first place. So those are the wrong reasons to make a movie and it ends up becoming an antagonist in your moviemaking process.

The film was praised by both critics and casual audiences alike for its emotional plot, interesting characters, and amazing visual effects.

12 Most Universally Loved Sitcom Characters Of All Time

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Sitcoms are famous for producing a whole host of lovable characters, but there are a few that come out as clear favorites among wider audiences. The sitcom is one of the most endearing formats on TV, providing audiences with comfort watches that they can revisit time and time again, and a cast of characters who largely remain familiar throughout the show.

With that said, every sitcom character typically needs a set of flaws that make them more interesting and serve as a building block to create narratives around these individuals. And while some flaws are innocuous, there are others that can make a sitcom character insufferable.

On the flip side, there are a handful of sitcom legends who have cemented their place in audiences' hearts thanks to pretty flimsy flaws, or their charming and quirky personalities, or simply being a figure who often sees the best in others, and thus ends up having their best shine through.

With that said, the first person on this list is, of course, Ted Lasso. Played by Jason Sudeikis, Ted is a charming American man who moves to the UK to coach a football team, and while he has little to no knowledge about the sport or English customs, his endless positivity, warmth, and ability to draw out people's best serves him well in his new position.

Ted is also incredibly bright, with a high degree of emotional intelligence that allows him to tap into what his peers are feeling and connect on a level that many others struggle to replicate. Ted's charm and optimism earns him a spot as one of the most beloved characters on TV.

Next up, Phil Dunphy is remarkably similar to Ted Lasso in some ways, due to his incredible positive outlook and his warmth, but he does also possess a great deal more naïveté than Ted. Phil is clumsy, corny, and desperate to get approval from others, whether that be his wife, kids, father-in-law, or clients.

However, despite his surprising lack of self-awareness, Phil's depth of love and support for his family is the thing that makes him an undeniably endearing character that people can't help but fall in love with. Ty Burrell plays the part with incredible confidence and energy, and it all comes together in perfect harmony on the show season after season.

Matthew Perry may have been a more colorful character in life whose choices didn't always lead him down the healthiest paths, but his time as Chandler Bing on Friends will forever keep his memory alive in the hearts and minds of audiences everywhere. The interesting thing about Chandler is the fact that he is such a guarded character, always hiding behind humor and sarcasm.

However, despite his tendency to try and keep his emotions at a distance, Chandler becomes one of the most vulnerable and real characters on the show, especially in the moments he shares with Monica, or as his friendship with Joey evolves. Chandler is a beautiful soul in a bristly package, and it makes him all the more lovable.

Another character who likes to keep his emotions buried deep, Ron Swanson from Parks and Recreation, is the Director of his local Parks and Recreation department of government, which makes his unabashed contempt for all forms of politics and red tape all the more intriguing.

Ron Swanson is a simple man who enjoys simple things, and he has no respect for how government bodies are supposed to run, instead holding onto the job because he finds it to be an easy and low-effort role where almost nothing ever happens, that is until Leslie Knope sets her mind to opening a new park in Pawnee.

Nick Offerman is absolutely fantastic as Swanson, and the character remains a wonderful contradiction whose desire to not work is rivaled only by his intense loyalty to his friends and his commitment to supporting their dreams.

The late and great Catherine O'Hara left her mark on the TV landscape with her spectacular turn as Moira Rose, the matriarch of Schitt's Creek. While Moira is initially a hard woman to love or respect, and her self-absorbed and theatrical nature make her a bit of a pain, Moira's growth throughout the series is something to behold.

As the character begins to warm up to small-town living, and become someone that fans could engage with on a deeper level, it's hard to look back at those earlier moments without a tenderness and admiration for this character, who is ultimately on a journey of growth and understanding.

The 1950s sitcom, I Love Lucy, was a foundational entry in the genre that set the tone from which many other TV comedy shows would draw inspiration. Lucille Ball plays the titular lead, and brings a tremendous physical comedy that, when paired with Lucy's wacky schemes and overtly expressive face, makes her a character that is impossible not to fall in love with.

With that, the show earned its title, and the series continued to prove just how popular it was over six seasons. Today, I Love Lucy may not be the most familiar show for younger viewers, but anyone who picks up the series will ultimately find themselves captivated by the charm of Lucy Ricardo.

A few decades later, in 1985, The Golden Girls launched on TV and became an instant classic. And in no small part, that was thanks to the incredible ensemble cast that brought together four strong and vocal personalities who all had their own distinctive voice, and explored their wonderful maturing friendship.

Still, among these four charming women, Betty White's Rose Nylund stands out as one of the most lovable, thanks to her innocence, naïveté, and her ability to balance out the rest of the girls when things got heated. Rose is a literal treasure who remains one of the most beloved characters from TV, even all these decades later.

Rowan Atkinson struck gold in developing this silent character, Mr. Bean, who became a global sensation without ever saying a word. Employing a great deal of slapstick humor, intense facial expressions, and a certain innocence, Mr. Bean is a character that seems to transcend time and other barriers to engage with fanbases the world over.

He is a simple man, who sets out to complete simple tasks. However, due to his lack of awareness, and his clumsy nature, Mr. Bean often gets caught up in trouble that would never impact on typical people. But all of that simply makes him more engaging and adorable.

Andy Samberg's Jake Peralta is the embodiment of golden retriever energy in the form of an immature New York City detective. Jake is an eternally youthful and playful character who wants to get the best out of life, and is always ready to gamify his situation.

Jake also experiences profound personal growth over the course of the series, as he becomes more vulnerable and forms closer relationships with people like Captain Raymon Holt and Amy Santiago.

Community is a show that is brimming with colorful characters, but while the flaws for many of these individuals are initially overwhelming, Abed's (Danny Pudi) quirk is that he views everything through a meta lens and constantly references pop culture.

While everyone else around him embarks on a journey to become the best version of themselves, Abed is often the anchor that helps others to recognize where they are making mistakes, and course correct. He is intensely loyal, curious about human connection and boldly honest. All of which adds up to make him one of the best characters on TV.

Apple TV's "Unadaptable" 3-Season Series Is Mandatory Viewing for Sci-Fi Fans

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Julio is a Senior Author for Collider. He studied History and International Relations at university, but found his calling in cultural journalism. When he isn't writing, Julio also teaches English at a nearby school. He has lived in São Paulo most of his life, where he covers CCXP and other big events. Having loved movies, music, and TV from an early age, he prides himself in knowing every minute detail about the things he loves. When he is older, he dreams of owning a movie theater in a small countryside town.

By now, it's no secret that Apple TV has a knack for releasing great science fiction; you can pretty much choose one at random, and it will still be a great choice. Saying one of them is the best feels even a little unfair to the others, so we have to raise the bar and look beyond the screen and individual taste, focusing instead on what kind of accomplishment it represents. All that considered, it's almost impossible not to choose Foundation, the adaptation of Isaac Asimov's seminal sci-fi saga. Before its release, these books were considered straight-up unadaptable, so, in many ways, the greatest achievement of Apple TV's Foundation isn't just being an incredible work of science fiction, but that it exists at all, really.

Between original series and adaptations, science fiction has had a boom in productions ever since the dawn of the streaming age. The one work every fan of the genre had pretty much accepted would never get such treatment was Foundation. Even after its announcement back in 2018, everyone only believed the series was actually real when it premiered via Apple TV in 2021. That's because no other work in sci-fi has a scope as huge as Foundation — it was just too much to believe it could ever be made into a TV series.

Set in a future where humankind has conquered the cosmos, the original book comprises many smaller tales depicting humanity's efforts to overcome a thousand years of dark ages. The single common thread is Hari Seldon (Jared Harris), founder of psychohistory, a field of study that uses probability and psychology to predict future events on a galactic scale. Seldon devised a plan for future human leaders to follow, thus reducing the dark ages from tens of thousands of years to a single millennium.

It's a fascinating story and an easy read, filled with equally interesting characters. However, most never interact due to their respective stories being set decades and even centuries apart. This is a major roadblock for any kind of visual adaptation, because focusing on just one of them would mean neglecting centuries of human history in that universe. It just couldn't be done, and those centuries of human history are the whole point of Foundation. Something had to drastically change for these novels to fit the TV framework.

When translating a work of literature into visual media, it's always necessary to make some changes, including, sometimes, some severe ones. Modern television usually demands strong protagonists with whom the audience can sympathize, so, when David S. Goyer and Josh Friedman started working on adapting Foundation, this was one of the very first things to tackle — that and a way to unify and streamline the original narrative, so it could fit the episodic and seasonal framework.

Instead of multiple separate storylines spread out across the timeline, the series brings them together with time jumps and some classic sci-fi narrative devices. Thus, Apple's Foundation takes the main characters in the early Asimov books and turns them into actual protagonists, each with their own complexities and development arcs. Instead of two or three small chapters, then, characters like Gaal Dornick (Lou Llobell), Salvor Hardin (Leah Harvey), and even Seldon himself have central and much larger roles.

Despite its scope, though, the books are quite simplistic in worldbuilding early on, as the Foundation itself is still struggling to consolidate. Sci-fi television demands something greater, though, so the Apple series has grander and more cinematic settings than the books. Every planet has a complex culture and background, and even things like Seldon's vault, where his recording reveals the next steps of his plan every few decades, become central plot devices tied to the protagonists, keeping the story straight and fluid.

There was no way to make Foundation work as a series if it was an exact match with its book counterpart, and a significant part of why it's so good is how it allows itself to go beyond Asimov's original writings. The best example is the Genetic Dynasty, an entirely new idea created for the series. In the books, the Galactic Empire's decay is what causes the dark ages, but it's the series that actually goes into why that is, since the original focuses mostly on the other side, the Foundation.

For that, the series introduces one of the most brilliant concepts in recent sci-fi: the Genetic Dynasty. It perfectly sums up the notion that the Empire is so stagnant, to the point where every emperor is a clone of the same man, Cleon I. Brothers Dawn (Cassian Bilton), Day (Lee Pace), and Dusk (Terrence Mann) are essentially different versions of the same person, each struggling to establish their own identity while living under the shadow of their predecessor. Another great example is Demerzel (Laura Birn), whose role is considerably expanded from the books, made in the Apple adaptation into a central figure in the Empire's political machinations, with a twist coming straight from the author's wider Robot universe.

These additions give Apple's Foundation an identity of its own without losing sight of the ideas that made the original novels so influential. Ultimately, that's what makes it such a great adaptation, focusing more on understanding and expanding on the potential of the original story rather than simply adapting it. Bringing Foundation to television was always going to require some creative liberties, but the series shows just how much can be achieved when an adaptation understands what makes its source material special.

Foundation is available to stream on Apple TV.
