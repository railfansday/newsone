Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: A proposed liquefied natural gas project in Pakistan underscores close ties with the Trump administration, garment workers in Bangladesh protest for pay increases, and India’s opposition alliance rallies to call for the resignation of the chief election commissioner.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: A proposed liquefied natural gas project in Pakistan underscores close ties with the Trump administration, garment workers in Bangladesh protest for pay increases, and India’s opposition alliance rallies to call for the resignation of the chief election commissioner.

Since U.S. President Donald Trump returned to the White House last year, Pakistan has banked heavily on doing business with the United States. With the Democrats flipping both houses of Congress in the U.S. midterm elections on Tuesday, though, one has to wonder if Islamabad could come out as another loser.

For one thing, scrutiny of Trump’s business dealings will certainly increase, constraining his administration’s capacity to pursue deals abroad.

A big topic of discussion in Pakistan this week—along with the midterms—is the news that a consortium of U.S. companies has proposed a $4.4 billion liquefied natural gas (LNG) project in Port Qasim, near Karachi. It would be Pakistan’s first LNG storage facility, helping to shield the country from global energy shocks.

The proposal entails a large-scale storage terminal and eventually a 680-mile delivery pipeline. The plan is backed by the U.S. Trade and Development Agency, U.S. International Development Finance Corp., and the Export-Import Bank of the United States.

Washington’s support for the proposed LNG project underscores just how much the U.S.-Pakistan relationship has changed in Trump’s second term. Bilateral ties were downgraded after the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021, in large part because Washington had long viewed relations with Islamabad through a security lens.

The Trump administration turned this approach on its head. The United States now prioritizes ties with Pakistan, and Trump often praises the country’s leadership. Though counterterrorism still figures in the relationship, Washington has emphasized economic and commercial issues in its engagement with Islamabad.

In particular, the United States has taken a strong interest in Pakistan’s critical minerals and cryptocurrency resources. It has pushed for the country to import more U.S. goods, including energy supplies. In October 2025, Pakistan received its first-ever shipment of U.S. crude oil. Washington has pushed to remove trade obstacles with Islamabad, including ending a two-year ban on U.S. soybean exports.

Pakistan, which had long pitched its economic offerings to the United States unsuccessfully, has capitalized on the Trump administration’s newfound interest in its assets. The Financial Times reported last year that Islamabad went as far as to offer Washington the opportunity to build and manage a port on the Arabian Sea. (Pakistan publicly denies this.)

The proposed LNG project would be one of the Trump administration’s most ambitious commercial ventures in Pakistan, perhaps putting an exclamation point on the shift in the U.S. approach to the country. Yet that shift might not even be sustainable—especially given the midterm results.

The Trump administration’s commercial focus is largely driven by the president’s whims and by the economic interests of his family members and close associates. But this arrangement won’t outlast Trump’s term, which ends in two years. Further, with Congress in the hands of the Democrats starting in January, it could also become more difficult to pull off.

Conversations with Pakistani interlocutors this week revealed a combination of curiosity and concern about ties with the United States in the Trump era. There seems to be a desire to understand how the relationship improved so quickly alongside a sense that it might not last for long, given Trump’s unpredictability. The Democrats’ victory will only sharpen these concerns.

These worries reflect an evergreen challenge for the U.S.-Pakistan relationship: how to craft a strong partnership that can withstand political change in both countries and weather external shocks.

The last time the White House officially announced a policy on Pakistan was during Trump’s first term, in August 2017, when a new South Asia strategy included a hard-line approach toward Islamabad. It’s clear that the United States is overdue in conducting a formal review of its Pakistan policy, even as the relationship is being informally reevaluated and refashioned.

Garment protests in Bangladesh. Garment workers have taken to the streets in industrial areas of Dhaka, Bangladesh’s capital, in recent days to call for pay increases. Clashes with security forces have prompted the closure of dozens of garment factories and raised concerns about shipment delays.

Garment workers are a critical constituency in Bangladesh, numbering around 4 million, and the sector accounts for 80 percent of the country’s export earnings. Bangladesh’s ready-made garment industry is a global success story; it made the country one of South Asia’s best-performing economies until a slump in the last few years.

With the downturn, Bangladesh seems to be paying the price for relying so heavily on a single export—as globally competitive as it was—for so long. The protests are just the latest policy challenge for Dhaka, which is already struggling with economic stress, energy shortages, and the world’s most serious measles crisis.

These issues risk distracting Bangladesh from pursuing economic and governance reforms—something the government, which took office in February, was given a mandate to prioritize.

India’s opposition alliance rallies. On Sunday, the main opposition alliance in India staged protests in New Delhi to call for the resignation of India’s chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar. The rally drew tens of thousands of people and marked the latest mobilization against a voter roll revision that critics say favored the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party.

The youth-led Cockroach Janta Party has spearheaded the protests calling for Kumar to step down. This summer, the Cockroach movement galvanized protests over leaked exam papers that ultimately resulted in the resignation of the country’s education minister.

Now, India’s opposition is riding on the coattails of this activism. The success of the Cockroach movement is a gift to the opposition, which has long been hobbled by the absence of a popular leader and disagreements within its alliance.

Heat stress becomes serious vulnerability. The International Labor Organization (ILO) released studies last week warning that by 2040, workplace heat stress spawned by rising temperatures could lead to reduced working time equivalent to around 145,000 full-time jobs in Sri Lanka and 2,400 in the Maldives.

The reports estimate that these losses will be valued at around $26 billion in Sri Lanka and $576 million in the Maldives. The hardest-hit sector in Sri Lanka will be agriculture, along with services, manufacturing, and construction. In the Maldives, services and construction will be the most vulnerable industries.

The ILO findings are a reminder of the high economic cost of climate change in a region where economies are fragile—and where some of the most labor-dependent sectors are especially vulnerable to climate effects.

Last Sunday, around 200,000 people converged on the Bhutanese town of Gelephu to watch volunteers construct 108 stupas. (The number 108 is sacred in Buddhism.) The initiative is part of a campaign led by Bhutan’s king to transform Gelephu, near Bhutan’s border with India, into a “mindfulness city.”

Bhutan hopes to use the initiative, which emphasizes ecological sustainability and happiness, as an antidote to the country’s deepening economic stress. The stupa project is certainly an unorthodox way to tackle these problems. Critics will likely say it does little to reverse rising unemployment and a rapid outflow of highly skilled workers.

However, the project aligns with a long-standing Bhutanese principle known as “gross national happiness,” which measures national well-being not by income or other economic indicators but instead by the level of contentment among citizens.

‘How Are You Supposed to Have Sex with a Wicker Dick? I Wanted to See’

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When indie film distributor Black Bear released the first trailer for Wicker, it hit social media timelines with a bang. Based on the short story The Wicker Husband by Ursula Wills-Jones, the film follows a small village fisherwoman (Olivia Colman) who convinces a local basket weaver (Peter Dinklage) to build her a husband out of wicker (Alexander Skarsgård). Fans, naturally, went a bit feral over the scenes of Colman and a basket-ified Skarsgård consummating their marriage so rapturously they break the bed. Eventually, a common joke emerged: “Will this be available in 4DX?”

Sometimes, for the pure of heart, prayers are answered. On Oct. 28, Black Bear put Wicker in 4DX for a one-night-only event, allowing audiences to experience the low-fantasy romance complete with water, wind, snow, and all the thrusting their hearts desired. But while the idea initially stemmed from a joke, the demand was real. Tickets sold so fast that theaters extended showings until Nov. 5. And the romance fans who threw their cards down first tell Rolling Stone that what they thought might be an absurd night out turned into a raucous, communal experience — one they say highlights what audiences might want more of out of romance movies.

If you ask New York City resident Robert Belpasso, 32, he probably should’ve realized how serious people were about seeing the film in 4DX when he tried to buy tickets. Showings would populate and then sell out as he was trying to click purchase. “Initially, it was impossible to get tickets,” he says. “It was like fighting like a Ticketmaster war.”

Another New Yorker, Leen Dweik, and her friends didn’t even blink when it looked like the only available seats were at a theater in suburban New Rochelle — a 30- to 45-minute trip outside of the city. Luckily, seats opened up at a Regal in Times Square, but it really didn’t matter. They had no plans to miss it.

“I’ve never seen anything in 4DX before — I’m not necessarily interested in having things sprayed at me,” Dweik, 29, says. “But it did feel like if there was ever a film to try it for, I wanted to try it for this one. It was so clear that everybody who was there really wanted to be there. PSV Eindhoven endured a frustrating night in the Champions League as their defensive frailties were exposed in a 2-1 defeat by Club Brugge.

PSV started full of attacking intent and should have taken an early lead when Ivan Perisic put Sergiño Dest clean through on goal, but the defender’s shot was too close to goalkeeper Yann Sommer.

Instead it was the visitors who took the lead with their first real attack when Nicolo Tresoldi headed in a teasing cross from Joaquin Seys.

PSV equalised early in the second half as an outstanding pass by Sven Mijnans into the path Lutsharel Geertruida allowed the defender to charge to the byline. His pullback found its way to Kodai Sano, whose shot took a deflection off Seys into the net.

But five minutes later PSV were caught out as another razor-sharp pass by Seys released Carlos Forbs in space to net the winner.

The home side refused to give up, but were unable to find a way past Sommer in the closing stages as the Belgian side closed out the win.

Team captain Mauro Junior admitted that PSV had been undone by shortcomings at the back. “We had a lot of opportunities in the first half but we didn’t score,” he told Ziggo Sport.

“We want to attack and score goals, but we weren’ well organised today in defence. We need to keep our heads up and look forward to the next game.”

The result leaves PSV in 24th place, just above the elimination line, ahead of their next match away to Real Madrid on November 24, with four points from their opening four matches.

Viktor Gyokeres grasped his opportunity as Arsenal beat Slavia Prague 2-1 in the Champions League.

Gyokeres made the most of a rare start, his sixth of the season, with an assist and a stylish goal as the Gunners climbed to third in the Champions League table.

Noni Madueke scored the opener and Arsenal were cruising until the closing stages, when Wiktor Nowak pulled one back to leave Mikel Arteta’s men hanging on in stoppage time.

Gyokeres, whose only previous goal this season was in the Carabao Cup defeat at Fleetwood, was one of six changes Arteta made to the side which beat Liverpool 1-0 at the weekend.

Ben White and Piero Hincapie started in defence in the Czech capital with Martin Zubimendi and Mikel Merino slotting into midfield and Eberechi Eze a lively presence behind Gyokeres.

There was also a late run-out for youngster Max Dowman, who is suspended domestically but eligible for Europe following his red card at Fleetwood.

Madueke put them ahead in the ninth minute after Gyokeres muscled his marker off the ball on the halfway line and hooked the ball forward into space.

The England winger raced past the defence and slid his angled shot through the legs of Nazar Domchak.

The Slavia goalkeeper then had to come out and block from Gyokeres, tip Merino’s drive over the crossbar and make a fine reflex save from the Spain international.

Arsenal had a mild scare eight minutes before half-time when Tomas Holes got free in the area at a long throw and poked his shot wide.

But the Premier League champions immediately went up the other end and doubled their lead through Gyokeres.

The Sweden striker exchanged passes with Merino as he made his way into the area before opening up his body and clipping the ball across Domchak and in off the far post.

It was the 28-year-old’s first Champions League goal from open play since he scored against Kairat Almaty in January.

Arsenal were almost caught cold seconds after the break when Oskar Kubiak got forward and forced David Raya into his first save of the match.

Madueke almost had a second after more good play from Gyokeres, who held up Declan Rice’s pass before laying the ball off to Christos Tzolis.

Fit-again Tzolis fed Madueke out on the right and his fierce angled drive was blocked by the chest of Domchak.

Arsenal retreated for the remainder of the match and Gabriel had to clear a header from Slavia substitute Ange N’Guessan off the goal-line.

The VAR checked for a potential penalty after Merino knocked over Nowak as they challenged for the rebound, but it was deemed an accidental clash of heads.

In an unneccesarily nervous ending Raya had to make a full-stretch save to push Kubiak’s header against a post.

And the Gunners lost their clean sheet when Nowak converted David Jurasek’s cross for a deserved consolation for the hosts.

The World’s Most Famous Architects Are Building in Albania. I Had to Find Out Why

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“I'll try to focus on this, which is not the easiest thing today,” says Edi Rama, the embattled prime minister of Albania, after a night spent monitoring violent protests in the capital, Tirana. But Rama quickly warms to one of his favorite subjects. “Architecture,” he says, choosing his words carefully, “has the power to change societies. It’s like a strong medicine. It can heal, or it can kill.”

A journalist who covers architecture, I had been hearing about a building boom in Albania for several years. It seemed like half the architects I knew in New York were on a first-name basis with the prime minister, a working artist and, at 6’-7”, a former pro basketball player. So on October 19 I flew to Tirana—a short side-trip from Greece, where I was on vacation with my husband—to look at some of the new buildings and interview Rama about them. But I arrived during a season of civil unrest. The prime minister, whose assistant had told I needn’t postpone the trip because everything was fine, had moved to a secure location on the outskirts of the city; some of the buildings I had come to see had just been vandalized by protesters; and at one point I was accused, publicly, of aiding a government spy. (The charge was— need I even say this—absurd.) And, because I’m in the early stages of Parkinson’s disease, I experienced all this from a four-wheeled mobility scooter, which I had rented for about $100.

As I tooled around the city center, much of it seemed unaffected by the protests. In fact, most of Tirana was charming. But weren’t those Molotov cocktails we heard going off during an al fresco dinner? Those weren’t on my drinks menu.

What was on my menu in Tirana, a city of about 600,000 in a country of about 2.3 million, was confusion. Confusion about why the protesters were so angry, and about why the prime minister seemed so unconcerned. In his 13 years in office, Rama, 62, has been remaking as much of Albania as possible with the help of foreign architects. Even the heavily guarded villa where he agreed to be interviewed, a raw concrete building dyed bright red, is largely the work of the Spanish architect Pablo Bofill.

When protesters began demanding Rama’s resignation last May, he was about to host his second annual gathering of architects from around the world, one of the high points of his year. Architects like the Danish superstar Bjarke Ingels and the Chilean Alejandro Aravena, who won the Pritzker Prize in 2016, “pay to come here and talk and they love it,” Rama says. But this year the get-together, from June 3 to 5, drew protesters with signs that read “Starchitects go home!” Those protests presaged the more violent demonstrations in October and November.

I haven’t attended either of Rama’s conclaves, but what I’ve heard reminds me of my visit to Ordos, Inner Mongolia, in 2008, which I wrote about for The New York Times. A wealthy industrialist, aided by the artist Ai Weiwei, had brought 100 young architects from around the world to the isolated desert city. Their task was to design 100 villas for anonymous (and seemingly nonexistent) Chinese millionaires. The architects picked numbers out of a bowl to determine which of the 100 sites was theirs, and before flying home they were paid for their designs with wads of cash. None of the villas was built, but being in Ordos was a bonding experience for a generation of architects, a few of whom have recently been to Tirana. At least until the latest protests, the mood among Rama-anointed architects—giddy optimism coupled with uncertainty about what would actually be built—echoed the mood in Ordos almost 20 years ago. Both situations felt surreal.

Rama normally works in an office on the Boulevard of Martyrs of the Nation in the center of Tirana. But on October 20 the boulevard's sidewalks were missing hundreds of paving stones, which protesters had used to shatter storefronts. (Some that weren’t shattered bore hastily made signs telling protesters, “Don’t target us. We are with you.”) And some of the stones were reportedly thrown at police during nighttime clashes that authorities say left hundreds injured. Police have used tear gas and rubber bullets as protesters set small buildings, like an information kiosk in the vast Skanderbeg Square, on fire. Near the shell of the kiosk, workers scrubbed fresh graffiti off a statue of Skanderbeg himself.

The Tirana Pyramid, built by followers of the brutal dictator Enver Hoxha, who died in 1985, as his memorial, and turned into a public amenity by the Rotterdam firm MVRDV, was also damaged.

There’s a reason some Albanians—it’s impossible to say how many—resent the foreign architects and the attention lavished on them. In the 26 years since he became the mayor of Tirana and then prime minister, Rama has initiated or encouraged more than 50 international architecture competitions for projects ranging from parks to museums to large housing developments. Forty-eight of those were won by foreign architects (some working with Albanian firms), according to Adelina Greca, who leads the National Territorial Planning Agency, which organizes competitions for the government. Only two were won by Albanian firms working alone. In addition, Rama has played matchmaker, giving private developers the names of foreign architects he strongly recommends they hire.

As a result, Tirana’s compact city center is filled with buildings in bright colors and novel shapes. There is a so-called vertical forest, a skyscraper laden with trees and shrubs, by the Italian architect Stefano Boeri. The green-colored building is just a stone’s throw from the bright red Marriott by another Italian architect, Marco Casamonti. The Plaza Hotel, by architect Johan Anrys, of the Belgian firm 51N4E, starts as an ellipse at its base and morphs into a rectangle at its crown. But the champion attention-getter is a condo building by MVRDV in the shape of a head. Its facade and balconies resolve into facial features, most conspicuously a pronounced nose. The architects have publicly identified it as the head of Skanderbeg, a 15th century military hero. But some Albanians think it’s Rama. Both ideas are “urban legends,” says the prime minister. “It’s the form of a man through history,” he says, contradicting the architects’ own statement. Nodding at this reporter, he says, “It also looks like you."

Tirana's Rock (aka the Skandebeg building) is an MVRDV-designed condo in the shape of a head

A Marriott designed by Marco Casamonti of Archea Associati, left, and the “vertical forest” skyscraper by Stefano Boeri

The vertical forest, the Marriott and the Plaza Hotel are all handsome additions to the city. So, too, is the “head,” but is it too much of an architectural one-liner? Who, I wonder as I pass it several times a day, is going to want to live in the nose?

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.”

Anrys, who 22 years ago became one of the first foreign architects in Albania, told me that working there has taught him "what architecture truly means for improving society.” He said he knows that Albanian architects wonder, "Are we not good enough?" and believe we're taking jobs from them. "It isn't true. I have a studio in Tirana with 20 Albanian architects." Overall he says, the foreign architects have “brought Albanian architects more work than we've taken."

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.” The rest, he explains, see that having first-rate architects from abroad, including Herzog & de Meuron, OMA, and Diller Scofidio & Renfro, working in Albania will raise everyone’s game.

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.”

The foreign architects who win competitions are required to enlist architects licensed in Albania to help them execute their plans. Rama compares the situation to that of China, which 20 years ago was giving its most prominent building commissions to foreign architects, including some of the same ones working in Albania today. Chinese architects, Rama says, got a free education from the western masters.

“If we continue this kind of cooperation,” says Greca, who has worked for Rama almost continuously since 2003, “there will come a day when Albanian architects will win competitions not only here but around the world.”

Anti-government protesters on the streets of Tirana, with the art installation Tirana Moons by Austrian architect Chris Precht in the background

But Greca’s association with Rama has made her a controversial figure among local architects. On my first day in town, our route intersects that of a lunchtime protest march. We stop to watch. Greca notices many architects and architecture students in the crowd. (The Faculty of Architecture and Urbanism of the Polytechnic University of Tirana is nearby.)

Within minutes, Gent Progni, an organizer of the march, posts a photo of Greca and me on Instagram. The caption claims she had sunk so low as to use a disabled person (me) as cover to spy on the marchers. (Ten days later, the post has almost 7,000 “likes.”) Later, in a text responding to questions from this reporter, Progni wrote:

“My opposition is not to foreign architects but to the way Edi Rama uses prestigious international names to legitimize controversial development projects, often without transparency, meaningful public consultation, or accountability. This is a significant issue for our protest movement, but it is part of a much larger struggle against corruption, the concentration of power, and the sale of Albania’s public wealth.”

Rama himself isn’t happy with all of the new buildings in Tirana. He complained about one whose facades are made of stone “until a certain altitude,” where the cost-cutting developer switched to a printed material that only looks like stone. Other buildings stand partially complete, or complete but eerily empty. And in several cases developers have replaced foreign architects with local ones midstream, in order to complete buildings without meeting the original design specifications. “We need the investors to stick with the architects,” Rama says. But funding is less available than he would like. “Certain architecture needs a certain quality,” he says, philosophically. “But compromises are forced by the limitations of the place.”

The problems with his building boom go deeper than facade materials. There are accusations of money laundering and other forms of corruption, which grew louder at the end of May, when Rama agreed to support efforts by a company associated with Jared Kushner and Ivanka Trump to build luxury resorts on previously undeveloped land. It was a coincidence that the big architecture conference started just a few days later, leading directly to the calls for starchitects to scram. Among the protesters, that demand became entangled with environmental and anti-Trump sentiments in what became known as the Flamingo Revolution.

A housing project by Madrid-based firm SelgasCano

European journalist and curator Anneke Abhelakh has recently published a brilliantly illuminating book, The Albanian Files, which gives 60 foreign architecture firms eight or more pages to describe the work they are doing in Albania. Altogether, the book lists more than 500 projects. Of those, only about one-tenth have been realized. Rama says he is looking forward to the completion of many more.

But can that really happen, given the pressure to resign? The prime minister, who won the most recent election by the biggest majority of his career, says he isn’t going anywhere.

In fact, seemingly oblivious to the mood on the street, he wants to further celebrate his stable of foreign architects. Near the end of my half-hour interview, he mentions that he is thinking of turning The Albanian Files into a museum, with one room for each of the 60 foreign firms to show drawings, models and photographs of their work. He said he already has a site for what he says will be “a big store of dreams, hopes and challenges, illusions and delusions. It will be a great place to go.” Its designer will be chosen, naturally, in an international architectural competition.

I left Albania with lots of questions and thousands of photos to sort through. In one of them I have my arm around Rama. Why the affectionate pose? A reflex when the camera comes out. But I suppose also that, politics aside, I can’t hate a man who loves architecture.
