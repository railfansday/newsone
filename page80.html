Puka Nacua’s suspension won’t impact his availability for Seahawks games

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Marred with injuries, it's been a frustrating season for the Los Angeles Rams' wide receiver corps thus far and already without its top two pass catchers — their situation nearly just got worse.

Puka Nacua has been suspended three games without pay for violating the league's personal conduct policy, the NFL announced on Thursday. In addition, the league reportedly communicated to the superstar wideout that any further violation "will subject him to additional and more substantial discipline, including banishment from the League."

The move stems from an apparent incident on New Year's Eve 2025, where a woman alleged he bit her on the shoulder and made an antisemitic remark. Although she filed a civil lawsuit against Nacua on March 25, his attorney denied any remarks were made and classified the alleged assault as "horseplay."

Despite currently being on injured reserve with MCL and PCL sprains as well as turf toe, the suspension will take effect immediately — making Nacua eligible to be reinstated ahead of Los Angeles' Week 15 game against the Dallas Cowboys — one week before they're set to visit the Seattle Seahawks.

It currently remains unclear if he'll be cleared to suit up when first eligible to return.

However, considering the Rams placed him on injured reserve ahead of Week 10, he was already poised to miss a minimum of four weeks. In essence, Nacua won't miss any time as a result of the suspension.

As for the financial punishment, he was fined $961, 167 — a sixth of his 2026 base salary. There is precedent for players like Nacua getting suspended while on IR, although the other examples are not nearly as prominent in the NFL.

In his continued absence, Los Angles will seemingly continue to lean on a mix of veterans and far less experienced receivers — although WR Davante Adams (ruptured hamstring) could potentially return in the coming weeks and take on the lead role until Nacua is eligible to take the field.

NFL puts product over principles with Puka Nacua suspension | Opinion

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The NFL is tarnishing its precious shield with its suspension* of Puka Nacua – even more than the Los Angeles Rams wide receiver did with his reprehensible behavior.

The league has known since March that Nacua was being sued by a woman who said the player had bitten her on the shoulder during a New Year's Eve dinner, where he also allegedly said something antisemitic. But it wasn't until Thursday, Nov. 19, when Nacua is conveniently already on injured reserve, that the NFL saw fit to suspend him.

Follow along while I do the math: NFL players on IR have to miss a minimum four games before they can return. Nacua, who missed the first of those four games last week while dealing with a knee injury, is now suspended for the next three weeks.

Which means Nacua won't miss any more time than the Rams were already accounting for, and they – and the league – will get one of their biggest and most-productive stars back, healthy and rested, just in time for 6-4 Los Angeles to make a push for the playoffs.

Unless you're a Rams fan, that stinks to high heaven.

What Nacua did was apparently bad enough to merit a three-game suspension, but not bad enough for the NFL to shut him down when it would actually matter? So what, exactly, is the point of suspending Nacua at all? Because it sure doesn't tell him, or anyone else, that there are consequences for unacceptable behavior.

(Nacua's suspension* will cost him close to $1 million in lost salary. That'll be pocket change when he signs a new contract in the offseason, to say nothing of the endorsement money he's still getting.)

Apparently, the story the NFL is spinning is that if Nacua messes up again, the NFL disciplinarians are really going to let him have it! They pinky swear it!

Yes, the leagues' collective bargaining agreement with its players allows players to serve suspensions while on IR. That's not the point. The point is everyone has known for almost nine months that Nacua was going to be suspended, and the NFL just so happened to choose the least-inconvenient time possible, for both Nacua and the Rams, to do it. A time that helps preserve Los Angeles' hopes for a Super Bowl 61 appearance at SoFi Stadium, its home arena.

Other suspensions have been dragged out because of ongoing legal cases. That's not an issue, here, however. Nacua was never charged criminally. The civil lawsuit against him has not been resolved. If the NFL has already waited nine months to hand down punishment everyone was expecting, what difference would it have made for the league to wait another three weeks?

Except for the impact it would have on the Rams' playoff chances, that is, and the all-important postseason intrigue for a major-market NFL team.

It is also true that other players have served suspensions while on IR. According to Adam Schefter, Daniel Ekuale was given five games for violating the performance-enhancing substances policy after suffering a season-ending injury last year. In 2024, Sam Williams was suspended three games for violating the personal conduct policy while out for the season with a torn ACL.

Mike Woods got six games in 2023 for violating the personal conduct policy when he was already out with a torn Achilles. In 2018, David Parry was suspended for four games for violating both the personal conduct and PED policies when his season had already ended because of an ankle injury.

What's the difference between them and Nacua? Ekuale, a defensive lineman, spent seven years in the league, making 21 starts in 64 games and recording six sacks. Williams, a defensive end, has five starts in 58 games over four seasons. Woods, a receiver, had 12 catches in 15 games. Parry, a two-season starter at nose tackle in Indianapolis, played in four games his final two seasons.

Nacua is a two-time Pro Bowler who last year led the league with 129 catches and also had 1,715 yards receiving and 10 touchdowns. He's started all but one game he's played in his first four seasons, including seven this year. Despite missing last week, Nacua is fourth in the NFL with 70 catches.

Nacua shouldn't be punished more harshly because he's one of the NFL's top players. But neither should his star status earn him any favors. The NFL is insulting everyone's intelligence by claiming to take anything that reflects poorly on the league very, very seriously and then turning around and making sure Nacua's punishment won't cause anyone any real hardship.

The NFL's justice system has always been arbitrary. Turns out, it's not very just, either.

How the Lakers' Quentin Grimes wants to maintain his hot shooting, even off the bench

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

When Lakers public address announcer Derrin Horton introduced Quentin Grimes in the starting lineup Wednesday, the easy-going Lakers guard was met with an unusually loud ovation. Dropping a career-high 47 points with 10 made three-pointers less than 24 hours before warranted such a reception.

Grimes has used a recent promotion to the starting lineup to fuel a career scoring binge. But his official place in the starting lineup could be short-lived when Austin Reaves returns from a left heel bruise that's kept him sidelined for three games. No matter if Grimes gets his name announced at the beginning of the game or comes off the bench, he wants to maintain his rediscovered rhythm.

"I feel like we all respect each other's games, we know what everybody can do," Grimes said Wednesday after he scored 20 points in the Lakers' loss to the Utah Jazz. "JJ [Redick] gives everybody credit to go out there, no matter who it is, to be themselves, so I feel like whenever we get back whole I'll still be able to keep playing how I've been playing."

Reaves, who was injured during the third quarter against Milwaukee on Nov. 11, did on-court work Wednesday morning before the Lakers' game against the Jazz, and Redick said it went "well" but didn't have any further idea about Reaves' availability for Friday's NBA Cup game against the Sacramento Kings at 7 p.m. at Crypto.com Arena.

In the three games without Reaves, Grimes has flourished, averaging 29.7 points, 6.3 rebounds and 2.7 assists while shooting 57.4% from the field and 63.3% from three-point range.

It's not just the "statement" night in which he joined Kobe Bryant as the only Lakers players to make 10 or more three-pointers in a single game. In five starts this season, Grimes is averaging 24.8 points and shooting 55.3% from three compared with 5.9 points and 24% three-point shooting off the bench. The Lakers are 4-1 in games Grimes has started.

Lakers guard Quentin Grimes, shooting over Jazz center Jaxson Hayes, scored 20 points on Wednesday. (Eric Thayer / Los Angeles Times)

Grimes credited his recent success to finding a rhythm after a slow start to the season in which he was eight-for-33 shooting from three in the first 11 games before missing two games because of a sprained right pinky. During his career, Grimes has averaged 14.3 points per start compared with 8.5 points coming off the bench with 59.6% and 57.8% true shooting percentage, respectively.

Grimes has made a career out of shape-shifting. The 25th pick in the 2021 draft once was just trying to crack the New York Knicks rotation as a three-and-D player. With Dallas, where he played briefly with Luka Doncic, and Philadelphia, he had the ball in his hands more often, jumping between the starting lineup and the bench.

Now with two dynamic scorers in Doncic and Reaves, the Lakers are searching for new ways to let Grimes shine.

"A big part is like for him if he's gonna play off-ball, he needs to feel like he's touched the ball and he feels like he's in a rhythm," Redick said. "So, again, this is a new team, and we're feeling out a bunch of stuff for each individual guy. Our job as coaches is try to maximize each player and maximize the group, and there's a learning curve to that."

Grimes, whose relationship with Redick started in 2023 when Grimes visited Redick to work out one summer but accidentally wandered into his future coach's neighbor's house in the Hamptons, has cherished the open line of communication with the coaching staff.

"It's a good problem to have: a coach that wants to get you the ball, wants to get you into a rhythm, stay in that rhythm that I've been in," Grimes said. "So I feel like it's a good thing. He's really good at what he does. We're rolling right now. So I have no doubt he'll be able to keep me in this rhythm once we all get healthy."

Grimes was thought to be a strong contender for an opening day starting role next to Reaves and Doncic. But the Lakers started wings Matisse Thybulle and Ziaire Williams first along with center Walker Kessler, and then inserted 6-foot-8 forward Jake LaRavia in Williams' place when the Lancaster native missed three games because of a left foot injury.

Redick has said previously he liked the balance of those lineups: Reaves and Doncic for offensive punch, two perimeter, defensive-minded wings to back them up and hit open three-pointers and Kessler to anchor the middle.

The Lakers' lineup with Doncic, Reaves, Thybulle, LaRavia and Kessler still ranks second in net rating among five-man groups that have played more than 50 minutes together.

Instead of focusing on the starting unit, Redick wants to keep tinkering with lineups to find more minutes with neither Doncic nor Reaves on the court. Coaches think those moments could help other players get into the rhythm on their own so that during the second half when the staff is deciding which lineup will finish the game, everyone will be in a good flow.

"I think with this group and the way this group [is], it's just an egoless group," Redick said. "We're going to be able to be flexible with the starting lineup and flexible with matchups."

Japan core inflation soars to highest this year as energy subsidy expiry pushes up consumer prices

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Core inflation in Japan soared to a fresh high in 2026, coming in at 2.2% in October as higher energy costs and their knock-on effects pushed up prices in the wider economy.

Core inflation, which strips out prices of food, was lower than the 2.3% expected by Reuters-polled economists, but was higher than the 2.1% in prior month.

Headline inflation came in at 2.3% compared with September's 2.1%, while the so-called "core-core" inflation, which strips out prices of fresh food and energy, dipped slightly to 2.2% from 2.4% in the month before, government data released on Friday showed.

Energy subsidies for households expired in September, exposing consumers to higher prices brought about by the Iran war. Energy prices climbed 2.2% in October, a sharp spike from the 0.6% decline in September. Gas prices jumped 5.8% compared to the 2.4% rise the month before.

Fuel, light and water charges also saw a considerable acceleration, increasing 3.4% compared to last month's 0.8%.

Japan has reportedly approved reintroduction of energy subsidies in December to stave off higher imported energy prices as higher prices crimp growth, hitting consumption and net exports.

October inflation reading is likely to push the Bank of Japan toward another rate hike in December, despite an unexpected contraction in economic growth in the third quarter. Data from LSEG indicates that there is a 75% chance of a 25 basis point hike from the Bank of Japan."The BOJ is likely to look through the temporary weakness in 3Q GDP and focus instead on underlying inflation and wage dynamics," Ma Tieying, senior economist at DBS Group Research, wrote in a Monday note.

People who practice animal law depend on scholars and advocates in other fields to provide the information they need to do their important work, but does this advice really reduce or end exploitation? In their fascinating and important new book Empirical Animal Law: Testing the Assumptions of a Movement, Justin Marceau, David A. Dana, and Janice Nadler offer answers to this and other important questions by insisting that lawyers working in this field must empirically test their assumptions and learn from their mistakes.

This book should be read not only by lawyers and legal scholars but also by researchers like me. It's heartening and extremely important to see actual data about the effectiveness of research in the field of cognitive ethology, the study of animal minds, and whether or not what we learn makes a difference in the lives of the animals themselves. I remain optimistic that their detailed analyses will be a positive move for increasing animal well-being across diverse species.

Here's what Marceau had to say about this first comprehensive, data-driven account of how animal law operates in practice in which he and his co-authors call for a new empirically informed movement. This book is a gold mine of information and I couldn't agree more with what they write.

Marc Bekoff: Why did you and your colleagues write Empirical Animal Law?

Justin Marceau: It is hardly controversial to recognize that the law does a poor job of protecting the lives and interests of animals. The law allows activists who attempted to rescue beagles from cruelty to be treated as criminals, while shielding directly or through practice the very institutions that harm animals. Factory farming has increased across the U.S. in recent decades. Animals continue to be bred for our fashion, consumption, entertainment, research, and sport. And almost all of this is perfectly legal.

Much of this harm remains invisible to the public despite decades of work by activists and lawyers. For some in the movement, this means that all hope is lost. There are people out there claiming that animal protection efforts have failed. Our book is a bit more optimistic, or at least focused on what is working and what is not working.

Specifically, we are legal scholars, including Janice Nadler, a renowned social scientist, and we wanted to study the strategies and assumptions of animal advocates. What kind of assumptions are we making about how best to make progress for animals, and what could be done better? In short, we wrote the book to test the assumptions of our movement about what is working and what does not work.

JM: It is an academic book so we expect it to be useful to researchers. But we also wrote the book in an accessible format so that it can be picked up and discussed by animal advocates and lawyers across the country. Our critiques and guidance are borne out of the more than a dozen original empirical studies we conducted for the book, and we hope that the data we share in the book will help inform the next generation of advocacy on behalf of animals.

MB: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

JM: The book has several different types of studies and analysis: (1) studies on the role of incremental reform in achieving more radical reform or animal rights; (2) factors that influence our opinions about the moral standing of animals; (3) the impacts for animals and humans of a carceral or tough-on-crime approach to animal law; (4) the role of protests in social change; (5) the theory of “winning by losing” in litigation or lawmaking; and (6) questions of who are the best messengers and what are the best messages for animal advocacy and outreach.

We think that each of these topics and studies brings new insights into questions that the animal protection field has either ignored or taken for granted. The major theme of the book, captured by the subtitle, is: Testing the Assumptions of a Movement.

One set of studies that we think are particularly important examines the relationship between an animal’s perceived moral standing and their Intelligence, emotional intelligence, or sentience.

Animal lawyers and advocates have long assumed that the law will "follow the science.” That is to say, the law will inevitably provide more protection as we learn more about the intelligence or capabilities of animals. "Just show them more science!" it is often proclaimed.

Our studies, drawing on thousands of participants, suggest that learning more about the inner lives of animals probably does matter, but it is complicated.

Intelligence, for example, does raise moral concern for animals, but people may disregard such findings for animals they already eat, though not always, as our results for fish show. Thus, in our studies, describing pigs as highly intelligent did not significantly raise their moral standing.

Information demonstrating that an animal can feel pain, on the other hand, increased the perceived moral standing for every species we tested, including pigs and cows. And that is a pretty remarkable finding. Informing people about scientific evidence of sentience can significantly increase the animal’s perceived moral standing.

Perhaps the most intriguing finding in this section is that learning about an animal's emotional life (that the animal is loving and cooperative, and mourns its dead) may be the most powerful factor of all. In our studies, learning about the emotional lives of animals increased moral concern even for cows, an animal most people regularly eat.

The idea that animal emotions or emotional intelligence can impact or challenge our entrenched habits of consumption or compassion for animals is fascinating to us. So far as we know, we are the first to empirically test this idea and we think it deserves considerably more study.

MB: How does your work differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

JM: To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive effort to take up the key assumptions of animal advocates and subject them to empirical scrutiny. We can’t wait to hear what folks think about what we have found!

MB: Are you hopeful that, as people learn which kinds of information about animals’ minds, emotions, and sentience actually shift attitudes, researchers will do more to make their work accessible to a wider audience?

JM: This is key. Our research suggests that learning about the inner lives of animals can make a difference in how people perceive animals. This means that animal protection efforts need to do more to understand the science about animal lives and to translate it into forms that are digestible to the public at large.

Anthropic becomes one of world's most valuable public companies in stock market debut

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic has made one of the fastest trips from start up to IPO in history

Anthropic has become one of the most valuable public companies in the world after its shares began trading on the stock market, taking its valuation to roughly $1.76 trillion (Â£1.34 trillion).

The artificial intelligence (AI) company's Thursday debut on the Nasdaq stock exchange was a crucial test for the industry, given the huge amount of money being poured into advancing the technology.

Anthropic's shares flew past its allocation price of $83 each, showing there was plenty of excitement around AI.

Yet the company, which is behind AI chatbot Claude, will remain under intense scrutiny in the months ahead to answer the key question surrounding AI: is it all worth it?

In just a few hours of trading, Anthropic's stock seemed to show that investors remain uncertain of where exactly AI is heading. The share price rose immediately to $111.53, sending the company's market value to almost $2 trillion, before falling by the end of the day, with shares closing at $97.17.

Some investors said Anthropic's debut seemed to be a success, as shares rose roughly 17% during the day, but the broader tech market did not perform as well.

The tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite Index closed down nearly 2.3%. Stocks fell in the largest tech firms, including Google's parent company Alphabet, which was down by 2.9%, while Amazon lost almost 3.9%.

The buildout happening around this era of AI is among the most expensive endeavours in the history of technology, with more than $1 trillion being spent in the last year alone by a handful of major tech companies.

The speed at which investment has grown has led to ongoing concerns of a financial bubble.

"Bubbles don't burst on the day of the largest deal," Richard Whittle, an economist with the University of Salford said. "They burst later, if the positive future becomes suspect."

Simon Jack: Can Anthropic's record-breaking IPO stop the AI bubble bursting?

While the basics of the technology have been around for decades, the more recent advent of more powerful computers and related components have allowed the processing of more data than ever.

The result has been AI chatbots that simulate human speech and operate more quickly tasks like software coding, digital image generation, and searching the internet.

It represents a new way of using computers that has led to the rapid rise of Anthropic, which started just five years ago and is best known as the maker of the popular AI chatbot Claude.

As the first purely AI company to go public, Anthropic will almost certainly see significant swings in price on the stock market going forward, according to Ben Barringer, who leads technology research at Quilter Cheviot.

The "initial exuberance" will wear off, Barringer said, pointing to what happened with SpaceX earlier this year.

That company, led by Elon Musk, saw shares jump by about 10% on the first day of trading, taking the company to a record-breaking valuation of $2.2 trillion.

Within a month shares had fallen below their debut price, and have mostly fallen further since.

Most tech companies see their shares fall after hitting the stock market, often after reporting quarterly results for the first time.

But many such companies, including Meta, Amazon and Google, have gone on to become the largest and most profitable corporations in the world.

With Anthropic, investors are not only looking at a notable tech company with a major valuation, and a potentially big future in business, but a company that is the first of its kind on the stock market.

"There's no obvious peer to compare it to," Neil Wilson, an investor strategist at Saxo UK, said.

So, as individual investors and even researchers and investors at large funds ask themselves if Anthropic is really worth more than $1.7 trillion, or even more, there will be few answers available.

"We'll have a stab at it," Wilson said. "I don't think anyone really knows."

A few things will likely impact the company's success on the stock market in the near-term.

One of those things is the prospect of new laws governing the use of AI tools. In the US, where Anthropic is based, Democrats will be in many more positions of power come 2027, and several politicians have already said they will be looking to ensure AI tools are safe and have some guardrails attached.

Such regulation is currently unlikely at the federal level. US President Donald Trump on Thursday spoke about AI during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in China, alongside Chinese and Russian counterparts, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin.

Trump reiterated his position against "any form of robust regulation" around the technology, adding, "we won't allow that to happen, frankly."

The same day, however, a bipartisan group of US lawmakers formed the House AI Safety and Innovation Caucus, which will work specifically on AI regulation and safety considerations.

A significant driver of this kind of scrutiny is Anthropic itself. Company executives, including co-founder and CEO Dario Amodei, have been outspoken in their purported fears that future AI tools will become so capable they will outsmart humans, to the point where extinction is possible.

Anthropic has always put itself forward in public as an AI company that takes the safety of its technology very seriously.

Daniela Amodei, Anthropic's president and sister of Dario Amodei, boasted on Thursday that the company "had an AI safety team before we launched our first product".

Despite the company's claims that the technology it is building at a rapid pace could be dangerous, Anthropic has made one of the quickest trips from start-up to IPO in history.

The company reported second-quarter earnings before certain costs such as stock compensation of 94 cents per share, easily beating the analyst consensus estimate of 81 cents. Revenue rose 17%, to $496 million, surpassing the Street’s target of $487 million.

The results helped Elastic bounce back into profitability. Its net income for the quarter came to $171 million, rebounding from a loss of $51.3 million in the same period one year ago.

However, investors were less than impressed with the company’s guidance. For the quarter in progress, Elastic said it’s looking for earnings of between 90 cents and 92 cents per share on sales of $507.5 million to $508.5 million. That’s some way below the Street’s targets of 95 cents in earnings and $517.6 million in revenue. The company is not helped by the weak Euro, with currency conversions likely to cause a $4.5 million headwind on revenue in the quarter.

The full-year picture is not much better, as Elastic forecast annual sales of between $2 billion and $2.01 billion compared with the Street’s model of $2.01 billion.

Netherlands-based Elastic is one of Europe’s most successful software companies. It’s the developer of the popular open-source enterprise search platform Elasticsearch, which is used by thousands of businesses all over the world. The base platform is free to download and use, with Elastic making money by selling a special cloud-hosted version that ships with premium features not available in the open edition. The company also sells application observability and security tools.

In the last couple of years, Elastic has tried to cash in on the artificial intelligence boom by positioning Elasticsearch as a useful cog in the wheel that feeds real-time data into large language models to provide them with business context. A key capability is Elastic Context Engine, which gathers, structures, and serves business-specific context to AI agents so they can make accurate decisions with fewer tokens and lower latency.

Unfortunately for Kulkarni, investors didn’t seem to share his enthusiasm. The market was clearly expecting trouble on the horizon in the hours before the company published its report, as its stock declined 8% during regular trading. Once the results were out, it fell another 8% after-hours. That means Elastic’s stock is now up just over 1% in the year to date.

Elastic’s stock had rallied more than 150% between April and October, but virtually all of those gains have now been erased. Prior to today, the shares had already lost more than 20% this month amid broader concerns about the AI industry. Some investors continue to worry about the potential of AI technology to challenge the business models of software companies.

A new series will send Jessica Jones on a tour of the Marvel Universe, distancing the acerbic private eye-cum-superhero from her traditional stomping ground of New York City. Now, Marvel has revealed Jess' new costume for the upcoming adventure.

Jessica Jones turns twenty-five this year, first debuting in 2001's Alias #1 by Brian Michael Bendis and Michael Gaydos. Played by Krysten Ritter in the Jessica Jones TV show, the character typically stalks around New York in a leather jacket, jeans and boots.

Coming December 2026, Jessica Jones: Alias #1 reunites Bendis and Gaydos for a new adventure with their iconic creation. Now, in a new interview with Marvel, Bendis reveals the plan to force Jessica out of her comfort zone, theming the series on the idea that "Jessica can't get home." The hero's newest adventure will see her stranded in locations including the Savage Land, space and Doctor Doom's Latveria.

Michael Gaydos' cover for Jessica Jones: Alias #3 shows Jessica Jones in a leopard-print costume, standing beside a roaring tiger. In Marvel lore, the Savage Land is a hidden jungle in the depths of Antarctica, where dinosaurs rule and heroes including Kevin Plunder's Ka-Zar and Shanna the She-Devil hold sway.

The series is likely to push Jessica to the limit, as her adventures prevent her from seeing her family - husband Luke Cage, daughter Danielle and recently adopted son Joe (the biological son of longtime enemy the Purple Man.)

The family are shown together in a preview of the issue (below), with Jessica also shown remonstrating with a Doombot and crashing a meeting of New York's crime bosses, including Mr. Negative, the Owl, the White Rabbit and the Rose - a major figure in Bendis' beloved Daredevil run.

The preview also shows Jessica being approached by a new client, likely beginning her voyage across Marvel lore. Speaking to Marvel, Bendis says that Jessica's journey will allow him to visit past stories and characters:

I got excited about that idea because, yes, it's a struggle for the character, but it also allows me to look around the Marvel Universe, which I’ve been a part of, on and off, for many years. I kind of used the character to look around and observe, or revisit certain places that I had not been able to visit in a while.

Bendis also wants the anniversary return to be a celebration of the character and her journey so far, praising what other creative teams have done with the character in the years since her debut.

What I wanted to do was not only celebrate everything we did when we started, but to my complete delight, Jessica has been in the hands of some of my absolute favorite writers over the years: Kelly Thompson, Gail Simone, Sam Humphries. It has just been a delight to watch her being shepherded so carefully. When she debuted, she was reflecting a unique flavor, and now, she has evolved with the times.

What I wanted to do was tell a story that wasn't like, "Hey, let's flash back to that story we never told you from 25 years ago," but be very forward in momentum about who Jessica is today, what she wants, what she's fearful of losing, and what she can do. This is a Jessica whose husband was just Mayor of New York. They're now parents with very unique children, but they also want to continue their journey as heroes and investigators. I was eager to revisit the characters, having gone from where Luke and Jessica were emotionally in the first issue of Alias to now, and it just reflects how much they've grown and how much they've grown together.

The idea of characters getting specific costumes for adventures set in the Savage Land is an old one, applied nowhere more famously than Rogue. Debuting in 1990's Uncanny X-Men #269, Rogue's costume was shredded, giving her what many fans consider one of her most iconic looks.

Marvel has revisited the look multiple times - most recently in 2025's Rogue: The Savage Land - with variant covers and statues frequently celebrating the design. However, the costume is controversial with many fans, acting as a locus for debates around objectification in superhero media.

While Jessica Jones has been a sexual character since Alias - which was published under Marvel's mature reader MAX imprint - she's also a character defined by agency and significant trauma around the subject. Her costume design has been a major aspect of her characterization since the very beginning, whether as a PI or during her flashback appearances as Jewel and Knightress.

It's likely that Jessica Jones' new look will be used to critique or parody how Marvel has handled Rogue's Savage Land redesign, with Rogue as a character who quickly became acclimatized to the Savage Land, while Bendis states, "I don't think there's any place on the planet that Jessica wants to be in less."

Coming later this year, Marvel will celebrate Jessica Jones' anniversary with a new series designed to push her as far outside her comfort zone as possible.

Put a capital gain into an opportunity zone fund only if you’d reinvest that money anyway and can leave it there for 10 years. For money invested from 2027, the tax on the gain is put off for five years and trimmed, not erased. The fund’s own growth goes tax-free only after a decade.

The 2025 tax law that made opportunity zones permanent set those terms, and they start on Jan. 1, 2027, when the Treasury’s new zones take effect.

After you sell stock, a business, or property at a gain, you have 180 days to put that gain into a qualified opportunity fund, which invests in designated low-income areas called opportunity zones. The tax on the gain is deferred for five years. Then 10% of the gain goes untaxed, or 30% if the fund keeps at least 90% of its assets in rural zones. Hold the fund at least 10 years, and its growth is tax-free when you sell, though growth after its 30th year is taxed.

Say a fund at least holds its value, and you put a $200,000 gain from a 2027 stock sale into it. Your 2032 return reports $180,000 of that gain, and that tax comes due while the fund still holds your money. Sell after 10 years, in 2037 at the earliest, and the fund’s growth is tax-free.

The switch to the new rules makes timing matter. Gains already deferred under the old rules become taxable on Dec. 31, 2026, and IRS guidance bars rolling them into a new fund. A 2026 gain whose 180 days run into January 2027 can wait and go in under the new rules.

Only the gain qualifies, so any extra money you add counts as a separate investment with no tax break. Getting the full break also means leaving your money in projects in low-income areas for a decade, and the manager’s fees cut your return. The state securities regulators’ association warns that you could escape the tax and still lose your whole investment.

So judge the fund as an investment first. Read what it will build and where, look up its manager and anyone selling it to you on FINRA’s BrokerCheck, and get every fee in writing.

Asterid is the future mother of Katniss who first began dating Burdock during the events of the second Quarter Quell. She is also a close friend of the Donner twins, standing with them at the reaping and sharing an emotional goodbye after Maysilee’s name was called.
