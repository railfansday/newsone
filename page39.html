Fontaines D.C. Is in World-Beating Form on Dopamine Chamber Tour: 7 Best Moments From Amsterdam

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The band's European tour is kicking off a lengthy global jaunt.

For the past decade, the idea of a rock band once again capturing or defining the zeitgeist has been considered fanciful at best, or otherwise deluded. Genres rise and fall with popularity, but rock? The chances were slim, with culture geared towards solo pop acts, rapid rises for rappers and dance crossovers.

Now, we have Fontaines D.C. to fly the flag. Since 2019, the Irish five-piece has worked diligently to become a contender: debut album Dogrel was a ramshackle arrival and 2020’s A Hero’s Death cut through despite the industry stasis of the pandemic. Skinty Fia (2022) proved that the band could further evolve and grow as songwriters, before 2024’s Romance brought Fontaines acclaim outside Europe and onto their big battleground: America.

Romance featured a number of gigantic tunes – “Starburster” (195 million streams on Spotify); “Favourite” (131 million) – and landed the band its first Grammy nomination in the best rock album category. Fontaines’ lime-green aesthetic – slimier, sicklier than Charli’s shade of Brat – and fearless innovation marked the true arrival of a band who could attract cross generation fanbases and impress critically and commercially.

Its follow-up, the recently-released Dopamine Chamber, is the group at the peak of its powers, embracing synth-pop, industrial sounds and classic songwriting for a critical and commercial smash. The album hit No. 1 in the UK Singles Chart and scored the year’s third-biggest opening week, behind only Harry Styles and Olivia Rodrigo. On the Billboard 200, they landed at No. 14, their highest-ever peak.

Across Europe, the picture was similarly rosy, with No. 1s in the Netherlands, Portugal, Ireland and Belgium and top five finishes in Denmark, France, Poland, Sweden. Little surprise, then, that the band wanted to kick off the extensive live slate on the continent with a run of arena headline shows, which will hit the U.K. and Ireland in the coming weeks. Even bigger dates are set for 2027 including the London Stadium and a historic appearance at Slane Castle in Ireland.

As Fontaines’ triumphant run begins in earnest, these were the seven best moments from the show at Amsterdam’s Ziggo Dome on Tuesday (Nov. 10).

The group has previously curated top notch support acts for its tours — Hayley Williams, Madra Salach, Blondshell among them — and industrial punk-rock duo Chalk is another perfect match. Appearing on the whole European run, including Fontaines’ U.K. dates in the coming weeks, the Belfast-born pair are punishing to the extreme: “Get F–ked,” “Pain” and “Can’t Feel It” are well-suited for venues of this stature. Chalk’s harsh yet melodic sound shows that the influence and intensity of Nine Inch Nails still casts a long shadow in contemporary music.

“Six Shot Morning” sounds beefy on record, but live, the intensity and broodiness is cranked to the nines. Consider it a quasi-sequel to “Romance,” the self-titled curtain-raiser on Fontaines’ previous record, which also came into its own in a live setting. As the new opening song on the Dopamine Chamber set, “Six Shot” in its euphoric live arrangement resembles something celebratory, despite the oblique lyrics.

A long road is ahead for the lads as their tour stretches well into 2027 and touches nearly every corner of the earth. There’s no signs of self-preservation, but just a meticulously well-oiled machine that is capable of knocking out 22 songs with little in the way of crowd work or breathers. The show is utterly engrossing, even though their stage production is relatively minimal for venues of this size: a shimmering drape and vibrant lighting are just as impactful as any wraparound LED screen. “Roman Holiday” is so lyrically dense and mesmerizing, it has the rich depth and intrigue of an engrossing short story that stops the world around you, if only for a few minutes.

Frontman Grian Chatten carries a heavy load for the lads, acting as the band’s beating, bleeding heart. Throughout the show he marches and skips down his mini runway – the only member to utilise it – geeing up the crowd, or bending over to sing directly to the front row, and always finding the right moment to do so. During “In the Modern World,” he picks out a young fan on the front row and delivers the middle eight to her and her only. When he’s done, he floats away like it was nothing; she sinks to her knees in disbelief, leaning on her friends to hold her up. He just has that effect on people.

Dopamine Chamber’s centerpiece “Tongue” is, to many, the band’s most challenging song to date, particularly given that a memorable guitar moment arrives in the song’s final moments. Instead, it eschew the six-string for a twinkling piano riff and a bouncy bass groove, in what feels like a nod to Massive Attack’s Mezzanine. In the live setting, Tom Coll’s percussion provides a punchier beat and elevates the intensity of the song’s chorus.

Since its release in 2024, “Starburster” has felt become the band’s signature tune. At the time it was a departure from their scruffy post-punk sound, embracing elements of hip-hop and a Beatles-esque middle eight; in lesser hands, it would have been a right old mess. Over time, the stature has only grown, and when it arrived as the main set’s final song, the standing area turned into one giant mosh pit as Chatten geed them up for chaos.

Alongside “Starburster,” Skinty Fia track “I Love You” is the other contender in that battle for the band’s signature song. They vary in tone, however, but show the two sides of a very similar coin: “Starburster” is showy and silly; “I Love You” totally earnest and arresting. It features some of Chatten’s finest lyrics, particularly as he reflects on the failure of young people to be able to build a life in their home city of Dublin amidst economic uncertainty. He delivers two immaculate post-chorus verses full of greatness: “I love you like a penny loves the pocket of a priest,” he tells the crowd. Chatten is full of lines that rattle around your head for days, weeks, months and years. This song has about five of them.

Wet-weather and high-wind motorcycle riding: 10 habits for grip and visibility

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learning how to ride a motorcycle safely in rain and wind comes down to traction, visibility and control, and these 10 techniques cover each one

Weather does not change how a motorcycle works. It changes how much room a rider has to get things wrong. On a dry, calm day, a tire has grip to spare, the road ahead is easy to read, and the bike holds its line with little effort. Rain and wind take some of that margin away, and they do it in different ways.

Rain is a traction and visibility problem. Water sits between the tire and the pavement, which reduces grip and lengthens the distance needed to stop. Spray from other vehicles and a fogged or streaked visor make hazards harder to spot. Surfaces that feel solid in the dry, such as painted lines and metal covers, can turn slick once they are wet. After a dry spell, the first rain brings oil and grime to the surface of the pavement, and the start of a storm is especially slippery as a result.

Wind is a stability problem. A crosswind pushes a motorcycle sideways, and a gust can move it off its line with little warning. Gaps between buildings, exposed bridges and the wake of a large truck can all produce sudden shifts in the air hitting the bike and rider.

Knowing how to ride a motorcycle safely in rain and wind means managing both sets of problems, often on the same ride. The techniques involved are not exotic. Most rely on restraint, preparation and paying attention earlier than usual.

The 10 slides that follow fall into three groups. The first covers control: speed, following distance, smooth inputs and braking. The second covers the road and the machine, from slippery surfaces and standing water to tire tread and pressure. The third covers wind and the rider: handling crosswinds and truck turbulence, waterproof gear, a clear visor, being seen in spray and knowing when to stop.

Each slide stands on its own. A rider can go straight to the situation at hand. None of it replaces formal rider training or the guidance in a motorcycle's owner's manual. What it offers is a practical set of habits for rain and wind, whether on a daily commute or a long trip.

The core adjustment for riding in the rain is to reduce speed and open up the gap to the vehicle ahead. With less grip available on wet pavement, a motorcycle needs more road to stop or change direction.

Speed has an outsized effect on stopping. The energy a braking system has to shed rises with the square of speed, not in a straight line. Braking distance grows the same way, so even a small cut in speed shortens it by a larger proportion. On a wet surface, that extra room can decide whether a sudden stop ends safely.

Following distance works alongside lower speed. A common way to judge the gap is to pick a fixed marker, such as a sign or a lamp post. Count the seconds between the vehicle ahead passing it and your own arrival. In wet weather, add time to whatever gap you would hold on a dry road. A longer gap also means less of the spray from the vehicle in front reaches the rider. That spray coats a visor and hides the road surface.

Take the most care in the early minutes of a storm. After a dry spell, oil and other residue build up on the asphalt. The first rain lifts that film to the surface before there is enough water to wash it away. Grip during that stretch can be lower than the wet road suggests. A shower after days of dry weather therefore deserves a slower pace than the same road gets in steady rain.

Rain also cuts visibility in both directions. A rider sees less through a wet visor. Drivers have a harder time spotting a motorcycle's narrow profile through streaked windshields and fogged side windows. Lower speed gives everyone more time to notice one another and react.

Watch the mirrors as well. Drivers behind may not adjust their own following distance in the wet. Slowing steadily and early gives them warning rather than forcing them to react to a sudden change.

Smooth, gradual control inputs are the core of wet-weather motorcycle riding. A wet tire has less grip to give, so any sudden change in throttle, steering or lean can ask for more grip than the tire has. That is how a slide starts.

The reason comes down to how a tire shares its grip. Each contact patch has a limited amount of traction. That traction is split between cornering forces and the forces of speeding up or slowing down. On dry pavement there is usually plenty left over. On wet pavement the total shrinks. A jerky input that would go unnoticed in the sun can use up what remains.

Throttle control comes first. Opening the throttle quickly can spin the rear tire, especially on a powerful bike or in a low gear. Rolling the throttle on slowly and steadily lets the tire take up the load in stages. Riding a gear higher than usual can also soften how power reaches the rear wheel.

Closing the throttle deserves the same care. Snapping it shut can cause abrupt engine braking, which shifts weight forward and can unsettle the rear tire. Rolling off gradually keeps the chassis calm. The clutch follows the same rule. Releasing it gently, particularly on downshifts, avoids a sudden jolt to the rear wheel.

Some modern motorcycles offer a rain riding mode that dulls throttle response, and some add traction control. These systems help, but they work within the grip the tire has. They do not replace a steady right hand.

Steering inputs need to be measured as well. Pressing the handlebar with steady, progressive force tips the bike into a turn without a sudden load on the front tire. Quick, stabbing pressure on the bar does the opposite.

Lean angle is the third piece. Using less lean in wet corners keeps more traction in reserve for the unexpected. Riders can shift their upper body slightly toward the inside of the turn. That lets the motorcycle stay more upright at the same speed. Picking a cornering speed before the turn begins, rather than adjusting it mid-corner, means fewer changes while the bike is leaned over.

Use both brakes in the rain, applying each one with lighter, more gradual pressure than you would on dry pavement. Start braking earlier, do most of it while the bike is upright, and treat anti-lock brakes as a backup rather than a reason to brake late.

The front brake does most of the stopping on a motorcycle. Under braking, weight shifts forward and presses the front tire into the road. That added load gives the front tire more grip. The shift takes a moment, and on wet pavement the tire has less grip to spare while it happens.

The safer method is to squeeze the front lever gently at first. Add pressure as the front end settles and the fork compresses. Grabbing the lever hard before the weight transfers can lock the front wheel.

The rear brake plays a supporting role. It helps settle the chassis and adds some stopping force. As weight moves forward, the rear tire carries less load and locks more easily. A light, steady press on the pedal is enough for most wet stops. Stamping on it invites a skid.

Timing matters as much as pressure. Braking earlier spreads the work over a longer stretch of road. Each brake can then be applied more softly. An early start also leaves room to adjust if the surface turns out to be slicker than it looked.

Do the bulk of your braking in a straight line, before a corner. A tire has a limited amount of grip to share between turning and slowing. Braking hard while leaned over asks it to do both at once. In the wet, there is less grip to divide.

Skids still happen, and the right response depends on the wheel. A sliding front wheel cannot steer the bike. If the front locks, release the lever at once and reapply it more gently. If the rear locks while the bike is traveling straight, keeping the pedal down until the bike stops is usually the safer choice. Releasing it suddenly can let the tire regain grip with a jolt and snap the bike sideways.

Anti-lock brakes, or ABS, monitor wheel speed and pulse brake pressure to stop a wheel from locking. That lets the rider keep steering during a hard stop. ABS does not shorten stopping distance on every surface, though. Total grip is still set by the tires and the road.

Some motorcycles also use linked or combined brakes, where one control applies force at both wheels. The owner's manual explains how a specific bike's system works.

Water on the discs and pads can briefly delay the initial bite after riding through heavy spray. A light application of both brakes before a planned stop clears that film and confirms the brakes respond.

Painted road markings, manhole covers, steel construction plates, metal bridge grating and rail or tram tracks all turn slippery in the rain. The safest approach is to spot them early and steer around them where possible. Cross any you cannot avoid with the motorcycle upright and stable.

Painted lines are the slick surface riders meet on almost every road. Lane dividers, stop lines, arrows and crosswalk stripes form a smoother layer on top of the asphalt. That layer gives a wet tire less texture to grip than the road around it. Crosswalks and turn arrows near intersections put that smooth surface exactly where a rider is most likely to stop or turn.

Manhole covers and utility access lids are usually made of cast iron or steel. Wet metal is smooth, and a tire finds far less grip on it than on rough asphalt. Many sit in the travel lane. Some are also set slightly above or below the road surface, which adds a bump to already poor footing.

Steel plates laid over construction trenches create the same problem across a larger area. Their edges can be raised, and a raised edge can deflect a tire. Treat a plate like a manhole cover that takes longer to cross.

Steel bridge grating, the open mesh decking found on some drawbridges and older spans, makes a motorcycle feel as though it is wandering. That sensation happens on dry grating too. In the rain, the wet metal adds a loss of grip to the wobble. Hold a steady speed and line across it rather than correcting every twitch of the front end.

Rail and tram tracks call for a specific technique. Cross them as close to a right angle as possible. At a shallow angle, the tire can slide along the wet rail or drop into the groove beside it. Either can steer the front wheel out from under the rider. In cities with streetcar lines, tracks often run lengthwise down the street. Choose a lane early so a turn does not force a shallow crossing.

The rule that ties all these surfaces together is timing. Finish braking, leaning and changing speed on the asphalt before the slick patch. Ride across it upright, then resume on the far side. Looking farther down the road gives time to pick a line that avoids most of these hazards entirely.

On wet roads, pick a lane position that keeps the motorcycle out of standing water and off the oily center strip. If a tire starts to hydroplane, hold the bike upright and steady, avoid hard braking, and let the tire regain contact on its own.

Hydroplaning happens when a tire cannot push water away fast enough. A thin layer of water builds up between the rubber and the pavement. The tire then rides on top of that water instead of gripping the road. A motorcycle has only two contact patches. That leaves it less margin than a car when grip disappears. Higher speed raises the risk because the tire has less time to clear water from its path.

Many roads are built to shed water. A crowned road rises slightly in the middle and slopes toward the edges. That design sends runoff toward the shoulder and gutter, where puddles tend to gather. Riding close to the curb in heavy rain often means riding through the deepest water on the road.

The center of a lane brings a different problem. Cars and trucks drip oil and other fluids there. The buildup is heaviest near intersections and toll booths, where vehicles sit idling. Water on top of that film makes the center strip one of the slickest parts of the lane.

The tire tracks left by traffic ahead are often a better line. Cars push water aside as they pass. Their wheel paths can carry less water than the surrounding surface as a result. On roads worn by heavy trucks, those same paths can sink into ruts that hold water. Watch the surface ahead and adjust the line as conditions change.

Puddles call for caution for another reason. Muddy water can hide a pothole, a broken pavement edge or debris. There is no way to judge depth from the saddle. Steering around a puddle is safer than riding through it. The move should be gradual, and the space beside the puddle should be clear.

If the front end feels light or vague in deep water, keep the handlebars straight and the body relaxed. Ease off the throttle gently rather than snapping it shut. Avoid steering corrections, and stay off the brakes until the tire bites again. A steady, upright motorcycle usually regains grip once it reaches shallower water.

Tires with healthy tread and correct inflation give a motorcycle its best chance of holding the road in the rain. Check both before any wet ride. Set the pressure to the figure the manufacturer specifies for that bike.

Tread grooves have a specific job on wet pavement. They give water somewhere to go, channeling it out from under the contact patch so the rubber can touch the road. As a tire wears, those grooves get shallower and move less water. A worn tire can feel fine on a dry day and struggle to clear water in a downpour.

Most motorcycle tires have tread wear indicators molded into the grooves. These are small raised bars that sit below the tread surface. Small arrows or the letters TWI on the sidewall often mark where they are. When the surrounding tread wears down level with those bars, the tire is due for replacement. A tread depth gauge gives a more exact reading than the eye alone.

Wear is rarely even. Bikes ridden mostly on straight highways tend to wear the rear tire flat across the center. That squared-off profile can make the bike feel reluctant as it tips into a corner. Look across the full width of each tire, not just the middle.

Pressure deserves the same attention. The correct figures are listed in the owner's manual. Many bikes also carry them on a label on the frame or swingarm. Front and rear pressures usually differ. Some manufacturers list a higher figure for carrying a passenger or luggage.

Check pressure with the tires cold, before setting off. Riding heats the air inside and raises the reading. An underinflated tire flexes more, steers heavily and builds up heat. An overinflated tire shrinks the contact patch that grips the road.

A pre-ride look should also cover the rubber itself. Cuts, bulges, sidewall cracking and embedded nails or glass all need attention before the bike leaves the driveway. In the U.S., a tire's manufacture date appears on the sidewall as a four-digit code showing the week and year it was made. Rubber hardens as it ages, and harder rubber offers less grip on a slick road.

Waterproof riding gear, a fog-free visor and high-visibility clothing solve three problems that rain creates beyond lost traction. They keep a rider dry and focused, able to see the road, and easy for other drivers to spot.

The outer shell comes first. Wet-weather riding gear comes in two broad styles. Some jackets and pants use a built-in waterproof, breathable membrane, either laminated to the outer fabric or fitted as a separate liner. Other riders wear standard gear and pull a one-piece or two-piece rain suit over it when the weather turns.

The junctions between pieces matter as much as the fabric. Rain tends to get in at the collar, the wrists, the waist and the tops of boots. Pant legs should sit over boots rather than tucked inside them. That way, water runs off the boot instead of into it.

Glove cuffs follow the same logic. Some designs go inside the jacket sleeve and some go over it. The goal is to stop water from running down the arm and pooling inside the glove.

Hands are where wet gear causes the most trouble. Waterproof gloves keep fingers warm enough to feel the clutch and brake levers. Soaked leather gloves can turn heavy, lose their feel and slip on the grips.

The visor is the next weak point. A helmet visor fogs when warm, moist breath meets a cold surface. Cracking it open clears the fog but lets rain hit the face and eyes.

An anti-fog treatment applied to the inside of a clean visor reduces condensation. A pinlock-style insert goes further. It is a second clear lens sealed against the inside of the visor. The trapped layer of air keeps the inner surface warm enough to resist fogging, much like a double-glazed window. The visor has to be designed to accept one.

The outside of the visor needs attention too. A clean visor sheds water better than a dirty or scratched one. Scratches also scatter the glare from oncoming headlights at night. Dark tinted visors block light a rider needs under heavy cloud or after sunset.

Being seen is the third job. Rain, road spray and gray skies make a dark-clad rider on a dark motorcycle hard to pick out. Fluorescent colors such as yellow-green and orange stand out in daylight and dull, overcast light.

Fluorescent fabric depends on daylight to glow. After dark, it does much less. Reflective panels and strips fill that gap by bouncing headlight beams back toward drivers. Gear that combines both covers day and night riding. A fluorescent vest with reflective strips, worn over an existing jacket, adds both features without replacing the jacket.

A relaxed grip and loose arms are the foundation of handling crosswinds on a motorcycle. A rider who stays loose lets the bike absorb a gust and settle back onto its line. A rider who stiffens up sends every push of wind straight into the handlebars.

Tension is the instinctive reaction. When wind shoves a motorcycle sideways, the urge is to clamp down on the bars. Locked elbows and a tight grip turn the upper body into a lever. Each movement of the torso then becomes an unintended steering input. The bike can wobble more from the rider's reaction than from the wind itself.

The lower body should do the work of holding on. Squeezing the tank with the knees keeps the rider anchored to the motorcycle. Pressing the balls of the feet onto the pegs adds stability. That frees the hands to steer with a light touch. Bent elbows and a slightly lower posture also present less of the upper body to the wind.

A steady crosswind calls for a different response than a sudden gust. In a constant wind from one side, the motorcycle needs to lean slightly into it to hold a straight line. That lean comes from gentle forward pressure on the handlebar on the windward side. The rider does not need to force it. Light, steady pressure is enough for the bike to find its balance.

Gusts are harder to manage. They arrive and disappear without warning. When a gust stops abruptly, a rider who was leaning into it can drift toward the side the wind was coming from. Staying loose lets the bike straighten up as the pressure drops. Small corrections keep the motorcycle from swinging across the lane.

Reading the landscape helps a rider anticipate gusts before they hit. Gaps between buildings, breaks in a tree line and the end of a sheltering hillside can all send sudden wind across the road. Swaying treetops, flags standing straight out, blowing dust and rippling grass show the strength and direction of the wind ahead.

Lane position adds a margin for error. Riding on the side of the lane the wind is coming from leaves more room to be pushed downwind while staying inside the lane.

Equipment changes how a bike reacts to wind. Tall windscreens, top boxes and side luggage all add surface area for the wind to push against. A fully loaded touring bike presents more side area than the same machine without its bags. Lower speeds give a rider more time to respond to each push of wind.

Big trucks, exposed bridges and the exits of overpasses are where wind tends to hit a motorcycle hard and with little warning. Give large vehicles extra room, and expect a sideways shove at each of these spots before it arrives.

A tractor-trailer at highway speed pushes a large mass of air ahead of it and around its sides. A rider passing one can feel a push away from the truck near the cab, then a change in pressure along the trailer. On a windy day, the truck also blocks the crosswind for a few seconds. The bike settles in that shelter, then takes the full force of the wind again as it clears the front of the cab.

Being passed by a truck produces the same effects in reverse. The answer is to plan for them. When overtaking, make the pass decisively rather than lingering alongside the trailer. When a truck approaches from behind, move toward the side of the lane farther from it to add lateral space. Do this only if the surface there is clean.

Rain makes trucks harder to deal with. Their wheels throw up heavy spray that can blank out a visor for several seconds. Dropping back widens the gap and lets that mist thin out before it reaches the rider. Truck drivers also have large blind spots. A rider who cannot see the driver's face in the truck's mirrors should assume the driver cannot see the motorcycle.

Bridges carry their own risk. A deck high above a river or valley has no trees, hills or buildings to break the wind. Gusts there are often stronger than on the approach road. Some bridges post high-wind warnings or fly windsocks that show the direction and strength of the air moving across the deck.

Overpasses and tunnels create a similar hazard. The structure blocks the wind briefly, and the bike can be hit sideways the moment it emerges. Open stretches of highway, road cuts through hills and the crest of a rise work the same way. Knowing where shelter ends lets a rider prepare for the gust before it lands.

Pull over when cold, fatigue or the weather itself starts to outpace your ability to control the motorcycle. Stopping at a safe spot to wait out a storm costs time. Pushing on through conditions beyond your control can cost far more.

Cold is often the first warning sign. Wet clothing draws heat away from the body. Air moving over a rider at road speed adds to that chill. Early symptoms include shivering, stiff or numb fingers and clumsy use of the controls. Hands that cannot feel the brake lever or clutch cannot apply them with precision. Once shivering becomes hard to stop, it is time to find shelter and warm up.

Mental fatigue builds in a similar way. Riding in rain or wind demands constant scanning for hazards, along with small corrections to keep the bike on its line. That workload tires a rider faster than a dry, calm ride does. Missing a turn, drifting within the lane or noticing a hazard later than usual are signs that concentration is slipping.

Some conditions call for a stop no matter how alert the rider feels. Lightning leaves a motorcyclist exposed on open roads. Hail strikes a rider directly and can cover the road surface. Floodwater hides its depth and any damage to the road beneath it. Rain heavy enough to obscure the road edge or nearby vehicles is another reason to get off the road. So are gusts that repeatedly push the bike across its lane.

Official alerts help with that call before and during a trip. In the U.S., the National Weather Service issues wind advisories and high wind warnings. In the U.K., the Met Office publishes weather warnings by region.

Where a rider stops matters too. An enclosed building, such as a gas station store, a cafe or a rest-area building, offers warmth and protection from lightning. A highway shoulder or the space under an overpass does not. Both leave a stopped rider close to fast traffic, often where drivers have limited time to see them.

Use the break to recover. Drink something warm, swap in dry gloves if you carry a spare pair, and check the forecast or radar. Ride on once your hands feel normal on the controls and the worst of the weather has moved through.

The Netherlands Has €231 Million for Cleaner Inland Ships. It Still Needs Electric Freight Corridors.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Netherlands is preparing to spend €231.2 million over four years helping inland ships replace fossil-fuel propulsion. The first €36.93 million tender opens on November 17, 2026, and battery-electric vessels are among the eligible investments. This is a serious step toward decarbonizing freight on the waterways that link Dutch ports and Europe's industrial heartland. But the programme's most important scoring calculation rewards emissions avoided per euro of propulsion subsidy while excluding separate subsidies for the shore infrastructure on which the ship may depend. That distinction matters when the commercial product is a complete freight service, not an electric vessel sitting beside a quay.

The new Dutch tender is considerably more sophisticated than a technology demonstration fund, yet it leaves an important accounting boundary. My TFIE Strategy Briefing assessment follows that boundary from subsidized vessels to working routes, drawing on my open, 98-page Inland Freight Electrification report.

The RVO programme can support battery-electric systems, some hybrid arrangements and other propulsion technologies, together with eligible charging and refueling facilities. Its published explanation prioritizes electrification where practical. That is sensible: electric inland vessels are already operating in the Netherlands, and a subsidy should encourage the pathway that provides the required service at low emissions and acceptable cost.

The scheme also recognizes that a vessel does not operate in isolation. Applications receive points for commercial cooperation, cargo-owner agreements and the quality of supporting infrastructure arrangements. These are material criteria, not decorative promises. They are a welcome correction to programmes that fund novel equipment and assume the market, terminal and utility will somehow arrange everything else.

Yet most of the available points are tied to projected emissions reductions per euro of propulsion support. That is a consistent measure for comparing applications for individual ships. It is not an accounting of the entire investment required to turn a diesel freight route into an electric one. One barge may need access to battery exchange at both ends of its journey, reserve battery inventory, grid capacity, a crane or handling system and a dependable service agreement. Some of those facilities can serve many vessels, making it hard to allocate their cost and value to any single propulsion application.

Zero Emission Services, or ZES, has already shown what putting the pieces together looks like. Its swappable battery containers allow inland vessels to obtain charged energy without each owner purchasing and maintaining every battery required by the route. At Moerdijk, a terminal arrangement combines battery exchange, handling equipment and access to an existing electricity connection. The ZES network is extending beyond isolated demonstrations, although operating sites, planned stations and funded vessel conversions must not be counted as the same thing.

The difference between a capable ship and reliable freight is familiar to ports. Charged batteries must be available at the right time, terminals must handle exchanges without disrupting cargo work, and the energy-service provider needs enough committed traffic to finance its containers and chargers.

The Netherlands deserves credit for recognizing these dependencies. It need not discard vessel-level grants or wait until every future route is planned before making early awards. But a second programme-level measure is needed alongside emissions per propulsion subsidy: what complete, repeatable electric freight service is each funded project helping create, and which shared assets make that service possible?

This matters all the more because the freight being carried will change. Fossil-linked cargo is likely to decline as coal and petroleum use falls, and ports should not size batteries, terminals and connections around the assumption that today's bulk flows continue indefinitely. A route with strong contracted demand may justify shared charging equipment while a low-utilization route requires a different architecture or timetable. The answer depends on the freight task, not merely on the specifications of an available vessel.

The Dutch programme is a promising way to accelerate the first investments. Its next achievement should be to show that those investments connect: cargo moving on named routes, vessels using dependable shared energy infrastructure, and operating data strong enough to finance the following corridor without perpetually reinventing the subsidy. The ship is the visible part of the transition. The corridor is what makes it commercially useful.

The airline’s product turnaround has been surprising, and surprisingly fast. They’ve introduced new business class suites, improved regional cabins and internet, upgraded coffee, and put money into lounges. They’re also reversing years of bad product decisions, including plans to bring back seatback screens and add more extra legroom coach seats.

Their returning coach food reservation program, announced with an initial Dallas–Fort Worth–Los Angeles test, should help passengers get the item they actually want. Mostly, though, I like that American seems to be loading enough food that there’s still something left when the cart reaches my aisle.
