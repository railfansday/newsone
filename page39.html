My Store Turned Into Wingdings for Exactly One Reason, and It Was 3,410 Bytes

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On 7 November 2026 I added analytics to my store and rewarded myself by breaking the entire site's text. Every word on every page rendered as garbled symbols in my browser. The build was fine. The files were fine. The words were fine, somewhere underneath whatever my browser had decided they were.

The site is a small store built with Astro, deployed on Vercel. The change that morning was tiny and boring: install Vercel Analytics, drop the component into the base layout, deploy. The kind of change you make between sips of tea.

Then I opened the store and it looked like the text had been fed through a paper shredder and reassembled by an enemy.

I checked the content files. Clean. I checked the build output locally. Clean. I rebuilt. I stared at fonts, because garbled text feels like a font problem the way a cough feels like a lung problem. It usually isn't, but the feeling is strong.

Here is what I had not checked: the actual bytes of the page my browser received.

The analytics component injects an inline script into the . My layout had the tag sitting after other elements, and the injected script landed before it. In the served HTML, the charset declaration ended up at byte offset 3,410.

When a server sends an HTML page without declaring the character encoding in the Content-Type response header, the browser has to guess. The HTML spec gives it a prescan step: it reads the beginning of the byte stream looking for a charset declaration.

The catch is the size of that beginning. The prescan only looks at roughly the first 1,024 bytes.

My charset tag was at byte 3,410. From the browser's point of view, it did not exist. So the browser fell back to a guessed encoding, guessed wrong, and rendered UTF-8 text through the wrong lens. Every multi-byte character smeared into symbols. The page was never broken. It was being read in the wrong language by a reader following its own rules correctly.

And my response headers carried no charset either, so there was no second chance. The meta tag was the only witness, and it was standing 3,410 bytes away from the only place witnesses get heard.

Move the charset meta to be the first element inside . Before the title. Before everything.

One line, relocated. Redeploy. In the new served HTML the charset sat at byte offset 44, comfortably inside the prescan window, and the store read like a store again. The checkout page returned 200, the text was clean, done.

Total code changed: the position of one tag. Total time spent suspecting my content, my fonts and my build before looking at the byte offsets: considerably longer, and I'd rather not be precise about it, which tells you everything.

Encoding bugs wear a perfect disguise. They look exactly like content bugs. The words are what's visibly wrong, so you interrogate the words, and the words are innocent, and they stay innocent no matter how many times you rebuild them.

The debugging rule I now follow, learned at byte 3,410: when text renders wrong, check how the bytes are being read before checking what the bytes say. View source. Count where the charset declaration actually sits in the served document, not where you placed it in your template, because frameworks, injectors and "helpful" components rearrange furniture in your without asking. My template had the tag in a perfectly reasonable position. The served page had it somewhere else entirely. The template is a promise; the served bytes are the truth.

This applies to anything that injects into your head section: analytics, tag managers, A/B testing scripts, consent banners. Every one of them pushes your own tags further from byte zero. Most of the time it costs nothing. The day your headers don't declare a charset, it costs you your whole site's legibility, and the bill arrives looking like a content problem.

I'm collecting these now, the bugs where the cause-to-symptom ratio is most insulting. Mine: one misplaced tag, 2,386 bytes past the limit, every word on a live store unreadable.

What's yours? The one-character typo that ate a weekend, the config line that took down the thing it was supposed to protect. Smallest cause, biggest symptom. I want to feel better, and also worse.

Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation for System Design, With Worked Examples

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 10 • Originally published at letinix.com

System design estimation is a way to turn an ambiguous product description into approximate traffic, storage, bandwidth, and capacity requirements. The goal is not to predict an exact production number. It is to expose the order of magnitude, make assumptions visible, and identify which part of the design deserves attention first.

A useful estimate follows a repeatable sequence: define the workload, calculate average and peak requests per second, split reads from writes, estimate data growth, calculate network throughput, and translate the results into design choices. The arithmetic should be simple enough to explain aloud and detailed enough for another engineer to challenge.

Note: Treat every number in the worked examples as an explicit hypothetical input, not a benchmark or a universal industry default. Replace assumptions with measured product analytics, load tests, payload samples, and retention requirements when sizing a real system.

Start by describing what the system does. “A social feed” is not a workload; “two million daily active users each request twenty feed pages and publish half a post per day on average” is a workload you can calculate.

Write down the assumptions before doing arithmetic:

Ask which values are known, which are estimates, and which are deliberately conservative. If a prompt does not provide a value, choose a round assumption, say why it is plausible for the scenario, and show how the result changes if it is wrong. In an interview, this is part of the answer—not a detour before the answer.

A day contains 86,400 seconds. For quick mental arithmetic, 100,000 seconds is a convenient approximation, but use 86,400 when showing a worked calculation. The approximation makes the result about 13.6% lower than the exact division, so it is useful for a first pass, not a substitute for checking a decision near a capacity boundary.

For traffic, remember the order of magnitude: one million requests per day is 1,000,000 ÷ 86,400 = 11.57 requests per second, or roughly 12 average requests per second. Ten million per day is about 116/s; one hundred million per day is about 1,157/s.

Be precise about data units. Decimal units use powers of 10: 1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes and 1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes. Binary units use powers of 2: 1 KiB = 1,024 bytes, 1 MiB = 1,048,576 bytes, and 1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes. NIST documents the distinction between SI and IEC binary prefixes. NIST: Definitions of the SI units — binary prefixes

Use one convention throughout a calculation. In the examples below, storage totals use decimal GB and TB; file sizes use MiB where binary units are more natural. State the convention rather than switching between MB and MiB as if they were identical.

These formulas provide a starting model. They do not capture every system cost, but they expose the main quantities that influence an initial architecture.

RPS means requests per second. QPS means queries per second; teams sometimes use the terms interchangeably, but define whether a “request” is an external API call, a database query, or an internal operation. One external request can fan out into several internal calls.

Do not assume one peak factor fits every product. Calculate it from observed time-bucketed traffic when possible: peak interval rate divided by the average rate for the same interval definition. If no telemetry exists, state a scenario assumption and test alternatives. A factor of 3 in an example means “we are modeling a peak three times the average,” not “all systems peak at 3×.”

Calculate read and write rates separately. A read-heavy product may need to serve a large volume of repeated data, while a write-heavy product may need sustained ingestion, ordering, durability, or downstream processing. A combined RPS figure can hide the real constraint.

Add indexes, metadata, compaction space, snapshots, backups, and derived data separately. A copy factor is a simplified planning input, not a universal statement about how a managed database or object store implements durability. Compression also changes the stored size; estimate it from representative data rather than assuming a fixed saving.

Calculate ingress and egress independently. Use the relevant request or response size for each path. Add protocol overhead, encryption, retries, fan-out, and retransmission where they materially affect the estimate. For large files, a small request count can still produce substantial bandwidth.

If the capacity target is below full utilization, use ceil(peak_RPS ÷ (sustainable_RPS_per_instance × target_utilization)). The per-instance rate must come from a representative load test or a defensible capacity measurement for the actual workload, software, instance type, and latency target. Do not copy a generic “requests per server” number into a design and present it as fact.

Assume a feed service has 2 million daily active users. Each user loads twenty feed pages per day and creates half a post per day on average. Model peak traffic at four times the daily average. Assume each post record occupies 1,000 bytes and each feed response transfers 12,000 bytes. These are illustrative inputs.

The workload is roughly 40 reads for every write. That ratio does not prove a cache is required, but it tells you the read path deserves more attention than the write path in the first design pass.

This is application response payload only. It excludes transport overhead and any media served separately. If feed responses include images, model image delivery as a separate path rather than hiding it inside an average API response.

The 1.095 TB figure excludes indexes, metadata, backups, and media. It is not a complete database capacity plan.

Design implications: investigate caching or precomputing frequently requested feed data, and consider read replicas only if measured database read pressure justifies them. Keep writes on a simpler path initially unless ordering, fan-out, or consistency requirements change the picture. Separate media delivery from feed API responses, and validate the assumed read distribution: a highly personalized feed may not benefit from the same cache strategy as a feed of mostly shared content.

Assume 500,000 daily active devices each emit 200 events per day. Each stored event, including its basic metadata, averages 600 bytes. Model peak ingestion at five times the average and retain events for 30 days. Use a three-copy scenario for a first storage estimate.

1. Calculate event volume and ingestion rate.

The 5.4 TB estimate excludes indexes, compression effects, retention gaps, backups, and temporary compaction space. If event payloads vary substantially, calculate by event class or use a measured size distribution instead of relying on one average.

The request rate is more revealing than the raw bandwidth in this example. The system must accept and durably process nearly 5,800 small events per second at the modeled peak, even though the byte rate is modest.

Design implications: consider an append-oriented ingestion path, batching, and a durable queue or log when producers and consumers need decoupling. Partitioning can distribute sustained write volume, but the partition key must avoid concentrating one hot tenant or event type on a single partition. Define ordering requirements before choosing a partition strategy: global ordering is much more restrictive than per-device or per-aggregate ordering. Use retention and compaction policies that match the actual query and audit requirements.

Assume 100,000 daily active users upload two files each per day. The average file is 8 MiB. Model upload peaks at eight times the average. Assume each uploaded file is downloaded three times on average, downloads peak at four times their average, and files are retained for 30 days. For a simplified capacity scenario, count three stored copies.

The request rate looks small. The payload size changes the capacity picture.

The conversion uses 8 MiB = 8,388,608 bytes and decimal MB for the final rate. This is a payload estimate, not a provisioned network target; protocol overhead and burst shape still matter.

Design implications: this is a data-transfer and storage problem, not simply an API request-count problem. Consider direct-to-object-storage uploads using short-lived, scoped upload authorization so application servers do not proxy every file byte. Use asynchronous processing for malware scanning, metadata extraction, and thumbnail generation when those tasks do not need to block the upload response. A CDN may reduce origin egress for repeatedly downloaded files, subject to access control and cache policy. Confirm whether “three copies” is actually how the chosen storage service bills or exposes durability; do not multiply managed storage by three if its documented storage model already accounts for redundancy.

The examples are not directly comparable capacity benchmarks: their workloads, payloads, and assumptions differ. Their purpose is to show how the same method reveals different constraints.

Run these checks before turning estimates into an architecture:

An estimate is useful only when it changes a question or decision. Use the numbers to identify what to investigate—not to select a database or cache by formula alone.

For an interview, narrate the assumptions, show the arithmetic, identify the largest constraint, and explain what you would measure next. For a real design, record the inputs, source, confidence, and owner beside the estimate. A useful next step is to put the assumptions and capacity implications into a technical design document, where reviewers can challenge the numbers before they become infrastructure commitments.

No file, no stack, no line. Just a message that tells you nothing.

You have window.onerror wired up. You followed a tutorial. So why is the one thing you needed missing?

By the end of this article you will know exactly what window.onerror hands you and what it hides. You will build a small, tested error monitor in TypeScript that catches runtime errors, failed resources, and rejected promises, and delivers them reliably. And you will find out why that dashboard shows line 0.

Keep that line 0 question in mind. We'll answer it near the end.

Before touching code, let's set up the problem. Browsers expose three separate failure channels, and most tutorials only cover one.

Per MDN's documentation for the Window error event, the error event fires on window when a resource fails to load or can't be used, for example when a script throws. MDN also notes that it only fires for script errors thrown synchronously, such as during initial load or inside event handlers. A rejected promise with no handler fires a different event, unhandledrejection.

If you only set window.onerror, you cover the first row and nothing else. And as you'll see, even that row has traps.

Before we get into the examples, a quick note: The code snippets provided here are meant purely for understanding the concept. Some syntax shown may reflect patterns from earlier Angular/React versions. Always refer to the official documentation for the most current API and syntax.

There's a fifth trap that bites people who copy handlers from old snippets. MDN documents that for onerror on Window, returning true cancels the default behavior, so the error won't appear in the console, though the current script still stops executing. A handler that ends with return true makes development miserable because your own errors vanish from DevTools.

MDN notes that onerror on Window and WorkerGlobalScope is the only event handler property that receives more than one argument, for historical reasons. That asymmetry only applies to the property. If you use addEventListener('error', ...), you get a single ErrorEvent object whose properties (message, filename, lineno, colno, error) match those five arguments. The return-true rule does not apply there either.

That's the first reason I'd pick addEventListener every time: it composes with other code, and it has one argument shape.

The browser treats the error as "muted" when the script isn't same-origin or CORS-approved. It's a privacy rule, not a bug, so there's nothing a handler can do after the fact. The only fix is at the delivery layer: serve your own bundles from the same origin, or serve them with CORS enabled and mark the tags with crossorigin.

Third-party scripts you don't control (ad tags, chat widgets) will stay muted unless their vendor sends CORS headers. Decide whether you want to filter those out of your dashboards, because "Script error." with no location is unactionable noise. I would drop them or tag them as low priority rather than alert on them.

Why the true? Resource load failures fire an error event on the element itself, and that event doesn't bubble. Your window listener would never see it in the bubbling phase. The capture phase runs on the way down, before the target, so a capturing listener on window sees every element's error event. MDN also says the event is an ErrorEvent for script errors and a plain Event otherwise, which is why the code branches on instanceof ErrorEvent.

Here's what most developers get wrong: they log event.reason as-is. A rejection reason can be anything, a string, an object, undefined. Always check instanceof Error before reading .stack.

Capture-phase listeners on window also see errors that other code already handled with try/catch? No. A caught exception never becomes an error event, so you only see what the page failed to handle. Resource errors are different: there's no try/catch around an , so every failed image, script, and stylesheet reports. That can be loud. The Death of the Prompt: Why Autonomous Agents Are the Real Paradigm Shift in Python Development

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’ve all been there. You spend hours meticulously engineering a prompt. You add a few "few-shot" examples, inject some XML tags to keep the output formatted, and wrap it in a clean Python script using the OpenAI SDK. It works brilliantly for the exact test case you wrote. Then, you hand it to a user, they type something slightly unexpected, the model hallucinates, your regex parser crashes on unescaped JSON, and your morning is ruined.

Welcome to the ceiling of prompt engineering.

For the past few years, the software engineering world has treated Large Language Models (LLMs) like high-tech oracles: you ask a question, and you hope you get a pristine answer. But as developers attempt to build complex, production-ready AI applications, a glaring limitation has become impossible to ignore. Chains and static prompts are fundamentally reactive. They follow a rigid, developer-defined path from A to B. If a step fails, if data is missing, or if the user injects a novel curveball, the chain snaps.

The industry is undergoing a massive epistemic shift. We are moving away from reactive prompt processing and toward proactive execution via Autonomous Agents.

In this deep dive, we are going to tear down the theoretical foundations of autonomous agents, contrast them with the sequential chains you might already be building, and write the Python code to build a self-correcting agentic loop from scratch.

The concepts and code demonstrated here are drawn directly from the comprehensive roadmap laid out in the ebook AI Autonomous Agents with Python, you can access the free github repo with all the code examples, exercises MIT Licensed and see the ebook details link.

To understand why autonomous agents are taking over software architecture, we have to look at what came before. In Book 7 of our architectural progression, we mastered chains—predefined sequences of steps, often involving multiple LLM calls, data transformations, and tool usages designed to solve bounded problems.

When you build a sequential chain, you are acting as an absolute micro-manager. You write code that dictates:

The flow is completely rigid. It is a traditional if/else control flow wrapped around a probabilistic reasoning engine.

What happens if Step 1 returns a poorly formatted summary because the input text was too chaotic? The chain breaks. What happens if Step 2 requires an unexpected intermediate step—like fetching external context from a vector database because the summary lacked domain specifics? The chain breaks.

A chain lacks two things that define true software engineering resilience: dynamic planning and self-correction.

Think about traveling from Point A to Point B.

An Agent, in the context of Python and LLMs, is a goal-oriented control system that uses an LLM as its reasoning engine. It navigates uncertainty, manages external state, and utilizes a diverse set of tools without requiring you to script every single micro-decision.

For an LLM to transcend its role as a static text-generator and become a true Agent, it must operate within a perpetual control structure. The industry standard model for this is derived from the OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act), originally developed by military strategist John Boyd.

Let’s break down how these four phases map directly into a Python application architecture:

The loop begins by gathering all relevant data points. This includes the initial high-level user goal, the raw results from the last executed tool, and any changes in the external environment. The Observation phase is purely about ingestion—receiving raw data from the outside world.

This is the most critical phase. The Agent does not simply look at raw data; it contextualizes it within its mission, its memory, and its current state. It compares the observation to the overall goal, queries its long-term memory for past experiences, and synthesizes a rich, contextualized prompt for the LLM.

Here, the LLM acts as the core strategist. Based on the synthesized context, the LLM generates an internal monologue (often via Chain-of-Thought prompting), selects a specific external tool, and formats the exact parameters required for that tool.

The Action phase is where the Python environment takes over. The agent framework intercepts the structured instruction generated by the LLM, validates it, and executes the corresponding external Python function or API call. Once execution finishes, the results are fed right back into the Observe phase, restarting the loop.

The transition from prompt engineering to agent engineering represents a maturation of our industry. We are no longer just writing text transformations; we are building autonomous control systems.

By wrapping LLMs in an OODA loop, equipping them with Pydantic-validated tools, and giving them the capacity for iterative self-correction, we unlock software that can reason through uncertainty, handle messy real-world data, and execute multi-step objectives without constant human babysitting.

Dustin Hoffman Recalls Being Held at Gunpoint in Bed and Early Gritty NYC Days Living with Robert Duvall and Gene Hackman

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Dustin Hoffman is opening up about his younger years with the release of his memoir, “Look at Me.”

The actor, who broke into Hollywood with his role in “The Graduate,” appeared on the Nov. 9 episode of the “Conan O’Brien Needs a Friend” podcast and revealed that he had once been held at gunpoint by a man whose wife he was having an affair with during his “youth youth.”

“I was having an affair with a married woman,” Hoffman, 89, admitted. “She thought that once her husband went to asleep, that he stayed asleep.”

Explaining that he had been invited to the woman’s home when her husband had presumably gone to bed, when suddenly it had become clear that the man was well aware of what was going on.

“There we are, having a festival of sorts, and he walked in with a gun,” the “Rain Man” actor shared. “He pointed it at me and he said, I remember what he said, ‘Get out, get out.’”

“When you have a gun pointed at you,” he explained, “You realize how, any second, any second he’s going to pull that trigger. His finger is on the trigger and you think any second he’s going to. You realize, ‘That’s it, I’m gonna die. I’m gonna die, the next second, the next second, the next second.”

The actor recalled that in that moment, he came to the realization that “you will do anything to avoid getting killed.”

At another point in the interview, O’Brien asked the Los Angeles native to detail his move to New York. Hoffman relocated across the country in 1958 at 21, hoping to pursue a career in acting.

As O’Brien put it, Hoffman worked “a lot of absurd odd jobs” at this point in his life, including a role at Macy’s, where he was tasked with selling toys—like foosball and table hockey—to adults.

As an aspiring actor, Hoffman put his own spin on the job, and decided to embody a character of his own making; a retired, injured French hockey player.

“Gene Hackman was right there at adult games, also,” Hoffman said, noting that the fellow actor was his coworker at the time. Decades later, the pair would go on to work together again, starring in the 2003 film, “Runaway Jury.”

Hackman and Hoffman weren’t just coincidental coworkers. They lived together, too.

“You go home to your roommates, and they are Gene Hackman and Robert Duvall. The three of you, I’m picturing a bunk bed with three levels. And you guys are snoring in unison like ‘The Three Stooges.’ That’s what I’m picturing,” joked O’Brien.

Hoffman said that the three men, who eventually each became very famous actors, “didn’t live together quite like that.”

Although he seems to look back on the experience with a sense of humor now, his memoir tells a different story. In an excerpt from the book, Hoffman wrote that he “was so frightened of New York.”

“I didn’t want to go outside. I’d been excited to get there, but once I arrived it scared the hell out of me,” he said. “All these thousands of people who knew what they were doing, whereas I was completely in the dark. It was intimidating.”

At one point, Hoffman lived with Hackman and his first wife, Faye Maltese, in “a little apartment on the East Side.”

“I slept on the kitchen floor next to their refrigerator, which shook and made a lot of noise, like it was having a heart attack once or twice a night,” Hoffman recalled.

Hoffman had originally only planned to stay with Hackman and his wife for a few days, but the arrangement lasted three weeks before “the Hackmans ran out of patience and told me it was time to move on.”

Hackman advised Hoffman to, “go up Riverside Drive and knock on the doors and ask to speak to the superintendent,” to find a new place to live. The actor recalled starting at 60th Street and Central Park West, and knocking on “every door of every building all the way to the Hudson River and back again” until he eventually found a place on 109th street.

He rented a top-floor apartment in a building in Spanish Harlem for $80 a month, and knew he’d “need roommates to afford it,” which is where Duvall came in.

“We had a lot of rowdy nights in that apartment: pizza, unemployed actors, candles in chianti bottles,” Hackman wrote. “I would play the piano. Duvall would sing country music songs. If someone had pointed at Bob and Gene and me at one of our parties and said, ‘Those three are going to be movie stars,” everyone in the apartment would have laughed.”

Even after Hoffman got his first big break, it was a while before he’d find his footing financially. During the podcast interview, he said he was paid $3,000 for his role in "The Graduate," which was released in 1967. But after the film’s premiere, he still needed more funds.

“You didn’t have any money, and so you went back to the unemployment office and someone at Life magazine took a photo of you,” O’Brien said. “The most famous face in America at the time, waiting in the unemployment line to get a check.”

Hoffman confirmed that the story was true, adding that the check was for about $50.

“$55, I think was the most you could get,” he continued, quipping that at that time, people “could live on” about “$200 a month.”
