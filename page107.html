Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: What a Missed Deadline Taught Me About Switching Off

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

I missed a deadline last week. The piece you’re reading was supposed to be filed before I headed out on a long weekend to Italy for my wife’s birthday.

The main problem was I simply ran out of time to write the piece. An urgent stakeholder ask out of the blue, a couple of unanticipated work fire drills, and a long-standing evening social commitment earlier in the week made for a perfect storm, and something had to give.

The other problem and consequence was that I carried a chunk of guilt into the break.

It caught up with me at 6 am on the first morning, when I found myself drafting this column in my head. I should have been dozing peacefully. I continued to ruminate on my lengthy to-do list at work deep into day one as we headed from Venice into the Dolomites. My body was in Italy, but my brain was clearly still at my desk.

Switching off, particularly from work, is hard for most of us, and especially in an age of ubiquitous screens and wall-to-wall information competing for our attention. For me, that always-on reality plays a major role in the difficulties I have disconnecting from the daily grind.

I’m fortunate to love my job, and if I’m candid, I probably take a little too much of my identity from it. I hold on tight, and the phone in my pocket is an ever-present enabler.

A meme that has stuck with me over the years goes something along the lines of being an adult is staring at medium screens all day only to come home and stare at a little screen while a big screen is on in the background. The irony is not lost on me that I came across this rather withering take on modern life while scrolling on said little screen.

Regardless of the role the screen plays, psychologists who study employee well-being describe what I was missing for the early part of the weekend as “psychological detachment.” A state where you are genuinely able to not think about work while away from it. The science suggests it’s an important thing to work on.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 32 studies found that vacationing has a strong effect on well-being, and that the effect lasts longer than earlier research suggested. A second, smaller analysis of eight studies points to detaching from work, along with physical activity, as among the most helpful things to do while you’re off. In another study that tracked teachers during a two-week break, exhaustion, anxiety, and low mood all declined, while those who kept ruminating about work recovered less.

Whatever the science says, I know that for me the act of stepping away from work and being disciplined about not checking back in while I’m away is critical for my mental health and for staving off burnout. Getting out in nature and hiking or cycling or swimming keeps me present. It resets and recharges my batteries, and it tops up my creativity in a way that I find benefits what I am then able to bring back to the office.

Sure enough, as we drove deeper into the mountains on a crisp, blue-sky day, I could feel the golden autumnal hues and majestic rock formations drawing me in, closer to an elemental feeling of calm and happiness. That sense of equilibrium stayed with me all weekend, and while I’d be lying if I said my mind didn’t occasionally drift to my desk, it was fleeting, with the knowledge that all the things would be waiting for me when I got back. Just like this piece was.

With Thanksgiving on the horizon, and the holidays coming thick and fast thereafter, I’ll leave you with this bit of hard-earned advice: if your job is deeply entwined with your life and part of who you are, as mine is, time away can sometimes feel like a small act of disloyalty. It isn’t. In fact, it’s probably the most important thing you can do to remain faithful to your work ethic, and to yourself.

And if you’ll be working over the holidays, or you’re not working right now, or you don’t have the benefit of someone on hand to carry some of the load for you (shout out to all the Grannies out there), or your vacation looks very different from mine, that’s all okay. Find the pockets where and when you can: an evening, a walk, an hour in front of a screen, or better yet a book, and let work wait. Above all else, be kind to yourself. That’s how you really give yourself a break.

Robin Hough is the Editor-in-Chief of Healthline, and in addition to this regular Letter from the Editor column, he can be found every other Wednesday in our Healthline Wellness Wire newsletter.

Nvidia stock suffers longest weekly losing streak since 2012 as market value sinks by $1.7 trillion

📅 2026-11-13  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia investors turned bearish before this week's Jensen Huang saga. But the selling on Wall Street accelerated in the past few days, sending the stock to its sixth straight weekly drop, the longest losing streak since 2012.

The chip giant, which spent most of the year as the world's most valuable company, has lost 30% of its value during losing streak. That's a drop of roughly $1.7 trillion in market cap, and it fell below Apple in the process.

Huang, who co-founded Nvidia and led it for 33 years, resigned on Thursday after two separate extramarital affairs — one with an alleged Chinese spy and the other with an employee — were made public. The board named finance chief Colette Kress as interim CEO while it conducts a search for a full-time successor.

Nvidia shares fell 13% this week, the most since April 2025. News of the first reported affair hit the front pages over the weekend, when Huang sent a letter to employees apologizing for being "unfaithful to my wife." The board convened late Wednesday after The New York Times reported on the other affair, this one with the company's vice president of health care.

By early Thursday morning, Huang was out, capping a stunning fall for the Nvidia co-founder, who had emerged as one of President Donald Trump's top allies in the corporate world.

"With the unfortunate departure of CEO Jensen Huang we expect a challenging transition for NVIDIA over the next few months," Gil Luria, an analyst at D.A. Davidson, wrote in a report on Thursday. Luria lowered his price target to $200 from $300, but maintained his buy rating. "We believe the market has appropriately adjusted for this transition," he wrote.

Last week, prior to Huang's departure, Nvidia shares dropped about 9%. Much of the decline came after the midterm elections, which resulted in sweeping victories for Democrats and the passage of dozens of ballot measures that could slow the artificial intelligence buildout.

In the weeks before the election, Nvidia's stock was sliding alongside the rest of the AI trade on concerns that rising interest rates would punish debt-heavy companies. While Nvidia is wildly profitable, big customers like neoclouds are reliant on the debt markets.

Heading into next week, the pressure is on Kress. Nvidia is scheduled to report quarterly results on Tuesday. Analysts are projecting 91% year-over-year revenue growth to $108.9 billion, according to LSEG. That's up 18-fold from the same quarter in 2022, just before OpenAI launched ChatGPT and kicked off the generative AI era.

Analysts at Oppenheimer wrote in a report on Thursday that, even though they see the company reporting solid earnings, "we expect fundamentals to take a backseat near term."

"The departure of Mr. Huang could, in our view, translate to a lingering share overhang," they wrote.

“You want to feed a dog as closest as they would eat in the wild,” an employee at the counter explains. “Like what a wolf would eat.”

Of course, the more natural the pet food, the more expensive.

While most Americans still go for the big brands like Purina, Blue Buffalo and Pedigree, a growing segment of pet owners are spending big bucks for their pet to eat like a king. Industry analysts estimate the market for “clean label” or “natural” pet foods was worth some $14 billion in 2025, and is expected to grow to $23 billion by 2030.

But differentiating the natural from the unnatural isn’t always so easy, for pet owners without advanced degrees in chemistry. If you were to flip over the $289 bag of K9 Natural Lamb Feast, you’d see it contains such ingredients as dipotassium phosphate, magnesium oxide, selenium yeast, copper proteinate and thiamine mononitrate. These hard-to-spell ingredients are listed as different vitamins and minerals, important morsels of pet nutrition. Still, a granola-loving dog owner might get confused.

A recent federal class action, filed in August in Los Angeles, illustrates the point. In it, the named plaintiff, a San Diego woman named Nicole Flick, claims Instinct Pet Food contains mixed tocopherols and citric acid â both of which are used as preservatives âÂ despite marketing itself as being made without artificial colors or preservatives. And while there is a natural form of citric acid that can be extracted from fruit, nearly all of the world’s citric acid is manufactured through a cheaper industrial process.

As for tocopherols, they too can be found in a natural form, but most pet food makers use “mixed tocopherols” in order to extend their products’ shelf life.

“Rather than occurring as incidental constituents of the finished products, they are intentionally introduced as preservative systems to maintain freshness and prevent rancidity,” Flick writes in her complaint. “Plaintiff prefers to purchase foods free from artificial or synthetic ingredients and reviewed and relied on the label statements described above in making her purchase.”

M.I. Industries, which owns Instruct, filed a motion to dismiss, arguing that citric acid and tocopherols are chemical preservatives, not artificial preservatives. But Chief U.S. District Judge Cynthia Bashant wasn’t convinced, denying the motion earlier this week.

“The regulations are not quite so black and white,” wrote Bashant, a Barack Obama appointee. “Whether the processes used by Instinct result in ingredients that are ‘synthetic’ or ’nonsynthetic’ under the relevant regulations is a question of fact the court is not at liberty to resolve at this stage.”

Neither set of attorneys responded to emails requesting a comment on the ruling. The case remains ongoing, and will now proceed toward discovery and trial.

Savannah Welna, a canine nutritionist, says anyone obsessing over whether or not tocopherols are naturally extracted or synthesized in a lab is “missing the forest for the trees.”

“The word natural is very relative,” Welna said. “A lot of ways a vitamin or mineral might be manufactured is going to be what they consider to be unnatural. And dog food in and of itself â a dog eating the same thing every day is unnatural.”

She added: “Commercial pet food is a pretty unnatural process. Itâs very hard to formulate a fresh commercial food without some natural additions.”

At a dog park by the Silver Lake reservoir, not far from the Healthy Spot, most dog owners said they weren’t worried about the ingredients of pet food. Some said they eschew most store-bought pet food altogether and cook for their dogs.

“We want to give him as long as life as possible,” one woman said, although she still gives her dog store-bought kibble at times. “Iâm sure they put all sorts of shit in it.”

How Kelly Rowland Keeps Her Eczema Flare-Ups Under Control During Winter

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Wintertime can bring everyone down with its colder weather and less sunlight. But for those with eczema, this time of year can wreak havoc on their skin, too.

That’s the case for Grammy-winning singer, actress, and Destiny’s Child alum Kelly Rowland, who has been living with eczema since her 20s. During the winter months, she often dreads the cold.

“[You] don’t know what the environment is going to give you—because mine is also environmental—or what the weather is going to give you,” Rowland told Healthline.

Cold weather and indoor heating can reduce humidity, drying out the skin and triggering or worsening eczema flare-ups.

“[What we know] about the skin barrier is that it really is like a gate or wall against the outside world,” Camille Howard-Verovic, MD, a dermatologist at Active Dermatology, told Healthline.

In healthy skin, the barrier controls what enters and leaves the skin. However, for people with eczema, Howard-Verovic said they have skin barrier dysfunction, which allows substances such as allergens and irritants to penetrate the skin.

Cold weather can increase transepidermal water loss, a process in which moisture escapes through the skin’s outer barrier and evaporates into the surrounding air.

“People with eczema have a tremendous amount of transepidermal water loss, so you’re just drier,” said Howard-Verovic. “[You’re] not holding onto the water as well, so that causes the itching and the rash that we feel when we have eczema.”

Rowland first noticed eczema flares behind her ears as well as on her elbows, arms, and the back of her knees.

“[It] looked like a rash, so that’s what I thought it was all this time, and I dealt with it for so long until after I had my son and then it started to get worse on my face,” she said.

She eventually went to a dermatologist who diagnosed her with moderate-to-severe eczema, a chronic inflammatory skin condition that can cause intense itching, painful skin cracking, thickened skin, and redness or swelling.

When Rowland learned that eczema can be genetic, it made sense to her.

“I remember seeing big rashes on [my mom’s] neck and her arms…so she had it, and I guess I was the lucky one,” said Rowland.

At first, she tried eliminating foods that she thought might be triggers. She also bathed in oatmeal.

“[I] literally have had a tub full of oatmeal, like literally went to the store to get store-bought fresh oats,” she said. “I tried everything, and it doesn’t help until you go talk to a doctor about it.”

At first, she tried prescription topical medications but found them cumbersome.

“I don’t have the time, if I’m being honest, and we live in a world that is moving so fast…I started buying like three tubes of whatever it was I was using and losing it; it was just too much for me,” Rowland said.

Rowland said she found relief with Ebglyss, a prescription injectable medication for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis. She has also partnered with Eli Lilly and Company, its manufacturer, to raise awareness about eczema.

Rowland said she’s sharing her experience to help others living with eczema, including people she’s met who have experienced greater challenges managing the condition.

“One lady was telling me about how she’s having a tough time [looking] in the mirror cause she doesn’t know who’s looking back at her, and she saw her skin before eczema and after,” said Rowland.

When she first had symptoms, she said she felt embarrassed, too.

“[Because] it was just in the middle of my arm space and…[if I] put out my hand to shake someone’s hand, you don’t know if somebody’s judgy,” she said.

Rowland hopes being open about her condition will encourage others to advocate for themselves and talk with their doctor about treatment options.

Because eczema can look different on deeper skin tones, Howard-Verovic noted that a dermatologist can help make the correct diagnosis.

“Some people may not even know that they have eczema,” she said. “We know that eczema is a rash; it can appear red on certain skin tones, and it can be grey or white on certain skin tones, so it can look a little different.”

In addition to medication, Rowland said managing stress helps reduce her eczema flare-ups.

“Before [when] I went on tour, because I was under so much stress, it flared up like crazy, like you wouldn’t believe,” she said. “We were in the back trying to figure out how to cover certain things up, and we figured some of it out, and some of it was really tricky, but what helped was when I actually just calmed down…not that it helped overnight, but it did just kind of bring the flare down.”

She said red light therapy, feeling grounded outside without her shoes on, getting vitamin D from natural sunlight, and cooking are ways she reduces ongoing stress.

“Cooking calms me when my kids aren’t here, and I have a really good [Zen] playlist on,” said Rowland. “[If] you ask me a question [in those moments], I might give you my heart; I’m just so happy.”

She also tries to cut back on sugar, which she believes triggers flares.

Another way to reduce flares, according to Howard-Verovic, is to avoid hot showers, especially in the winter.

“[We] know eczematous skin likes lukewarm water,” she said.

Because Howard-Verovic and her daughter both live with eczema, she set a rule about when it’s time to get out of the shower.

“[If] you see condensation on the bathroom mirror, you’ve spent too much time in the shower,” said Howard-Verovic. “[That] really hot, hot water feels really good in that moment…but when you get out all that water evaporates, and there’s no moisture in the atmosphere, and it dries your skin out, and now your skin is unhappy again.”

If you prefer to take baths, she said use gentle cleansers.

“[There] are bleach baths for really, really bad flare-ups,” she noted.

Diluted bleach baths are sometimes recommended for people with eczema, but they should only be used according to a healthcare professional’s instructions.

The AI Code Maintenance Myth: Why 'Unmaintainable' Isn't the Real Problem

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at tamiz.pro

Somewhere between the first 'AI writes a sorting algorithm' demo and today's million-line codebases, a comforting narrative hardened into conventional wisdom: AI-generated code is unmaintainable. It's too clever, too dense, too inconsistent. Reviewers dread it. Junior engineers can't read it. It's a ticking debt bomb.

This narrative is wrong — not because AI code is maintainable, but because the diagnosis is. 'Unmaintainable' is a label we apply when we can't find the intent behind a change. AI doesn't fail at producing maintainable code; it fails at producing code that carries intent. Those are different problems, and confusing them has led teams to waste enormous effort reviewing line-level style when they should be fixing the architectural and organizational gaps that AI exposes — a distinction with real consequences for how we build and review AI-assisted codebases.

The claim usually takes one of three forms:

The core error is treating maintainability as a property of individual lines of code. It isn't. Maintainability is a property of the system — the relationships between code, tests, documentation, architecture decisions, and the humans who hold context about why things are the way they are.

When an engineer says 'this AI code is unmaintainable,' they're usually describing a specific experience: they open a file, see 400 lines of plausible-looking logic, and cannot answer the most basic question — why does this branch exist?

That's not a code-quality problem. That's a context problem. The code is fine; the intent is missing. And intent is missing because the AI never had it in the first place.

A human writing code is constantly negotiating with the system: the module's boundaries, the invariants that must hold, the edge cases the tests don't cover, the three prior attempts that failed. That negotiation leaves traces — a comment here, a defensive check there, a slightly awkward function that signals 'this was written around a constraint.' AI, by default, generates the average implementation. It produces the most statistically likely code for a prompt, stripped of the scars that encode a system's history.

So the real question isn't 'is this code maintainable?' It's 'does anyone know what this code is supposed to do, and why?'

Here's the uncomfortable truth: AI-generated code often scores better on mechanical maintainability metrics — cyclomatic complexity, duplication, adherence to style guides — than the human code it replaces. Models are trained on vast corpora of well-structured code, and they reproduce its shape faithfully.

What they can't reproduce is the rationale. The decision to use a particular algorithm, the awareness of a platform quirk, the knowledge that a certain code path exists only to handle a legacy data format. These aren't in the training data; they're in the heads of the engineers who built the system.

This is why 'just review harder' fails as a strategy. When you review AI code, you're not reviewing a diff; you're reviewing a claim — 'this code does what the prompt asked.' But the prompt rarely captures the full set of constraints the code must satisfy. The gap between prompt and constraint set is where maintainability dies.

Here's the part that should worry teams more than 'unmaintainable code': AI doesn't create technical debt from nothing — it amplifies and conceals what's already there.

Consider a codebase with weak test coverage and muddled architecture. A developer prompts an AI to add a feature. The model produces code that fits the surface of the codebase — it uses the right imports, matches the naming conventions, slots into the existing patterns. But it can't know that the existing patterns are themselves the problem. It can't see that the module boundary it just crossed was already leaking abstractions. It happily extends the wrong architecture, making it more entrenched.

Worse, the code looks clean. It's formatted, it's type-safe, it has no obvious smells. So it sails through review, and the team's technical debt grows by a few hundred lines without anyone feeling the pain. The debt is invisible because the code is too plausible.

This is the real maintenance crisis: not that AI writes bad code, but that it writes good-looking code that quietly deepens systemic problems.

The most important shift AI has caused is in where engineering effort goes. For decades, the bottleneck in software development was writing code. Now it's reading it — specifically, reading code you didn't write and don't understand.

When AI generates 80% of a feature, the human's job changes from author to curator. Instead of expressing intent through code, they must:

This is a fundamentally different skill set. It's closer to code review of a stranger's pull request than to writing your own. And most teams haven't retrained for it. They're still applying review heuristics designed for diffs where the author had full context.

The result is a paradox: AI makes code generation nearly free, but makes comprehension the bottleneck. Teams that don't adapt will find that their velocity is capped not by how fast they can write code, but by how fast they can understand and trust it.

If the problem is missing intent, then the fix isn't better prompting or stricter linting. It's restoring intent to the system. Four things matter more than anything else:

1. Architectural contracts, not code. Before generating anything, define the invariants, the boundaries, and the interfaces the code must respect. AI is excellent at filling in a well-specified slot and terrible at inventing one. The more you can move intent into explicit contracts — types, interfaces, architectural tests — the less you depend on the AI to guess it.

2. Tests as the carrier of intent. Tests are the most reliable way to encode what a system is supposed to do. A strong test suite doesn't just catch regressions; it tells the AI — and the next engineer — what the behavior must be. Teams with deep test coverage will find AI-generated code far more trustworthy, because the verification loop is automated.

3. Ownership and context. The single biggest predictor of maintainable AI code is a human who owns the module and holds the context. 'AI wrote it' is fine; 'no one owns it' is fatal. Assign a named owner for every generated block, and make context transfer — docs, ADRs, design notes — part of the workflow, not an afterthought.

4. Review for intent, not style. Stop reviewing AI code for formatting or duplication. Review it for why: does this code belong here? Does it respect the module's purpose? Does it handle the failure cases the system actually has? This requires reviewers to think about architecture, not just the diff.

'AI code is unmaintainable' is a convenient myth because it lets teams off the hook. It says: the problem is the tool, not the process. But the evidence points the other way. AI produces code that is mechanically clean and semantically hollow. The maintenance problem is that we've been treating code as the thing to maintain, when it's actually the intent — the architectural decisions, the tests, the ownership, the context — that needs constant upkeep.

AI didn't create this problem. It just made it impossible to ignore, because now we can generate code faster than we can generate understanding. The teams that thrive won't be the ones that review harder. They'll be the ones that build systems where intent is explicit, tests are deep, and every line has a human who knows why it exists.

That's a much harder problem than 'unmaintainable code.' But it's the real one.
