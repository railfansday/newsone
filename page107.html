At least 21 killed after migrant boat sinks off Senegal

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The incident occurred near Kebemer off the coast of northwestern Senegal. Authorities on El Hierro in the Canary Islands have declared an emergency after a marked increase in migrant boat arrivals

At least 21 people were killed after a small boat carrying migrants sank off the coast of northwestern Senegal, Senegalese authorities said.

Thousands have died in recent years after embarking on boats from the Senegalese coast in order to reach Spain's Canary Islands through the perilous Atlantic route.

According to the Senegalese Interior Ministry, only one person was rescued from the capsizing, and 21 bodies were recovered.

The ministry added that the toll was "provisional" and that search operations were still ongoing.

The incident occurred near Kebemer, some 180 kilometers (around 112 miles) north of the capital, Dakar.

On Saturday, the ministry stressed the government's "determination to combat people smuggling networks, notably through strengthened cooperation with neighboring countries."

Senegalese authorities said they intercepted 142 migrants on a similar boat aimed at reaching Spanish territory on Friday. The boat included nationals of multiple West African countries.

Authorities on El Hierro Island in the Canary Islands declared an emergency on Saturday after a marked increase in migrant boat arrivals.

El Hierro officials say that over 3,300 people have arrived on around 30 boats since the start of the month.

"We are very concerned... by this sharp increase in arrivals," Alpidio Armas, head of the El Hierro regional council, told Spanish television.

"This is precisely what has led us to declare an emergency in order to ensure a framework of cooperation with higher-level institutions," he added.

Armas said that conditions at the island's hospital have become "undesirable."

El Hierro is home to about 11,400 residents.

Migrant arrivals reached a record high of 46,000 in 2024. Afterwards, arrivals have fallen sharply due to increased surveillance and cooperation with African countries.

Janusz Kaminski, the Polish Cameraman Who Taught Hollywood How to See

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The cinematographer behind 'Schindler's List,' 'Saving Private Ryan' and 'The Diving Bell and the Butterfly' is THR's Visionary of the Year.

Like pretty much every great cinematographer, Janusz Kamiński is Polish. As much as any single artist of his generation, he has also taught Hollywood how to see.

Kamiński, winner of THR‘s 2026 Visionary Award for excellence in cinematography, presented at the Camerimage festival on Saturday, grew up in Ziębice, a small town where, as he remembers it, the world was gray. “Although we didn’t feel it was bleak, it was bleak,” Kamiński says. “No billboards. No advertisements. The streets were dark. People dressed in darker tones.” In winter, the houses were heated with coal, and the smoke from every chimney stayed in the air. “The air had this thickness in it, like a fog, because of the coal particles.” Related Stories Movies To Make a Doc About a Nomadic Cyclist, the 'Perfect Road' Filmmakers Spent 3 Months in a Campervan Movies Camerimage Opens With a Robot, an AI Reckoning and a Minute of Silence for Kraków

But there was a lot of longing in that gray. “Poland is a very lyrical country, to some degree,” he says. “There’s that melancholy, that certain wish for something else.” Theater troupes passed through, and the local cinema promised a world beyond the town. “We were exposed to work that we didn’t experience in real life, but we wanted to experience,” Kamiński says. “The primary reason I left home is because of the theater and movies, and a life of color.”

Kamiński left Poland as a political refugee in 1981, dreaming of American movies. He spoke no English. Landing in Chicago, he spent two years learning the language and then enrolled part-time at Columbia College. In an introductory filmmaking class, he found himself in a group of five. “We had to each pick: One would be the writer, one the actor, one the director, one the producer, and one the cinematographer,” he recalls. “We literally picked straws. I got cinematography. I was 22.”

It was love at first sight. “I found out I really enjoyed creating images. It was just fascinating for me to see the flickering image on 16 mm black and white,” he says. “Just that mystery: Will it be in focus? Will it be properly exposed? Will it have a story?” A professor told him he was good at it and should keep going. “This is not a sad story, but it was the first time anyone every told me I was good at something,” says Kamiński. “In Poland, when I grew up, nobody’s parents said that kind of thing. It was: You have food, you eat, you’re good.”

All his classmates at Columbia wanted to direct, so Kamiński shot their movies. He loaded a station wagon with equipment every weekend and made student films, and picked up commercial work as a camera assistant, an electrician and a grip. One commercial spot a month would pay $250. “My rent was $280, so I was doing good,” he says. “I had a job, and for an immigrant, it’s essential that we support ourselves, because there was no mommy, no daddy, no cousins.”

In the early days, The Hollywood Reporter came to his aid. Back then, in the back of the magazine, THR ran lists of movies going into production, including location shoots. “That’s how I got my first jobs, through The Hollywood Reporter,” says Kamiński. “I’d look at that films listed and I wouldn’t call on a Tom Cruise movie, but if it was something like Bambis from Hell, I’d call the producer up and say ‘Hi, my name is Janusz Kamiński. I’m a cinematographer. Would you like to look at my work? I got my first two features from that.”

Kaminski knew Chicago would not get him to Hollywood. “Most of the movies that would originate in California would come into Chicago and use secondary crew members,” he says. “I don’t want to be secondary.” His girlfriend filled out an application to the American Film Institute, and he was accepted. “That was 1986/87. I moved to California, another place I’d never been to. I had no car.” He rented a place within walking distance of campus.

A classmate’s husband, a cinematographer, was shooting a movie for Roger Corman and needed a key grip. Kamiński took the job. “Meanwhile, the gaffer quit. So I said, I like lighting. Let me. So I became the gaffer.” He kept shooting second unit and small independent films while he finished school.

The real break came through his demo reel. A friend of a friend was Diane Keaton’s assistant. “He shows my demo reel, my student film demo reel, to Diane Keaton and, suddenly, I have a job shooting for Diane Keaton.” The film was Wildflower, a period TV movie with Patricia Arquette and Beau Bridges. Steven Spielberg saw it and offered Kaminski another TV gig, Class of ‘61, a pilot Spielberg was producing. The show, about West Point cadets on the eve of the Civil War, didn’t get picked up. But Spielberg liked what he saw. When the pilot wrapped, the two men met. “He says, ‘I’d like you to film my next movie. I’m doing a black and white movie called Schindler’s List. Would you be interested?’”

Spielberg first went off to do Jurassic Park. “It took him eight to nine months to finish editing,” Kamiński recalls. “All that time I knew I had a job coming with Steven Spielberg. But nobody believed me. Only when I shot the movie, it became reality.”

With Spielberg, Kamiński went home to Poland. The film he shot there, full of the gray light and smoky streets he had grown up in, changed how Hollywood shot history. He says the two of them barely discussed the visual style beforehand. “We knew it would be shot in Poland and be black-and-white, but we didn’t have a deep, in-depth conversation about how we’re going to make this movie,” he recalls. “We just wanted to strip away anything that would take us away from the story, so there was no crane, no dolly and no Steadicam. I brought in the idea of using handheld.”

Schindler’s List won Kamiński his first Oscar for Best Cinematography. Saving Private Ryan won him a second. The script for that film, he points out, contained one sentence about the landing at Normandy: “Now the troops land at Normandy. That’s it. So as a cinematographer, you have to create the language that corresponds to the story, to the emotions, to the sets and the locations.” What he and Spielberg came up with, all handheld, drained of color and chaotic, rewrote the language of the war movie.

Shooting Julian Schnabel’s The Diving Bell and the Butterfly, Kamiński took another phenomenal visual approach to depict the story of a man with locked-in syndrome, only able to see the world through a single functioning eye. In the film, won Camerimage’s Golden Frog honor in 2007, the lens shifts sharply in and out of focus, blurring most of the field of vision while some details pop with intense clarity.

“The script, and the book it was based on, are great,” says Kamiński, but provided little guidance. “The whole description is: ‘The hero returns from darkness, from being unconscious for the last two weeks, and starts seeing the world through one eye.’ That’s it.”

Despite certain aesthetic preferences — ”I do like strong light, particularly backlight, I like characters in half-light, going into and out of the shadows. I like smoke” he says — Kamiński does not have a signature look. His approach is film follows function. Cold blues and metallic shadows to evoke the tech-dystopia of Minority Report. Retro pastels and smooth tracking shots to make the 60s swing in Catch Me If You Can. Grainy, handheld shots of muted oranges and browns in Munich to give the story the feel of a 1970s conspiracy thriller.

The Kamiński/Spielberg collaboration, which now stretches over 30 years and more than 20 films, has to count as one of the most significant artistic collaborations in American cinema history. His work with Kamiński marks the second phase of Spielberg’s career, as the director moved from glossy, classical Hollywood imagery, sharp lights and bright colors, to a grittier, more naturalistic style, appropriate to the more mature, morally-ambivalent stories he wanted to tell.

Kamiński does not claim credit for the director’s evolution. “Steven was not ready to do Schindler’s List until he was ready,” he says. “He was moving that way and I entered his life at the right point where he was able to accept the aesthetics I gave him and embrace them. I think what he saw in me was someone new with a new point of view, someone who wasn’t biased by Hollywood, with a fresh approach. He saw me as a partner. And I saw him as a partner.”

The partnership continues on Spielberg’s latest, Disclosure Day, which has Kamiński working in a different register. “As I’m getting older, things are becoming more simplistic,” he says. “I want to go slightly more reserved, relaxed.”

For the film, a sci-fi thriller about aliens, but set in the present day, he says he aimed for “something more naturalistic, more contemporary.” In one scene, in a safe house, a gray, very direct light comes through the window, slightly green, with a little flare in the background. “It’s got austerity in the images,” Kamiński says, “and that comes from growing up in Poland, with the images from my childhood.”

Next up is another collaboration with Spielberg, on an untitled Western, the director’s first, which Spielberg hopes to shoot next year. Kamiński is already imagining the landscape, drawing, as always, from the world he grew up in.

“We used to go to this region in the Sudeten Mountains, where it was just beautiful, rolling hills, looked like parts of Montana,” he says. “The lay of the land, the snow on the ground, the fog.” All these elements exist in America too, he acknowledges, but “those who shoot American movies tend to come from somewhere else, and we see these same things differently. That’s why there’s room in this profession for men and women from different countries who bring different aesthetics to American movies.”

Can a hacker really do anything with your IP address?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An IP address won’t hand over your passwords, but it can give attackers somewhere to aim

In November 2021, Twitch streamer xQc lost his internet connection shortly after playing "Crab Game." The game's developer then warned streamers to avoid public lobbies after discovering that the game could expose players' IP addresses to distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks. Although xQc couldn't confirm an attack caused that specific outage, the developer soon switched to newer Steam networking to hide IP addresses.

IP addresses basically help route data to and from websites and games. Now, most home routers let multiple devices share one public IPv4 address, while IPv6 can give each device its own public-facing address. But before you pull out the tinfoil hat, understand that neither type of IP address gives someone access to your passwords or personal data. However, both can give a hacker a target to aim at, and that's enough to raise an eyebrow.

An IP lookup is a quick way to reveal your internet service provider and approximate city or region. Back in 2016, a Kansas family sued MaxMind after its database mapped approximately 650 million unrelated IP addresses to their farm, prompting repeated law enforcement visits over incidents involving people elsewhere.

A typical DDoS attack floods a target with traffic from multiple sources. It's the same kind of attack Bluesky blamed for its server outages, and on a smaller scale, it could interrupt gaming sessions, video calls, or even an entire household internet connection. A firewall may offer little help once the internet connection is overwhelmed.

To access a device, an attacker needs a way in, such as an exposed security camera, remote-access software with a security flaw, or a home server with weak password protection. IPv4 port forwarding or permissive IPv6 firewall settings can expose those services.

Some IPv6 addresses are used only within local or private networks. Temporary IPv6 addresses can change over time, making individual devices harder to track, although devices on the same home network may still share a recognizable address prefix.

IPv6 can give devices their own public IP addresses, which sounds riskier than it is. A properly configured router firewall can still block unsolicited connections while allowing responses to traffic initiated from inside your network.

An OpenWrt user on Reddit turned on IPv6, tested their home server remotely, and discovered that their remote-access and file-sharing ports were reachable from outside the network. Their firewall had been set to allow forwarding, and changing that setting to reject closed the opening.

In October 2026, Microsoft disclosed CVE-2026-105016, a critical vulnerability in its QUIC implementation. It could allow specially crafted IPv6 traffic to execute code in affected .NET applications on Windows. Microsoft's advisory hasn't mentioned whether attackers had exploited the flaw.

So rather than disabling IPv6 altogether, check your router's firewall settings for both protocols, remove unnecessary remote-access rules, and install security updates. Microsoft mentions that turning off IPv6 could result in problems with some of the Windows components. And while a VPN can hide your usual public IP address from websites if it handles both IPv4 and IPv6 traffic correctly, it isn't going to patch vulnerable software. It's also worth checking that your VPN is working before you rely on it.

Antarctic blue whales survived near extinction, but can they fully recover?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Antarctic blue whales are slowly recovering from whaling, but their population remains around 2% of its former size.

Several whale populations that were hunted hard in the 20th century have already climbed back to their old numbers. Antarctic blue whales, the largest blue whales on Earth, are not one of them.

Their numbers are rising again, but a new estimate suggests there are still only about 2% as many as there were before whaling began.

Since the last recorded catch in 1973, the population has grown nearly tenfold, from about 418 whales to about 4,018 in 2024.

The team got those numbers from a computer model rather than a head count, so each one comes with a lot of uncertainty.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington’s School of Marine and Environmental Affairs, led the work with colleagues in the US, Australia, Japan and South Africa.

It’s the first full assessment of these whales in more than 20 years.

“No whale was hunted quite to the extent that Antarctic blue whales were hunted,” Rand said.

In 1904, whalers killed 11 Antarctic blue whales. The hunt grew so fast that by 1930 they were taking 30,405 in a single year.

Breeding couldn’t keep up with a hunt on that scale. In many seasons, whalers removed between 10% and 40% of the whole population.

By the time the last recorded catch occurred in 1973, whalers had killed a total of 347,662 Antarctic blue whales.

The team estimates that about 206,000 Antarctic blue whales lived in the Southern Ocean before whaling, and that only about 418, roughly 0.2%, were left by 1973.

Even a ban didn’t stop the killing right away. The International Whaling Commission agreed to end the commercial catch of these whales in 1963, but illegal Soviet whaling carried on for about another decade.

Counting whales spread around an entire continent is hard, so the team pulled together three kinds of information, starting with the old catch records.

The second came from ship surveys, including three full trips around Antarctica between 1978 and 2004 that recorded every blue whale the crews saw. Japanese ships also surveyed part of the same waters from 1989 to 2008.

The third came from photographs, since each whale can be recognized from pictures of its sides.

By tracking how often the same whales reappeared in photos from 2003 to 2019, the team estimated about 2,889 in Antarctic waters for the 2018 to 2019 season.

The researchers then combined all three in one computer model. It estimated how many whales were alive in every year from 1904 to 2024.

The model’s best estimate for 2024 is 4,018 whales.

The estimated range runs from about 1,900 to 8,400 whales, and even the high end would be less than 5% of the population before whaling.

So the whales are coming back, but from an extremely low starting point.

The team estimates the population is growing by about 4.5% a year, slower than the 7.3% a year reported in the last assessment, published in 2004.

Co-author Trevor Branch of the University of Washington’s School of Aquatic and Fishery Sciences led that study.

The team traced the difference to updated Japanese survey numbers and the new photo-based count.

“I think this is relatively realistic for the kind of population growth we would expect from them,” Rand said.

If growth continues along the model’s path, Antarctic blue whales wouldn’t reach even 60% of their old numbers until around 2102.

The authors note that this date depends on how many whales lived there before whaling, a number that is itself uncertain.

Other whales have recovered much faster. Blue whales off western North America, gray whales in the eastern North Pacific and North Pacific humpbacks have all returned to or passed their pre-whaling numbers in the past decade.

But most of those populations weren’t pushed down nearly as far. Growth that starts from a few hundred animals takes a long time to add up.

“I think it’s probably an open question whether they will make it back to pre-whaling levels,” Rand added.

Antarctic blue whales feed mainly on Antarctic krill, small shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean. That food supply may have changed.

Large baleen whales dive deep, eat several tons of krill a day and excrete iron-rich waste near the surface.

Scientists think that waste feeds the tiny ocean plants krill depend on, so losing millions of whales may have meant less krill.

Blue whales returning now will likely face more competition for that krill. Humpback, fin and Antarctic minke whales eat it too, and they may be recovering faster.

Krill fishing is projected to grow, and earlier research has linked shrinking sea ice and warmer water to fewer krill.

The experience of southern right whales shows how recovery can stall.

Several of their populations are having fewer calves, with longer gaps between births, even though they’re still well below their pre-whaling size.

If the Southern Ocean can now support fewer blue whales than it once did, the authors wrote, the old population size may no longer be a realistic target.

The new numbers are probably on the low side. Ship surveys assume observers spot every whale directly in the ship’s path, but blue whales are often underwater, and some likely get missed.

Studies of other blue whale populations suggest those misses could make the survey estimates 4% to 25% too low.

The photo count assumes whales mix randomly around Antarctica each year. In fact, they return to the same ocean basin 54% to 64% of the time, compared with about 33% if they moved at random.

Both problems point toward more whales and faster growth than the model shows. Neither would change the main finding, which is that the population is growing but still far from recovered.

Keeping track is also getting harder, because full ship surveys around Antarctica have largely stopped.

Underwater microphones can pick up blue whale songs, but scientists still can’t turn the number of calls into a count of whales.

“Antarctica is a big place, and it’s not just one country’s ocean,” Rand said. “We really need more monitoring and continued monitoring to figure out what is going to happen to this species in the future.”

The authors called for more ship surveys, more photographs and more listening.

Without that work, researchers won’t be able to tell whether the recovery holds up as the Southern Ocean changes.

The full study was published in the journal Endangered Species Research.

But it’s not a miss either, and I am interested in seeing just how much of the Spirit vacuum Frontier chooses to operate.
