Seagate's new hybrid drive raises some old questions

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The FireCuda Forge revisits an old idea, with some familiar questions.

Seagate doesn’t think hybrid drives are done just yet. On Tuesday, the company announced the launch of the new FireCuda Forge line of storage drives. The new drives combine the high capacity of a traditional plattered HDD with the speed and responsiveness of a user-supplied solid-state drive (SSD). The new hybrid storage solution is Seagate’s first to be powered and managed by the new Alkemi intelligent storage platform.

The FireCuda Forge is available right now through Seagate’s web store, and comes in 8TB ($699) or 20TB ($899) HDD configurations—neither of which includes the required SSD. Users will have to bring their own SSD, with some specific restrictions. The FireCuda Forge is compatible with M.2 2280 NVMe drives that are PCIe Gen3 or higher. The maximum supported NVMe capacity is 1TB, which means that’s as much data as you’ll be able to dedicate to storage on the “fast side” of the hybrid setup. The NVMe will require minimum speeds of 2,400MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write, and a unit with DRAM cache is recommended. Seagate claims 2,500MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write speeds for the combined system, along with USB4 and support for Windows 11 and macOS 26+.

Credit: Seagate Credit: Seagate

After dipping to historical lows earlier in the decade, storage pricing per TB has exploded amid the AI boom. According to data collected by Tom’s Hardware, some solid-state drives now cost two to three times their historic lows, and it’s not just SSDs. Mechanical drives haven’t been immune either. According to PCServerParts, a Seagate Exos 24TB drive that sold for $298.99 in the summer of 2025 was priced at $699.99 by August 2026.

Hybrid drives had largely fallen out of fashion since their heyday in the early to mid 2010s. The promise then was similar to what Seagate is offering now. A large HDD is combined with a smaller SSD and handed to the user as a single storage pool. Apple introduced its own hybrid solution back in 2012 with the Mac mini’s Fusion Drive. When Ars opened up a unit, the Fusion Drive system was found to write in 128KB tiered blocks. Information was generally written to the SSD first, and was then migrated to the HDD as needed. If the files were frequently used, they might find a more permanent home on the SSD side of things.

As solid-state storage continued getting cheaper, the hybrid approach was largely abandoned. Fast-forward to our current storage cost predicament in 2026, and Seagate’s line of thinking that everything old is new again starts to hold water. All storage costs have exploded, but the 2012 math still works today. NAND flash is still fast, but expensive, and traditional mechanical drives are at least comparatively cheaper per TB for higher capacity storage.

The problem with Apple’s Fusion solution was its fragility. If one of the two drives failed, that was likely it. Recovery depended on both drives coming together as a single volume. If one of those two disks experienced a physical failure, recovery became difficult since the remaining working drive depended on the now broken drive’s data to rebuild the full system picture.

According to Seagate’s press release, the Alkemi intelligent storage platform takes a different approach. Where Apple’s Fusion Drives used tiering managed by macOS, Alkemi manages the storage pool directly from the enclosure. Seagate says, “Alkemi learns and adapts over time to keep high-demand content fast and accessible on a user’s SSD, while the HDD provides space for growing libraries. Behind the scenes, Alkemi manages data placement, giving users the simplicity of a single storage drive.” The product data sheet PDF from the FireCuda Forge website describes the Alkemi system as a two-tier storage system, but doesn’t elaborate any further on whether the system uses SSD caching, automated tiering, a combination of the two, or something else entirely.

The announcement doesn’t address how the Alkemi platform manages the data between the two drives. The old Intel Smart Response Technology kept failure risks low by mirroring data from the mechanical HDD to the faster SSD, with the HDD retaining the authoritative copy. The optional write-back mode could still hold new data on the SSD, though. Apple’s Fusion Drive instead tiered data in 128KB blocks across the storage pool, storing data on both drives. The distinction matters, and is probably why Ars asked the same question after Apple’s hybrid announcement 14 years ago.

Seagate’s announcement speaks of “intelligent data tiering” without further explanation. The data system promises that your most accessed files will be quickest to access via intelligent storage management, but stops short of explaining whether one drive going down means failure for the whole system. There’s also no mention of how the Alkemi system distributes data on first boot. Does all the new written data go directly to the SSD until the 1TB cap is crossed, and then the system starts determining which files are important enough to remain on the speedier drive? Seagate seems to be keeping things mum for the moment, but as the drives have already launched for sale, there should be answers to all of these questions coming soon.

Seagate hasn’t published technical documentation regarding how the Alkemi system handles drive failure or repair. Until it does, the new FireCuda Forge drives offer the same appeal as Apple’s old Fusion Drives: SSD speed with HDD capacity, along with the same questions Ars was asking about hybrid storage years ago.

GE Vernova's backlog is large and supports predictable future revenue. However, there is a range of outcomes for its future valuation based on the sustainability of order flows through 2030.

Power, electrification, and wind turbine company GE Vernova's (GEV -3.90%) backlog recently increased to $197 billion at the end of the third quarter. Furthermore, it's now highly likely to be beyond the $200 billion figure that CEO Scott Strazik believes is a "humble milestone" on the way to long-term growth. That growth will be driven by services revenue from its rapidly growing installed base of gas turbines (power segment) and electrical equipment (electrification segment) and services sales. Here's a look at what an investment in the stock could be worth in a few years.

2030 may seem a bit far out, but the reality is GE Vernova's backlog makes its revenue relatively predictable. For reference, the midpoint of management's 2026 revenue guidance is $47 billion, or less than a quarter of the current backlog.

In the company's Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) filings, GE Vernova reported that 97% of its equipment backlog, or remaining performance obligations (RPO), of $104 billion is expected to be recognized within five years, as is 54% of its $92.6 billion in services RPO. Adding these figures together gives a figure of $150 billion by the third quarter of 2031. However, that's only part of the story, because GE Vernova will also generate revenue from new orders signed during the period in question (about a third of its equipment RPO is recognized within a year).

All told, GE Vernova's revenue is relatively predictable, and, based on the Wall Street consensus from Visible Alpha, it will generate $79.3 billion in revenue in 2030 and $13.1 billion in free cash flow (FCF), implying a price-to-FCF multiple of 14.9.

The question then turns to what GE Vernova should trade at in 2030 given its growth prospects thereafter. Based on the visibility in its backlog, a conservative estimate would assume it is a mature cash-cow type of company thereafter and place it at 20 times FCF in 2030. This implies it's 34.2% undervalued right now, assuming it's trading at 14.9 times 2030 FCF. As such, $10,000 invested now would be worth $13,420 in 2030.

That said, investors should note that each gas turbine installed generates a long-term stream of higher-margin services revenue, and the more equipment installed (notably its largest HA turbines), the longer the revenue stream. For example, Strazik believes the HA turbines currently under contract will generate $100 billion in future services revenue over the next 20 years.

As such, if GE Vernova continues to win orders at the current rate in 2030, it will trade closer to the 25x FCF it trades at in 2027, as investors price in future growth. This implies it's 67.8% undervalued, meaning a $10,000 investment will be worth $16,778.

All told, something between the $16,778 and $13,420 figures seems reasonable to me, and the good news is the company's orders tend to be very early cycle (securing power for a future data center is a critical first step), so if there really is going to be a slowdown in AI investment as a result of recent political events, then GE Vernova investors will see it first in the company's order trends and be able to adjust accordingly.

Why Websites Show the Wrong Server Location When You Use a VPN

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are many reasons why someone might use a VPN, but most often, it’s to conceal your real geographic location or prevent websites from tracking your online activity. It can also be a way to get around location-based restrictions that stop you from accessing certain websites or platforms.

When you connect to a VPN server in the United States, websites are supposed to detect that your online traffic is originating from the U.S. That way, you can access online platforms that use IP filters to block non-U.S. traffic or circumvent regional censorship when travelling outside the U.S. But if you connect to a VPN server in the United States while you’re vacationing in Mexico, and a website thinks your IP address is from Brazil, that might not solve your problem and may even add unnecessary complications.

If you’ve encountered something similar with your VPN provider, there are several fixes you can try. Depending on why your IP location is being misinterpreted, one of these should help you access the internet from the server location of your choice.

IP geolocation isn’t infallible. Websites rely on third-party IP databases to look up your location based on your current IP address. Maxmind, which has one of the largest such databases in existence, claims that its GeoIP products have a 99.8% accuracy. Lesser-known providers with smaller IP databases may be far less accurate.

Websites can also use more than just your IP address to estimate your location. For example, they can send WebRTC requests to your browser or directly query your device’s GPS. Moreover, VPN providers often have physical servers in one country while their IP addresses are registered to another country, and this discrepancy can also create issues. Let’s take a closer look at each of these problems individually, so you can find the fix that works best for you.

WebRTC is an open-source technology that lets your browser reduce latency when engaged in high-bandwidth tasks like streaming or video calls. Based on the IETF standard RFC 8828, it lets a website choose between your VPN server and your real IP address so it can establish the quickest path to transmit large amounts of data and reduce wait times or buffering.

But the catch with WebRTC is that it exposes both your VPN server and ISP-assigned addresses to websites, which can reveal your real IP location and cause websites to reject your VPN server country. Firefox now comes with built-in protections to prevent WebRTC leaks, while Chrome and Edge support third-party extensions like WebRTC Control that you can use to eliminate these issues. To check if your browser is leaking your real IP address during WebRTC requests, you can use a free tool like BrowserLeaks.

Even when a browser isn’t leaking your real IP address, DNS resolvers may fetch inaccurate location information if their nearest servers are too far from your current location. For example, a user in São Paulo may be geolocated to Chile when using Google Public DNS because that’s where Google’s nearest resolvers are located.

If you toggle on DNS leak protection in your VPN app, this will force websites to use your VPN’s resolver to perform DNS lookups. That often solves the problem, but, again, you can confirm the fix worked through a service like BrowserLeaks or IPLeaks.

Websites rely on IP databases like Maxmind, IP2Location, IPinfo, etc. to look up your exact location, but these databases get outdated quickly and need constant updating. Most of these databases also correct errors on request, but that takes some time. Also, each website has its own rules for how often it queries IP databases for updates.

Even when locations are updated in the actual database, a website may be sitting with outdated information unless it actively has refreshed it recently. MaxMind recommends doing this at least twice a week, but that’s ultimately up to the specific website.

VPN providers often rely on virtual servers that use IP addresses registered in a different region than the server’s physical location. For example, ExpressVPN notes that it serves IP addresses in Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru, Uruguay, and Venezuela through servers located physically in Brazil.

While this is usually to help with the lack of local infrastructure or strict surveillance laws in the region, many databases and analytics tools will flag the discrepancy for websites, leading to confusion and inconsistency regarding your actual server location. In these cases, your best bet is to switch your VPN server or use a dedicated IP address by signing up for a paid add-on for your VPN.

Before investing in a specific solution, it’s better to do a thorough lookup to make sure why your IP address is pointing to the wrong location and whether a WebRTC or DNS issue, virtual servers, or something else is the cause. Here’s how I’d go about it, step by step:

We’ll start by recording your baseline IP address, country, city, and ASN. With your VPN disconnected, navigate to IPConfig.co on your browser to grab your default IP details. I like this tool because it’s free and uses echoip to pull your location data straight from Maxmind. If you prefer using Terminal instead of your browser, you can also run curl ifconfig.co/json to grab these details in plain text format.

Now connect your VPN server. Then visit IPConfig.co again to note down the changed details.

Make sure the Country and Timezone fields match your current VPN server location. Also make sure the ASN (organization) field does not list your home ISP.

In case these details do not match your VPN server, run a WebRTC leak test and a DNS leak test using BrowserLeaks. If either of these tests doesn’t return your VPN server’s IP address, you’ve found your culprit.

If both the leak tests come back clean but a specific site still gets your location wrong, the issue is likely originating from an outdated database or virtual server.

There’s no surefire way to solve IP location mismatches for good. But if it happens often enough to disrupt your internet use, you can consider switching to a different VPN server and getting a browser add-on that prevents WebRTC leaks. If that doesn’t work, I’d consider buying a dedicated IP address that isn’t based on a virtual server. While many IP databases like MaxMind or IP2Location also have ways to request a location correction, that takes a while to go through and isn’t worth the effort unless it’s a permanent IP address registered through your ISP.

How SCSKiller is trying to fix shader stuttering in PC games

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's difficult to state just how much I hate shader stutter, even in the best PC games. I'm probably the world's biggest arachnophobe and absolutely loathe mushrooms, yet I'd rather have a trained tarantula serve me deep-fried shiitake than endure the infamously stutter-heavy Star Wars Jedi: Survivor. At least, that was until I downloaded Shader Compilation Stutter Killer (SCSKiller).

This free app promises to heavily reduce or even completely get rid of shader compilation in modern PC titles. But can this open-source software actually deliver? I've tested it out with a handful of games that are notorious for stuttering, and when it's working at its best, SCSKiller is ingenious. Are you a frame-obsessed PC gamer? You need to download this app ASAP, and here's why.

Before explaining why SCSKiller is great, let's address what shader stutter actually is. In short, it's a brief visual hiccup that looks like a frame rate drop to the untrained eye. Why is this a PC-specific issue and not a console one? Because PC development isn't based on fixed hardware, whereas studios can pre-compile shaders focused on exact PS5 or Xbox Series X specs pre-release.

While frame dips and shader stutters can occur at the same time (here's how to improve FPS for better gaming), they're actually separate issues. Frame drops normally happen because of hardware-related bottlenecks; where either the GPU or CPU can't render certain visual elements or physics tasks quickly enough.

Shader stuttering is different. These little programs tell your GPU how to draw various in-game effects, like shadows, reflections or lighting. In many modern PC titles — especially Unreal 5 Engine games — shader stuttering occurs because the rendering pipeline is having to produce effects in real-time. As the next frame waits for the shader to compile, a brief frame spike may easily occur. Once you're aware of them, in-game stutters can be so distracting that they ruin immersion.

Common variety stutters can sometimes be largely overcome if you own a VRR display. Variable Refresh Rate screens (like NVIDIA G-Sync and AMD FreeSync models) dynamically match the refresh rate of your panel to the number of frames your GPU is currently drawing, substantially smoothing out visual performance. VRR displays can also help fix screen tearing in PC and console games.

President Trump's Defamation Claim Against BBC Can Go Forward

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From today's decision by Judge Jeffrey Kuntz (S.D. Fla.) in Trump v. BBC:

In Trump v. CNN (S.D. Fla. 2023), aff'd (11th Cir. 2025), Judge Singhal concluded that "the complained of statements are opinion, not factually false statements, and therefore are not actionable." In Trump v. Dow Jones & Co., Inc. (S.D. Fla. 2026), Judge Gayles dismissed President Trump's claims that the Wall Street Journal failed to adequately investigate claims it reported.

Those cases involved a news entity reporting on a story. Here, the news entity created the story. BBC admitted as much by confirming it had edited a video to make it appear as though President Trump made a statement he never made.

And it acknowledged it chose to release the video in a documentary only a week before the 2024 Presidential Election, at the time it would have the most impact. To make sure it was seen in Florida, BBC even emailed U.S. and Florida based commenters with instructions to on how to view the documentary.

Whether President Trump can prove these claims and establish he sustained damages at summary judgment or trial is left for another day. For now, after careful review, and being otherwise fully advised, BBC's Motion to Dismiss is granted [as to the defamation claim] ….

On October 28, 2024, one week before the 2024 Presidential Election, BBC broadcast a documentary titled "Trump: A Second Chance." In the Documentary, a twelve-second segment portrayed President Trump giving a speech to his supporters on January 6, 2021:

We're going to walk down to the Capitol and I'll be there with you. And we fight. We fight like hell and if you don't fight like hell, you're not going to have a country anymore.

This two sentence, twelve-second segment, represents the core of Trump's complaint. However, the issue with the statement is that Trump never said it. The above statement is actually two parts of a speech spliced together. Instead, the first remarks that Trump made were:

Now it is up to Congress to confront this egregious assault on our democracy. And after this, we're going to walk down and I'll be there with you. We're going to walk down, we're going to walk down. Anyone you want but I think right here, we're going to walk down to the Capitol and we're going to cheer on our brave senators and congressmen and women, and we're probably not going to be cheering so much for some of them.

Nearly fifty-five minutes later, Trump continued:

[B]ut I said 'Something's wrong here, Something's really wrong, can't have happened.' And we fight, we fight like hell, and if you don't fight like hell, you're not going to have a country anymore.

Additionally, less than a minute later, Trump told his supporters to peacefully walk to the Capitol, stating:

I know that everyone here will soon be marching over to the Capitol building to peacefully and patriotically make your voices heard[.]

It is those two statements, edited and spliced together, that form the alleged defamatory twelve-second segment that aired in the Documentary. BBC admits Trump never made the statements as aired. The Chairman of BBC apologized to Trump, stating that the BBC "editing unintentionally created the impression of a single continuous section of your speech, rather than excerpts from different points in the speech." This, the BBC chairman stated, "gave the mistaken impression that you had made a direct call for violent action. That was an editorial error and it should not have happened." …

The court concluded that President Trump had adequately alleged actual damages and "actual malice" (i.e., knowing and reckless falsehood) on BBC's part, though of course the court noted that both would still have to be proved eventually as a factual matter. An excerpt:

For purposes of the defamation test, ill-will is different from actual malice. An intention to portray a public figure in a negative light, even when motivated by ill-will or evil intent, is not sufficient to show actual malice unless the publisher intended to inflict harm through knowing or reckless falsehood. However, ill-will or motive, when combined with other evidence, may be taken into account for actual malice….

Here, Trump alleges that BBC has a history of unfair or improper coverage toward him. In addition to the Documentary itself, Trump highlights the Prescott Memorandum, a 2022 BBC Newsnight episode, and the "2024 BBC News Shooting Hoax." As an important reminder, the state of mind required for actual malice must be "brought home" to the person in the BBC's organization responsible for the publication of the Documentary. This principle is relevant as it relates to the 2022 Newsnight episode and the 2024 "Shooting Hoax." Again, Trump has not alleged that either of these incidents involved the creator(s) of the Documentary. That said, these incidents are not merely portrayal in a negative light, but examples of BBC playing fast and loose with the factual accuracy of their media content. With respect to the Newsnight episode, the BBC seemingly did not care about the falsity of the portrayal until the later Documentary became an issue. Such examples can be said to be probative of BBC's state of mind as it pertains to recklessness in light of BBC's non-action toward false statements about Trump.

Thus, Trump paints a picture that demonstrates the BBC has a record of allowing the publication of false statements in their portrayal of Trump. BBC asks the Court to put on blinders and treat the Panorama team as if they were blocked off from the rest of the organization, ignorant of everything else going on at BBC. At this procedural stage [i.e., a motion to dismiss, where plaintiff must merely plausibly allege facts that could lead to liability, rather than proving them -EV], the Court simply cannot ignore the circumstantial evidence presented. To do so would make it extremely difficult to ever prove a defendant's subjective state of mind in any defamation case….

Trump alleged that BBC's editing of the speech was an "intentional and malicious effort to falsely and deceptively portray President Trump as having called for violence." In support, Trump alleges that it "would have been impossible for BBC's journalists and producers to splice together two distinct parts of the [s]peech from nearly fifty-five minutes apart unless they were acting intentionally." On its face, the editing may independently establish the plausibility of actual malice. BBC tries to frame this editing as ordinary editing done to condense a seventy-minute-long speech into twelve seconds. But the Court is not persuaded.

Editing is often required. However, given the time disparity between the two statements, the distance itself tends toward a plausible inference that BBC knew or should have known that the segment they were creating would not and could not have been a truthful statement. This was not trimming around the edges but creating something different than what was stated in the speech. BBC also had the entire speech available to them for nearly three years by the time of the Documentary.

In addition to the editing itself, BBC's Chairman issued an apology:

The way the speech was edited in the documentary gave a misleading impression of what you said. The editing unintentionally created the impression of a single continuous section of your speech, rather than excerpts from different points in the speech. This gave the mistaken impression that you had made a direct call for violent action. That was an editorial error and it should not have happened.

However, the BBC now appears to disagree with the apology issued by the Chairman. BBC's Chairman admitted that the documentary gave a misleading and mistaken impression that Trump made a direct call for violent action. Strangely enough, BBC strays from that position in the Motion to Dismiss, referring to statements made by January 6 defendants as evidence that the speech may be interpreted as a call to violent action. But BBC's own Chairman acknowledged the editing was misleading in his letter to Trump….

As a final matter on the issue of actual malice, Trump alleges that BBC strayed from accepted standards of journalistic integrity. Such a claim on its own would not amount to actual malice, since actual malice requires "more than a departure from reasonable journalistic standards." A failure to investigate, standing on its own, does not indicate the presence of actual malice. To show actual malice, there must be some showing that the BBC purposefully avoided further investigation with the intent to avoid the truth.

BBC first argues that allegations BBC failed to contact Trump for comment before publishing are insufficient to establish actual malice. On this point, the Court agrees.

BBC then argues that allegations that BBC omitted portions of Trump's speech are also insufficient to establish actual malice. Alterations of quotes that "materially alter" the meaning of Trump's statements could support a finding of actual malice…. BBC did not just alter the quote but created a new quote by splicing two statements together and passing them off as a single continuous sentence. To the extent that there was no material alteration to the meaning of Trump's speech, that simply cannot be the case. The Chairman's letter makes it very clear that BBC acknowledges there was a material change of meaning when they edited the speech to create a single continuous segment that did not exist.

BBC then argues that the apology "militates against a finding of actual malice." … Today, retractions can be issued near instantaneously for online publications thanks to the internet. The Court acknowledges that media entities, like The Washington Post, or BBC, are not perfect, and make mistakes. However, it cannot be said that a retraction the next day is in the same ballpark as a retraction over a year later, and only after being threatened with legal action. The temporal distance here makes the retraction a weak indicator as to BBC's state of mind at the time of publication.

Further weakening BBC's argument, BBC was aware that such editing created a false impression as early as 2022. Former Acting White House Chief of Staff Mick Mulvaney, in relation to the 2022 Newsnight episode, stated on-air: "Your video actually spliced together the presentation.… [Y]our video makes it look like those two things came together." As BBC's own Clive Coleman stated "[t]he first lesson … you're taught as a broadcast journalist is that you do not joint two bits of footage together from different times in a way that will make the audience think that it is one piece of footage." Considering these facts, BBC's ultimate retraction in 2025 reads less like a readiness to retract, and more like a reluctance to retract.

The Court acknowledges that events from 2022 do not directly bear on the state of mind at the time of publication. But neither does a retraction over a year after publication. Hence why courts are often reliant on circumstantial evidence when making state of mind determinations. Still, Trump's allegations regarding BBC's departure from journalistic standards are more than enough to plausibly alleged more than mere negligence….

Of course, all of this does not prove BBC acted with actual malice. But it does make it plausible. And Trump need only allege facts sufficient to give rise to a reasonable inference that the BBC edited and published the Documentary "with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not." Trump has done so here….

And the court held the BBC's statement could be viewed as a false factual allegation, rather than merely "convey[ing] an opinion of what the audience understood Trump to say":

As Trump correctly points out, a spliced segment that creates a sentence that Trump never spoke represents the exact kind of false statement of fact that courts hold as the sine qua non for recovery in defamation. Whether the audience "reasonably interpreted" the segment to convey that Trump called for violence is irrelevant….

The court also held that Florida courts (including the federal courts) had jurisdiction over the BBC. But the court rejected Trump's Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act claim:

… FDUTPA is limited to acts "in the conduct of any trade or commerce." The statute defines this as "the advertising, soliciting, providing, offering, or distributing" of goods, services, property, "or any other article, commodity, or thing of value." … "[M]agazines and newspapers often have commercial purposes, but those purposes do not convert the individual articles within these editorial sources into commercial speech." Both advertising and subscriptions are typical features of newspapers, whether online or in-print. Even if BBC has commercial purposes, that does not convert the Documentary into commercial speech….

Supreme Court seems split over federal agencies' ability to fine for law violations

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Capitol is seen behind signage for U.S. Department of Labor in Washington, D.C., in August 2025. Jim Watson/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

At the Supreme Court, the justices seemed divided on Tuesday in a case that could undermine the ability of many federal regulatory agencies to fine businesses that violate federal laws.

For decades, farmers have relied on a federal immigration program that allows them to temporarily employ migrant workers to pick crops.

In addition to wages, farm owners must provide basic necessities, such as housing, access to meals and water. The whole system is essentially policed by the Department of Labor to ensure that foreign workers don't undercut domestic laborers.

In that role, the department fined Sun Valley Orchards, based in New Jersey, a half-million dollars for unsanitary conditions, including providing unsafe drinking water, housing people in 20-bunk rooms, broken sinks and other violations, including failure to pay promised wages.

Sun Valley is disputing the charges and contends that the Department of Labor imposing monetary fines based on its administrative proceedings is unconstitutional. Sun Valley also says the process denies them the right to a jury trial when and if they appeal to a federal court.

At the high court Tuesday, Chief Justice John Roberts strongly suggested that the Trump administration, and many administrations before it, have exceeded the power of the executive branch. In this case, potentially wading into the judicial branch's territory.

Addressing Deputy Solicitor General Sarah Harris, who represented the Labor Department, Roberts said, "It seems to me you're just trying to leverage … the broad authority [of the executive branch] beyond the justification for its application."

Justice Sonia Sotomayor asked how disruptive it would be for the federal courts to enforce the law instead of the designated agency (in this case the Department of Labor).

Harris responded succinctly, "If the court were to say civil penalties always have to be in Article III courts, that's kind of the nuclear option."

Harris added that such a ruling would disrupt programs as diverse as Medicare, Medicaid, the federal student loan program and more.

That prompted this question from Justice Elena Kagan: "Why not just go to court always?" Harris quickly responded, "I think it would be catastrophic." She went on to explain that a system like that would inevitably result in massive under-enforcement of the law, given limited resources.

Lawyer Robert Johnson represented Sun Valley Orchards. "To be sure," he said, "the statute contemplates penalties, but it says nothing about where those penalties should be adjudicated."

Johnson further contended that penalties should be imposed by the federal courts, not the administrative agencies that do most of that work under current law.

To that Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson replied, "It seems like you're just sort of fighting the reality of … what Congress was trying to do … in ensuring that we don't have farm workers coming in and undercutting domestic workers."

A more skeptical observation came from Justice Brett Kavanaugh: "What you're proposing is really at war with clarity," he said, adding, "I think that's not great systematically going forward."

Justice Samuel Alito pointedly added, "What you're outlining is sort of a hodge-podge opinion, and maybe sometimes courts write that sort of thing," to which Justice Kagan quickly chimed in with a chuckling crowd in the court, "Not ever us!"

In rebuttal, Deputy Solicitor General Harris was unusually candid. "It would be a big deal for the government to lose, but an even bigger deal would be to have a mess," she said. She later added, "A lot of other statutory regimes also would be on the cutting-room floor."

And if that's the case, she said, the administration will have to go to Congress. Her voice rising, she added, "Please tell us now ... if we need to get Legislative Affairs on it right now, because this will snowball."

Neocloud infrastructure darling CoreWeave Inc. delivered an impressive revenue beat and raised its full-year guidance today as it demonstrated that demand for artificial intelligence computing remains strong, pushing its stock higher in late trading.

The report comes at a prickly moment for the AI industry as investors increasingly sour on heavily indebted cloud infrastructure startups, while the broader market continues to get to grips with the spy scandal surrounding Nvidia Corp. Chief Executive Jensen Huang.

The company reported a third-quarter loss before certain costs such as stock compensation of $1.27 per share, trailing Wall Street’s target of a $1.16 per share loss. But the 168% growth in revenue meant sales came to $3.66 billion, topping expectations of $3.55 billion. All told, CoreWeave reported a net loss in the quarter of $709 million, rising from a $110 million net loss in the same period one year earlier.

Investors may be pleased with CoreWeave’s guidance, though. Company executives said they’re now looking for between $1.275 billion and $1.375 billion in adjusted operating income and $13 billion to $13.3 billion in revenue for the full year. Wall Street analysts are looking for just $1.07 billion in adjusted operating income and $12.86 billion in total sales. CoreWeave’s forecast is notably much higher than the $960 million to $1.15 billion in adjusted operating income and $12.4 billion to $13.2 billion in revenue it had forecast three months earlier.

The report was well-received by investors, as CoreWeave’s stock climbed more than 5% in late trading, helping put the brakes on a slide that has seen the company lose more than 40% of its value over the last month. Many investors have sought to abandon stocks that are closely tied to the AI infrastructure buildout amid fears that rising interest rates have made capital much more difficult to come by. Recently, some CoreWeave bond yields have topped 16%.

CoreWeave Chief Executive Michael Intrator (pictured) told analysts on a conference call that most of the company’s capital requirements in fiscal 2027 will be sourced from clients via pre-payments on infrastructure services and from hardware partners such as Nvidia. He added that the company will also seek to raise a “limited amount” in outside funding.

When pressed on this, CoreWeave’s finance chief Nitin Agrawal said the company plans to raise new debt that will be tied to a set of contracts. “At current yields we do not intend to issue additional parent-level unsecured debt through 2027,” he told analysts. “By contrast, the asset-level secured debt market has remained stable, with our loans continuing to trade around par value.”

CoreWeave, along with other neoclouds like Nebius Group N.V., which is set to report its own earnings results on Thursday, has become increasingly reliant on its hardware partner Nvidia as a major benefactor. Nvidia provides CoreWeave with not only the graphics processing units needed to populate its data centers, but also much of the capital it needs to procure those chips.

Eessentially it’s recycling the money it earns throughout the AI economy in order to keep everything ticking over. But investors have become concerned about the long-term viability of what looks like an increasingly circular economy, and Nvidia’s stock has struggled to maintain momentum this year.

With this week’s revelations that Nvidia boss Jensen Huang had an affair with a suspected Chinese spy, the chipmaker’s stock has lost almost 10% of its value to start the week. Nvidia’s board of directors has responded by launching an internal investigation, hiring the services of the high-profile legal firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to look into Huang’s personal life and determine if any company or national security secrets were compromised.

The fallout from that probe will likely have a strong bearing on CoreWeave’s fortunes going forward, as will next week’s widely anticipated initial public offering from AI leader Anthropic PBC. In an update to its IPO prospectus earlier today, the model maker said it’s hoping to achieve a $1.4 trillion valuation through its share sale.

In the meantime, CoreWeave continues to forge ahead with its buildout. During the quarter it said it’s planning to build its first data centers in Asia. It also announced a new cloud-based service called Forge for running and evaluating AI models.

The company said it hopes to have 1.85 gigawatts of active compute power in service by the end of the year, and anticipates its capital expenditure will reach between $37 billion and $39 billion. That’s more or less in line with the Street’s forecast of $37.69 billion in capex this year.

At the end of the quarter, CoreWeave’s total debt was approaching $47 billion. Like its much larger peers in the cloud computing industry, such as Amazon Web Services Inc., Microsoft Corp. and Oracle Corp., CoreWeave is racing to add more compute capacity to serve its clients.

But there are signs it may struggle to do this in the U.S. Last week, American voters either backed restrictions on data center construction or rejected pro-data center development measures in 40 of 41 states.

Intrator addressed those concerns in the conference call, insisting that he wasn’t overly concerned. “If data center construction across the sector were to slow, installed compute becomes scarcer and the knock-on effect is that GPU pricing may stay higher for longer,” he said.

In the meantime, CoreWeave continues to see demand for cloud compute escalate, and is now sitting on a massive $132.5 billion revenue backlog, Intrator said. That’s up from just $104 billion three months earlier.

Sony has dug up another plot in its expanding live-service graveyard, and is lowering the still-warm corpse of Haven Studios' Fairgames into the dirt. The shooter, which was revealed in 2023, has been canceled, though PlayStation says there will be no layoffs as a result of its decision to end the project.

"The studio will shift its focus to collaborating with other development teams across PlayStation Studios, while also exploring new creative opportunities," a PlayStation representative tells Bloomberg, who first reported the news.

Fairgames was revealed in a 2023 trailer which stylized the title as "Fairgame$." The trailer featured no in-game footage, but presented a stylish version of a Payday-style shooter focused on heists. But the game never materialized again. The last major news on the project came in 2025, when Haven Studios founder Jade Raymond – a veteran Ubisoft producer who'd worked on Assassins' Creed – left the company.

Bloomberg reports that Fairgames was ultimately canceled after a poor showing during a recent test session with thousands of players. "While the test demonstrated strong operational excellence, live ops readiness and community engagement, player feedback and retention results were mixed and fell short of the goals we had set," according to an internal memo.

PlayStation had apparently reworked the game multiple times since it was first revealed, and prior to the test had planned to showcase it again under a new title, according to the report.

While PlayStation once had extensive plans for live-service games, it's canceled so many of those projects that it's now difficult to keep track of them all. The death of Concord just two weeks after launch is perhaps the highest profile example, but that's just the most memorable corpse among an extensive trail of bodies.

A multiplayer God of War title from Bluepoint and a live-service game from Bend Studio were canceled in 2025. A multitude of unannounced projects were iced amid mass layoffs at PlayStation in 2024, including a Twisted Metal game and an original project from the publisher's now-defunct London studio. Naughty Dog's The Last of Us multiplayer game died in 2023. Bungie has reportedly had numerous projects scrapped since it was acquired by Sony. PlayStation has even killed not just one, but two projects helmed by Call of Duty veteran Jason Blundell.

The Las Vegas Raiders traded quarterback Kirk Cousins to the Minnesota Vikings for a 3rd round pick (the Vikings' second in the round) Tuesday. it opens the door for the Raiders play No. 1 overall draft pick Fernando Mendoza, who general manager John Spytek confirmed Tuesday afternoon will be the starter moving forward.

Here are some notes about their deadline decisions:

The Raiders, at 4-5, didn't decide to make it a fire sale. They also didn't opt to add at the trade deadline either as they have lost five of the last six games after starting 3-0.

NFL reporter Jordan Schulz reported that the Raiders did receive recent trade calls on star defensive end Maxx Crosby, but resisted them after a nixed trade with the Baltimore Ravens in March. Because the Raiders have already agreed to trade Crosby once, expect them to receive more calls in the future, including next offseason.

There is no word if the Raiders had any interest in pending free agents, wide receiver Tre Tucker and safety Jeremy Chinn. It will be interesting to see if they try to extend them in the offseason. Spytek said he received calls on multiple players without getting into detail.

Despite needs at some posts, including at wide receiver and defensive tackle, the Raiders did not add at the deadline.

Spytek said the Cousins' trade is not an indication the team is giving up on the season.

Coach Klint Kubiak said Mendoza is prepared, physically and mentally, for this task and he praised Cousin for his influence on the rookie. Spytek said Mendoza has passed every test since being drafted and he helped the Raiders make the tough decision to deal Cousins. Spytek said Mendoza's play in practice was difficult to ignore.

Spytek said he only received calls on Cousins and did not make any himself. The talks with Minnesota emerged Tuesday morning after Carson Wentz's horrible showing in a loss Monday night. Spytek said the team did not expect this to happen before Tuesday, thus he had no previous conversations with Cousins about it.

Spytek said he and Kubiak spent a lot of time before agreeing to the Cousins trade, but, ultimately decided it was best for the franchise.

Why AAON Stock Popped Today

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Effective heat control systems are an increasingly vital component of the AI boom.

Shares of AAON (AAON +19.31%) leaped 19% on Tuesday after the heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) equipment maker boosted its full-year growth forecast.

AAON's net sales soared 84% year over year to $707 million in the third quarter.

Growth in the company's artificial intelligence (AI)-focused BASX segment was particularly robust, with net sales up a whopping 207% to $231 million.

AAON's high-performance thermal management solutions help to keep data centers cool. The HVAC infrastructure supplier has been ramping up its manufacturing capacity to meet the surging demand for its products.

"Compute density is increasing, power availability remains constrained, efficiency matters more, and thermal management becomes increasingly more important as these systems become more power dense," CEO Matt Tobolski said during a conference call with analysts.

AAON's impressive sales growth, combined with its cost-containment initiatives, drove its profit margins higher. Operating income rocketed 129% to $100 million, while adjusted earnings per share climbed 124% to $0.83.

These strong results prompted AAON to lift its full-year guidance. Management now sees net sales growing by 72% to 75% in 2026, up from a prior forecast of 55% to 60%.

"Customer engagement remains strong, our opportunity pipeline is healthy, and we continue to strengthen our position with strategic data center customers," CEO Matt Tobolski said.
