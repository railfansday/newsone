Inflation Hotter Than Economist Expectations, Raising Odds of 2027 Rate Hikes

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Inflation picked up speed in October, as the latest Consumer Price Index (CPI) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) accelerated faster than predictions from economists.

The October CPI saw the all-items index grow 0.4%, a slight decrease from last month’s 0.6% rise, but “hotter than expected,” according to Navy Federal Chief Economist Heather Long. Original forecasts were at 0.2% to 0.3% increases, as noted by Realtor.com® Senior Economist Jake Krimmel.

Inflation’s jump this month was due to increases in shelter inflation (up 0.3%), contributing to over 30% of the increase. Food inflation also advanced in October by 0.5%. Energy inflation, which decelerated in October, still saw a rise of 0.7%.

This month’s data does not include annual inflation numbers as the BLS noted it did not collect data in October 2025 due to the government being shut down at the time. As a result, it cannot calculate 12-month percent changes.

With the data available, Long said October’s increase “implies about a ~4% inflation rate for the past year.”

The index for all items less food and energy—core inflation—grew by 0.3% in October, the same rate of growth it saw in both September and August. Indexes that contributed to increase include household furnishings and operations, recreation, medical care, motor vehicle insurance and education.

Long said this month’s data “underscores why so many Americans voted the way they did in the mid-term elections,” speaking of the “blue wave” that overtook the elections last week.

“The same supply-side and geopolitical pressures are still at work, with energy playing an important role and tariff-driven goods inflation not going away,” added Krimmel. “These are the costs most salient to consumers and voters, as we saw, yet the Fed has less direct control over them.”

He noted that for the Fed outlook, a “December hike now looks almost certain, with odds jumping to 99% this morning.”

“Additional hikes in early 2027 look more in play as well, with the 10-year Treasury up 9 basis points just 25 minutes after the CPI report release,” Krimmel continued.

Looking at housing, Krimmel said that near-term there could be “upward pressure on mortgage rates again” due to rising Treasury yields and the potential of a rate hike from the Fed.

Breakthrough cosmic hydrogen measurement could transform our view of the Universe

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Advances in computing and astronomy have led to increasingly complex attempts to chart the large-scale structure of the Universe. For the past decade, scientists have been working to map one aspect in particular to see whether it matches observations of the cosmic web: cosmic hydrogen. For the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, neutral hydrogen was the only element in the Universe (known as the "Cosmic Dark Ages") and provided the feedstock for stars, galaxies, and the cosmic web.

Today, it remains the most abundant element in the Universe and can be found everywhere - the interstellar medium (ISM) and the intergalactic medium (IGM) - in abundance. For the past decade, scientists with the MeerKLASS Collaboration have been trying to measure how neutral hydrogen "clumps" in the distant Universe. In a study published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS), the team announced the first direct measurement of the large-scale structure of cosmic hydrogen.

The MeerKLASS (MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey) Collaboration is an international group of astronomers conducting cosmological surveys using the MeerKAT radio telescope. Their survey relies on a technique known as HI intensity (or 21-cm intensity) mapping to detect the combined emissions of galaxies within large segments of the sky. This enables the creation of 3D maps of the cosmos that show the distribution of matter (gas, dust, stars, galaxies, clusters, etc.) in the cosmic web.

Members of the MeerKLASS collaboration at their biannual 'Busy Week' meeting, hosted at the University of Torino in September 2025. Credit: SARAO

This is no easy task, since the 21-cm signal must be extracted from all the other emissions of the Universe (the "foreground noise"), which are about 10,000 times stronger than the hydrogen signal itself. To accomplish this, the team chose to operate each dish in the array as an independent telescope, rather than combining all 64 dishes into a single interferometer. This was an entirely new observation mode for MeerKAT that traded sharp angular resolution for very high survey speed.

For their survey, the team analyzed data collected by the MeerKAT array across ~250 square degrees of sky. This enabled the detection of a neutral hydrogen signal that originated 4.5 billion years ago (or 9 billion years after the Big Bang). The team's measurements produced many independent maps from different dishes and observing times. These were then cross-compared to suppress individual instrument effects and reinforce the structure of cosmic hydrogen.

Their results showed that cosmic hydrogen mirrors the large-scale structure of the cosmic web, advancing a technique that could transform how cosmologists map the Universe. Dr Steven Cunnington, a Senior Research Fellow at Purdue University's Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation, led the development of the analysis pipeline that enabled the mapping of cosmic hydrogen. Since 2015, he has also co-led one of the working groups within the MeerKLASS Collaboration.

As he expressed in a University of Portsmouth release:

This result feels particularly special because it's the culmination of a journey that began when I started my PhD at Portsmouth over a decade ago. I am now fortunate to be part of a much wider international collaboration sharing this common goal. Many of the challenges addressed in this work have shaped my own research career, so seeing our team deliver this measurement is a remarkable milestone to witness.

While sci-fi novels have never been short of stories that strike a humorous chord with readers, there are a few standouts in the genre that have a lighter appeal than others, like Douglas Adams' The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy or Terry Pratchett's Discworld series, along with this new release. Though many of the elements that come with sci-fi fiction tend to be deeper and darker than the average story, readers often get the trade off that sci-fi novels have a specific sense of humor, one that indulges in chaos to explore a novel's larger ideas. Both Adams and Pratchett bring that irreverent quality to their writing, as does Andrew H. Murray.

Murray, whose novel Everything Happens So Much, debuts today, November 10, has built a world where absurdity and philosophical observations are able to co-exist, in a similar vein to Adams, Pratchett, and some of sci-fi's best. While the combination of serious and silly is often tough to balance, sci-fi authors have long been able to do so with imaginative world building, unconventional humor, and stories that have genuine emotional stakes unfolding as things unravel. Bringing Pratchett's distinctive approach to fantasy and Adams' sense of observation together, Everything Happens So Much is able to create something special.

While Murray's focal point in Everything Happens So Much, Esme Good, may be a fairly ordinary character, the way in which her story takes shape is reminiscent of the greatest sci-fi comedies of all time, bringing the absurdist multiverse comedy to life.

Murray's novel, Everything Happens So Much, focuses on Esme Good, a fairly ordinary young woman whose life has become defined by its pure lack of excitement. Esme's dead-end job leaves her with a standard life in an unremarkable city, and her circumstances leave her looking for more. Hoping for an escape from the bleak monotony of her everyday life, Esme finds herself indulging in The Elsewheres, potions that allow her to disappear from her life into alternate versions of herself for brief 15 minute intervals. The alternate realities allow Esme to feel some sense of excitement, if only for a few moments.

While the idea of alternate realities may be affirming for some, visiting other versions of herself leaves her concerned that she's living her worst possible life. As Esme moves through balancing her everyday with doses of alternate realities, she finds herself at the center of an incident that makes her markedly more important in her own universe, with consequences that ripple out to her alternate realities. Murray's distinctive voice gives Esme's journey, which ultimately finds her in uncharted territory fulfilling a prophecy, an ironic tilt that ensures readers are never entirely certain about what's coming next.

Although there are high-level comparisons to be made between Everything Happens So Much and Hitchhiker's Guide because of their elements of a cosmic journey, the bigger comparisons between Murray's style and Adams' has to do with their shared sense of humor. Murray's moments of absurdity stand out against the larger implications of the story, and while they have shared sensibilities, are vastly different than the ways that Adams' weaves his own absurdist humor throughout Hitchhiker's Guide. Murray, who has openly shared that he was influenced by Adams, is able to take his own style and infuse it with a similar absurdist quality.

Everything Happens So Much isn't just inspired by the humor and the journey of Adams' most famous novel, but also takes Terry Pratchett's Discworld into the mix. With genuine slapstick humor written into Esme's journey, Murray was able to find himself inspired by elements of Pratchett's strange and delightfully funny universe, infusing them into the narrative in his own way. With a combination of alternate reality fiction and a sense of humor that stands out among the more serious sci-fi titles of the current era, Murray's Everything Happens So Much puts the genre on an exciting new journey.
