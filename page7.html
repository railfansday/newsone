Airbus may be leaving Microsoft Office, but for its 36,000 defense staff, Office isn't leaving them

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Planemaker's 2-to-3-year plan to ditch Redmond's suite stops short of the 22% who can't go near the cloud

Airbus has a plan to phase out Microsoft Office, but about 22 percent of its roughly 165,000 employees will stay on it because their sensitive data must remain on-premise.

In March 2018, Airbus signed a contract with Google to move its workforce - 130,000 strong at that time - onto the cloud-based Workspace suite. Years later, compatibility issues mean some users still need Excel.

Catherine Jestin, Airbus's executive vice president of digital, told The Register that Google is still working to resolve software interface conflicts between departments and improve features.

Last year, for example, Google tripled the number of cells available in Sheets, which let Airbus "reduce significantly the number of users on Microsoft." This year, Google improved the compatibility of document watermarking. Contracts, for instance, are often exported from Excel to Sheets by one department, edited by another, and sent back.

"Sometimes we had compatibility issues when you were commenting, the comments were lost, or they were not transferable in one way or the other," she said.

"Now they [Google] have made it totally compatible, which means that it makes it much easier for us to decommission people working on contracts… the legal department, the sales department, purchasing department, making it much easier to have that internal collaboration."

Airbus and Google have a roadmap to eliminate Microsoft Office. Jestin expects the migration to finish in "another two to three years."

Not every part of the company will switch. The Defence division, which accounts for the 22 percent (more than 36,000 people), will keep using classic Microsoft Office.

"We will not get 100 percent out of Microsoft because again, for the defense industry people, they cannot [use] Microsoft Office 365 or Google Workspace, they have to stay on-premise, and the only solution that is available on-premise is Microsoft. So Microsoft Office will remain for colleagues who are working in the defense and space programs."

8 Thriller Books Better Than Harlan Coben

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Harlan Coben is one of the best authors of psychological thriller books of his generation, and it's impossible not to be impressed by the complexity and volume of his contributions.

However, Coben is far from the only great author putting out thriller novels that take the genre to the next level. Coben is a master of his craft, but there are thrillers from the past and present that have clearly influenced his work and paved the way for his success. Harlan Coben's Netflix shows have brought his books to life onscreen, but many of these titles have gotten screen adaptations, like so much of Coben's work.

Coben's novels are some of the best thriller books of the decade, but it's important to look to the past as well when finding the best, most boundary-pushing thrillers that will stay with you forever.

The first installment of Colson Whitehead's Harlem Trilogy, which was recently completed in 2026, Harlem Shuffle transports the reader to the world of Ray Carney in 1960s New York City. By day, Ray is a furniture salesman, but there's much more than meets the eye to the charming protagonist who leads Harlem Shuffle. Connected to a ring of unforgettable characters and light-fingered thieves, Ray is pulled back to a life of crime he's been trying to leave behind.

Whitehead is the author of Pulitzer Prize-winning works like The Underground Railroad and Nickel Boys, and it's clear that Whitehead is at the top of his game and having a lot of fun with Harlem Shuffle. The reader quickly becomes endeared to Ray, his family, and his city throughout Harlem Shuffle​​​​​​, making it easy to keep following Ray's journey in Crook Manifesto and Cool Machine.

Harlem Shuffle is available for purchase here.

It makes sense that Amy Adams was tapped to play Camille Preaker in the miniseries adaptation of Sharp Objects​​​​​, a book that makes the reader question everything they thought they knew. An immersive narrative that transports the audience to the small Southern town where Camille was raised and must return when a string of young girls starts disappearing. Her complicated relationship with her past and with her brother comes into sharp relief throughout the story.

The author of incredible thrillers like Gone Girl and Dark Places, Flynn began her career with Sharp Objects, which was her debut novel. Camille's mother and half-sister slowly draw Camille back into their world and the complex social politics of the town, making her reflect on parts of her childhood she hoped would stay buried. Flynn's background in journalism and investigative reporting influences the gripping narrative of Sharp Objects.

Sharp Objects is available for purchase here.

A dark, intense thriller unafraid to dive into some of the most brutal parts of the human psyche, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo is famous for a reason. Written by Stieg Larsson, it's considered not only one of the best thrillers but one of the best books of the 21st century, and this comes through from the beginning. Though it isn't always easy to read, as the characters go through a lot throughout the story, it's well worth reading.

Concerned with sexual politics and the culture of violence against women in male-dominated spaces, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows the story of the journalist Mikael Blomkvist and private investigator Lisbeth Salander and their investigation of the disappearance of a wealthy man's niece who vanished 40 years ago. However, the truth behind the family and the connection between Mikael and Lisbeth is more complicated than it first appears.

Following the massive success of I Will Find You, Harlan Coben is teaming up with Netflix once again for his next book-to-screen adaptation.

Set in Victorian Britain, Fingersmith follows the unexpected relationship that develops between a rich young woman and the thief posing as her maid. Sarah Waters' 2002 novel is split into three parts and explores the perspectives of both women, Susan Trinder and Maud Lilly, who find themselves falling in love despite the betrayals, backstabbing, and stealing that they commit against each other.

Park Chan-wook's landmark thriller, The Handmaiden, is loosely based on Fingersmith, and both are unique takes on the subtle balance between crime and romance woven through the story. Every character in Fingersmith has a secret, and Waters does a great job of keeping the reader guessing, as the motivations behind their actions could never be predicted. Fingersmith features amazing LGBTQ+ representation, which can sometimes be overlooked within the thriller genre.

Fingersmith is available for purchase here.

Perfectly balancing elements of dark academia, mystery, and litfic, The Secret History is characterized as a modern classic for a reason and helped put Donna Tartt on the map. Set against the backdrop of a small, elite college in New England, The Secret History explores class, morality, and the complex dynamic of a group of students who let their experimentation in their studies take them over the edge and into a place of darkness.

With vivid characters who are impossible to forget and one of the most immersive thriller settings of all time, The Secret History takes unexpected turns the reader won't see coming. Though the novel lends itself to being adapted for the screen, The Secret History can only be enjoyed as a novel for now. However, Tartt's 2013 book, The Goldfinch, is another compelling work of fiction that will scratch the same itch, and was made into a movie in 2019.

The Secret History is available for purchase here.

Arguably the best true crime book of all time, Truman Capote's In Cold Blood is a must-read work not just for thriller fans, but for audiences of all kinds. A portrait of the Clutter family and their shocking murder, and also an exploration of the United States during a period of social and cultural transition, In Cold Blood examines the fallout of a crime that seems to have no motive.

Every fictional thriller written after In Cold Blood was impacted by the legacy and influence of Capote's work. Deeply emotionally impactful and brilliantly paced, In Cold Blood is a great book to start with for readers looking to get into the Capote canon. Though In Cold Blood doesn't follow the same thriller formula as Coben's texts, it will enrapture the reader and demonstrate why true crime never fails to find an audience.

In Cold Blood is available for purchase here.

Before The Girl on the Train was brought to life onscreen in 2016 in the film starring Emily Blunt, it captured the attention of thriller fans thanks to the story by Paula Hawkins. Many books have emulated the format of The Girl on the Train, but few have succeeded in capturing the essential elements that take this novel to the next level. Hawkins' protagonist, Rachel, is a lead character easy to identify with as she grapples with the shock of seeing something she shouldn't have.

When she witnesses an incriminating moment between a couple she watched from the window on her daily train commute, it sends her life into chaos and changes the way she sees the people in her life forever. Taking the reader on a journey they'll never predict, it's no surprise that The Girl on the Train has generated such a large fanbase and that it's just as popular as many of Coben's works.

The Girl on the Train is available for purchase here.

We Have Always Lived in the Castle might have been published in the 1960s, but that doesn't make Shirley Jackson's timeless thriller any less captivating or relevant. Atmospheric and deeply disturbing, We Have Always Lived in the Castle brings elements of psychological horror to its thriller story. Told from the perspective of Mary Katherine, the novel sees her grapple with the isolation of living with her uncle and sister on a gothic estate.

The novel unfolds in the wake of the deaths of everyone in Mary Katherine's family except her, her sister, and her uncle, raising questions of who was behind the brutal poisoning of the others. No one is above suspicion in We Have Always Lived in the Castle, and readers will be enthralled by the story's world from the first page. Adapted for the screen several times over the years, the novel will get under the audience's skin as effectively as any Harlan Coben book.

We Have Always Lived in the Castle is available for purchase here.

This Rare Stock Market Signal Has Only Happened 5 Other Times Since 1871. Here's What History Says Happens Next.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The market has seen big pullbacks every time the CAPE ratio has climbed above 30.

By Geoffrey Seiler – Nov 23, 2026 at 4:30AM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

As 2026 winds down, the stock market is set to have another solid year of gains. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) is up just a little over 7% on the year, as of the close on Nov. 17. This would be the index's fourth consecutive year of gains if it can finish the year strong.

Now, this year has been a little bit different than past years, with value stocks nicely outperforming growth stocks. This can be seen in the total return performance of the Vanguard Morningstar Growth ETF (VUG -5.32%), which is down about 7.4% for the year, versus the 13.9% gain in the Vanguard Morningstar Value ETF (VTV -1.62%), as of Nov. 22. If this performance rate holds through the end of the year, it would be just the fourth time since 2010 that the Vanguard Value ETF outperformed its growth counterpart.

While the market's leadership has changed this year and there has been some rotation out of artificial intelligence (AI) stocks into other sectors, one thing that hasn't changed is the S&P 500's potentially frothy valuation. One of the best market valuation metrics is the Cyclically Adjusted PE Ratio (CAPE), which was co-developed by Yale economist Robert Shiller. It was designed to help level out periods of unsustainably high and low earnings periods. It does this by measuring the P/E ratio of the S&P 500 based on the index's average inflation-adjusted earnings from the past 10 years.

The CAPE ratio currently sits just below 40 times, one of the highest levels in history. In fact, when the metric is backtested, the CAPE ratio has only been above 30 on five other occasions since 1871, spanning more than 150 years. Meanwhile, history is quite clear about what typically comes next. Let's look at when the CAPE has hit 30 and what the aftermath has been.

History shows that when the CAPE exceeds 30, a big market sell-off tends to follow. However, it sometimes can take years to play out, and in many cases, the market has rebounded pretty quickly.

As diabetes association’s turmoil continues, what does it mean for patients?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Liz covers cardiovascular and metabolic disease, asking why we’re not doing better or even losing ground against the world’s leading killer. Send tips on Signal at LizC.22.

Five months after the American Diabetes Association fractured over a political protest and its extended fallout, one question remains unanswered.

What’s the bottom line for people living with diabetes?

Although any impact on patients may be hard to trace now, experts told STAT, they expressed concern that the impasse could stymie advancement in diabetes care. At the heart of the matter is the role the ADA and other professional organizations play in setting guidelines and sharing research, whether in their journals or at wide-ranging meetings that attract thousands to present and to learn.

The ADA dispute began after five members were ousted from its 2026 scientific meeting in New Orleans. The sparring continues, fueled by high-profile resignations and dismissals, a 1,221-member boycott, and the appointment of unidentified editors to manage journals. In October, an internal ADA review acknowledged some missteps but doubled down on its rebukes of members who objected to Trump administration policies decimating scientific research and proposing political reviews of individual projects.

A scheduled appearance by Jay Bhattacharya, director of the National Institutes of Health, inspired the five members to pass out copies of an editorial published in an ADA journal critical of diminished NIH funding. He did not appear, but armed security officers evicted those who shared the printouts outside the hall where he was expected to speak.

Bhattacharya was not the speaker originally suggested for the meeting, Mark Atkinson, who resigned as ADA’s 2026 scientific sessions planning committee chair, told STAT. His choice was to assemble a panel of academic experts to discuss artificial intelligence and its influence on diabetes care, but ADA leadership and its advocacy committee selected Bhattacharya instead.

These and other details regarding the dispute have accumulated, along with high-level resignations. But when asked if the continuing controversy matters to patients, the answer from four prominent voices is yes, but maybe not yet.

To illustrate his concern for the ADA’s future, Atkinson cited examples of how the ADA helped patients in the past.

“The ADA is one of many agencies that have advocated for lower insulin costs,” he told STAT. “They’re one of many agencies that have argued for increased federal funding for research. They’re advocates for standards of care.”

David Nathan, a noted researcher and founder of the Diabetes Center at Massachusetts General Hospital, is far more concerned about the broader impact of federal funding cuts on the scientific enterprise than he is about ADA tumult. Still, he’s also hearing from people who link that ongoing national crisis to what has happened at ADA.

They’re afraid of what’s going to happen to diabetes research, he said, starting with the NIH withdrawing support and then aggravated by the ADA discord.

“It’s really one blow on top of another,” Nathan told STAT. “All of this has coalesced to make everyone anxious, uncertain about the future of diabetes research. And the ADA should at this point be standing up and being something that we can all rally behind. Instead it is being fractured by its own actions.”

In a written response to STAT’s request for comment, the ADA press team said Tuesday, “The ADA has outlined the next steps that we plan to take to learn and improve based on the facts that came to bear from the review. This includes a review of event protocols, procedures, and code of conduct.”

In the near term, the 2027 ADA guidelines to be issued in December were already close to completion, physicians familiar with their production told STAT. The ADA confirmed their publication online in Diabetes Care next month. The agenda for the next annual scientific sessions, slated for June in Washington, D.C., is not yet set.

Meanwhile, the journals have been in limbo.

Since June, the ADA member boycott has erased the editorial masthead for Diabetes Care, its flagship journal. Editor-in-chief Steven Kahn, one of the five escorted out this past June, is part of the boycott, as are deputy editors. Chris Kohler, vice president of publications at ADA, resigned in protest when an interim editorial board was appointed without making their names public.

Kohler’s resignation letter said the appointment of unidentified interim editors violated editorial independence, scientific integrity, transparency, and accountability.

“I cannot, in good conscience, endorse or assume responsibility for an approach that I believe will compound the very problems it is intended to solve,” his Nov. 6 letter said.

Nathan, too, was severe about the unidentified editorial board. “To put it in strike terminology, these are scabs,” Nathan said. “I hate to tarnish them in that way, but they are working against this boycott. And the ADA has not announced the name of a single one of them. That’s weird.”

ADA told STAT the names would be made public.

“While the boycott continues, the ADA will remain relentless in our work to provide the resources that healthcare professionals are counting on. That is why we have established an interim editorial board for Diabetes Care that consists of highly experienced and respected healthcare professionals who have dedicated their careers to caring for people living with diabetes, including endocrinologists, primary care physicians focused on diabetes care, diabetes educators, and more,” the ADA press statement said. “The names of the interim editorial board will be placed on the Diabetes Care website soon.”

Elizabeth Selvin, a deputy editor at Diabetes Care who is also boycotting ADA, expressed concern that a weakened journals might dilute future guidelines.

“I worry that the CEO is treating the journal as a marketing tool for the organization rather than a scientific asset that should be protected and governed by standard publishing ethics,” she wrote to STAT about chief executive Charles Henderson. “A concern is that because typical governance structures to ensure editorial independence are not in place, we need to simply trust that ADA is not making decisions that will benefit industry more than patients.”

In its message to STAT, the ADA said the current editorial team’s term was set to end in December 2027. ADA has opened applications for a new editor-in-chief and said it will work with the selected candidate to confirm a transition plan and the appointment of deputy editors and associate editors.

In addition to the journals, large meetings are another forum for scientific exchange.

John Buse, an endocrinologist at the University of North Carolina and a former president of the ADA, is looking elsewhere.

“I am currently not submitting papers to any ADA journals, but there are many other outstanding journals to pick from,” he wrote to STAT. “It pains me, as of my 600 or so publications, no journal has published more of them than Diabetes Care historically.”

Journals are the way to communicate, Atkinson said.

“Without having medical journals, you don’t have a real way to impact how patients are treated in their care,” he said about the impasse. “This has just got to end soon because the losers in all of this are people with diabetes.”

The same holds true for scientific meetings. Buse plans to attend the Endocrine Society meeting in Toronto, which he has previously gone to only infrequently. He does regularly go to the European Association for the Study of Diabetes, which will meet this year in Budapest, calling it a strong meeting with much to offer. Another option is the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology meeting in San Diego.

“I do hope the issues are resolved soon and that I will attend the ADA, but the plan today is not to attend the scientific sessions in D.C. in June,” he wrote. “If the negotiations with ADA go nowhere and the boycott persists, there is great risk that the ADA loses its standing as the authoritative voice in diabetes. I am sure that the Endocrine Society, AACE, EASD, and others could carry the mantle in the future if the diaspora of thought leaders persists.”

Nathan said he and his colleagues are also thinking about where to present the results of their research, predicting scientists will find other venues. And so will participants and sponsors.

“Industry is interested in having a large audience at these annual meetings. If they start leaving, or if their support starts weakening, the ADA will suffer from it,” Nathan said. “As will people with diabetes. As will the scientific enterprise. I mean, this is just a colossal blunder on the part of the ADA.”

The ADA said there are plans to meet with some of the people leading the boycott to work towards a resolution.”

“They seem to be procrastinating, frankly, with us,” he said. “We have tried to get a meeting with them for easily more than four months. I think they’re hoping that this will just go away, and it’s not going away.”

Opinion: A Chinese research entity FOIA’d my scientific work. I had to give it to them

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We ranked the 25 most valuable publicly traded Chinese drugmakers

Duncan, Ph.D. is the Thomas J. Watkins memorial professor of reproductive science in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology in the Feinberg School of Medicine at Northwestern University.

Team science — or collaborative science across disciplines and institutions — is essential for scientific progress as modern problems are too complex for a single person, laboratory, or even field to solve alone. Some of the best work happens when scientists from different countries collaborate.

However, U.S. policies in place and in development increasingly curtail this vital foreign collaboration. There are foreign funding restrictions, visa and entry restrictions for researchers, mandatory institutional compliance and audits with respect to foreign collaborations, and tight export controls. These policies were implemented as biosecurity measures to protect from “countries of concern,” including China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

Meanwhile, proprietary scientific research is easily accessible to anyone, including foreign actors, through a policy meant to protect Americans’ right to government information. In other words, we are locking the front door while the back window is still wide open.

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1966 in response to concerns that the government was too broad and unaccountable. FOIA gives anyone the right to request access to records held by federal government agencies.

This means that through FOIA, anyone — U.S. citizens, noncitizens, and foreign nationals — can access U.S. government-funded science by completing a simple online form and paying a minimal fee. While FOIA is an incredibly important tool for citizens in a free society, it was never intended to allow anyone around the globe to access proprietary U.S. science.

People are readily climbing in through the open back window. As a scientist in the U.S., I personally experienced the impact of such a FOIA request. I was notified by a FOIA coordinator at an NIH institute that my scientific ideas, supported by a funded grant from the U.S. government, were being requested by a Chinese entity through a simple online request form. They only had to pay a $25 fee.

By law, I had to comply and release the grant to them. The requester was listed as a “Consultant” and identified by a generic email address and physical address in China that could not be verified.

I am not the only one to have this experience. The FOIA coordinator told me offhand that this specific individual had a track record of requesting numerous grants from different NIH institutes every year. More broadly, over a decade, the NIH received more than 13,300 requests for grant proposals through FOIA. (Meanwhile, journalists’ FOIA requests often face major delays and other hurdles.)

This loophole has significant negative ramifications on our society. Scientific grants contain unpublished scientific data and sensitive intellectual property. The release of this material could:

1) harm personal and institutional financial interests by divulging patentable ideas, product concepts, and precompetitive information;

2) mislead the public through exposure to unvetted and untested hypotheses and preliminary data;

3) ruin careers and livelihoods of U.S. scientists; and perhaps most importantly,

4) damage U.S. interests by allowing foreign nationals or foreign corporations to be the primary beneficiaries of work funded by the U.S. taxpayer.

Our policies are fundamentally not aligned to protect science. We say we are blocking competing foreign interests, yet through FOIA, anyone can access this science.

If the U.S. truly wants to protect science, Congress needs to close the open window that FOIA affords. Proprietary U.S. science should be exempt or excluded from FOIA. Failing that, those who can access our material must be scrutinized.

Almost exactly four years after receiving the FOIA request, a research team from China published findings that closely matched my team’s original hypothesis — one our own later experiments did not support. While I cannot say whether the FOIA request, my grant, and this recent publication are related, the timing has made me think more about how research, including my own, is ultimately used in these situations.

Opinion: If the AI bubble bursts, it could create a serious problem for health care

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We ranked the 25 most valuable publicly traded Chinese drugmakers

Adler is chief scientific officer at the PATHORA Institute of Pathology & Tissue Medicine.

The Oct. 30 sell-off on Wall Street ignited doubts about whether the huge investment in artificial intelligence will earn the returns investors expect. Those concerns have persisted, with even Anthropic’s initial public offering on Nov. 19 failing to halt the broader tech sell-off.

If the AI bubble bursts (assuming it is a bubble) and takes some medical AI companies down with it, health care will face a serious problem. A health AI company’s market value and the medical value of its technology are two different things. If funding runs out because investors decide to walk away, hospitals could lose the chance to complete evaluations of potentially useful medical tools. Giving up on medical AI would be a financial decision — but it may look like a clinical one. The financial markets will decide which medical AI technologies survive before hospitals have had the opportunity to establish whether these technologies benefit patients.

If investment in medical AI technologies experiences a sharp fall, companies will lose funding, forcing them to reduce their activities or stop supporting their technologies completely. The impact of such an event could be felt across the entire health care system: Companies are developing AI tools ranging from interpreting scans to identifying patients at risk of developing medical complications due to treatments. Clearly, some of these tools will eventually prove useful in clinical settings, while others will not. The point is that neither an impressive AI tool nor a falling stock price tells us which AI tool deserves a place within the health care system.

To illustrate this point, let’s take cancer clinical trials as an example. In one study, researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Emory University examined electronic medical records using AI systems to identify patients who may meet the eligibility criteria for inclusion in clinical trials. Determining eligibility involves a demanding manual screening process examining things like tumor characteristics, previous treatments, and lab results.

Interestingly, the findings revealed that using the AI system improved accuracy of assessing enrollment eligibility for the cancer clinical trials, but it didn’t reduce screening time significantly. It’s not clear whether such improvements would lead to an increase in patient enrollment. In another study, researchers in Australia from the Victorian Cancer Registry at Cancer Council Victoria and Monash Health utilized an AI system to analyze pathology reports with the goal of identifying patients eligible for enrollment in cancer clinical trials. The AI system was able to successfully identify more eligible patients when compared to standard screening procedures, but ultimately utilizing the AI system didn’t result in increased patient enrollment into the clinical trials. The study didn’t establish why, which brings to focus that screening improvements doesn’t necessarily lead to higher clinical trial participation.

Taken together, these two studies illustrate the potential utility of AI systems in health care. They also make clear that improvements are needed before such AI systems become an integral part of health care system.

Based on my experience in senior leadership in oncology drug development, I think that medical AI systems should be developed and evaluated based on the general framework that we currently apply in the research and development of new innovative cancer drugs. What I mean by that is when it comes to the AI systems promising early results are important, but it is critical not to celebrate a success too early. Ultimately the value of the AI system depends on whether it eventually benefits the patient. We need to determine whether using a particular medical AI leads to better clinical decisions, better patient identification, reduction of unnecessary work, or improved access to treatment.

If the AI bubble bursts, these success metrics could become more difficult to achieve. Companies developing medical AI systems may lose their funding, downsize, or even withdraw support for their products. Worst of all, they could close before hospitals have finished assessing their AI systems.

Clinicians could, of course, return to doing their work as they did before the introduction of the medical AI system. But that would mean the health care system loses the opportunity (and valuable data) to determine whether such medical AI tools could have offered meaningful improvements and patient impact.

The clear answer is not to try and save each struggling AI company, but to make sure that hospitals and health care system are able to complete their assessments of medical AI systems even if a given company reduces its services or even goes out of business.

Before adopting such medical AI systems, hospitals should establish clear success criteria and build systems in place to track, store, and ensure that the research data generated about the medical AI systems are available for independent evaluation even if the AI company supporting such a system no longer exists. Such plans would make sure that any financial difficulties that may affect a relevant AI company would not prevent the hospital or the health care system from determining whether a given medical AI system could benefit patients.

David Adler is chief scientific officer at the PATHORA Institute of Pathology & Tissue Medicine, with more than 15 years of experience in oncology research and drug development.
