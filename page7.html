On Nov. 13, 1971, Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit Mars. At the time, scientists didn't know much about the Red Planet.

NASA had several Mars flybys before Mariner 9. Those flybys showed that Mars had a cratered surface, so some scientists thought that Mars looked just like the moon. Then Mariner 9 blew blew their minds.

When Mariner 9 got to Mars, it arrived in the middle of a dust storm. It saw some tall structures sticking through the clouds. When the dust settled, images from Mariner 9 revealed that these structures were the tops of huge volcanoes.

One volcano, Olympus Mons, is the tallest in the solar system. Mariner 9 also spotted an enormous canyon called Valles Marineris. This canyon is five times longer than the Grand Canyon in Arizona.

Mariner 9 worked for one year in orbit. When it ran out of gas, NASA shut it off. But the spacecraft remains one of the most famous Mars voyagers of all time.

Mariner 9 cemented its place in space history when it entered into orbit around Mars, the first human-built spacecraft ever to do so.

The NASA spacecraft actually beat the Soviet Union's own Mars 2 spacecraft to the Red Planet, even though Mars 2 "had an 11-day head start," NASA said in a mission description. (Mars 2 would go on to be the first human-built craft to impact the surface of Mars when it crashed into the Red Planet.)

But it was the science from Mariner 9 that transformed Mars from a mysterious Red Planet into a full world for humans to explore.

Mariner 9 carried four primary instruments to study Mars, including a camera system for imaging, an ultraviolet spectrometer, an infrared spectrometer and an infrared radiometer. The probe mapped 85% of Mars with those tools during its nearly one-year mission.

The Mariner 9 spacecraft discovered 20 giant volcanoes on Mars (including Olympus Mars) and made key observations to map Valles Marineris, which runs 2,500 miles (4,000 kilometers) long acros the Tharsis region and is 120 miles (200 km) wide.

"The canyon was named in honor of Mariner 9," NASA said in an overview.

In all, Mariner 9 took 7,329 photos of the surface of Mars, mapping the planet down to a resolution of between 1/2-1 mile (1-2 km). It also snapped at least 80 photos of Phobos and Deimos, the two small moons of Mars.

By February 1972, Mariner 9's supply of gaseous nitrogen, which it used to feed its attitide control thrusters, had run dry. NASA finally shut down the mission in October 1972.

Mirendil raises $1B in funding to speed up AI model development

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mirendil Inc., a provider of software that speeds up artificial intelligence development, has raised $1 billion in early-stage funding.

The startup stated on Thursday that Kleiner Perkins and Clearlake Capital jointly led the Series A investment. They were joined by more than a half-dozen others including Google LLC.

Mirendil was launched last year by two former Google DeepMind and Anthropic PBC researchers. Chief Executive Officer Behnam Neyshabur is a co-inventor of SAM, a popular algorithm that makes AI models better at tasks they were not specifically trained to perform. Chief Technology Officer Harsh Mehta, in turn, spent his time at Google DeepMind and Anthropic working on pretraining projects. Pretraining is a term for the initial phase of the AI development workflow.

Mirendil’s first product, Lor, is a platform that automates many of the tasks involved in model training runs. The company says that the software does so by splitting work among hundreds of AI agents.

Large language model development involves a significant amount of trial and error. Researchers create multiple versions of an algorithm that differ from one another in small ways and compare them to find the most performant variant. According to Mirendil, Lor can automate that workflow. If an error crops up during the evaluation, the platform fixes it automatically.

Earlier this year, Mirendil asked one of its researchers to build a coding model that can compete with leading open-source LLMs. Usually, such projects are carried out by teams of specialists. Mirendil says that its automation technology enabled the researcher to build the model without any help from colleagues. The project cost $10 million and took about 3.5 months to complete.

The company’s agents wrote more than 99% of the code that powers the model, which is called Mirendil-Base. They set up infrastructure, found a suitable training algorithm and collected training data from the web. The agents then refined both the training dataset and Mirendil-Base’s architecture to boost output quality.

The LLM is the foundation of a specialized coding model called Fern that the company unveiled last month. According to Mirendil, it’s designed to automate programming challenges commonly encountered by AI labs. Fern can process 10 million input tokens per prompt, or 10 times more than Claude Opus 5.5. That’s the equivalent of about a half-million lines of code.

Fern is based on a version of Mirendil-Base with 100 billion to 500 billion parameters. Late last month, the company previewed two upgraded versions of the latter model with between 500 billion and 10 trillion parameters. Mirendil plans to use those algorithms to develop more capable LLMs.

Why Viruses Make You Sneeze: COVID Proteins Directly Hijack Sensory Nerves

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Neuroscientists have discovered that SARS-CoV-2 viral proteins directly activate pain-sensing nerves in the airways to trigger sneezing, coughing, and body aches, overturning the long-held view that symptoms stem solely from immune inflammation.

The study shows that the viral enzyme PLpro activates the wasabi-sensing TRPA1 receptor on sensory neurons, offering new therapeutic targets for acute viral symptoms and long COVID.

For decades, textbooks have explained the misery of respiratory infections, the racking coughs, relentless sneezing, headaches, and full-body aches, as collateral damage from the immune system. According to conventional wisdom, infected airway cells and responding immune cells release a storm of inflammatory mediators that secondarily irritate nearby nerve endings.

Now, a study published in the journal Neuron reveals that coronaviruses do not wait for the immune system to sound the alarm; their viral proteins directly hijack the peripheral nervous system.

Led by Dr. Diana Bautista, professor of molecular and cell biology at UC Berkeley, member of the Helen Wills Neuroscience Institute, and a Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI) investigator, the research demonstrates that specific SARS-CoV-2 enzymes directly stimulate airway sensory neurons, triggering immediate sneezing, coughing, airway inflammation, and heightened pain sensitivity.

When SARS-CoV-2 infects host cells, it manufactures two specialized enzymes, papain-like protease (PLpro) and main protease (Mpro), to slice long viral polyproteins into functional components needed for viral replication. Once the virus kills the host cell, these viral proteases spill into the surrounding airway tissue, coming into direct contact with specialized pain- and irritant-sensing neurons known as nociceptors.

Bautista, who previously discovered that the TRPA1 ion channel acts as the sensory receptor for pungent chemicals in wasabi and garlic, found that SARS-CoV-2 hijacks this exact molecular pathway:

To test how PLpro behaves in a living organism, Bautista’s team applied just two microliters of purified PLpro to the nostrils of mice. While plain water occasionally elicited a single sneeze, PLpro triggered explosive bouts of up to 30 sneezes per minute, alongside heightened mechanical pain sensitivity.

Convincing peer reviewers that mice genuinely sneeze required rigorous physiological proof. The team acquired a specialized $75,000 plethysmograph to record the characteristic respiratory lung spasms accompanying each sneeze, matching patterns documented in other sneezing mammals, and dyed the nasal fluid blue to measure the expelled aerosol volume.

While antiviral medications like Paxlovid target Mpro to halt viral replication, numerous experimental inhibitors targeting PLpro were developed during the pandemic without yet reaching clinical approval.

“We think that these protease inhibitors that people spent a lot of time and money developing could be important not just for preventing viral replication,” said Bautista. “They could also help treat symptoms like coughing, which could limit viral spread, and potentially prevent severe COVID-19 in individuals who are susceptible.”

Furthermore, direct viral sensitization of nociceptors offers a compelling biological mechanism for chronic neurological and respiratory complaints in long COVID—including persistent cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, and headaches.

“There are many modes by which viral and bacterial infections can act in our bodies — there are distinct molecular and cellular routes of triggering this type of airway hyper-reactivity,” Bautista noted. “So far, it looks like they can drive symptoms through both the immune system and the nervous system, using different molecular mechanisms.”

How does SARS-CoV-2 cause sneezing, coughing, and body aches so quickly?

Researchers discovered that when virus-infected cells die, they release viral enzymes, specifically papain-like protease (PLpro) and main protease (Mpro), that directly stimulate pain-sensing nerves (nociceptors) in the airways, triggering immediate sneezing, coughing, and pain rather than waiting solely for immune inflammation.

What is the TRPA1 receptor, and what does it have to do with COVID-19?

TRPA1 is an ion channel on sensory nerves famously known as the “wasabi receptor” because it detects irritating chemicals in wasabi and garlic. The study found that SARS-CoV-2’s PLpro enzyme directly activates TRPA1 on airway nerves, causing the release of neuropeptides that trigger coughing, sneezing, and pain.

Could these findings lead to new treatments for COVID-19 or long COVID?

Yes. Blocking the PLpro enzyme with targeted protease inhibitors could calm overactive sensory nerves, helping to stop severe coughing, reduce airborne viral spread, and potentially alleviate chronic neuropathic symptoms like persistent cough, chest pain, and headaches seen in long COVID.

A coronavirus protease activates sensory neurons via TRPA1 to drive sneeze, cough, and pain

Sneezing, coughing, and pain are common symptoms of respiratory viral infection. These responses are initiated by airway-innervating nociceptors that detect noxious stimuli, but how SARS-CoV-2 activates these neurons remains unclear.

Here, we show that the coronavirus papain-like protease (PLpro) directly activates airway-innervating nociceptors in mice and human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived nociceptors. PLpro triggers sneezing, coughing, acute pain, and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) release in the airways.

Both the proteolytic activity of PLpro and the host ion channel transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) are required for neuronal activation and sneezing. Infection with the mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 or the murine coronavirus MHV-A59 also induces sneezing in mice, and MHV-A59-evoked sneezing is TRPA1-dependent. SARS-CoV-2 infection of the trigeminal ganglia altered transcriptional programs associated with nociception, including upregulation of the TRPA1-CGRP signaling pathway activated by PLpro.

Together, these findings reveal a direct mechanism by which SARS-CoV-2 triggers pain and airway reflexes and identify PLpro and TRPA1 as potential therapeutic targets for COVID-19.

Major GTA 6 Update Confirmed Before Launch

Grand Theft Auto 6 will doubtless have plenty of post-launch support in the long run, including the inevitable addition of a GTA Online successor that's expected to possibly arrive in 2027. The real question, however, is just how much Rockstar can manage in the dwindling pre-launch period, as it looks like fans won't be waiting very long for one update to hit the game.

Not long after fans who pre-ordered the game gained access to its gigantic preload file, they've already started encountering the first of more downloads to come. The Version 1.002.000 update is now available to download on both PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S, and it seems relatively substantial.

X user GTA 6 Countdown reported on the addition of the 1.002.000 update for GTA 6, which landed on both of the game's current platforms today. On PlayStation 5, the patch requires a 1.073 GB download, while it takes up 2.18GB on Xbox Series X/S.

This download is presumably a straightforward day one patch for the game, likely addressing problems that remained in the build made available for preloading. As a consequence, fans shouldn't expect that new content is actually being added in the patch, and the 1-2 GB download is ultimately a drop in the bucket compared to the game's overall file size, which is well over 200 GB.

Nonetheless, it also suggests that there's more at play than a single crash fix, which could probably be resolved in a matter of megabytes. The 1.002.000 patch likely packs a solid number of tweaks and fixes into the download, hopefully resolving the most serious issues that might linger in the release build.

GTA 6 has had a long road to launch, with the November 19 date coming at the end of a series of delays. On one hand, this means that the game should have gotten plenty of additional polish in the meantime, and fans can expect it to launch in much better shape than it would have in 2025. On the other hand, it also means that Rockstar ultimately had to draw the line somewhere and commit to this release date, and the game may still be in a race to the finish line.

Hopefully, the pre-launch patch will go a long way in getting GTA 6 ready for the millions of fans waiting for the launch. Considering the sheer volume of expected players, there's no doubt that some bugs will still crop up here and there, and the most dramatic or amusing ones should quickly make the rounds on social media platforms. If everything goes well, though, GTA 6 may be able to avoid a launch like Cyberpunk 2077, where the issues end up overwhelming much of the praise surrounding the core of the game.

There isn't long now until fans can test things out for themselves. Grand Theft Auto 6 is set to launch on November 19, 2026, so we'll just have to see if Rockstar squeezes in any more updates in the last few days before release.

How ‘Tuner’ Sound Designer Johnnie Burn Used Dolby Atmos to Mimic Hyperacusis

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Johnnie Burn, the Oscar-winning sound designer behind “Hamnet” and “The Zone of Interest,” found Daniel Roher’s “Tuner” to be one of his most personal projects to date.

In the film, Leo Woodall plays Niki, an expert piano tuner with acutely sensitive hearing that is sometimes debilitating — in fact, it’s one reason Niki hasn’t chased his dreams of being a pianist. But it’s perfect for his profession, and he finds out that it’s perfect for safecracking too. But Niki gets in too deep with a gang of thieves as he becomes involved in a lovely relationship with gifted musician and composer Ruthie (Havana Rose Liu). Oscar-winning documentarian Daniel Roher (“Nalavany”) takes viewers on a thrill ride centered on hyperacusis, a rare condition that affects one’s hearing. Burn tells Variety he experienced a mild version of hyperacusis in his late teens. “People who suffer from it chronically go through a devastating experience of not being able to possibly recover or go outside. I had this situation where I witnessed an explosion, and I went 100% deaf for about 20 minutes. When my hearing came back, I had hyperacusis and extreme sensitivity to everyday sounds.”

When Burn received the script, his first response was, “I know what this is.” His next step was researching what this world sounded like. To understand that soundscape, Burn had microphones placed inside his eardrums and went about his daily life. “I was recording things from my ears’ point of view, and playing it back later to understand what things become fairly loud that your brain would normally dial out because they’re not threatening, such as putting a pan in the dishwasher or even a shower tap — everyday sounds that can break through.”

The goal was to teach audiences how Niki interacts with his environment and what the dangers are. Burn worked to create various sound levels, including one from Niki’s point of view that allowed audiences to understand his world and another where Niki has ear defenders on, which help restrict sound. One example Burn gives is when Niki sits at a traffic light and a motorbike comes alongside him. “That’s really about teaching the audience that there are these different states in which he can be listening to things,” Burn adds. “The aim was to have the audience experience it alongside him, which is obviously with hyperacusis. It’s not like deafness or anything. It’s a situation where everyday sounds can become dangerously loud for you.”

Burn drove that home in the Dolby mix of the film, pushing the sound design. He and Roher sat in the mix, fine-tuning every decibel. “The different points of view of Niki’s hearing were a fundamental part of whether or not the film worked. I believe we cracked that. The Dolby Atmos mix was a further development of that because it gave us the ability to predominantly play sound at a much greater volume all around us.”

Music as an Intervention in Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Posted November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Music has long been part of ceremonial and healing practices involving psychedelics, and it has played a role in Western psychedelic research for decades. But as researchers learn more about how music influences patients during psychedelic-assisted therapy, an important idea is emerging: Music isn't simply something playing in the background. It is part of the therapeutic intervention itself. However, a recent 2026 systematic review in the Journal of Clinical Psychology highlighted how little data has been gathered to date about the role of music in psychedelic clinical trials.

In psychedelic-assisted therapy, set and setting are at the core of the process. Set refers to the patient's mindset, expectations, and emotional state, while setting encompasses the physical, social, and therapeutic environment in which treatment takes place.

Music is an important part of that setting. Early psychedelic research sometimes involved sessions conducted in relatively sterile environments with little sensory stimulation. Over time, researchers and therapists came to recognize the importance of creating a more supportive environment, including the use of music. Even the absence of music could create a response.

One of music's most valuable functions is that it provides a sense of movement throughout a dosing session. A piece of music begins, develops, and eventually ends. Another takes its place. The emotional tone, rhythm, and instrumentation change, offering patients a reminder that whatever they are experiencing in the moment will also change.

A 2026 review published in Brain and Behavior suggested that music can intensify emotions, influence brain networks involved in meaning and imagery, and affect patterns of brain activity during psychedelic states. However, the researchers also emphasized that the evidence remains limited.

One of the challenges in selecting music for psychedelic-assisted therapy is that no two people hear music in exactly the same way. A piece of classical music might evoke peace for one person and sadness for another. Choral music may feel deeply meaningful to someone with a spiritual background but uncomfortable to someone whose religious experiences have been painful. Even a particular instrument can carry strong personal associations. For this reason, it is virtually impossible to choose music that is completely "neutral."

Every musical selection has the potential to influence a patient's experience, whether through its emotional qualities, cultural associations, or personal significance. This doesn't mean that every piece of music will have a profound effect. Some patients may barely notice what is playing, while others may find the music central to their experience.

A 2018 study published in Psychopharmacology, titled "The Hidden Therapist," examined patients receiving psilocybin treatment for depression. Researchers found that patients' responses to music, particularly their sense of resonance with it, were associated with aspects of their psychedelic therapy experience and changes in depressive symptoms. The findings suggest that how patients relate to the music may matter as much as the music itself.

One of the more interesting aspects of music in psychedelic-assisted therapy is that patients are not encouraged to avoid every uncomfortable response. In preparation sessions, therapists may discuss what to do if a patient encounters music they dislike or find emotionally challenging. For example, if a song brings up sadness, a patient might be encouraged to notice what is happening and consider whether they can remain with the feeling for a short time rather than immediately trying to escape it.

Even the experience of disliking a piece of music can offer an opportunity for observation. What happens when something feels unpleasant? What thoughts, sensations, or emotions arise? Is it possible to experience discomfort without immediately needing to change it? These questions connect to a broader principle of psychedelic-assisted therapy: learning to approach difficult emotions with curiosity rather than automatic avoidance.

But there is an important distinction between allowing space for discomfort and imposing it.

If music becomes overwhelming, distressing, or simply feels inappropriate for the moment, patients should be able to communicate that to their therapists. Depending on the research protocol, the music may be changed, skipped, lowered in volume, or temporarily turned off.

Patient autonomy remains essential. Music may be part of the therapeutic intervention, but patients should understand their options and have opportunities to express their preferences and concerns.

Music's role in psychedelic-assisted therapy may continue after the effects of the medication have subsided. Integration is the phase of treatment in which patients reflect on their experiences, explore their meaning, and consider how to apply insights to everyday life.

For some patients, listening to music from their dosing session can help them reconnect with emotions, memories, or perspectives that emerged during treatment. Sometimes, playlists are made available to participants after their dosing session. Some have reported returning to the music during quiet reflection, while others listen during ordinary activities such as gardening or getting ready for the day.

For certain patients, the music becomes a meaningful reminder of their therapeutic experience, sometimes years later. What such experiences suggest is that music may provide a connection between the dosing session and the ongoing work of therapy.

The compound is only one part of psychedelic-assisted therapy. The therapeutic relationship, preparation, environment, integration, and music all help shape the patient's experience.

Music may offer comfort, evoke memories, or help patients navigate difficult emotions. At times, silence may be more appropriate. What matters is not simply what is played but how each patient responds. By approaching music with intention and flexibility, therapists can make it a meaningful part of individualized care
