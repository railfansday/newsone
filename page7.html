Slavia Prague vs Arsenal. UEFA Champions League.

Report as Arsenal move up to third in Champions League league phase table with 2-1 win over Slavia Prague; Noni Madueke and Viktor Gyokeres scored as the Gunners withstood second-half pressure to continue their unbeaten start in the competition

Football Features Writer @nicholaspwright

Arsenal survived a second-half onslaught from Slavia Prague to win 2-1 at the Fortuna Arena and continue their unbeaten start to the season in the Champions League.

The Gunners, runners-up last term, appeared on course for an emphatic victory after scoring twice through Noni Madueke and Viktor Gyokeres in a one-sided first half.

But Slavia roared back into the contest in the second period and gave Arsenal a scare as Wiktor Nowak's late goal from close range set up a nervous finale for Mikel Arteta's side.

Arsenal hung on to claim their third win in four Champions League games but needed a goal-line clearance from Gabriel Magalhaes and several saves from David Raya, who brilliantly pushed an Oskar Kubiak header against the post shortly before Slavia's goal.

Slavia had 17 shots in the second half, having only had one before the break as Arsenal were made to work for a result which looked a foregone conclusion after their dominant start.

Arteta freshened up his side with six changes from Sunday's 1-0 win over Liverpool and scored their early opener when Madueke finished coolly having been put through on goal by Gyokeres.

The striker added the second with a sumptuous, curling finish after combining with Mikel Merino but Arsenal missed chances to extend their lead before the break and Slavia responded.

A switch from a back four to a back three allowed the hosts to commit more bodies forward and they created a succession of chances as Arsenal were made to sweat for their victory, which sees them move up to third in the league phase table.

Winter Storm Watch vs. Winter Storm Warning: What’s the Difference?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A winter storm watch and winter storm warning mean different things. Here's what each alert signals and how to prepare for dangerous weather.

Tracking severe weather can sometimes feel like it requires a degree in meteorology. With complex scientific terms and technical jargon, it can be difficult to understand what it all means and what should be done to prepare. That’s when the difference between a winter storm watch and a winter storm warning matters.

Both alerts come from the National Weather Service, but they call for different levels of urgency. Here’s how the alerts work and what homeowners should do when they see one.

A winter storm watch means a significant winter storm is possible, but forecasters aren’t yet sure where or when it will hit or how severe it will be. According to the National Weather Service, watches often arrive a day or two before a storm and can cover heavy snow, sleet, or ice.

They give residents time to prepare, even though the storm may not reach their area. As the forecast becomes clearer, the National Weather Service may upgrade the watch to a warning, issue an advisory, or cancel it altogether.

A winter storm warning means dangerous winter weather is expected or already happening. Heavy snow, sleet, or ice could make travel unsafe and cause other problems, including power outages. The amount of snow or ice needed to trigger a warning varies by location.

“Each office knows the local area and will issue Warnings, Watches or Advisories based on local criteria,” according to the National Weather Service. “For example, the amount of snow that triggers a ‘Winter Storm Warning’ in the Northern Plains is typically much higher than the amount needed to trigger a ‘Winter Storm Warning’ in the Southeast.”

Warnings generally arrive closer to a storm’s arrival than watches, although forecasters can issue one before snow or ice begins falling.

A winter weather advisory means snow, sleet, or ice is expected to cause problems, but forecasters don’t anticipate conditions severe enough for a warning. An advisory may signal slick roads or other travel disruptions. While the weather may be less severe than during a warning, residents should still take precautions and monitor local forecasts.

A winter storm watch gives homeowners time to prepare before conditions worsen. That includes checking heating systems, protecting exposed pipes from freezing, and stocking up on food, medications, and other essentials.

“Your primary concerns at home or work during a winter storm are loss of heat, power and telephone service and a shortage of supplies if storm conditions continue for more than a day,” according to the National Weather Service.

Homeowners should have flashlights, spare batteries, and a charged phone in case the power goes out. Checking smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, locating snow shovels, and stocking up on ice melt can also help.

Once a warning takes effect, homeowners should finish their preparations and avoid unnecessary travel. Staying indoors is generally safest during severe winter weather.

If the power goes out, residents can close off unused rooms and wear extra layers to stay warm. Portable generators should always remain outdoors, away from windows and doors. Grills and ovens should never serve as heating sources because they can create serious safety hazards.

Residents should continue monitoring weather alerts throughout the storm. Once conditions improve, homeowners can check for damage and begin clearing snow and ice from walkways and driveways.

Waymo And Uber's Robotaxi Partnership Is Officially Ending: Report

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

If you want to ride in a driverless Waymo in Austin or Atlanta, you can currently only do that via the Uber app thanks to an exclusive partnership between the two companies. That will change in 2028 because Waymo has decided to go solo in those two cities.

Waymo Co-CEO Tekedra Mawakana told Bloomberg on Wednesday that the company will exit its partnership with Uber in Austin and Atlanta starting in January 2028. The reason? Mawakana said the Uber app doesn’t guarantee a driverless ride. You can enable autonomous rides via settings in the Uber app, but the chances of one showing up depend on how many Waymos are actually nearby. If there's no Waymo nearby, riders are matched with human drivers.

Uber offered to add a dedicated robotaxi ride option through its service so that customers would match with driverless cars more easily, but Waymo reportedly declined that.

“In the early days, we would partner because we didn’t have the scale,” Mawakana told the outlet. "...in that partnering, it was a little bit of a crapshoot," she added.

Customers were now demanding a more consistent experience, Mawakana said. That’s why, starting in January 2028, Waymos in Austin and Atlanta will operate the same way they’ve been operating in over two dozen other U.S. cities: via Waymo’s own dedicated app. With that, current partner Uber will also become a direct rival in those two cities.

The whole point of this partnership was to help Waymo scale and give riders exposure to its cutting-edge driverless technology. Uber is the go-to ride-hail app for millions of riders already. The company also has vast experience in fleet operations and logistics.

Waymo, by comparison, is far smaller but is growing quickly. It’s a fully-owned subsidiary of Google parent Alphabet, which is valued at over $3.5 trillion as of writing. Waymo currently conducts over 500,000 paid driverless trips each week. It’s already operational in 15 U.S. cities and plans to add over a dozen more in the coming months. It's in a comfortable lead in the robotaxi race in the U.S., far ahead of Tesla and others.

It now seems more confident about going solo in Austin and Atlanta. However, it’s still partnering with Lyft for driverless rides in Nashville, although that partnership isn’t exclusive. Waymo also serves Nashville through its own app.

Uber also has grand ambitions when it comes to robotaxis. The company plans to have 700 robotaxis online by the end of this year and 2,000 by the end of 2027. It has emerged as a broker of robotaxi technology, inking deals with several companies including Rivian, Lucid, Wayve, and others to offer their driverless vehicles on the Uber app.

Two Hikers Dead in Bear Attack in British Columbia, Leaving Officials to Look for Answers

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A young grizzly in British Columbia (Photo: Colleen Gara / Getty Images)

Two hikers are dead after a suspected bear attack on Indigenous lands in British Columbia, provincial wildlife officials say. The incident comes just a year after a bear encounter in the same area left four people seriously injured.

Rescuers discovered one body on the afternoon of Nov. 2 near the Snooka West Trail in Bella Coola, leading to a search for a second, missing victim.

That search concluded late Tuesday night when searchers found the second hiker dead. Officials with the B.C. Conservation Officer Service (BCCOS) believe both were attacked by a grizzly bear, and efforts are ongoing to locate and trap any bears in the area for assessment.

Authorities and family members have since identified the pair as 48-year-old Jennifer Balcarras and her daughter, 20-year-old Selah Reed.

“It is not yet clear whether more than one bear was involved,” BCCOS Investigator Jeff Tyre told Backpacker. “Any captured bear will be assessed. If it is determined to have been involved in the attack, it will be dispatched in accordance with provincial human-wildlife conflict response guidelines to protect public safety.”

Family members first reported Balcarras and Reed overdue on Sunday, Nov. 1. Police and SAR operators found the first human remains on Monday afternoon, and called BCCOS due to evidence that suggested an animal was involved.

The victims’ remains have since been returned to their families, and the Snooka West Trail remains closed.

In the wake of the deaths, tribal authorities of the Nuxalk Nation, the First Nations people who live in the Bella Coola valley, expressed their support. Samuel Schooner, elected Chief of the Nuxalk Nation, said in a statement that the tribe understands firsthand the trauma this incident will inflict on the victims’ family members and on the survivors of last year’s attack.

That incident occurred near Highway 20 on Nov. 20, 2025, when a grizzly attacked a local school group taking a lunch break. Adults were able to repel the animal with bear spray and a bear banger, but not before it severely injured four people, including three children. The four individuals were rushed to the hospital in varying conditions, and seven others required care at the scene.

No one died, and investigators continued to search for grizzlies in the area, eventually determining three bears could have been involved. Over the following two weeks, officers captured eight adult and juvenile grizzlies for forensic testing. All eight animals were eventually collared and relocated after they could not be forensically connected with the attack.

A total of 24 officers were involved in the response to that attack. Some reportedly drove 14 hours overnight to hand-deliver samples to the Alberta Wildlife Forensic Laboratory. BCCOS described both the response and the incident as extraordinary.

“In my 34 years of experience, I have not seen an attack like this with a large group of people,” Kevin Van Damme, with BCCOS, told Global News at the time. “So this is extremely rare and I think the public needs to understand that part.”

As 2025 field operations concluded, the Conservation Office reported decreasing bear sightings and pointed out that grizzlies in the area typically transition from hyperphagia to denning in or around December.

It’s unclear how long the current search will continue. A spokesperson for BCCOS said investigators were unavailable for comment due to heightened field activity.

“Our thoughts are with the family of the victims during this tragic time,” the agency said. “We are in contact with the family to offer any support we can as we continue to investigate this incident.”

Prediction markets cement their status after forecasting Democratic sweep of Congress

📅 2026-11-04  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Prediction market platforms cemented their role in election forecasting on Tuesday, when markets accurately predicted a host of races before the polls closed, and projected outcomes before news outlets.

Democrats were seen as favored to flip both the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate on prediction markets before the first polls closed at 6:00 p.m. ET Tuesday, and were seen as narrow favorites to take at least 53 Senate seats, while a 54th was forecast as a toss-up.

So far, Democrats have won 54 Senate seats — including wins in Iowa, which prediction markets placed at 50-50 odds, and South Carolina, where Republicans narrowly favored. Outstanding results remain in Mississippi and Alaska, where prediction markets give Democrat Scott Colom and Mary Peltola 90% odds of winning.

Leading prediction market platforms were actively projecting races on X Tuesday night. Those projections, at times, came before many media outlets made their own projections, or before even a substantial number of votes had been counted.

Kalshi was confident making those calls, as long as odds for a candidate were above 99% and trading activity for the opposite candidate "dried out,"  said Jack Such, a spokesperson for the platform.

Polymarket celebrated on X how it projected races, saying that in 10 key races, the platform called races ahead of outlets including CNN, Fox News and the Associated Press by a median 31 minutes.

"It's now routinely hours between when prediction markets call it versus the TV networks," said Polymarket CEO Shayne Coplan in an X post. "No knock to them, but now there's Polymarket, and many people are realizing they want to know what's happening in real time."

Even in races where markets initially favored the candidate who eventually lost, they quickly adjusted once initial vote counts came in. In South Carolina, Democratic Senate candidate Annie Andrews was seen as the favorite on Kalshi to win shortly after 7:30 p.m., only 30 minutes after polls closed. In Texas, where Democratic state Rep. Gina Hinojosa ousted Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, Hinojosa's odds on Polymarket jumped from 39% to 73% within five minutes of initial poll closings.

"You now have financially motivated number crunchers doing this, around the world, that give you that signal just that much faster," said Flip Pidot, a member of the board of directors at the Coalition for Political Forecasting.

Still, a successful night for prediction markets shouldn't stop efforts aimed at stronger regulation of the platforms, said Michelle Kendler-Kretsch, a researcher at the Anti-Corruption Data Collective, a nonprofit. She cited a recent Reuters/Ipsos survey that found most Americans are against using prediction markets to trade on elections.

"Risks and vulnerabilities didn't change overnight just because the markets were accurate this time around," she said.

Right before the midterms, prediction market forecasts had missed major global elections, including Brazil's presidential race, where Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva only had about 20% odds to win on Kalshi before polls closed.

Prediction markets also whiffed in some high-profile Democratic primaries over the summer, forecasting extremely high odds for progressives in Wisconsin and Michigan, for example. The Michigan Democrat, Abdul El-Sayed, narrowly won his primary before going on to win Thursday's general election.

Academics who've researched prediction markets for decades say the platforms aren't wrong when candidates with low odds on markets end up winning races.

"If you toss two coins, there's a 25% chance that you'll throw two heads in a row, and nobody would be terribly surprised if you did," said Rajiv Sethi, an economics professor at Barnard College.

Despite their early success on Tuesday, Dartmouth College economic professor Eric Zitzewitz said it's still not clear if casual observers understand how market probabilities work.

"Some people who are coming to [prediction markets] for the first time will see 55 by 45 odds and think that, 'Oh the candidate with 55 is way ahead,'" he said.

As prediction markets gain traction, however, the public will improve at understanding how to interpret their odds, Zitzewitz said.

With another election under their belts, prediction markets will remain a force through the next presidential election, experts said.

"Last time around [in 2024], prediction markets were in the discussion, but they were in the discussion in a way that was a little bit unsure," said Harry Crane, a professor of statistics at Rutgers University. "This [was] the first true prediction markets election."

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required.”

During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using.

This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today.

In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity.

Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L).

Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure.

As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack.

As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work.

Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources.

This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment.

Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote.

The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time.

After Training for My First Marathon, I Felt Like My Body Didn’t Look Like the Runner I Thought I’d Become. Experts Helped Me See Otherwise.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Marathon training changed how I saw my body—even when it didn’t change how I looked.

Two weeks out from my first marathon, I looked in the mirror and didn’t see the version of myself I thought I would. There was no six-pack, not even a hint of ab definition. My glutes weren’t particularly sculpted, and I wouldn’t exactly describe myself as lean like many runners I see.

At 5-foot-10, I’ve never been the smallest girl in the room. And while my long legs certainly help me cover more distance, I expected months of marathon training to leave me looking more visibly athletic, with more muscle definition and a physique that reflected just how hard I’d been working. I thought that after months of early alarms, added protein, and steadily increasing mileage, I’d look in the mirror and see a different body type.

Somewhere along the way, I absorbed the idea that a runner’s body should look a certain way. When mine didn’t match that image, I started wondering if I wasn’t training hard enough, if I was doing something wrong, or if my body simply wasn’t responding the way it was supposed to.

As I approached race day, I thought about why I was holding myself to that standard in the first place, especially because I was running the New York City Marathon on behalf of Planned Parenthood of Greater New York, an organization whose work centers on bodily autonomy and compassionate care.

Why was I putting so much pressure on my body to look different when it was already doing something incredible? I realized training for a marathon isn’t just about building endurance; it can also mean confronting the expectations you place on yourself.

So many people have an idea of what a “runner’s body” is supposed to look like, particularly fed by what we see online. When you see the same body type represented over and over, it’s easy to start associating that appearance with athleticism. But running a marathon is an enormous physical undertaking, and there’s no single body type that proves you’re capable of doing it.

Clinical sport psychologist Hillary Cauthen, PsyD, explains that the concept of “a runner’s body” is “largely a socially constructed ideal influenced by media representation, sport culture, and the tendency to associate certain physical characteristics with athletic success.” She adds that distance running has historically been represented by a lean body type, particularly smaller-framed athletes, which can leave some recreational runners questioning whether they fit the mold.

Comparison is a large part of this problem, too, says Cindra Kamphoff, PhD, mental performance coach and founder of the Mentally Strong Institute. “We see elite runners and start believing that’s what we should look like,” she explains.

Marathon training is practically a master class in comparison, making it hard to avoid. You’re tracking your pace, watching your mileage climb, sharing runs on Strava, and seeing what everyone else is doing. It can feel like your body should be changing right alongside your stats.

There’s also a particularly frustrating point of tension for women, who can feel caught between the cultural expectation to be smaller and the athletic ideal of looking lean and strong. Carinthia Bank, LCSW, a social worker who works with runners on body image, describes this as the “female athlete paradox,” in which women are judged against both ideals.

For example, a woman might recognize that building strength and fueling adequately are essential to marathon training, yet still feel pressure to maintain a smaller frame or worry that eating more to support her mileage will change her body in ways she doesn’t want. These competing expectations can shape how women judge their bodies, even as they get stronger and more capable, Bank explains.

Marathon training can also increase the time we spend thinking about our bodies, particularly when we’re experiencing physical or emotional discomfort, Bank adds. Many runners tend to think they’d feel better if their bodies were different, which can lead to judgment and blame toward the body they have.

That’s the trap I found myself in. While I was training for 26.2 miles, part of me was still measuring my progress against an imaginary finish line where I’d emerge with a completely different-looking body. I would accomplish something I’d never done before, like running my farthest distance, yet I was using my appearance to dictate my progress.

Cauthen offers a simple definition to keep in mind: “A runner’s body is not a specific shape or size. It is a body that runs.”

Here’s the thing about marathon training: Your body adapts to the physical demands of running, but those changes don’t guarantee an aesthetic transformation. You might build muscle, experience weight fluctuations, or notice an increase in appetite as your body works to fuel and recover. “Improved fitness does not always result in weight loss or a smaller body,” Cauthen says.

There’s also a practical reason not to make getting smaller one of your goals in marathon training. Kamphoff encourages runners “to focus on fueling for performance rather than restricting for appearance,” reminding us that “you can’t expect your body to perform at its best if you’re constantly trying to make it take up less space.”

Marathon training requires substantial energy, and restricting food to change your appearance can leave you without enough fuel to support your workouts and recovery.

Plus, fixating on your size can affect more than physical performance. “Focusing on getting smaller can distract and discourage us from actions that are helpful to performance and mental health,” Bank says. When you’re preoccupied with changing your body, you can miss out on learning what helps you perform, enjoying the process, and appreciating everything your body allows you to do.

I had to confront the fact that I’d been treating my appearance as a report card for my training. If I was working hard enough, I assumed I’d eventually see visible proof in the mirror. But I realized I can become a stronger, more capable runner without looking the way I imagined I would.

For me, that meant recognizing that the body I’d been criticizing was the same one carrying me through early morning runs and increasingly long distances. My body wasn’t failing at becoming a runner’s body—I’d been expecting it to meet a standard that had very little to do with what I was asking it to do.

I wish I could say that learning all of this made me instantly love every inch of my body, but that wouldn’t be honest. I’m still working on it, and I still have moments when I catch my reflection and wonder why I don’t look the way I imagined I would after months of training. But I’m trying to shift my focus from what my body looks like to what it’s allowing me to do, with the help of these expert tips.

“One of the most important shifts runners can make is moving from body evaluation to body appreciation,” Cauthen says. Instead of asking whether I look like someone who runs marathons, I’m trying to ask myself what my body has helped me accomplish. And the answer is a lot more impressive than whether or not I have visible abs.

As I got closer to race day, I continued to remind myself that I don’t have to look a certain way to feel proud of what I accomplished. My body carried me through months of training, and that’s worth celebrating all on its own.

I’m also learning to give myself a little more grace when my expectations don’t match reality. Bank reminds runners that “human beings usually get uncomfortable when there’s a difference between what we wanted and what we get,” and encourages compassion rather than self-criticism.

I don’t have to ignore the fact that I expected marathon training to change my appearance, but I also don’t want those expectations to overshadow everything else I’ve gained, including running farther than I ever thought possible, learning how to fuel and maintain energy, and showing up for myself.

Cauthen recommends distinguishing between observing a physical change and assigning a negative meaning to it, which has really stuck with me. “Noticing that your legs have become more muscular, your weight has fluctuated, or your clothing fits differently is an observation,” she explains. “Deciding those changes mean you are too big, not fit enough, or don’t look like a runner is an interpretation.” This helped me realize how quickly I can turn a neutral observation into self-criticism.

Instead of jumping to conclusions, Cauthen says it’s helpful to ask yourself a few questions when those negative thoughts creep in. For example, “is this thought based on evidence or comparison?” and “am I evaluating my body based on appearance or how I feel and function?” These questions challenge the assumptions that we tend to make about our bodies.

Kamphoff encourages runners to develop what she calls an “inner coach” instead of listening to an inner critic. One strategy she recommends is to “capture your wins” by writing down three things you’re proud of after training. The idea is to shift your attention from perceived imperfections to the progress you’ve made.

I’m trying to practice this by celebrating the things that don’t show up in a mirror. For example, I’ve learned how to pace myself and keep going when a run gets uncomfortable. “Your body has been showing up for you mile after mile. Make sure your self-talk is showing up for your body, too,” says Kamphoff.

Ultimately, I’m learning that I don’t need to love how my body looks every single day to respect what it does for me. “Body acceptance does not require loving every part of your appearance every day,” Cauthen says. “Sometimes it starts with respecting your body’s needs and appreciating its efforts, even when your feelings about your appearance are complicated.”

I can have complicated feelings about the way I look and still be proud of the athlete I’ve become. And I can show up at the starting line without meeting some imaginary physical standard first.

That’s the mindset I carried with me to the marathon. I ran 26.2 miles for a cause I believe in, after months of training that challenged me physically and mentally. My body didn’t have to look a certain way to deserve that moment.

After all those early mornings and hours of preparation, I finally crossed the finish line, put the medal around my neck, and walked out of Central Park feeling something I hadn’t quite felt throughout training: pride. Not because my body looked different, but because of everything it had just done. I felt so grateful for my legs that kept moving, my strength that carried me through the tough miles, and a body that got me to the finish line without injury.

No, my body may not have looked like the “marathon body” I once imagined, but it was a marathon body. I didn’t need a mirror selfie to prove what I’d accomplished: I ran all 26.2 miles. Honestly, what could be more impressive than that?
