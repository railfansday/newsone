Appalachian Trail Gear: What Worked, What Didn’t, and What I Changed (Part 1)

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I found that, just like in the real world, things break, wear out, and sometimes need to be replaced. Just as you budget for an unexpected car repair, you need to plan for gear replacements on the trail. Your shoes aren’t going to last the entire hike (mine lasted about 500 miles!), and sometimes things break when you least expect them.

Take my sleeping pad, for example. It unexpectedly started losing air, and thankfully, my hiking buddy let me borrow his “trail couch,” a foam sleeping pad, until I made it to Daleville, Virginia. Then there are the upgrades you might choose to make along the way. Maybe you realize you can get by with a smaller, lighter tent and decide it’s worth the investment to shed some weight from your pack.

The point is, preparing for a thru-hike isn’t just about buying all your gear before you start. It’s also about budgeting for the things that will wear out, break, or simply need an upgrade along the way. Just like maintaining a car or keeping up with home repairs, taking care of your gear is part of the journey.

I started and ended with a Base Weight of about 13.5 lbs without food, fuel, or water.

So, let’s look at the gear I started with, what held up, what I replaced along the way, and what I carried to the finish line.

I won’t go into a deep dive on my entire gear list, but if you’re curious about the specifics, you can check out my full gear list that I started with on my profile here and what I finished with on LighterPack here.

What I Started With: Big Agnes Tiger Wall UL2

What I Finished With: Hyperlite Mountain Gear Crosspeak 1

I had plenty of experience with the gear I started with, and honestly, it worked! I loved pretty much everything I owned, so I didn’t see a reason to replace perfectly good gear just to upgrade to the latest and trendiest options. That said, I did budget for a new tent.

Originally, I planned to start with the Zpacks Duplex, a tent my fiancé and I use on our backpacking trips. The only catch? He’s usually the one who sets it up for us. Since this was going to be a solo adventure, I needed to be confident setting up my own shelter. It was the middle of winter in Vermont before my hike, and after a few unsuccessful attempts at practicing on my own in the snow, I just wasn’t feeling confident enough to bring it along.

Instead, I decided to start with my Big Agnes Tiger Wall UL2. At 2.5 pounds, it’s a freestanding tent that’s practically dummy-proof, offers plenty of space, and, most importantly, was something I had tons of experience setting up on my own.

First time pitching my new Crosspeak 1 tent.

I love freestanding tents, but I had my eye on a Dyneema version. The one I wanted wasn’t scheduled to hit the market until April, so my plan was to use the Tiger Wall until it launched. Easy enough, right? Well, then a material issue delayed the release.

Meanwhile, Hyperlite Mountain Gear launched its CrossPeak 1 at Trail Days, and they were offering a deal on it. I didn’t hesitate for a second. I may have downsized on space, but this tent checked a lot of boxes for me and was worth the upgrade.

What I Started With: Gossamer Gear Gorilla 50L

What I Finished With: Gossamer Gear Mariposa 60L

I’ve been a huge fan of Gossamer Gear packs ever since hiking the Wonderland Trail in 2023. In 2024, I invested in the Gorilla 50L for my Tahoe Rim Trail thru-hike, and it turned out to be the perfect pack for me and my needs. So naturally, I figured I’d take it along for the Appalachian Trail. No regrets… at least at first!

The first two months on the AT were pretty dry, and I sometimes found myself carrying up to 4L of water to ‘dry camps’. Resupply options were also more spread out than I expected, which meant carrying five to six days of food at a time. The Gorilla is designed to comfortably carry around 25 pounds, with a recommended maximum of 30 pounds, and I found myself pushing those limits pretty regularly.

My beloved Gorilla started ripping on both sides of the bottom corners.

Eventually, my lower back started chafing from the pack rubbing against it, and the side seams began to split. I was so sad because I genuinely loved this pack! The removable back foam pad doubles as both a frame and a sit pad, which is such a great feature. The side pockets are positioned at just the right height, making it SUPER easy to grab my water bottle and slide it back in without stopping or asking someone for help. And the large exterior mesh pocket? Another favorite.

Rather than give up on Gossamer Gear altogether, I decided to stick with the brand and upgrade to its larger pack, the Mariposa 60L. With a maximum recommended load of around 35 pounds, it seemed like the perfect solution for my heavier carries. I never ended up maxing it out, and it gave me the extra capacity I needed.

Eventually, I also got better at dialing in my resupplies, and my water carries dropped back down to around 2L. But even with the lighter loads, I struggled with the Mariposa for a long time. I’m not entirely sure why, but it felt and sat so differently from my Gorilla. I even had the Gossamer Gear team fit me at Trail Days, yet I still felt like the pack I’d purchased was too small for me.

Over time, it seemed to get more comfortable. Or maybe I just got used to the pain and discomfort. Honestly, I’m still torn about whether the Mariposa will be the pack I reach for on my next thru-hike or if I’ll be looking elsewhere. I loved my Gorilla, but my experience with the Mariposa left me with some mixed feelings about what pack comes next.

Navigating the large boulders on the way up Mama K, featuring my Mariposa.

What I Started With: Enlightened Equipment Enigma 20-degree down

What I Finished With: Enlightened Equipment Revelation APEX 40-degree synthetic

I started the trail March 9th with a 20-degree down quilt, but by the time I reached Pearisburg, Virginia, I was ready to make the switch to my 40-degree synthetic quilt.

For me, the 20-degree quilt just wasn’t the right fit for the entire trail. I tend to run hot, and even early on, I found myself getting too warm at night. I was more than happy to swap it out for the lighter, cooler 40-degree option when I had the chance.

Of course, there were still colder nights ahead, especially once I reached the White Mountains in New Hampshire. When temperatures dropped, I made do with what I had, occasionally pulling my rain pants over my wool baselayer tights for a little extra warmth.

Looking back, I’m glad I made the switch. I didn’t need a 20-degree quilt for the entire trail, and having the option to adjust my sleep system as the seasons and temperatures changed worked out really well for me.

What I used: Therm-a-rest NeoAir Xlite Regular Wide

Good sleep is SO important on the trail. When you’re hiking all day, having a comfortable place to rest and recover at night makes a huge difference. I’ve had this sleeping pad for a few years now, and I absolutely love it.

I originally had the regular size but found it too narrow, especially as a woman and a side sleeper. So, I invested in the Regular Wide, and it’s been perfect for me. This version also has an improved inflation valve, and I find it a bit quieter than the original model and some of the other sleeping pads on the market.

Remember what I mentioned earlier about budgeting for gear repairs and replacements? Well, here’s a perfect example.

5-mile trail NERO, it dumped rain, and many of us called it a day and squeezed into a shelter by 2 pm.

I’m guessing that on my last night before reaching Pearisburg, Virginia, I managed to puncture my sleeping pad. It was pouring rain, and honestly, I was just happy to find a spot, get my tent up, and crawl into something dry. Somewhere in that process, I must have pitched my tent over a stick or something sharp.

I didn’t notice anything was wrong until my first night back on the trail after taking a zero in Pearisburg. My pad was noticeably deflated. I figured it was probably just the temperature change, so I blew it back up and went to sleep. Except I found myself doing that two more times before morning.

I tried all the usual tricks to find the leak. I inflated the pad, pushed down on it, listened for escaping air, and ran my hands all over the surface, hoping to feel even the tiniest bit of air escaping. I even attempted the water-and-soap trick, but no luck. I just couldn’t find the hole.

Thankfully, my trail buddy Viking came to the rescue and lent me his foam sleeping pad until we reached Daleville, Virginia. I was incredibly grateful, but I’m not going to lie, those few nights sleeping on what felt like the bare ground were rough!

When it came time to replace it, there wasn’t much debating what to buy. I loved this sleeping pad enough to purchase the exact same one. Sometimes you find gear that just works for you, and there’s no reason to mess with a good thing.

These trekking poles were a gift from my fiancé, and one of the things I loved most about them was how incredibly lightweight they are. They’re among the lightest options out there, weighing 4.9oz per pole. I was a little surprised that I didn’t see more of them on the trail. Maybe it’s the nearly $200 price tag, or perhaps people are hesitant about investing in carbon fiber poles because of the possibility of them breaking.

I saw plenty of bent poles and broken tips along the way, but guess what? My LT5s survived all 2,200 miles without a single bent shaft or even needing a replacement tip!

Well, almost all 2,200 miles. One of my poles did decide to go on its own solo adventure by leaping into the Delaware River. I had set the pair down while stopping to take a photo at the Pennsylvania–New Jersey state line, and apparently, one decided it had somewhere else to be. I’ll give Gossamer Gear this: these poles do float! Unfortunately, I couldn’t get the attention of any of the paddlers below to help rescue it.

The one pole that decided to stay with me to Katahdin. The other went on its own solo adventure into the Delaware.

I reached out to Gossamer Gear, and they were incredibly kind and sent me a replacement. So, while I technically lost one pole to the Delaware, I was able to finish with a full set.

My one complaint with these poles is the twist-lock adjustment mechanism. It tends to get stuck, and there were plenty of times I had to wrestle with it to get the poles adjusted. Sometimes I’d even hand them over to Jerzy to manhandle them for me!

Despite that one frustration, I was really happy with these poles. They’re lightweight, held up remarkably well over the entire trail, and I’d happily recommend them to someone looking to save weight on their setup.

What I Finished With: Altra Olympus 275 Matryx

I’ve been an Altra fan since 2018. I’ve tried Hoka, I’ve tried Topo, and nothing has quite compared to the zero-drop platform and roomy toe box that Altra offers.

I started the AT with the Olympus 6. I’ll admit, I wasn’t completely sold on this version when I first tried it the summer before my thru-hike. The fit felt a little different from previous versions, and I wasn’t a fan of that odd little heel pillow. But overall, the shoe worked well for me, and I got about 500 miles out of that first pair.

New on the right vs. old pair on the left that I had since Marion, VA, to 501 Shelter in PA.

One of my favorite things about the Olympus is the extra cushioning. When you’re putting in miles day after day, that added cushion makes a big difference. I did experience some heel blisters, particularly along the sides of my heels. Over time, those eventually turned into pretty impressive calluses. As the shoes approached the end of their life, the blisters and foot fatigue became my signal that it was time for a new pair.

That decision came when I stopped at Marion Outdoors to replace my shoes. That’s where I was introduced to the Olympus 275 Matryx. It offered the familiar fit and features I loved, but instead of the heel pillow, it had a built-in gaiter guard.

I still preferred wearing my separate gaiters with these shoes. Although the built-in gaiter guard meant there weren’t traditional gaiter attachment points, I found a workaround. I simply hooked the front of my gaiters onto my shoelaces. The outfitter initially gave me some adhesive Velcro to help secure them, but that eventually peeled off. Surprisingly, the gaiters still stayed pretty well in place without it.

Altra Olympus 275, featuring how I’d attache my gaiters to the shoe laces.

All in all, I went through five pairs of shoes during my thru-hike. I probably could have squeezed the entire trail into four pairs, but I wanted my final 120 miles to be as comfortable and pain-free as possible. My general rule of thumb became replacing my shoes around every 500 miles. If I tried to push them much further, I’d quickly pay for it with blisters and foot fatigue.

After 2,200 miles, I was pretty happy with that system. Your shoes are one piece of gear I’d absolutely budget for before starting a thru-hike. They’re not going to last forever, and waiting until your feet are telling you it’s time for a new pair isn’t always the best strategy!

That covers the gear that carried me through the miles, but what about everything I wore along the way? From socks and underwear to the layers that got me through everything from hot Virginia afternoons to chilly New Hampshire nights, I’ll be breaking down my clothing system in the next post.

Why you should never put your PlayStation 5 on a carpeted floor

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Your PS5 needs a better home than a carpet, and you should keep the vents clear, wherever it ends up.

Sony is a bit ambiguous about the PlayStation 5 and carpeted flooring. Whether you read the downloadable PDF guide or the PlayStation Blog, it references avoiding long-fiber carpet only. However, there are three different PS5s in play, which include the Slim, the PS5 Pro and the original model. The original has no vents on its vertical base, while the Slim and the Pro have small vents on the vertical base and come with little rail stands for horizontal placement. With that knowledge, it's reasonable to assume a Slim and Pro can sit horizontally on carpet, and a first-gen PS5 with its base is fine vertically. But with Sony's basic, innocuous statement to go on, there are no distinguishing suggestions for each.

To be clear, you shouldn't place any PS5s on a short-pile or long-pile carpet, because the issue is what's hiding there. Even if you clean it regularly, carpet is a super magnet for every spore, dead skin flake, dust mote, virus and bacteria you can imagine. It's a cornucopia of tiny terror frenzies that, if viewed under a microscope, would have you ripping your carpet out the same day. And you're placing your vent ports within 1 or 2 inches of it.

Besides, putting any console on the floor is practically begging for it to get stepped on or kicked. Long-pile carpets hide things like Lego bricks, gleefully awaiting the approach of bare feet. Step on one of those and you're falling on your PS5 before you know what bit the bottom of your foot. Horizontal is better on a short-pile carpet, but again, those vents are awfully close to an around-the-clock collection of microscopic horrors. If your PS5, regardless of model, has been on carpeted flooring for a while, it's time to make a change and do a PS5 console cleaning to boot.

A small table or an open TV stand is a much better accommodation for a PS5, so long as the console has plenty of room around it. Sony recommends a minimum of 4 inches around each side of the PS5. Jamming it into a cubby hole like a book won't cut it. If that's your only option, you're better off finding a small stand rather than putting it back on the carpet. The vents need to breathe, and the optimal environment is a clean and open one.

The location and placement of a PS5 matters greatly to avoid overheating, so don't worry about whether or not the system is hidden. A Slim model is a little easier to blend in with the surrounding aesthetic, but the Pro and original models are pretty large and tall. If you have to, make the space. That is, if you want your console to last as long as possible. Over time, things change, objects get tossed on the table or someone sets something down and forgets about it. Simple mindfulness will take care of that in short order. Just keep the area clean and maintained. Now and then, give your vents a quick vacuum. Use a low-power handheld and make sure the PS5 is off and unplugged before you start.

Playing high-performance games for hours on a poorly maintained console in a tight bookshelf will effectively damage its internal components over time. Crashing in the middle of a game is an obvious warning sign. Overly loud fans that never drop to a lower speed or "Your PS5 is hot" messages popping up on screen make things pretty clear as well. If these things are happening and your system is sitting on the carpet, take it as a sign. ‘Pokemon’ Anime Studio OLM Trains AI Coloring Tool on Just Three Frames in Japan Government-Backed Project

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OLM, the Japanese animation group behind the “Pokemon” anime series and films, has developed an AI coloring tool that picks up a show’s look from just three hand-painted frames, OLM Digital board director Yotsukura Tatsuo said at the Taiwan Creative Content Fest (TCCF) in Taipei.

The tool came out of ANIMINS, a 15-month research project Yotsukura led under GENIAC, a generative AI program overseen by Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. The project, which looked at how anime creators could put AI to use, wrapped in February. Related Stories Taylor Swift's 'I Knew It, I Knew You' and 'La Bola Negra's 'La Nieve' Among Nominees for Hollywood Music In Media Awards

Because AI models usually need vast amounts of training data that studios struggle to assemble, OLM took the opposite approach. An artist colors three frames by hand, and the system learns that show’s line shapes and color palette on the spot in about 90 seconds, then colors each remaining frame in around 15 seconds. The more frames an artist paints by hand, the more accurate it becomes.

“Strictly speaking, it isn’t generative AI,” Yotsukura said.

Training only on a production’s own frames means no other works are harmed, he added, and the tool can adapt immediately when a new character or creature, such as a dragon, appears. The point OLM most wanted to stress, he said, is that it fits into existing anime workflows without disrupting them. At proof-of-concept stage, Yotsukura estimated it has cut painting workload by around 30%, though the checkers who review every colored frame have somewhat more to do.

Toon Boom Animation, the Canadian maker of animation software Harmony and Storyboard Pro, is tackling the same bottleneck. Product marketing director Harry Ravelomanantsoa said the beta of the coloring tool in Ember, the company’s AI suite, is wrapping up, and in-betweening is expected to enter beta within a couple of weeks. One Manila studio that tested the coloring tool cut the painting time on a scene of 90 seconds to two minutes from 15 hours to two or three.

OLM employs around 600 staff across Japan, Malaysia, Thailand and the U.S. “As you probably saw passing through the first floor, we make the ‘Pokemon’ anime – not the games – including the TV series and the theatrical films,” Yotsukura said, referring to the 2026-27 Pokemon Asia Championship Series: Taiwan Premier Ball League, held Nov. 7–8 on a lower floor of the Taipei Nangang Exhibition Center, where TCCF is running Nov. 6–9.

The group’s slate also includes kids’ titles “Beyblade” and “Hanakappa” and the adult-skewing “The Apothecary Diaries.” Yotsukura, who also serves as CTO for the group’s Malaysian and Thai arms, noted that an in-house R&D division is unusual for a Japanese animation company.

The group is experimenting with AI for in-betweening as well. But Yotsukura said the bigger gains may come from indirect uses across productions that involve more than 100 staff. With a startup partner from the GENIAC project, OLM is exploring anime-specific AI agents that could help new directors or production managers joining a series that has run for a decade or more, for instance by recalling what a director intended for a given cut. Agents could also speed up approvals and license management for secondary uses of its titles, which Yotsukura called the most revenue-critical part of the business. Because those areas are sensitive, OLM is proceeding with care, he said.

Yotsukura likened the gap between AI demos and production reality to gacha, the Japanese capsule-toy machines where each JPY500 ($3.30) turn may or may not yield the prize you want – roughly what an AI generation credit costs, he said. AI gets close enough to 80% to raise expectations. “Raising it from there to 90 or 100% turns into work so hard it can make you heartsick.”

Hobbyists may be happy with 80%, he said, but professional animators need tools that return their direction 100% of the time and let them make corrections. Anime is also highly sensitive to color, and AI tools that edit an image can shift its RGB values even when the result looks fine.

“The creative stays with the artist, and the mechanical time goes to AI, to Ember,” Ravelomanantsoa said.

Yotsukura said he agreed almost entirely with that split. “AI can only do what it has learned,” he said, adding that the kind of images that move audiences in cinemas, and the never-before-seen ideas behind hit anime, still have to come from people.

He compared AI’s arrival to that of 3DCG, which took around 20 years to be accepted in anime. “Twenty years ago, I kept rejecting it, saying, ‘How could this ever be used in anime?’ Now it’s unthinkable for anime not to have 3DCG,” he said. AI will move far faster, Yotsukura said, because its quality is already high from the start.

Charles Beirnaert, chief creative officer of Blue Spirit, the French studio behind Netflix’s “Blue Eye Samurai,” said the studio uses no AI at all on that series. “We’re not allowed to use AI for the Netflix projects,” he said. Blue Spirit asks every time at what stage AI might be permitted, he added, and the answer is always no – though he acknowledged the rule may not apply to every Netflix partner. On an Amazon series, the studio may use AI only to pre-visualize characters and robots, with none of it reaching the final image. Netflix’s published guidelines for production partners, issued in August 2025, require them to disclose any intended use of generative AI, with written approval needed for certain uses, including generative AI material in the final product.

Away from those shows, Beirnaert’s team is testing AI on a series it is greenlighting as a conventional CG show, with a shorter YouTube version under consideration. A team of around 20 spent roughly three months on the traditional pilot. Using AI, one person recreated that work, covering everything from modeling and layout to animation, within days. The animation fell short of the original, Beirnaert said, but the backgrounds came out better.

“For me, AI is not meant to replace animators. The day every animator is replaced, I will change job and do something else,” Beirnaert said.

The two diverged when an audience member asked how much money AI could save. Beirnaert offered what he called a random estimate of €1 million on an €8 million budget, and said AI may help projects that stall at 80%–90% financed find the rest. Yotsukura said OLM is in a different position: it is brought in only after a budget is fixed, and sees AI as a way to gain more creative freedom within that budget rather than to shrink it. A year or two ago, the question was how many staff AI could replace, he said. Now it is shifting toward new shots that AI tools made possible.

Moderator Kao Chieh, a lecturer at Shih Chien University, put the split down to a difference between Eastern and Western animation companies.

Japanese anime already has creativity in abundance, Yotsukura said, but technology could play a bigger part, with the two driving the industry forward like a pair of wheels. “I think ‘not using AI’ is probably no longer an option from here on,” he said.

6 moments that defined Rob Gronkowski’s Patriots career

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Rob Gronkowski is a New England Patriots Hall of Famer, and it is not hard to see why. Not only was he an integral part of the organization's Dynasty 2.0 in the 2010s, he also performed at the highest level throughout his nine seasons with the team.

When he left the Patriots after the 2018 season — one capped by his third championship — he did so as the owner of 15 NFL records. While some of those have been broken over the last seven years, his impact on the Patriots and the NFL as a whole is undeniable.

Breaking it down to a handful of moments is therefore a challenging task. This collection is therefore more of a "greatest hits" than a comprehensive journey through his oeuvre.

Despite questions about a back injury and even though he fell asleep during his pre-draft visit to Gillette Stadium, the Patriots forced their luck in the second round of the 2010 NFL Draft. Trading up two spots to leap-frog the Baltimore Ravens, they secured Gronkowski with the 42nd overall selection. That moment, which saw him celebrate on stage with his entire immediate family, already set the tone for what was about to come.

Coming off a solid rookie season, Gronkowski fully broke out in Year 2 to establish himself as the best tight end in the NFL and a player with sky-high potential. There were multiple plays during his impressive 2011 campaign that can be picked as examples, but one stands out above the rest: a 49-yard catch-and-run against Washington that showed his rare blend of speed, power and elusiveness. It was one of the first times in his career that he showed that he was a different animal.

Even with Peyton Manning gone, Patriots vs. Colts was still one of the NFL's best rivalries in the mid-2010s. And one particular matchup stands out in regards to Gronkowski: Week 11 of the 2014 season. He finished the game with a non-conspicuous stat-line of four catches, 71 yards and a touchdown. That score, however, was arguably the most spectacular one of his nine-year career: like a "runaway track," as Al Michaels said on the broadcast, he ran over defenders on his way to turning a short third-down conversion into a 26-yard score. Of course, his performance that day was also remembered for something else.

A true tight end, Gronkowski was a dual-impact player both due to his immense receiving prowess and his abilities as a blocker. His contributions in the second area oftentimes go undervalued, but they also define who he was as a player: a powerful and physical presence at the point of attack. Him blocking standout edge Terrell Suggs 1-on-1 in the 2011 AFC Championship Game is a prime example of that, and so is one of the most re-watched moments of his career, when he threw Colts safety Sergio Brown "out of the club" in 2014.

A large part of Gronkowski's success as a Patriots was not just his physical superiority over his opponents, but also his ability to be on the same page as his quarterback; it is why Tom Brady made sure to recruit him to Tampa Bay to close out both players' careers. The chemistry between the two was on constant display for nine years, but if we had to choose one moment as an example — despite the eventual unsatisfactory outcome — it would be the 2015 AFC Championship Game in Denver. Going against a generational defense, Gronkowski converted two massive fourth downs in the late fourth quarter.

Gronkowski's Patriots story cannot be told without also mentioning the injuries he had to overcome, and his Super Bowl history reflects that. He was severely limited against the Giants in 2012 and unable to play against the Falcons in 2017; he also did have a profound impact on the game in his three other appearances, including, in Super Bowl LIII. That particular game put a perfect bow on his career with the team. Slowed by years of physical challenges, he still managed one last hurrah against the Rams: going up the seam as part of the New England's famous Hoss-Y-Juke concept, he hauled in the biggest catch of the game — one that helped set up the only touchdown in the Patriots' eventual 13-3 victory. It was Gronkowski's final catch as a Patriot, and a worthy send-off.
