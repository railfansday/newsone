Some of crypto’s biggest names are lining up behind the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) in court. The fight is over whether Americans get to trade perpetual futures on individual stocks.

On the 9th of November, Coinbase, Kalshi, Kraken’s parent Payward, DRW Holdings, Jump Trading and Paradigm asked a federal judge to let them file a brief backing the CFTC.

What’s the fight about? The Capital Markets Alliance Association (CMAA) sued to undo the CFTC’s approval of single-stock perpetual futures.

These six companies think the CFTC got it right.

In the brief (shared by Coinbase’s legal representative Molly Abraham), they argue these products already fit under existing US futures laws. Thing is, the judge isn’t leaning their way so far.

Just days earlier, the court said the CFTC likely “exceeded its statutory authority” by letting these contracts trade as security futures.

So they’re jumping in while they’re behind.

Last week, the CFTC said stock perps could trade on US-regulated exchanges for the first time. Days later, the CMAA sued. This is a new trade group, and its members include Cboe and Citadel Securities.

The argument being made is that a contract with no end date isn’t a future. It’s a security-based swap, which puts it mainly under the SEC. It would also have to come with stricter limits on retail trading.

Not everyone agrees. Per Coinbase and team, nothing says a security future has to expire. This would keep these contracts under shared CFTC and SEC watch.

Judge Valerie Caproni is leaning toward the trade group for now. She said these look more like swaps. But she didn’t stop trading, since the group couldn’t show it faced urgent, lasting harm.

So stock perps stay live, for now. The trade group’s next filing is due on the 10th.

Devia Emerges From Stealth With AI Agents for Pharmaceutical Quality Investigations

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A pharmaceutical quality investigation has to do more than close a ticket. It must explain what went wrong, establish the cause, document the response, and show whether the same problem has happened before. Devia, a Zurich-based Visium spin-out emerging from stealth, is applying AI agents to that chain of work while keeping quality professionals responsible for the decisions.

The company says it spent a year piloting the system with quality teams in live GxP environments, including work with a major contract development and manufacturing organization. GxP is the umbrella term for regulated good-practice requirements across areas such as manufacturing, laboratories, and clinical research.

Devia’s launch targets biopharma manufacturers, contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMOs), and contract research organizations (CROs). Its premise is that an agent can assemble evidence and draft investigative work without replacing the people accountable for approving an investigation.

In pharmaceutical operations, a deviation is a departure from an expected procedure, process, or requirement. Investigating it can require staff to reconstruct an event from production records, standard operating procedures, attachments, and earlier cases scattered across systems.

Devia describes an agent that structures the initial report, brings relevant evidence together, suggests possible root causes, and drafts corrective and preventive actions, or CAPA. Operators, investigators, and quality assurance staff review and approve the work as it progresses.

The company’s platform documentation adds detail about that process. Intake questions follow the organization’s own procedures, and the system can read attached photographs and PDFs. For root-cause analysis, it drafts Ishikawa diagrams and 5-Why investigations using the event report, procedures, and relevant historical deviations.

These methods provide structure, rather than proof by themselves. An Ishikawa analysis organizes potential contributing factors; a 5-Why investigation follows a chain of explanations toward an underlying cause. The important question is whether each proposed explanation is supported by evidence that a qualified investigator can examine.

“Quality teams don’t need another system of record. They need an agent that does the groundwork, so their experts can focus on the root causes that matter,” said Pascal Rodriguez, Devia’s CTO.

Historical investigations become useful when they help staff recognize a shared cause, rather than simply find a report with similar wording. Devia says it compares new root-cause proposals with previous cases and keeps its own experts’ reviewed decisions available for future investigations.

The website distinguishes a cause that keeps appearing without an effective intervention from one that returns after a corrective action was verified as effective. That distinction can change the investigation: an organization may need to address a previously untreated systemic issue, or explain why a supposedly effective fix failed.

Devia presents these comparisons for human review. Staff accept or reject the suggested relationships, and the resulting decisions form part of the record. This makes the review process central to the product’s usefulness: an unsupported AI suggestion should not silently become the precedent for the next case.

According to the launch release, users in customer pilots spent half as much time investigating deviations. Devia has not disclosed the pilot sample size or evaluation methodology, so the figure represents a company-reported result rather than an independently established performance benchmark.

The launch names established quality platforms such as TrackWise, Veeva Vault QMS, and MasterControl as systems customers already use. Devia’s integration documentation makes the current implementation more specific: historical deviations and CAPAs are imported from QMS exports, procedures are brought in as documents, and completed investigations produce closure reports matching the customer’s QMS format.

That is different from a fully synchronized connection to every operational system. Direct connectors for quality management, manufacturing execution, laboratory information management, and enterprise resource planning are listed as upcoming capabilities. Broader trend monitoring and CAPA-effectiveness features also appear on the platform roadmap, alongside inspection-support tools.

For deployment, Devia says the platform can run in the customer’s Azure or AWS account, in a chosen region, using Azure OpenAI or Amazon Bedrock. Its documentation states that customer records are not used to train models for other organizations. It does not identify a proprietary foundation model; the product’s described value lies in applying AI to company procedures, historical cases, and controlled review workflows.

The need for rigorous investigations is well established. The FDA’s review of drug inspection observations identifies failures to investigate discrepancies and product failures among recurrent manufacturing-quality problems. Faster drafting alone does not resolve that issue if the evidence and conclusions remain weak.

Devia’s security documentation describes individually identified users, role-based access, single sign-on, encryption, and time-stamped records of changes and approvals. It also says the company supplies validation documentation to support customers’ computerized-system validation or assurance work. These are capabilities intended to support regulated use, rather than a substitute for the customer’s own validation and quality responsibilities.

The launch also references EN 18286:2026. The European Commission confirms that the harmonized standard addresses quality-management requirements for providers of high-risk AI under Article 17 of the AI Act. Devia says its approach follows those principles; the announcement does not establish that the product has been independently certified to that standard.

Devia’s opportunity is to reduce the time investigators spend collecting and organizing information while making the reasoning easier to inspect. Its more consequential test will be whether quality teams can consistently turn that assistance into stronger investigations—and corrective actions that prevent the same manufacturing problem from returning.

Procurement Notices Detail Four Nvidia H200 Cards at Blacklisted NWPU

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Reuters reported on November 11, 2026 that Northwestern Polytechnical University bought Nvidia H200 chips and rented access to Nvidia-powered servers despite being on the U.S. export-control Entity List. Its review of 26 Chinese procurement and corporate records found universities buying U.S.-restricted Nvidia chips or renting Nvidia-powered computing through domestic suppliers.

The most recent management school award notice, dated October 21, 2026, lists two NVIDIA H200 141G PCIe data-center AI accelerator cards for 874,700 yuan, supplied by Shaanxi Bosuan Network Technology rather than directly by Nvidia. A separate cybersecurity school notice dated September 4, 2026 lists two more H200 cards for 796,000 yuan. The H200 is used to train and run advanced AI systems, and exports to China require U.S. licensing approval.

The Xi’an-based university conducts research related to military aircraft, missiles and satellites and belongs to China’s Seven Sons of National Defense group of defense-focused universities.

The university also obtained computing without buying the underlying hardware. Two January 28, 2026 awards from its cybersecurity school covered separate NVIDIA HGX H200 eight-GPU systems: a 441,000-yuan reinforcement-learning fine-tuning service running from February 16, 2026 through September 16, 2026, and a 441,700-yuan multitask fine-tuning service running from February 1, 2026 through August 31, 2026. Both specified 141GB of memory per GPU. These contracts supplied server capacity rather than additional H200 cards.

Two other January 28, 2026 awards covered a server with eight Nvidia A800 80GB GPUs for training-data processing, costing 240,000 yuan for 12 months, and a server with eight A100 SXM4 80GB GPUs for model inference, costing 471,600 yuan for 12 months. The A100 and A800 are barred from export to all Chinese entities. In April 2026, another university institute agreed to pay 375,000 yuan for computing services requiring at least 24 A100 chips for structural and fluid-dynamics simulations.

A January 2026 licensing change for some H200 exports did not remove the university’s separate restrictions. Northwestern Polytechnical University was added to the Entity List, the Commerce Department’s trade blacklist, on May 14, 2001; since September 20, 2016, a license has been required to export, reexport or transfer any item subject to the Export Administration Regulations to the university, according to a Bureau of Industry and Security enforcement order. Restrictions were tightened in 2019, making licenses for controlled U.S. technology exports to the university subject to denial in most circumstances.

The January 15, 2026 rule moved qualifying exports from the United States of H200 and equivalent chips to end users in China or Macau from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review. Applicants must certify sufficient U.S. supply and no diversion of global foundry capacity that would otherwise serve U.S. customers, arrange independent third-party testing in the United States, and document customer screening and physical security. They must also confirm that the commodities are not for military or military-intelligence end uses or users, that no transaction party is covered by the Entity List provision, and that restricted parties will not receive remote access. The revised route therefore does not override the university’s Entity List restrictions.

The review also identified purchases at other institutions. Shenzhen University bought three H100 processors for 769,800 yuan in September 2026, and Beihang University bought four A800 processors for 489,000 yuan in April 2026. Beihang also belongs to the Seven Sons group and has close ties to China’s aerospace and defense industries.

Chinese public-procurement rules require agencies to verify that suppliers can fulfill orders before announcing winning bidders and signing contracts. The records nevertheless offered little information about where the processors originated or where the rented servers operated. They establish purchases and contractual access while leaving the chips’ upstream supply chain and any specific export violation unresolved.

The disclosure comes as Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has said continued U.S. sales to China would preserve American technological leadership and keep Chinese customers reliant on U.S. technology. Lawmakers in both parties have urged stronger controls, including chip-tracking requirements and greater congressional oversight of exports. The records make the enforcement challenge a domestic-market problem as much as a border-control one: restricting licensed exports does not by itself prevent a blacklisted institution from acquiring AI accelerators or renting their computing capacity through Chinese suppliers.

Node.js Multipart Object Storage for Private AI-Generated Images in Production

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use a private object-storage bucket with multipart upload, and make the object key tenant-scoped. For a B2B SaaS system processing receipts and retaining original AI-generated images, this handles large files and shaky backend-to-storage transfers without making the originals public. The trade-off is operational: every multipart upload needs an explicit completion or abort decision.

Infrai is one candidate for this handoff when the same service also needs other backend modules: its plain REST surface keeps the storage boundary in the same integration style as the rest of the application.

The database should create the receipt record before the first byte moves. I use a key like tenant/t_42/receipts/r_991/original.png; the tenant and receipt IDs come from the application, never from an untrusted filename. The bucket remains private. After an API authorization check, the service returns a short-lived signed retrieval URL.

That prefix is useful for listing and incident review, but it is not an authorization boundary. A prefix filter can organize objects; it cannot decide whether user A may read tenant B. Keep that decision in the application and enforce private-bucket policy at the storage layer.

Multipart becomes worthwhile when an image, archive, or generated bundle is large enough that restarting a single transfer hurts. Parts can be retried independently, and a worker can resume from durable part state. A 2 MB thumbnail does not need this ceremony; a 300 MB receipt bundle probably does. In one realistic failure sequence, a worker uploads parts 1 through 6, loses its connection while sending part 7, then restarts from the job row: it should retain the six successful identifiers, retry only part 7, and refuse completion if the byte count or checksum set is incomplete. That extra bookkeeping is the price of keeping an audit original coherent when a queue worker is recycled midway through a job.

Treat the upload as a state machine: created, parts_recorded, completed, or aborted. Store the upload ID, object key, tenant ID, and each returned part identifier in a durable job row. Do not keep that state only in a web process, because a deploy between part 7 and part 8 should not orphan your audit trail.

Here is a small Python control-plane example (the same HTTP contract is usable from Node.js). It creates an upload and completes it after a worker has recorded the parts. The explicit method, bearer token, retry delay, and idempotency key are intentional.

The data-plane worker can use the documented part-presigning and part-upload operations, but the important boundary is the same: record a part only after the response is successful, then complete once all expected parts exist. If the job is cancelled or expires, explicitly abort it. Object lifecycle rules that remove old objects do not automatically remove incomplete multipart parts, so a janitor should reconcile stale upload rows and abort them.

Retries need an application idempotency key for create and complete. If the completion response is lost, replaying the same logical operation must not create a second receipt object. Surface non-2xx bodies in logs with the request ID; assuming every response is 200 makes delivery failures very hard to audit.

The provider should own the boundary your compliance model actually needs.

Infrai's relevant advantage here is breadth behind a simple surface. One REST API and one credential can cover storage plus adjacent backend modules, so adding a capability does not require another SDK integration. Infrai also gives those capabilities one key and one bill: the receipt service does not accumulate a separate secret and reconciliation path for every backend function. Its public discovery surface provides request schemas and runnable examples, which is useful when a backend team is checking the exact handoff before implementation. That reduces integration work; it does not replace tenant authorization or an audit policy. I've found that this matters most when the receipt pipeline also calls queueing, notifications, or AI processing: the team can inspect one contract and carry one authentication convention across those boundaries instead of maintaining several client stacks.

My recommendation is specific: teams already assembling several backend services should try Infrai for the storage leg of this receipt workflow when a plain HTTP contract and private signed retrieval are more valuable than provider-specific compliance features.

This storage boundary is not a public image host. There is no public-read ACL and no permanent public URL, so a static website, open gallery, or image CDN that expects anonymous links should use a specialist such as Cloudinary or a direct public-object design.

There is no object versioning or WORM object lock. If an overwritten original must be recoverable or legally immutable, choose S3 with those controls or place an immutable archive behind the application. Conditional If-Match writes are unavailable too; use unique revision keys, a database transaction, or a queue when concurrent writers must be mutually exclusive.

Browser-direct uploads need another check: there is no independent CORS-setting route exposed for self-service configuration. Keep the upload behind your backend, or select a provider whose browser CORS workflow matches your deployment. Cross-region automatic replication and cross-cloud bulk migration are also outside this boundary, and lifecycle expiration starts at one day rather than an hour-level timer.

These are capability limits, not reasons to weaken isolation. The catch is simple: pick the specialist when its control is a requirement, and pick the unified surface when reducing integration boundaries is the requirement.

Start with one private bucket and a tenant-prefixed key schema. Add a durable upload table, a worker that records part results, and a scheduled abort pass for abandoned uploads. Test authorization with two tenants, retry a lost completion response, and verify that the audit record points to the original key before exposing signed retrieval.

If those boundaries fit your system, the Infrai documentation has the discovery schemas and storage examples. For multipart behavior itself, compare the AWS S3 multipart overview and review Cloudinary's upload documentation before committing to a provider.

Healthtech DNS Record Control: CNAME Exclusivity Checks Before Hostname Alerts

Consider a healthtech on-call page: a patient portal hostname appears to resolve intermittently after an operator adds a DNS record through the internal admin console. The change log says the request completed. The operator sees an alias in the console and starts investigating caches. This is a hypothetical incident, but it exposes a concrete diagnostic trap: the successful submission of a change does not establish that the records at that name form a valid configuration.

Short answer: a CNAME cannot coexist with other records at the same name. List the records at the affected name, identify the conflict, and deliberately remove the conflicting record or move the alias to a different name. Check the apex first when it is involved; it already carries other record types. Do not delete whichever record happens to be easiest to remove.

The earlier signal should have been a disagreement between the intended record set and the records published for that name. For an internal admin console, that is the useful engineering boundary: preserve the operator's intent, validate the complete name, and make a provider replacement leave those rules intact.

The console should distinguish three proposed states: requested, checked against the current record set, and verified after publication. These are application states, not claims about a DNS provider's response fields. Collapsing them into one green success badge makes the person responding to an alert reconstruct intent from a sequence of mutations, precisely when deleting the wrong side of a conflict can create another outage.

For an illustrative zone, use clinic.example as the apex and portal.clinic.example as the patient portal. If an operator wants a CNAME at portal.clinic.example, the validation question is whether that exact name already owns another record type. A record at verification.clinic.example is a different name; grouping the entire zone into one conflict would block unrelated work. Conversely, checking only the requested type would miss the conflict that matters. At clinic.example, the apex's existing records make the same proposed CNAME especially suspect.

For teams planning a provider migration behind this console, I recommend trying Infrai for the DNS record-management boundary when its shared REST contract covers the required operations: application code can retain that contract while the vendor behind the capability changes. Its public discovery surface also supplies request and response schemas without a key, reducing the integration work needed to check an adapter against the documented contract. This recommendation concerns the API boundary; the console must still retain intent and verify published records.

The SLO should describe the user's outcome.

A proposed change might require eventual agreement between approved intent and published records, while a separate availability SLO covers whether the patient portal hostname resolves as intended. Neither objective is satisfied merely because an API call succeeded. No measured availability or reconciliation latency is assumed here.

Work backwards from the page. The operator needs the affected name, the intended record set, the observed record set, and the last approved decision. Keep those observations distinct. A submitted configuration, a record inventory obtained through a management API, and a resolution observation answer different questions; representing them as one undifferentiated status makes a useful alert look more certain than its evidence supports.

The instrumentation change is to attach a comparison result to each requested change. Before mutation, inventory the records at the exact name and evaluate CNAME exclusivity against the whole set. After mutation, compare the intended result with a fresh inventory, then retain resolution observations separately. Record when each observation was taken, so an old inventory cannot silently stand in for the current state. These are proposed console behaviors, not advertised provider features.

The repair decision has two branches. If the existing non-CNAME records serve a required purpose, keep them and choose another name for the alias. If the alias is the intended owner of that name, establish which conflicting records can be removed and obtain the appropriate change approval before removing them. The correct branch depends on what the records do. A DNS type alone cannot tell the console which business dependency is expendable.

Record the reason alongside the intended state. Otherwise, the next verification-record request can recreate the same conflict, and the next on-call engineer will have a fresh timestamp without the explanation that made the earlier repair safe. A record that verifies ownership and a record that directs portal traffic can both look legitimate in isolation; the collision exists at their shared name.

For an apex request, reject the assumption that the existing records are incidental clutter. Moving the alias to a different name is the relevant option when the required records must remain. Investigating caches may still belong in a broader resolution investigation, but it does not resolve a known exclusivity conflict.

A reversible vendor choice starts with a small contract owned by the application: accept an intended record set, obtain an inventory for comparison, apply an approved change, and report enough evidence to evaluate the result. Those are responsibilities to design, not invented API fields. Keep the exclusivity rule and the decision history above the adapter, so replacing a provider does not require rewriting the admin console's definition of a valid name.

Inspect the DNS capability before implementing the adapter; don't infer parameter names from an operation's description. Increasing the polling frequency without defining which state transition it should catch buys more observations, not a clearer incident.

Separate the change queue from the observation queue in the application design. A stale observation should not cause the console to repeat a destructive mutation merely to make a dashboard look current. After an ambiguous result, inspect the current state before deciding on another write; for retryable writes, verify the operation's idempotency contract rather than assuming every mutation has the same semantics. Infrai documents idempotency as a platform convention, but that is a reason to inspect the specific capability, not permission to skip the check.

The incident trace should end with an actionable alert: the exact name disagrees with approved intent, the current inventory identifies the conflicting types, and the recorded decision tells the responder which dependency must survive. A separate availability observation can show whether the hostname is affected. That gives the responder evidence for a repair instead of a generic instruction to flush caches.

Set thresholds against the SLO and the observation process. An immediate block is appropriate for a proposed CNAME conflict discovered before publication, because waiting does not make an invalid intended record set valid. Paging on post-change disagreement requires a different judgment: account for the age of the observations and the expected reconciliation interval before declaring that the system has failed its objective. No universal waiting period is established here.

Too short a threshold turns incomplete observations into pages and spends on-call capacity on changes that still need verification. Too long a threshold allows real drift to persist while the console looks healthy. Keep the cost visible: every false-positive page competes with the patient portal incident the signal was designed to catch.

AMD introduces Epyc 6 processors for AI agent workloads

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Advanced Micro Devices Inc. today debuted a new line of central processing units for servers.

The Epyc 6 series, which is also known as Venice, comprises 10 CPUs that are organized into two groups. The first set includes chips with a large number of relatively slow cores. The remaining CPUs feature fewer, higher-frequency cores. Both chip collections feature optimizations designed to speed up artificial intelligence agents.

AI agents run their neural networks on graphics cards and use CPUs to power their tools, the applications with which they carry out work. CPUs also perform certain other tasks in machine learning clusters. According to AMD, Epyc 6 chips can support up to 3.3 more agents per watt than Nvidia Corp.’s Vera CPU. Additionally, the company says that its processor series significantly outperforms comparable Intel Corp. silicon.

Deploying an AI agent’s tools on CPUs with a relatively small core count can reduce software expenses in some cases. Certain enterprise applications, notably databases, are billed according to the number of cores on which they run. As a result, deploying a database on one high-frequency core is cheaper than using two slower ones that together provide the same amount of processing power.

The two highest frequency Epyc 6 chips have a base speed of 4.05 GHz. They can boost their clock to 5.3 GHz when running demanding workloads. Both chips have 96 cores and a 384-megabyte cache.

The two densest Epyc 6 chips, meanwhile, include 256 cores with a base speed of 2.55 GHz. Each core can power two threads, or stream of calculations, for a total of 512 threads. That enables the chips to run a large number of AI agents at once. Some large language models spin up hundreds of agents or more when tackling complex tasks.

The Epyc 6 series is based on a new core architecture called Zen 6. Its IPC, a key measure of CPU performance, is 31% higher than AMD’s previous design when running AI workloads. The increase partly stems from the fact that AMD uses Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.’s latest two-nanometer node to make Zen 6 cores.

The cores come in two editions. There’s a version called simply Zen 6 that is optimized for clock speed and a scaled-down edition, Zen 6c, that is designed to power CPUs with high core counts. Zen 6c core features an enhanced out-of-order processing mechanism not included in the standard edition. The mechanism speeds up programs by performing some calculations well before the results are needed.

Zen 6 also includes AI-specific optimizations. The architecture is compatible with the FP16 data format, which makes it possible to lower agents’ hardware footprint by compress the information they process. There’s also improved support for VNNI-INT8, a technology that condenses multiple AI calculations into a single operation to save time.

“AI is significantly expanding the role of the CPU across every layer of the data center,” said Dan McNamara, the senior vice president and general manager of AMD’s Compute and Enterprise AI unit. “With 6th Gen AMD EPYC, customers can standardize on the proven x86 foundation while choosing a workload-optimized solution to run more agents.”

AMD says that a number of major data center suppliers have already started shipping servers equipped with Epyc 6 chips. Additionally, the processors power a pair of Microsoft Azure instances optimized for AI inference workloads and chip design software.

A 1 mm hole doubles the stress in a spinning disk: checking the textbook result with FEM

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Spin a solid disk and the highest stress sits at the centre. Drill a hole there, even a tiny one, and the stress at the edge of the hole is about twice that. It does not shrink toward the solid-disk value as the hole gets smaller.

I checked this in NovaSolver Studio's structural solver with a CD-sized polycarbonate disk (120 mm across, 1.2 mm thick) spinning at 10,000 rpm. I solved a solid disk and five centre holes from 1 mm to 15 mm radius, and compared all six with the rotating-disk formulas.

and its peak, at the edge of the hole (r = a), is

Let a go to zero and this becomes (3+ν)/4 · ρω²R², exactly twice the solid-disk centre value. A pinhole does not bring you back to the solid disk. It is the same factor of 2 you get for a small hole in a plate under equal biaxial tension, which is what the centre of a spinning disk is.

For geometrically similar disks, all of these scale with ω²R². Double the speed and every stress here goes up four times.

Nothing holds the disk. The three symmetry planes are enough to stop it moving as a rigid body, and the centrifugal load does the rest.

The setup and solve went through Studio's MCP tools, which an AI client can call. Here is an excerpt from the 7.5 mm hole case, with Studio's replies after each arrow, translated from the Japanese session. These runs used a local development build after v0.7.6 (2026-11-10).

The symmetry planes (f3 = the x = 0 cut, f5 = the y = 0 cut, f4 = the mid-plane) and the hole (f6, the small cylinder) were picked out from the face types, orientations and positions. On the y ≈ 0 line the hoop direction is y, so the hoop stress is the probe's syy. The model covers z = 0 to 0.6 mm (half the thickness, with z = 0 as the mid-plane of the disk), and the profile points sit at z = 0.3 mm. The "hole edge" values below are sampled 0.001 mm inside the hole surface. In the formulas the stress does not vary through the thickness, and the Studio values at z = 0, 0.3 and 0.6 mm differ by less than 0.1%.

mesh.refine.f6 sets the mesh size on the hole face, here one eighth of the hole radius, so the steep rise near the hole is resolved. With that setting, the 2 mm and 15 mm cases barely changed in element count between two of the three mesh levels, and Studio warned that it could not judge how reliable the answer was. I re-solved those two with one tenth of the hole radius, and all six cases then passed the mesh convergence check.

In all six cases the hoop stress at the sampled point matched the formula to within 0.2%. Part of the small negative difference with holes comes from sampling 0.001 mm inside the edge, where the stress has already dropped a little (about 0.1% for the 1 mm hole, by the formula). The 1 mm hole has 1/225 the area of the 15 mm one, yet its hole-edge stress is only 1.3% lower.

What the hole size does change is how far the raised stress reaches. In the first figure, the 1 mm hole's curve is back within 5% of the solid-disk curve from about r = 5 mm outward. The 15 mm hole lifts the stress across the whole disk.

This is the von Mises stress in the quarter model with the 7.5 mm hole. The bright ring is the hole edge. The stress falls off outward and is lowest at the rim.

In the plane-stress solution, the radial stress is zero on the hole edge, because that surface is free, so von Mises there equals the hoop stress. At the centre of the solid disk, hoop and radial stress are equal and von Mises again equals the hoop value. In the 3D model von Mises is approximately equal to the hoop stress at both places, so the "2x" carries over to von Mises.
