Tottenham Hotspur midfielder James Maddison is unlikely to face punishment from the club after being pictured out drinking last weekend.

That is according to former Everton chief executive Keith Wyness, speaking exclusively to Football Insider, who believes that the issue is being blown out of proportion on social media.

Photos shared on X earlier this week showed the 29-year-old out drinking with friends in his home town of Coventry on Sunday night, following Spurs’ defeat to Leeds on Saturday.

However, Wyness does not believe that the former Leicester City man deserves to be criticised, provided that it was only an isolated incident for him.

Maddison has worked hard to fight his way back to full fitness in recent months, after an ACL injury ruled him out of almost the entirety of last season.

He has already made six Premier League appearances for Spurs so far this season, but has been left as an unused substitute by Roberto De Zerbi in the last two games.

Everton’s former chief Wyness – who served as CEO at Goodison Park between 2004 and 2009 and now runs a football consultancy advising elite clubs – believes that the “biggest issue” surrounding Maddison’s night out is the optics of an injury-prone player drinking alcohol.

The boardroom expert pointed out that alcohol will not help athletes when it comes to both performance and the health of their bodies, especially those who are vulnerable to serious injuries.

Nevertheless, speaking on the latest edition of Football Insider’s Inside Track podcast, Wyness outlined his feeling that some of the criticism towards Maddison has been unwarranted.

“Look, I think it's wrong to conflate Maddison being seen having a drink on a Sunday night; it's after the games have been played at the weekend and it's before an international break,” he said.

“The biggest issue I think here is that he's got a big injury record and does drinking help with recovering from injuries properly? That's the only question.

“Apart from that, I really don't see anything wrong with a player doing that. He's a senior pro, and I find it hard to criticise him for that, provided it was in moderation.

“But as I say, it just depends on the medical advice because alcohol and injury recovery don't always mix. But then again, it could be a completely different situation in terms of his recovery.”

Wyness previously told Football Insider at the start of November that Tottenham should be looking to hand a heavily appearance-based contract to Maddison next summer.

The club are approaching a crossroads over the player’s future, with his current deal in North London due to expire in the summer of 2028.

Wyness believes that Spurs could offer Maddison a bonus-heavy contract, that would see him earn a low initial wage, with the prospect for that to increase if he proves his fitness.

However, he also pointed out that an exit is also likely to be a possibility, rather Spurs taking the risk of potentially losing him for free.

New Richard Avedon Documentary Shows That Fashion Isn’t All Style—It’s Substance, Too

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When The Devil Wears Prada 2 hit theaters this past spring, it painted an accurate and depressing picture of how fashion publishing has dwindled, its creative juices squeezed. Miranda Priestly, is no longer calling on her assistants to ring Patrick, a reference to the fashion photographer Patrick Demarchelier, or anyone of that caliber.

As someone who spent a majority of their career in that industry and had a short stint at Vogue, the publication the “Devil Wears Prada” films were based on, it’s fair to say everyone goes in with a dream of what it means to work at a fashion magazine. Often, those fantasies are informed by the photographs of Herb Ritts, Helmut Newton, and Richard Avedon, the latter of which is the subject of a new documentary by filmmaker Ron Howard. Titled Avedon, it releases on Netflix today. As far as fashion documentaries go, it’s full of style and substance, backed up with anecdotes from Larry Gagosian, Tina Brown, Twiggy, Hilton Als, Samira Nasr, Isabella Rossellini, Calvin Klein, Avedon’s son John, and James Martin, the executive director of the Richard Avedon Foundation. Gagosian, who represents the Richard Avedon Foundation and has just opened an Avedon show in Beverly Hills, recalls going to brunch with the photographer, where all that was served was hard-boiled eggs and champagne. “I thought it was super elegant,” says Gagosian.

Using a combination of archival footage and interviews, Howard echoes the glitz and extravagance of fashion publishing’s bygone era of hefty budgets and creative freedom: Avedon’s photoshoots for Harper’s Bazaar and Vogue would last four days, and often featured models such as Lauren Hutton, Beverly Johnson, and Penelope Tree. Avedon managed to capture not just pretty faces, but the souls and secrets behind them.

Avedon grew up reading magazines such as Harper’s Bazaar, Vanity Fair, and Vogue at his father’s women’s retail business on Fifth Avenue. In them, he found the photographs by Martin Munkácsi, Edward Steichen, and Anton Bruehl.

He got his start in the 1940s, during World War II, working as a staff photographer for the United States Maritime Service, for which photographed around 100,000 faces on plain white backgrounds for ID cards with his Rolleiflex camera. “It’s existential white, the only thing you see is the geography of the face,” Avedon says in an old interview used in the film, referring to the way he spotlit his subjects against a white background.

After the war, he took courses in graphic design and photography at the New School, where he met Harper’s Bazaar’s art director, Alexey Brodovitch, who encouraged him to go to Paris in 1947 as part of the magazine’s mandate of “bringing back the glamor of pre-war Paris.”

At that publication, Avedon bore witness to a fashion revolution. In 1947, Christian Dior presented his New Look collection, an antidote to the rationed fabrics of the war, with full pleated skirts falling below mid-calf length. Avedon, who extravagantly captured five girls swirling in their full-length skirts, says that he lit each photograph he took in Paris as though he were reconstructing a Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers film.

He recalls photographing a model crying at a railway station. The picture was met with some acerbic feedback from Carmel Snow, the renowned editor-in-chief of Harper’s Bazaar, who coined the term the New Look: “Nobody cries in a Dior hat, Dick.”

Avedon’s work for Harper’s Bazaar shaped his career, paving the way for him to become a star beyond the fashion world. In 1955, he photographed one of his most famous images, Dovima with elephants; Gagosian reveals he once sold it for $2 million. By 1957, Avedon had shot to fame like Picasso (he photographed the artist in 1958) and had become the inspiration behind Stanley Donen’s film Funny Face, in which an Avedon-like photographer is played by Astaire. In a nod to the film’s inspiration, Avedon was brought on to shoot the stills used for the film’s opening credits.

Fundamentally, Avedon was always on a mission to capture the human condition. He firstly did so with stars and starlets to illustrate that nothing is really so perfect. Howard’s documentary seems to suggest that this was all related to Avedon’s own imperfect upbringing.

In Howard’s documentary, Avedon, who died in 2004, says that his parents always presented an idealized family in their scrapbooks and often borrowed a dog as a prop to exaggerate the image they cultivated. He grew up in a stern household with a father that was emotionally absent for a big chunk of his life until they reconciled later in life.

His sister Louise, was his first muse. Avedon says in the film that her “beauty affected me,” and that all the models he chose resembled his sister with their oval faces, high cheekbones, and brunette hair. Louise was catatonic and spent more than 15 years living in a psychiatric hospital, where she died in 1968 at age 42.

“One of the great themes in his work is alienation and isolation. He wants to know what’s going on inside human beings,” says John, Avedon’s son. In times of heartache, Avedon reveled in his work—such as when his first wife, the actress Doe Avedon, left him for another man that was blonde and tall, or when his second wife, Evelyn Franklin, fell into depression, leading Avedon to start living out of his studio. “Everything diminishes,” Avedon says here. “Work does not. It’s the one thing that doesn’t turn on you.”

Maybe because his life wasn’t so pretty, Avedon’s photographs didn’t always flatter. When he shot Nancy Reagan, for example, he abided by all of her handler’s guidelines. Then, in the final image, he focused on her claw-like hands, a contrast to her perfectly, smooth face.

Sometimes to get the image he wanted, he would manipulate his subjects, as he did when he shot the Duke and Duchess of Windsor (formerly King Edward VIII and Wallis Simpson). Avedon told them that, on the way to the photoshoot, his taxi ran over a dog. This was a lie, but they seemed to believe it, and Avedon captured the droop in their smile. Simpson told Avedon that she would destroy him when the photos were published, but Avedon, who was Jewish, described the couple as “compassionate,” despite the fact that they sympathized with the Nazis during World War II.

It’s evident through Avedon’s line of work that the photographer saw the world beyond the pages of Harper’s Bazaar and Vogue. He waited seven weeks in Vietnam to photograph the American generals of the war; the residents of the American West and the Chicago 7 also counted among his subjects. The film portrays Avedon as a complicated figure, one whose contributions exceed the field of fashion photography.

It also portrays him as a complicated person, but it could’ve gone even further. Howard shies away from claims about Avedon’s sexuality that have been voiced in books such as Avedon: Something Personal, by Norma Stevens and Steven M. L. Aronson, and Michael Gross’s Focus: The Secret, Sexy, Sometimes Sordid World of Fashion Photographers. Gross reports that Avedon had an affair with his high school classmate, the writer James Baldwin, who even worked on a book with Avedon called Nothing Personal. That isn’t addressed here. (Avedon’s foundation vigorously disputed certain episodes described in Something Personal upon the book’s release, but it did not outright deny the descriptions of the photographer’s sexual identity.)

Howard also glosses over the fact that Avedon stopped working for Vogue when Anna Wintour replaced Grace Mirabella as editor-in-chief, moving away from his cropped headshot covers. This is also skipped over entirely.

Howard is instead more focused on the glamorous ghosts of a vanished era. The fashion industry has been consumed by capitalism, and fashion magazines have lost their ability to influence. All we’re left with is tales of the good old days and a graveyard of nostalgic images. You don’t have to look too closely to see that the devil is in the picture. For proof, just look at one of the photographs featured here: Avedon’s 1952 portrait of the actor Charlie Chaplin, who raises two fingers to his head, as though he were Satan himself.

The Packers are dead last in the NFL in rushing offense, averaging 68.2 rushing yards per game. That is the fewest rushing yards per game for a team with a winning record through the first nine games of a season in NFL history, and bell cow running back Josh Jacobs is sidelined after undergoing finger surgery.

The Packers have also dropped the most passes, 23, which is seven more than the next closest team. Green Bay's 39 accepted offensive penalties are the third most in the entire league, and the Packers' offensive line is allowing an average time to pressure of 2.48 seconds, the second-fastest in the league, better than only the Carolina Panthers (2.46 seconds). That's because the Packers' best offensive lineman, right tackle Zach Bako-Bewele, went down with a season-ending injury in Week 2, and Green Bay is currently starting their third-string center. All of this is to say Love is doing a lot with not much help around him. He's earned the Packers' winning record.

Despite all those disadvantages and NFL defenses knowing he has to throw it because the Packers can't run it, Love is dominating. On Sunday, he rolled into Gillette Stadium and threw for 372 yards passing and three passing touchdowns while completing 23 of his 29 throws, in addition to adding a 13-yard rushing touchdown. When Packers coach Matt LaFleur subbed in his second-team offense into the game with eight minutes left in the fourth quarter, Green Bay led 45-21. Those 45 points are the most the Patriots have allowed in a home game since the 1970 AFL/NFL merger.

On Sunday against the Patriots, he also completed six of his eight deep passes, throws of 20-plus air yards, for 235 yards and a touchdown. That was Love's second game with at least six completions of 20-plus air yards in 2026, and no other quarterback in the NFL has done that in any other game this season, per the league's Next Gen Stats.

"I can't say enough about the play of Jordan Love. He makes my job a hell of a lot easier," LaFleur said postgame on Sunday. "He is standing in there, he's taking hits and still dealing the ball. ... Those are low-percentage throws, in theory, but he's making them look high-percentage. They're right on the money."

Britain’s coastal floods have a surprising connection to distant skies

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and Arctic help explain where extreme storm surges strike Britain’s coast and northwestern Europe.

Storm surges look like local disasters, but which stretch of Britain’s coast floods can often be traced to air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Those far-off patterns help decide whether northern or southern coasts take the high water.

A surge is hard to forecast, because wind, low air pressure, and the shape of the coast all play a part at once.

Understanding why surges hit certain places, such as East Anglia in eastern England, could give coastal towns earlier flood warnings.

Lisanne Blok of the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the British Antarctic Survey.

Her team studied more than 70 years of sea levels around northwestern Europe.

A computer model rebuilt those levels day by day from 1950 onward, using past weather data rather than tide gauge readings.

The team first removed the tides and the normal seasonal rise and fall of the sea. Then they used machine learning to sort the coastline into regions that tend to get extreme surges on the same days.

They ended up with six regions across the seas of northwestern Europe, plus eight smaller ones inside the North Sea.

“If we can identify conditions emerging in, for example, the Atlantic that might result in a storm surge in East Anglia, then we can pinpoint parts of the coast that should prepare for flooding,” said Blok.

Big, repeating pressure patterns over the ocean are what scientists call climate modes.

“Computer models used to forecast risk of coastal flooding don’t always account for large-scale climate modes, instead focusing on localized weather variables,” she said. “But what our findings tell us is that climate modes are really key.”

A storm surge builds when strong winds and low air pressure push a bulge of seawater toward land.

Sometimes it rises high enough to spill over flood defenses. If that happens at high tide, with big waves on top, the sea can reach extreme heights and flood the coast.

Over January 31 and February 1, 1953, a deep low-pressure system moved down the North Sea and pushed a huge mass of water along Britain’s east coast.

It arrived with a spring tide, the extra-high tide that comes around a new or full moon, and sea levels rose about 10 feet (3 meters) above predicted levels.

Floods swept through parts of Britain, the Netherlands, and Belgium.

Another surge struck Britain’s east coast on December 5 and 6, 2013, with water levels in some places higher than during the 1953 flood, but modern defenses prevented a disaster on that scale.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel shape, tightest between East Anglia and the Netherlands, pushes water toward land. Its shallow floor, roughly 30 to 490 feet (10 to 150 meters) deep, makes the surge taller.

On the worst 1% of days in the rebuilt records, surges added at least 22 inches (57 centimeters) to the North Sea’s water level, more than in any other region.

“The storm surges are very dynamic and complex in this region,” said Blok. “There is also heightened risk, because lots of people live along the low-lying coastal regions bordering the North Sea.”

Next, the team compared each region’s surges with those big pressure patterns.

“We can link regional storm surge extremes to very distinct climate patterns,” Blok said. “I didn’t expect such clear relationships, especially across the large distances involved.”

The strongest links were to pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere, including the North Atlantic Oscillation, a seesaw in air pressure between the regions around Iceland and the Azores.

When Iceland’s pressure is low compared with the Azores, the jet stream, a fast wind high up, moves north, and surges become more likely on northern and central coasts.

“As the storms shift to the north, they also deliver high seas to more northerly coasts,” said Blok.

When pressure over Iceland is unusually high instead, the pressure difference with the Azores shrinks.

The jet stream slows and bends south. Storms then take southern routes, bringing high seas to southern coasts.

Storms behind extreme surges off southwest England and west of Ireland tended to start near Newfoundland, off eastern Canada, thousands of miles away.

Storms behind surges farther north, in the seas toward Norway, more often began off Greenland or in the central North Atlantic.

Those southwestern surges were also more common in autumn, while the other regions saw most of theirs in winter.

Ali Mashayek, an associate professor in Cambridge’s Department of Earth Sciences, was the study’s senior author.

“Conditions in the Arctic have a big impact on UK weather,” he said. “That’s something we’ve known for a while, although we haven’t been able to link it to storm surge distribution before.”

Mashayek said Arctic warming will weaken the pressure difference that powers the jet stream, and “that means it is likely we will see a shift in UK storm patterns, including where storm surges occur, in future.”

But he added that “this is something we are still trying to understand.”

It’s time to view local weather extremes in a global context, Mashayek said. “We now have more evidence that the extreme weather in our backyard may be driven by what happens in very distant parts of the world.”

“This speaks to the broader issue that climate change and the extremes it induces are a truly global problem that need global solutions.”

The surge records left out waves and long-term sea level rise. The team also didn’t check whether the links change from season to season.

Next, the researchers want to run the same analysis on future climate projections, to see whether the surge regions shift as the planet warms.

The full study was published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

Trump wanted little Kei trucks for America, Toyota makes it happen

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Does this herald the start of a small car revolution, or is it a meaningless distraction?

The idea that Americans should be offered a greater choice of smaller cars has support from strange bedfellows indeed. After all, who could forget President Trump—usually one to take a maximalist approach to any problem—announcing last year that he wanted the Department of Transportation to make Japan’s diminutive “Kei” cars legal here in the US.

Ars’ attempts to get DoT to tell us more about the potential influx of little vehicles have mostly fallen on deaf ears. But this week, there has been movement on the topic, with Toyota announcing that it will bring back the Scion brand with the KEI-01.

Here in the US, the preponderance of humongous SUVs is a consequence largely of government policies, specifically corporate average fuel economy standards that made it less profitable to sell smaller, efficient cars. Japan’s Kei cars are what happens if the government instead restricts the size, engine capacity, and power output.

A Kei car must fit within the following dimensions: 11.2 feet (3.4 m) long, 4.9 feet (1.5 m) wide, and 6.6 feet (2 m) tall, with an engine displacement of no more than 0.66 L and an output of 63 hp (47 kW) or less. That’s a far cry from the more than 16 feet (4.9 m) of a full-size pickup truck, but it makes sense for a country where you’re not allowed to buy a car unless you have somewhere to park it, with those spaces being at an absolute premium.

Being affordable to buy and economical to run sure doesn’t hurt either, and last year, Japan bought almost 1.7 million Kei cars, out of total sales of 4.6 million vehicles. Depending on where you live in the US, it can be varying degrees of difficult to import a Kei car, at least until now.

Toyota killed off its Scion sub-brand more than a decade ago, ending the haggle-free approach and cars aimed at younger buyers in favor of selling more Toyotas. But now it’s back as “Scion by Toyota,” which the automaker will apparently use to experiment with less traditional ideas. But forget about a cool-but-boxy EV in the mold of the Honda N-One or Nissan Sakura.

“The future of mobility will not be defined by one vehicle, one technology, or one way of thinking,” said Simon Humphries, Toyota’s chief branding officer and head of design. “Scion by Toyota gives us a place to challenge conventional thinking, explore new possibilities, and create mobility in new ways. The KEI-01, SxS, and BKE concepts each represent a different possibility—and together, they show what can happen when curiosity leads the way.”

Before anyone gets too excited, the KEI-01 will be classified as a “low-speed vehicle,” which means it can’t go any faster than 25 mph (40 km/h). So if you were hoping to use one of these but need to drive on anything but surface streets, be prepared to get honked at, a lot, and not in a good way. In fact, unless you’re buying a fleet, Toyota won’t sell you a KEI-01 at all, at least not to begin with.

Oh, and it’s only available as right-hand drive, since that’s the side of the road Japan drives on. But it does feature all-wheel drive, so if you own a ranch—or perhaps a whole load of golf courses—the utility becomes a little more obvious. Toyota says the KEI-01 is suited to small business owners in urban environments, farmers and ranchers, organizations that have campuses that need fleets of facility vehicles, delivery drivers, and outdoor enthusiasts. The trucks will be shipped to Toyota’s assembly plant in Indiana as knock-down kits, adding 50 jobs to the plant in mid-2027.

Low-speed vehicles have been street-legal in parts of the US for many years now. In my neighborhood in Washington, DC, Circuit operates a low-speed EV shuttle on demand, and gas-powered golf carts are very popular in retirement communities. Pricing for the KEI-01 hasn’t been announced, but speculation runs from as little as $10,000 to as much as $28,000.
