The World’s Most Famous Architects Are Building in Albania. I Had to Find Out Why

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“I'll try to focus on this, which is not the easiest thing today,” says Edi Rama, the embattled prime minister of Albania, after a night spent monitoring violent protests in the capital, Tirana. But Rama quickly warms to one of his favorite subjects. “Architecture,” he says, choosing his words carefully, “has the power to change societies. It’s like a strong medicine. It can heal, or it can kill.”

A journalist who covers architecture, I had been hearing about a building boom in Albania for several years. It seemed like half the architects I knew in New York were on a first-name basis with the prime minister, a working artist and, at 6’-7”, a former pro basketball player. So on October 19 I flew to Tirana—a short side-trip from Greece, where I was on vacation with my husband—to look at some of the new buildings and interview Rama about them. But I arrived during a season of civil unrest. The prime minister, whose assistant had told I needn’t postpone the trip because everything was fine, had moved to a secure location on the outskirts of the city; some of the buildings I had come to see had just been vandalized by protesters; and at one point I was accused, publicly, of aiding a government spy. (The charge was— need I even say this—absurd.) And, because I’m in the early stages of Parkinson’s disease, I experienced all this from a four-wheeled mobility scooter, which I had rented for about $100.

As I tooled around the city center, much of it seemed unaffected by the protests. In fact, most of Tirana was charming. But weren’t those Molotov cocktails we heard going off during an al fresco dinner? Those weren’t on my drinks menu.

What was on my menu in Tirana, a city of about 600,000 in a country of about 2.3 million, was confusion. Confusion about why the protesters were so angry, and about why the prime minister seemed so unconcerned. In his 13 years in office, Rama, 62, has been remaking as much of Albania as possible with the help of foreign architects. Even the heavily guarded villa where he agreed to be interviewed, a raw concrete building dyed bright red, is largely the work of the Spanish architect Pablo Bofill.

When protesters began demanding Rama’s resignation last May, he was about to host his second annual gathering of architects from around the world, one of the high points of his year. Architects like the Danish superstar Bjarke Ingels and the Chilean Alejandro Aravena, who won the Pritzker Prize in 2016, “pay to come here and talk and they love it,” Rama says. But this year the get-together, from June 3 to 5, drew protesters with signs that read “Starchitects go home!” Those protests presaged the more violent demonstrations in October and November.

I haven’t attended either of Rama’s conclaves, but what I’ve heard reminds me of my visit to Ordos, Inner Mongolia, in 2008, which I wrote about for The New York Times. A wealthy industrialist, aided by the artist Ai Weiwei, had brought 100 young architects from around the world to the isolated desert city. Their task was to design 100 villas for anonymous (and seemingly nonexistent) Chinese millionaires. The architects picked numbers out of a bowl to determine which of the 100 sites was theirs, and before flying home they were paid for their designs with wads of cash. None of the villas was built, but being in Ordos was a bonding experience for a generation of architects, a few of whom have recently been to Tirana. At least until the latest protests, the mood among Rama-anointed architects—giddy optimism coupled with uncertainty about what would actually be built—echoed the mood in Ordos almost 20 years ago. Both situations felt surreal.

Rama normally works in an office on the Boulevard of Martyrs of the Nation in the center of Tirana. But on October 20 the boulevard's sidewalks were missing hundreds of paving stones, which protesters had used to shatter storefronts. (Some that weren’t shattered bore hastily made signs telling protesters, “Don’t target us. We are with you.”) And some of the stones were reportedly thrown at police during nighttime clashes that authorities say left hundreds injured. Police have used tear gas and rubber bullets as protesters set small buildings, like an information kiosk in the vast Skanderbeg Square, on fire. Near the shell of the kiosk, workers scrubbed fresh graffiti off a statue of Skanderbeg himself.

The Tirana Pyramid, built by followers of the brutal dictator Enver Hoxha, who died in 1985, as his memorial, and turned into a public amenity by the Rotterdam firm MVRDV, was also damaged.

There’s a reason some Albanians—it’s impossible to say how many—resent the foreign architects and the attention lavished on them. In the 26 years since he became the mayor of Tirana and then prime minister, Rama has initiated or encouraged more than 50 international architecture competitions for projects ranging from parks to museums to large housing developments. Forty-eight of those were won by foreign architects (some working with Albanian firms), according to Adelina Greca, who leads the National Territorial Planning Agency, which organizes competitions for the government. Only two were won by Albanian firms working alone. In addition, Rama has played matchmaker, giving private developers the names of foreign architects he strongly recommends they hire.

As a result, Tirana’s compact city center is filled with buildings in bright colors and novel shapes. There is a so-called vertical forest, a skyscraper laden with trees and shrubs, by the Italian architect Stefano Boeri. The green-colored building is just a stone’s throw from the bright red Marriott by another Italian architect, Marco Casamonti. The Plaza Hotel, by architect Johan Anrys, of the Belgian firm 51N4E, starts as an ellipse at its base and morphs into a rectangle at its crown. But the champion attention-getter is a condo building by MVRDV in the shape of a head. Its facade and balconies resolve into facial features, most conspicuously a pronounced nose. The architects have publicly identified it as the head of Skanderbeg, a 15th century military hero. But some Albanians think it’s Rama. Both ideas are “urban legends,” says the prime minister. “It’s the form of a man through history,” he says, contradicting the architects’ own statement. Nodding at this reporter, he says, “It also looks like you."

Tirana's Rock (aka the Skandebeg building) is an MVRDV-designed condo in the shape of a head

A Marriott designed by Marco Casamonti of Archea Associati, left, and the “vertical forest” skyscraper by Stefano Boeri

The vertical forest, the Marriott and the Plaza Hotel are all handsome additions to the city. So, too, is the “head,” but is it too much of an architectural one-liner? Who, I wonder as I pass it several times a day, is going to want to live in the nose?

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.”

Anrys, who 22 years ago became one of the first foreign architects in Albania, told me that working there has taught him "what architecture truly means for improving society.” He said he knows that Albanian architects wonder, "Are we not good enough?" and believe we're taking jobs from them. "It isn't true. I have a studio in Tirana with 20 Albanian architects." Overall he says, the foreign architects have “brought Albanian architects more work than we've taken."

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.” The rest, he explains, see that having first-rate architects from abroad, including Herzog & de Meuron, OMA, and Diller Scofidio & Renfro, working in Albania will raise everyone’s game.

Rama tells me—on-the-record and perhaps unwisely—that the only Albanian architects who have a problem with his overtures to foreign firms are “the mediocre ones.”

The foreign architects who win competitions are required to enlist architects licensed in Albania to help them execute their plans. Rama compares the situation to that of China, which 20 years ago was giving its most prominent building commissions to foreign architects, including some of the same ones working in Albania today. Chinese architects, Rama says, got a free education from the western masters.

“If we continue this kind of cooperation,” says Greca, who has worked for Rama almost continuously since 2003, “there will come a day when Albanian architects will win competitions not only here but around the world.”

Anti-government protesters on the streets of Tirana, with the art installation Tirana Moons by Austrian architect Chris Precht in the background

But Greca’s association with Rama has made her a controversial figure among local architects. On my first day in town, our route intersects that of a lunchtime protest march. We stop to watch. Greca notices many architects and architecture students in the crowd. (The Faculty of Architecture and Urbanism of the Polytechnic University of Tirana is nearby.)

Within minutes, Gent Progni, an organizer of the march, posts a photo of Greca and me on Instagram. The caption claims she had sunk so low as to use a disabled person (me) as cover to spy on the marchers. (Ten days later, the post has almost 7,000 “likes.”) Later, in a text responding to questions from this reporter, Progni wrote:

“My opposition is not to foreign architects but to the way Edi Rama uses prestigious international names to legitimize controversial development projects, often without transparency, meaningful public consultation, or accountability. This is a significant issue for our protest movement, but it is part of a much larger struggle against corruption, the concentration of power, and the sale of Albania’s public wealth.”

Rama himself isn’t happy with all of the new buildings in Tirana. He complained about one whose facades are made of stone “until a certain altitude,” where the cost-cutting developer switched to a printed material that only looks like stone. Other buildings stand partially complete, or complete but eerily empty. And in several cases developers have replaced foreign architects with local ones midstream, in order to complete buildings without meeting the original design specifications. “We need the investors to stick with the architects,” Rama says. But funding is less available than he would like. “Certain architecture needs a certain quality,” he says, philosophically. “But compromises are forced by the limitations of the place.”

The problems with his building boom go deeper than facade materials. There are accusations of money laundering and other forms of corruption, which grew louder at the end of May, when Rama agreed to support efforts by a company associated with Jared Kushner and Ivanka Trump to build luxury resorts on previously undeveloped land. It was a coincidence that the big architecture conference started just a few days later, leading directly to the calls for starchitects to scram. Among the protesters, that demand became entangled with environmental and anti-Trump sentiments in what became known as the Flamingo Revolution.

A housing project by Madrid-based firm SelgasCano

European journalist and curator Anneke Abhelakh has recently published a brilliantly illuminating book, The Albanian Files, which gives 60 foreign architecture firms eight or more pages to describe the work they are doing in Albania. Altogether, the book lists more than 500 projects. Of those, only about one-tenth have been realized. Rama says he is looking forward to the completion of many more.

But can that really happen, given the pressure to resign? The prime minister, who won the most recent election by the biggest majority of his career, says he isn’t going anywhere.

In fact, seemingly oblivious to the mood on the street, he wants to further celebrate his stable of foreign architects. Near the end of my half-hour interview, he mentions that he is thinking of turning The Albanian Files into a museum, with one room for each of the 60 foreign firms to show drawings, models and photographs of their work. He said he already has a site for what he says will be “a big store of dreams, hopes and challenges, illusions and delusions. It will be a great place to go.” Its designer will be chosen, naturally, in an international architectural competition.

I left Albania with lots of questions and thousands of photos to sort through. In one of them I have my arm around Rama. Why the affectionate pose? A reflex when the camera comes out. But I suppose also that, politics aside, I can’t hate a man who loves architecture.

Why Wayfair Stock Is Up Today

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shares of Wayfair (W +10.99%) leaped 11% on Wednesday after the home goods-focused e-commerce leader delivered stronger-than-expected profits despite a tepid overall economic environment.

Wayfair's net revenue rose 9.7% year over year to $3.4 billion in the third quarter.

The gains were fueled by an 11.4% jump in U.S. net revenue to $3 billion, marking the online shopping platform's fastest pace of growth since the early stages of the pandemic.

"At the start of this year, we told you we expected to take share in the U.S. at a pace we had not seen in several years," CEO Niraj Shah said. "With U.S. revenue growing double digits against a roughly flat category here in Q3, we've done exactly that."

Wayfair's active customers grew 4.7% to 22.2 million as of Sept. 30.

Although average order value declined to $311 from $317 the prior-year quarter, customers are ordering more often and spending more overall.

Orders delivered in the third quarter jumped 12.2% to 11 million, while last 12-month net revenue per active customer increased 3.1% to $596.

All told, Wayfair's adjusted earnings per share surged 33% to $0.93. That was well above Wall Street's estimates, which had called for per-share profits of $0.79.

Looking ahead, Wayfair sees "high single-digit growth" in revenue in the fourth quarter.

"The momentum we have on orders and customers gives us confidence that our share gains are structural, and we are still in the early innings," Shah said.

Darnold's backup, Drew Lock, is also dealing with an elbow injury. Per the NFL's projections, the Seahawks' chances of making the playoffs drop from 47 percent to 29 percent if they lose this weekend.

Packers fans might not like it, but the team is firmly in the "already caring about the results of other teams' games" portion of the season — and honestly has been for a couple of weeks now. The Seahawks and Rams are the only legit threats to push Green Bay out of the postseason, at least for now, and there hasn't been a lot of positive news to come out about those clubs early on in Week 9.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

This Train Offers a Front-Row Seat to One of Colorado’s Greatest Natural Wonders

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“Locomotives all have their own attitudes and personalities,” says Adam Clawson, the 24-year-old engineer at the helm of Royal Gorge Route Railroad, as our train winds along the Arkansas River. “They are their own living, breathing machines, and you have to learn their personalities.”

It’s a warm Saturday afternoon in October, and Clawson is at the controls of a locomotive he knows well: “Black Betty,” a jet-black, 3,000-horsepower diesel-electric unit that runs along a 12-mile stretch of tracks in Southern Colorado. Clawson is a third-generation railroader, following in the footsteps of his grandfather and great-grandfather, who both worked along this same route.

The Royal Gorge Route Railroad winds along the Arkansas River in Colorado.

As he eases the rumbling locomotive through the gorge, he offers a running commentary on the geology and history unfolding outside the window. Millions of years ago, he says, as the Rockies rose above the plains, the Arkansas River began carving its ancient path through the emerging peaks. Over time, the water wore away the hard granite, cutting a narrow, V-shaped gorge that plunges as deep as 1,250 feet in places.

The result is the Royal Gorge, a geological wonder nicknamed the “Grand Canyon of the Arkansas River.” Visitors can experience the dramatic 10-mile-long chasm from several perspectives: From above, while gazing down from the Royal Gorge Bridge, the tallest suspension bridge in the nation (or for more adventurous travelers, while rock climbing a 5-hour via ferrata); and from below, while paddling the Arkansas’s roaring rapids on a whitewater raft. But for travelers looking to sit back and savor the scenery passing by, there's no better viewpoint than the Royal Gorge Route Railroad's open-air car, standing face-to-face with the staggering canyon walls.

The writer's view aboard the Royal Gorge Route Railroad

This train line is as historic as it is scenic. Though the tourist train didn't launch until 1998, the route itself has been here for nearly 150 years—but it was close to never being built in the first place. In the late 1870s, a pair of competing railroad companies—the Denver & Rio Grande and the Santa Fe—duked it out for the chance to build tracks through this narrow canyon. Their two-year battle, which involved everything from court injunctions to sabotage to gun-slinging hired guards, became known as the “Royal Gorge War.” Eventually, the two companies signed a treaty and the Denver & Rio Grande prevailed, building tracks that eventually reached the lucrative mining hub of Leadville.

Decades later, the train still threads through the same Old West landscape, but the ride is decidedly more comfortable. Today passengers can choose from three classes: Vista Dome, which offers nearly unobstructed views of the gorge from glass-enclosed dome cars; Deluxe Class in the vintage dining car, where four-top tables with cushioned booth-style seats and a bar are nestled against the large windows; and Coach Class, with four-person table seating and the option to order food and drink from the casual grill menu and bar. On select dates, there are also First Class dining tickets available for lunch and dinner, with elevated menus and wine pairings. Regardless of where you sit, all ticket holders have access to the open-air car, which is standing-room only. With Clawson or one of his fellow engineers at the controls, all travelers have to do is sit back and let the scenery unfold.

The journey begins roughly an hour south of Colorado Springs in Cañon City at a historic depot originally completed in 1914. A few miles later, a dilapidated pipeline comes into view, running along the canyon wall just above the river. Completed in 1910, this eight-mile-long, mostly wooden structure provided water to Cañon City until 1973. As the train passes under the Royal Gorge Bridge, Clawson encourages me to open the cab window, lean back, and look up. The wind whips my hair as I stare at the bridge some 1,000 feet overhead. It’s 18 feet wide, according to Clawson, but from this vantage point, it looks like a thin wire that’s been strung across a gaping expanse.

As the train works its way through the river gorge, water rushes past and red rock walls tower on both sides.

Next, the train squeezes through the gorge’s narrowest and deepest section, the towering red granite walls growing taller and more imposing as they tighten around the tracks. Then we chug over the famous “Hanging Bridge,” which was an engineering marvel when it was completed in 1879—and remains one even today. At this point, the chasm narrows to just 30 feet wide, creating a bottleneck that required a little ingenuity to get a train through. Engineer C. Shallor Smith came up with a brilliant solution: He designed a 175-foot-long bridge that’s suspended above the rushing water by A-shaped supports anchored to the canyon wall.

Soon after, Clawson spots a pair of plastic kayaks hauled out on a small beach on the other side of the waterway. “We might get mooned,” he warns, referencing how paddlers traditionally greet the train when it passes. But when we spot the kayakers standing on the bank a few minutes later, they simply smile and wave. Clawson blares the train’s horn in response.

Eventually, the train reaches its western terminus, the town of Parkdale. Clawson hits the brakes and we jump down onto the tracks. Standing in front of the train up close, I’m struck by just how imposing she is. Thinking back to Clawson’s personality comment, I imagine her as fiercely independent—a self-assured, no-nonsense type of gal who’s not easily pushed around.

We clamber back into the cab and the train heads in reverse, retracing the route toward Cañon City. I relax back into my seat, letting the engine’s vibrations and the rhythmic drone of the wheels lull me into a pleasant daze. Outside, the rocky surfaces of the gorge’s walls alternate between light and shadow while the river glints in the midday sun. High overhead, I spot a turkey vulture circling on a thermal, its wings arching in a shallow V as it glides without flapping.

Clawson follows my gaze. “You can’t make this up,” he says. “Somebody might type into AI, ‘Take this picture of me and make it look like I’m in paradise.’ [But] all I gotta do is step outside."

NYC Votes to Cut Construction Red Tape in Bid to Spur More Homebuilding

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New York City voters overwhelmingly approved a plan to overhaul the city's notoriously complicated construction permitting process Tuesday, handing Mayor Zohran Mamdani a victory in his effort to accelerate housing development.

Question 4, one of five ballot measures approved in the Nov. 3 election, received roughly 68% support, according to preliminary election results.

The measure promises to simplify construction by bringing 40 types of permits and approvals currently handled by 18 separate government offices and agencies into one centralized digital system.

"New Yorkers voted for a government that moves as fast as they do," Mamdani said in a statement Tuesday night, adding that the reforms would "directly address the affordability crisis and help New Yorkers stay in the city they love."

However, the new system isn't required to launch until 2030. Even when it does, faster permitting will address only one of the many obstacles making housing so difficult and expensive to build in New York.

"No single reform will be a silver bullet for housing supply and affordability, especially in a city like New York," explains Jake Krimmel, senior economist at Realtor.com®. "Instead, each pro-supply policy change is a baby step in the right direction. And these marginal gains will add up."

The Big Apple needs approximately 700,000 additional homes over the next decade to meet existing and future demand, according to an August 2026 draft assessment from the city's housing and planning departments.

Yet developers filed plans for just 8,064 multifamily units in the second quarter of 2026, in a stunning 52% decline from the previous quarter, according to the Real Estate Board of New York.

That amounts to less than half the 17,500 new homes the city needs to add each quarter—and they're only proposed units, not completed homes.

Centralizing permits could help close that gap by cutting down the time and cost builders are forced to spend navigating city bureaucracy.

"Streamlining approvals can make a huge difference," Krimmel says. "It might not be the flashiest reform, but when it comes to new construction, time is money."

And permitting takes a very, very long time in New York City.

Of the nearly five years it takes to build a typical 20-unit apartment building in the Big Apple, roughly 1.5 years are spent securing permits alone, according to a September 2026 study examining construction timelines across 60 U.S. cities.

Now compare that to similar projects in Raleigh, NC, or Phoenix, AZ, where it takes roughly two years from application to completion.

"In a city like New York City with high land acquisition costs and a complex regulatory environment, predictability in timelines and financing can be critical for ensuring new construction can pencil," Krimmel says.

And research suggests those time savings could translate into more homes.

Cutting approval times by 25% could have increased multifamily housing production in Los Angeles, CA by 12.7% between 2010 and 2022, according to a 2026 study published in the Journal of Urban Economics.

When researchers accounted for additional development that faster approvals might encourage, the estimated increase reached 23.7%.

The question for New Yorkers, though, is when those reforms might translate into more housing options and lower rents. The median asking rent hit $3,785 in October—up 4.5% from a year ago.

Under the new rules, applicants would have one digital portal to submit documents, track applications, and obtain approvals, rather than navigating separate systems across multiple agencies.

But streamlining applications doesn't necessarily mean they'll be reviewed any faster.

In fact, the average time required to approve applications through the Department of Buildings' existing digital portal climbed from 8.3 days in 2020 to 20.3 days in 2025, according to a July report from New York State Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli.

And its hardly the only challenge. Krimmel says the overhaul won't address other barriers to construction, including zoning restrictions and the complexity of financing multifamily developments.

Still, he sees the reform as one step toward untangling a much larger problem.

"In the same way that a complex web of red tape and veto points is extremely good at creating barriers to new construction, real solutions will require the city untangles that web bit by bit," he says.

With the new system potentially four years away, the first test will be whether it actually shortens permitting timelines. Any corresponding increase in housing construction—and relief for renters—could take considerably longer.

As Mamdani acknowledged Tuesday night, "Now comes the work of turning these votes into results."
