Google's core updates arrive with a start time and a scope. Everything else you've read about them came from somewhere else.

The routine is familiar by now. Google posts a sentence. It has a name, a start time, and a note that the rollout may take up to two weeks. Within the hour, somebody has published a “what we know so far.” By the evening, there’s a list of winners and losers. Before the week is out, with the rollout still running, there’s a recovery guide in seven steps.

I’ve watched this for most of 20 years. The only part that has changed is the sentence. It used to be longer.

When Google launched what the industry later called Panda, in February 2011, they wrote a post about it. The post said what the change was for: sites that add little, copy from others, or just aren’t very useful. It said how big it was: 11.8% of queries. You could argue with the result, and plenty did, but there was a stated target to argue with.

Here is the entry for the November 2026 core update, which started on Nov. 10. It was released. It applies globally and in all languages. It may take up to two weeks. That’s the lot: three facts, none of them about your site.

Google has been open about why. In a Q&A on Search updates, it said it changes Search thousands of times a year and announces the ones it considers notable, about 10 a year when it last counted in public. Its core updates documentation adds that smaller core updates go out continually and aren’t announced at all. So a named update is a ranking change that Google decided to put on a calendar. The calendar entry tells you when. It tells you nothing about what changed, or about what happens in between.

The announcement shrank to a timestamp, and the commentary expanded to fill the space.

The date does have a use. Google’s own guidance uses it: wait a week after the rollout finishes, then compare that week with the week before it started. The date draws a line through your data so you know where to look. Visibility trackers do a similar job across the market; they show which sites moved.

What neither of them shows is why. Which system changed, and why it moved your site in particular, is known inside Google and nowhere else. A rank tracker and a strong feeling won’t close that gap.

That hasn’t slowed anyone down. An obscure tool vendor published a winners-and-losers study of the November update within a day or two of the launch. It gave the wrong start date and called it the sixth core update of the year. Google’s dashboard lists three. I’d say the analysis was rushed, but that implies there was something to analyze.

The closest thing I know to a core update is the annual performance review.

Anyone who has had a job knows the meeting. You walk in and get a rating that was settled long before the meeting was booked. It reflects the year you had. It also reflects the people you were measured against, because plenty of companies calibrate ratings across a team; you can do the same work as last year and drop a grade because three colleagues did better. Search works the same way. A page is ranked against every other page that could answer the query, so a site can lose ground without changing anything.

A core update is review day. It’s when the rating becomes visible. Nobody sensible leaves that meeting and asks what changed about the meeting.

The comparison stops in one place. After a review you get a manager, written feedback and a chance to ask what went wrong. After a core update you get a dashboard entry. That missing conversation is the entire market for update commentary: an industry of people offering to be the manager who explains your rating, none of whom were in the calibration room.

The strongest objection to all this is that the losses are real, and it’s correct. Companies do lose a large share of their organic revenue overnight, and people lose jobs over it. The practitioners who spend their time on recoveries are dealing with real businesses in real trouble.

There’s also evidence that sometimes the reviewer gets it wrong. In October 2024, Google invited a group of independent publishers hit by the September 2023 helpful content update to its campus. According to people who were in the room, they were told their content wasn’t the problem, and that they shouldn’t count on getting their traffic back. That is an awkward fact for anyone, me included, who says most affected sites had it coming.

Look at what it does to the recovery guide, though. If the content wasn’t the issue, “seven steps for improving your content” were never going to work. The publishers with the best case against Google are also the best evidence that nobody outside it could diagnose them.

Now the less sympathetic group, which in my experience is the bigger one.

Most of the affected sites I’ve been asked to look at over 20 years had the same history. Somebody decided what they could get away with before finding out what the market already offered. They hadn’t researched what customers needed or looked at how good the existing answers were. They picked keywords with volume, wrote about them, and launched.

When that didn’t work, they went looking for a shortcut, and social media had plenty. A thread with a promise that was too good to be true, and was. A “hack” from somebody who discovered SEO in the summer and by autumn had a conference slot and a YouTube channel. In office terms, that’s career advice from the colleague who joined in August and already runs a workshop. They copied it without asking why it would work. Some of these tricks do work for a few months, which is the worst outcome available, because the fall then lands inside an update window and gets blamed on the update.

Then they hired SEO to make it work, as if rankings were a delivery service for businesses that forgot to have a product. I’ve written about the wall that approach runs into; it hasn’t moved.

This is the employee who did the minimum all year and arrives at the review certain that the rating system has changed, and has brought printouts. In fairness to them, they have a problem that needs solving. “The algorithm changed” is something you can say to a board. “We built a site with nothing to say and then paid someone to get it found” tends not to make the deck.

So there’s a buyer for an outside cause, and the update coverage supplies one. It comes with a diagnosis nobody outside Google could have made and a retainer that renews before the next update can contradict it. The seller gets a client, and the client gets a story. The site stays exactly as useful as it was, which was the problem.

This trade feels legitimate to the people in it because of how the industry thinks about quality.

Take E-E-A-T. Google coined it for its quality raters, and Google’s own documentation says it isn’t a specific ranking factor. The industry read that and sold E-E-A-T audits anyway, with scores for a thing Google says it has no score for. Somewhere there is an author bio that was added to fix a business model.

The traits themselves are older than the initialism. Peter Morville’s user experience honeycomb had “credible,” “useful,” and “valuable” in it in 2004, as facets of a product, with no ranking attached. The industry needed Google to hand it an initialism before it noticed that people prefer things they can trust.

“JavaScript SEO” has the same shape: it’s progressive enhancement, which web developers were practicing before anyone gave it a conference track. I made that case in Stop Learning SEO and won’t repeat it.

If quality is a list of items to tick, then a core update is a new edition of the list, and you need someone to tell you what’s on it. There is never a shortage of volunteers. Back in the office, that’s working to the KPIs. The person who works to the KPIs has to know the moment they change. The person who is good at the job mostly finds out on review day that they still are.

So people who hold quality as a checklist need each update explained; the announcement explains nothing, and so somebody makes one up.

The first question a business could ask after an update is “What did this update change for my site?” Answering it would take the list of systems that changed, how much each one counts, and what every competing page did in the same fortnight. Nobody outside Google has those. You can still pay for an answer. It’ll arrive as a confident PDF with a traffic-light table, and it will tell you to improve content quality, show more expertise, and prune the thin pages.

Now read the PDF from the update before. It says the same thing. So will the one after the next update, because the advice was never derived from the update. If the remedy is the same whatever changed, the change taught nobody anything.

The second question is “what do the people I want as customers need that they can’t already get?” It’s a worse question to be asked. It has no villain and no deadline; it can’t be handed to a thread, and the answer may be that the business has nothing to add yet. It’s also slower and more expensive to answer than a PDF. But it can be answered by people you can find and talk to, and it could have been answered before the site launched.

The fair objection to the second question is Google. Google is in the business of taking in what other people know and serving it back as its own answer, and AI Overviews and AI Mode have made that the main event. The part of the web where people found you through a search result is being eaten, and Google is the one holding the fork. I’ve covered what that did to publishers’ traffic. So why polish anything for a channel that’s shrinking?

Because the second question was never about the channel. A site is a product, and what people need from it doesn’t change when fewer of them arrive through Google. What does change is how much of what you know you should hand over for free. I’ve argued that publishing everything is a terrible business strategy, and I still think so. Holding back the method is sensible. Shipping a site that’s a bare outline of what you’re supposed to be good at is a different decision, and it fails the customer long before it fails a ranking system.

The line between those two is thin, and it’s meant to be. It sits in a different place for every business, and no update, checklist, or thread will tell you where yours is. A search engine certainly won’t; it looks after its own interests, as it always has. The only people who know how much is enough are the ones you’re trying to sell to, which is one more reason to go and ask them.

In the office, it’s the gap between “what did the calibration committee change this year?” and “what did I do this year?” Only one of those is yours to find out, and it’s the one that doesn’t come with somebody else to blame.

Google will post another sentence soon enough. The recovery guide for it already exists. It’s the one from last time.

The data is very preliminary, but Vulcan failure may not be due to BE-4 engines

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Information about the Vulcan failure is being closely held by ULA and Blue Origin.

United Launch Alliance (ULA) confirmed Tuesday morning that its return-to-flight launch of the Vulcan rocket began failing during the initial stages of the mission.

“While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff, the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit,” the company said.

The launch company also confirmed reporting by Ars that the Centaur V upper stage and the solid rocket boosters performed nominally during the flight.

Critically, ULA did not provide any specific information about the “low booster performance.” This matters greatly because the core stage of the Vulcan rocket is powered by two BE-4 engines, which are provided by Blue Origin. That company’s own New Glenn rocket uses a similar but slightly more powerful version of the BE-4 engine. And New Glenn experienced its own catastrophic failure in May during a ground test, a failure that later was attributed to a main oxygen valve on one of the BE-4 engines.

Information about the Vulcan failure is being closely held by ULA and Blue Origin, who have only acknowledged that they’re working together to investigate the cause. According to two sources, the investigation is “incredibly complicated” and remains in the early stages.

However, according to preliminary data, two independent sources indicated to Ars that the problem is currently believed to have originated on the launch vehicle side rather than in the engines themselves. The issue may be related to systems that feed methane and liquid oxygen propellant to the engines.

ULA engineers are looking at the possibility that one of the two BE-4 engines started producing lower thrust shortly after liftoff and never ramped back up to full throttle for the remainder of the burn. Video observations from high-zoom tracking cameras appeared to show one engine slightly dimmer than the other after Vulcan jettisoned its solid rocket boosters nearly two minutes into flight. An asymmetric shutdown of the BE-4 engines could also have contributed to the sudden attitude motion indicated by ULA’s telemetry-driven real-time animation near stage separation.

The Federal Aviation Administration is overseeing the investigation, which is being led by ULA. Again, this information is preliminary, and no definitive conclusions can or should be drawn.

This was the fifth flight of the Vulcan rocket since its debut in January 2024. Two of the previous flights have experienced issues with the nozzles on its solid rocket boosters, but in both cases, the rocket was able to compensate for the reduced and asymmetric thrust and reach its intended orbit. The second nozzle issue caused Vulcan to stand down for nine months.

This latest setback is likely to sideline Vulcan for at least half a year, and probably longer. Most impacted will be the US Space Force, which has national security payloads waiting for a ride to space on Vulcan, and Amazon, which had more than a dozen launches of its Amazon Leo constellation on the manifest for 2027. It would exacerbate a worsening launch crisis in the United States.

There are potentially serious implications for Blue Origin as well, which is seeking to return New Glenn to flight as early as January. Last Tuesday, the company said a modified BE-4 main oxygen valve had successfully completed qualification testing.

“Our modified valve has already been incorporated into engines slated for upcoming flights, and we are now rolling it out across the remaining fleet,” the company said. “We expect to complete the anomaly investigation later this month.”

If Vulcan’s failure were ultimately due to a problem with the BE-4 engine, it would certainly delay the New Glenn rocket’s return to flight. This would be a setback not just for Blue Origin but for NASA, which is counting on the super heavy lift rocket to do much of the heavy lifting for its Artemis Program and Moon Base.

Bar Cruise Port Concludes Record-Breaking 2026 Cruise Season

Bar Cruise Port, which is part of the Global Ports Holding (GPH) network, has concluded its 2026 cruise season, welcoming a record 134,576 cruise passengers.

GPH said in a press release that this represents an 85 percent increase compared to 2025, marking a milestone in Bar Cruise Port’s continued growth and development.

The record-breaking season also highlights Bar Cruise Port’s capacity to accommodate increased cruise activity, since welcoming its first cruise call in 2016.

The press release added that with two quays measuring 290 and 200 meters in length, the port can accommodate cruise ships exceeding 300 meters, providing capacity for a range of vessels operating in the Adriatic.

Sedat Kara, General Manager of Bar Cruise Port, said: “The 2026 season also saw increased cruise activity, with two cruise ships welcomed on the same day on several occasions.”

“Looking ahead, the outlook for 2027 remains positive, with passenger numbers expected to grow further and surpass this year’s record. The results achieved this year mark another important step in Bar Cruise Port’s continued progress and long-term development,” added Kara.

GPH said that as one of Montenegro’s key cruise destinations, Bar combines its strategic location with a rich historical and cultural heritage.

The destination features a range of attractions, including the historic Old Town of Bar (Stari Bar), the more than 2,000-year-old Old Olive Tree of Mirovica and King Nikola’s Palace.

The nearby Lake Skadar, known for its diverse natural landscapes and rich birdlife, further adds to the destination’s appeal, offering opportunities to experience the region beyond its coastline.

Kansas City Current's grit earns them spot in NWSL Championship while Gotham's logistics problems linger

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

NEW YORK – The awkward logistics of Sunday's NWSL semifinal at Icahn Stadium, a quirky little venue sandwiched on an island in between Manhattan and Queens, were supposed to give way once Gotham FC and the Kansas City Current took to the pitch for one of the league's showpiece matches. When the Current's Croix Bethune opened the scoring in the 20th minute with an incredible looping shot off a free kick, the theme of the day was in motion. The league would make the best of the unideal circumstances with a top-quality soccer game, or at least with a few highlight-reel moments nestled into a match contested by a collection of the world's most talented players.

"I've been working on free kicks all year," Bethune said post-match, the Current justified in their decision to sign her last winter even if the validation was no longer needed, "and a lot more leading into the playoffs so just practice makes perfect."

Icahn Stadium's irregularities, though, seemed to reflect the game it unexpectedly became the host of six days earlier. The Current's 2-1 win came courtesy of a chippy game plan that would have been the case if the match had taken place at a traditional soccer-specific stadium but it felt especially at home at Icahn Stadium and amidst its elements. The teams played against the wind during New York's first properly cold weekend of the season, and Gotham faced a setting sun that impacted their vision during the first half. Bethune's impressive strike was really the only on-field spectacle to be witnessed on Randall's Island.

"We knew it was going to be a physical game, without a doubt," Current forward Haley Hopkins said. "Like I said a million times, Gotham's a very, very good team and so we had to be super disciplined in our structure and catch them on their transition and we were just able to put the ball in the back of the net when we had our opportunities."

The introduction of Gotham's Rose Lavelle, who has been dealing with niggling fitness issues over the last few weeks, did not change the smash-and-grab energy of the game. Much as there was a week ago in their quarterfinal win over the Seattle Reign, there was a dull quality to Gotham's attack despite their star power. Over 180 minutes of possession play, the NWSL Shield winners managed zero shots on target and 0.66 expected goals, their midseason marquee signing Sam Kerr managing no shots along the way and mustering just one attempt in her last five matches. To say they were unlucky would be a stretch but their lively start to the second half gave their failed attempts to put a shot on frame a comedic feel.

"I think we were kind of our own worst enemy today," midfielder Savannah McCaskill said. "I think at times we played into what they wanted to do and their style of play. They want a very chippy game. They want to get into tackles. They want to draw fouls. They want to break up the game. They want to play long and that's not our style. We want to keep the ball. We want to control tempo, and we like to play pretty football. That's [what] we pride ourselves on … but I think we need to find a better way to balance that out, especially in this league. How can we meet the demand of a chippy game while also controlling the tempo? I think we did a really good job when we played them at home the last time [a 2-1 win on Aug. 14]. They scored early but then we completely dominated the rest of the game and I think we failed to do that today and so I think that's really the determining factor."

In other words, it was the perfect setup for Gotham to equalize off an own goal from Laney Rouse, a week after benefiting from another own goal from the Reign's Jordyn Bugg. Those two goals would be the only ones in their postseason run, just as one might expect from the NWSL Shield winners and reigning champions who entered the playoffs as the favorites to win another title.

Gotham goalkeeper Ann-Katrin Berger then slipped into game-saving mode with two important blocks while defender Tierna Davidson did what she could to hold the Current at bay, attempting to create a villain arc rivaled only by Argentina's run to the World Cup final. Though the hosts had lucked their way into an even game by the 88th minute, the dark and cold conditions at a downsized stadium in an obscure part of New York meant the game beckoned for extra time. One final smash and grab was undoubtedly in the cards, though where it would come from was hard to guess – the Current had put in the work to win but soccer's chaotic nature had already weaseled its way into the game.

In the end, it was the Current's Haley Hopkins who had the final say in the 89th minute off of – what else – a set piece, a free kick that led the forward to finally evade Berger with a shot off the crossbar. After losing to them twice in three previous meetings this season, the Current earned their spot in next week's NWSL Championship (Saturday, 8 p.m. ET, CBS, Paramount+) on the back of a well-executed gameplan.

"As much as we tried to analyze certain teams in the league that have given them trouble, we really took information from the games we've played against them, all the ways they challenged us," Current head coach Chris Armas said. "There's been games even where they get high [expected goals], too much progression, too much in our box and we just tried to then look at all the scenes and moments when we made it hard for them so we just tried to take from yesterday and apply it to today, [that] type of thing. We gave up zero shots on goal in 90 minutes. The commitment from our players to just will themselves to it to stick to a match plan that was going to be largely about taking away spaces, controlling the middle of the pitch and if we can do that on the day – and and we did that by running after them, too, and pressing in moments of high intensity – we not only might stop what they're trying to do on one end and maybe force the turnovers that could challenge them so a little bit of a chess match there in moments."

The Current were no slouches, finishing fourth in the regular season and now on a  seven-game winning streak en route to next week's championship. Bethune's star quality shone on Sunday, while the team's record with two-time NWSL MVP Temwa Chawinga and U.S. women's national team standout Michelle Cooper is formidable. It did not erase the fact that they slayed a giant along the way, knocking out the Shield winners and a team that had picked up five major titles in four seasons.

"Gotham has just raised the bar around the league and in our country here, so the way they play, the way the coach sets them up, the talent on the team, the type of games they can put you in, you have to come with a plan," Armas said. "They're a team that, again, if iron sharpens iron type of thing, we try to push teams and we try to raise the bar."

Gotham, though, had not looked the part of the NWSL's gold standard team. Their drastic run, while currently headlined by USWNT players Lavelle and Jaedyn Shaw, is built on the back of an effective defensive structure that has carried an inconsistent offense. Lavelle and, in particular, Shaw have picked up much of the slack en route to the NWSL Shield and Kerr was supposed to bolster their attack after Esther Gonzalez's shock departure for Saudi club Al Qadsiah. They finished the regular season sixth in the NWSL for expected goals with 49.01 but had a genuine finishing problem, notching just 39 goals and ranking in the league's bottom six in that category. Head coach Juan Carlos Amoros is not sounding the alarm just yet but as they enter another offseason, the attacking composition of the team deserves yet another look.

"Of course, we want to create more chances, more shots," Amoros said. "We all do but we look at the way we play. We look at the way we create chances. We look at who does that. I think when it comes to these games, could we have [made] some better decisions? Yes, we did. We actually create some good moments. Yes, I wouldn't over-think about it. Something that I'm happy [about]? No but at the same time, we work a lot to make sure that those moments happen. So you know the set pieces, those situations, the open plays, final decisions … Definitely, something that we want to be better [at]. We want to score more goals. GTA 6 studio head Aaron Garbutt said that the most valuable lesson Rockstar learned ahead of GTA 5 was to kill its darlings, and "let what's best for the game dictate the way forward."

Speaking to Edge magazine back in 2013, Aaron Garbutt, who served as art director on GTA 5, was asked what the most valuable lessons Rockstar had learned from earlier Grand Theft Auto games had been. In response, he said that it was "mostly not to get locked down to anything, whether it's a process, a plan, a goal, an asset, an idea - anything."

Garbutt said the key was "to keep completely open-minded, adapt to the situation and always look for a better way forward, rather than stick with what's been decided previously. I don't mean chop and change for the sake of it, but let what's best for the game dictate the way forward. With a game that's as large and complex as this, the ideas need to be fluid, and you need to be paying as much attention as you can to every aspect to make sure they're working together."

His philosophy isn't exactly a brand-new one. The idea of killing your darlings - abandoning even your very favorite ideas if you find they no longer actually serve the project you're working on - has long been touted across the entire artistic industry. But applied to the complexity of a game like GTA 5, and its then-industry-leading open-world, the idea of knowing when to move on takes on increased importance.

Even back in 2013, Garbutt acknowledged that Rockstar was lucky to have an art team "that is extremely experienced at building open worlds," and was rarely stumped by brand-new problems. Even so, "there's always new things that come along [...] and the nature of how we approach development means that we need to be ready to adapt." For Rockstar, that's a layering approach, where the world is built first, and then missions, story, and game structure are added on top. "Each of these things influences the others massively," Garbutt said, "so each needs to adapt to the others. We are always playing and refining, adjusting each element of the game to play better with the others."

That approach puts Rockstar in a spot where even when it does do away with ideas that aren't working "we're rarely throwing away content." Instead, plans are adapted, and the studio is "constantly looking ahead with a clean slate, rather than sticking stubornly to old decisions."

It sounds basic, but Garbutt's idea here is a pivotal one in a medium as complex as game design. There's a though that few, if any, other forms of art are trying as hard to not get made as video games are, and when some ideas work well with certain features but clash with others, or take ages to actually implement (often at the expense of other systems), reworking them, or even abandoning them entirely, can be an important way to actually get projects finished on time. Sometimes that means that the grand ambitions of the developers don't get entirely delivered on, but if that means getting the game into players hands - or even avoiding having your studio shuttered around you - it's an important discipline to hone.

Talking Point: As A Parent, What Worries You Most About Gaming?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nintendo's guide aims to highlight the benefits

Over the summer, I replayed Ocarina of Time (1998) with my kids. I hadn't intended to, but it dawned on me that once the gorgeous remake landed, the original's visible polygons might not hold the same appeal for them. It was selfish, really, but I wanted them to experience the original magic as I did.

I held the pad but let them direct me, staying silent or feigning ignorance about puzzle solutions, letting them speculate and point out chests and switches I'd intentionally run past, involving them in an adventure they don't quite have the dexterity to control themselves.

I worried their attention might wander while watching for 30-60 minutes at a time, and I don't want to be that parent, forcing something I loved on my disinterested kids ("What do you mean 'Thunderbirds is boring'?!"). I was ready to pull the cord if they didn't take to it.

But no! They were pointing and problem-solving alongside me, jumping up and down, utterly engaged and loving the story and our shared experience.

Beyond the screen, Ocarina provoked laughter over dinner, and discussion about why characters reacted as they did, the cause and effect of actions, and the resulting emotions. Car journeys suddenly had a new soundtrack accompaniment, and the game's themes would come up while drawing or playing with Lego or finding a 'Deku' stick out on a walk. It became a rich source of inspiration for their imaginations, and a springboard to conversation and closeness with my kids. A fantastic family adventure we'll remember for years to come.

It's such a shame, then, when you see parents whose kids love games struggle to recognise the benefits and sheer joy they can bring, and potentially miss out on family experiences like mine. For something so ubiquitous and so fun, gaming can create real tension in the home and throw up generational barriers that take time and understanding to break down.

And with all the online concerns and other ills of the modern age, it's easy to see why some parents might perceive games as a dead-end, dangerous time sink that needs to be contained and quarantined, like fast food or social media.

According to a recent Ofcom report, 88% of children aged 3-17 in the UK play games on some form of device, and given Nintendo's evergreen, all-ages remit to deliver surprise and delight, addressing concerns of gaming-cautious guardians is something the company is keen on, beyond the bare minimum of secure account systems and Parental Control tools.

So that'll be why they've partnered up with Andy Robertson, founder of the Family Gaming Database and longtime friend of the site, for the recently published Parent's Guide to Gaming with Confidence.

Longtime Nintendo Life readers may recognise Andy from the various articles and reviews he's penned for us over the years, or from our partnership with Family Gaming to highlight accessibility information in our reviews and game pages across the network.

Produced in association with Nintendo, in this new guide Andy has also enlisted other experts in the fields of gaming, child psychology, and online safety to help parents of all gaming backgrounds digest the key questions and perhaps reframe how some of them view video games.

The prevalence of online gaming and voice chat worries me a lot. I don't want my kid speaking to strangers or picking up bad language or habits. Everything is so much easier (and tempting) to access than it used to be!

Available as a downloadable PDF, Andy explores common misconceptions and offers sound advice from expert contributors, including child psychologist Dr Amanda Gummer and Dr Catherine Knibbs, a child and adult psychotherapist specialising in online harm.

As you'll see from the breakout boxes on this page, uncertainties about other online players and predatory monetisation systems are the main sources of anxiety for NL staff with kids. "I'm very concerned about exploitative mechanics," says Alex, our senior video producer, "things designed to get their hooks into people's brains and make them dependent, or cough up wads of cash."

According to Ofcom's research, these are common worries:

The biggest type of concern among parents and carers of 3-17-year-olds who play games online is their child talking to strangers through games or via the chat functions (64%). This is followed by concerns about bullying by other players (60%), exposure to inappropriate content such as violence, bad language or disturbing material (59%), and pressure to make in-game purchases (51%).

Dr Knibbs highlights how "digital spaces can feel less familiar" than physical play spaces like playgrounds. The dangers can be less obvious, too, although she stresses that understanding the risks is better than avoidance. "When we learn about the content, the people and the spaces our children are engaging with, we are much better placed to help them navigate those experiences safely and positively."

It's not just about risk assessment, but also realising the potential benefits - and that doesn't require extensive gaming experience. "The goal ISN’T to become a gamer," Andy writes. "It is to better understand the people you care about most." You don't need to have conquered Elden Ring or any other game to participate and 'get' games.

Games with random or chance-based outcomes, especially with microtransactions that encourage you to spend money and try again, are what concern me most. As much as I enjoy it, Pokémon GO is a strong example of this (there’s a reason it was recently bumped up to PEGI 16), but thankfully, there are enough single-player adventures out there (Pokémon included!) that can minimise these often frustrating and FOMO-driven elements for younger players.

Another key point to stress to non-gaming parents might seem almost self-evident, but it's easily overlooked if recognising Mario and Pac-Man represents your sum knowledge of the medium.

For some, even now, 'gaming' remains an arcade-style competition for high scores, and that can create a disconnect between parents with preconceived notions and their kids, who are discovering that games can be so much more.

"A child who is creating, collaborating, problem-solving or connecting with friends is having a very different experience from someone passively consuming content," Dr Amanda Gummer writes in a section of the guide highlighting "one of the most common mistakes parents make" - that all games are essentially the same.

We can all recall 'Wow, games can do this???' moments when the scales fell from our eye. It might have been an emotional beat in Shadow of the Colossus, or a surprising Wii Sports session with grandparents over the holidays; whatever the source, getting the uninitiated to that point is a crucial first step. While it's all notionally 'reading', hopping between Wikipedia articles is obviously not the same as reading a poem or sitting down with James Patterson's latest or doomscrolling social media feeds or taking a crack at Ulysses, and once parents understand that the world of video games is equally expansive, they've got a foundation to build upon.

Andy takes time to break down what he terms "the myth of the isolated gamer" and how, in fact, games can bring families together. Living the Nintendo life as we do, multiplayer has been commonplace for decades, but the lone gamer in the basement/attic stereotype persists.

Likewise — and this has long been a personal bugbear when talking with friends and family — rationing 'screen time' feels like an oversimplification for parents guarding against passive media consumption. Lamenting how young people these days don't get enough fresh air is absolutely valid, and getting out in the park, playground, and beyond is crucial to a child's overall development, but it's not as simple as 'outside=good, inside=bad'. The storytelling, learning, collaboration, creativity, and deep experiences games can offer are enormously valuable.

As Andy points out, "understanding the quality of the experience is often more useful than simply counting the minutes." Bowling in Switch Sports, having the national news on in the background, diving into a Mario world, and throwing Bluey on Disney+ are all different types of 'screen time', each with their own benefits and potential drawbacks.

You may not have noticed, but Nintendo Life has grown recently. Behind the scenes, we've had new additions to the family, with two team members recently joining the ranks of Hookshot Media parentdom.

Across our editorial, video, and management teams, our collective tribe runs the age gamut from newborn to undergrad, so we've got a broad range of experience, making it fairly simple to gauge what's appropriate for our kids, suggest games they might like (or slyly leave them lying around to be 'discovered') while monitoring interactions and steering them towards the all-timers (hi, Ocarina) and, importantly, away from less healthy, obsessive patterns that we may have fallen into in our youth.

And yes, there are certain games, primarily targeting younger gamers, that we wouldn't install on any device in the house.

I don’t worry too much about microtransactions as I will maintain strict control over any potential in-game purchases. However, I dread the day that my daughter gets roped into playing something like Roblox. Substantial, well-made games can offer a lot of value for children, but Roblox is not an example of this.

Authorities in some territories are taking steps to mitigate the potential harm of the aggressive monetisation systems baked into platforms like Roblox. In the last week, the EU published its Digital Fairness Act proposal, which could impact any game featuring in-game purchases in the EU. Among other things, the bloc is seeking to curtail "addictive design", "dark patterns", and predatory monetisation tactics.

The DFA would only affect players in Europe, but regulation that forces real currency prices and lootbox odds to be displayed, those and similar systems defaulting to 'off' for minors, and parental consent being required to activate them are all positive steps.

While legislation could mean big changes for some developers and publishers, it's unlikely to cause Nintendo itself headaches.

With first-party software, in-game purchases are typically limited to DLC expansions or Switch 2 Upgrade Packs, and content-wise, it's a rare Nintendo game that I wouldn't feel comfortable playing with my kids in the room - something our teams over on Push Square and Pure Xbox have to be more cautious about, I'd wager.

My child is too young to play games on their own yet, but we’re having a blast with Mario Kart World’s Free Roam mode. It’s perfect, honestly: beyond the vibrant, fun characters, and chancing upon helicopters, lorries, and UFOs you can play as, seeing them gradually understand the geography of this vast world as you string one P-Switch mission to the next has made one of my favourite ever Mario Kart (and gaming) experiences.

Every kid is different, of course. Some may not be phased at all by a lurching ReDead in Hyrule Castle Town, while others might be kept up at night after glimpsing a creepy icon on the home screen. Handily, Switch's handheld hybrid format does make it easier to take the game away from the living room if, say, you need half an hour with Resident Evil Requiem away from eager eyes, and the console's aforementioned Parental Controls and accompanying app make monitoring the kids' gaming pretty simple.

Communicating to nervous parents not just the risks but the rewards is something we're passionate about on the team, and that's something that Andy's guide does particularly well. "Failure is a part of success," he writes. "Games create safe environments where mistakes are expected. This can help children build resilience without even realising it. Every setback becomes part of learning." Makes me feel better about all the times the dogs got me in Call of Duty.

Gaming with an infant is… difficult! (Try explaining that Fortune's Weave isn't touchscreen to a five-month-old). But I genuinely can't wait until my child is old enough to participate; as cheesy as it is, I think I'm going to well up when she picks her first starter Pokémon.

Confidence is a key term. Naturally, it's in Nintendo's interest to maintain its carefully curated family-friendly image, but giving parents who aren't embedded in the ins and outs of the gaming scene the confidence to engage is a win for the medium and everyone who loves it. A digestible guide like this for game-sceptic family and friends could come in handy over the holidays.

(To be clear, I'm not advocating becoming a gaming missionary at the dinner table and forcing pamphlets on relatives. But, you know, slyly leaving them lying around to be 'discovered' and having Switch Sports Resort ready to go after dessert? A round of golf and some bowling has worked wonders in the past!)

As with anything, a good grasp of the issues is essential, and every parent and child will have their own approaches, preferences, and ideas. For the NL team, though — and for me personally — sharing how games can enrich our family lives regardless of experience and ability is important. By alleviating concerns and highlighting benefits, we can open doors for new players and experience more of those treasured 'Oh, games can do this???' moments together.

The only thing I had a bit of struggle getting off was the smaller variation of the d-pad.

Honestly, I think the Rev5 feels great. Once you have a profile that suits you, this thing is just moulded to the hand, with each of its additional paddles easily within reach, and its physical customisation options making it feel tailor-made to you.

I love the travel of the trigger if going for long triggers, but it features a really satisfying mouse-click if you customise it to short triggers too. The TMR sticks do have that weightless feel that's typical for thumbstick utilising Hall Effect tech, but I adjusted to it quite quickly.

The easy profile swapping and customisation on the actual controller, and the feel in the hand, meant I was more inclined than ever to just use this thing for regular games, not just those competitive titles like The Finals or Modern Warfare 4. I actually played most of Zero Parades using this controller, just because it was comfortable to use, despite not using a single customisation aspect.

There is a pretty big flaw, however, regarding that screen – the lack of a touchpad. The button initially used to replace it is very small and not as easy to touch as I’d like when opening up maps or menus in-game. Of course, you can just use one of the other shortcuts for this if you’re so inclined.

However, it does mean if there ever is a game that utilises the touchpad, like Ghost of Yotei, for example, you’re kind of out of luck. Admittedly, there isn’t a huge number of games that utilise this feature, or have anything beyond a single-use touch, so most of the time, it’s a bit of a non-issue. However, it is something to consider if this is to be your main controller.

As is the case with these controllers – and the same with the last Nacon controller I reviewed – this thing doesn’t feature rumble on the PS5, meaning it’s severely lacking in the immersive department.

For a lot of people, that’s just not going to matter, but for me it’s an additional feature that always brings me back to the DualSense Edge – a pro controller with full haptics and adaptive triggers. What’s even worse is that there is actually a rumble motor in this controller, but it’s limited to use on PC.

I can’t knock the controller too much for that because outside of the DualSense Edge, most pro controllers won't feature any form of rumble. It’s just a shame because, for me, if it had rumble on PS5, it would be among my favourite controllers.

Despite my musing over a lack of rumble support on PS5, I do think the Rev5 is worth considering if you’re in the market for a pro controller.

It feels fantastic in the hand, with a satisfying click and travel to each of its buttons; the customisation is easy to change from a software perspective, but also incredibly accessible from a hardware perspective.

It features great build quality, the charger/dongle system is handy, and it comes with a pretty cool carry case. And you get all of this for about £20 to £30 less than most other pro controllers.

Is it perfect? No way. But it’s a pretty damn nice controller.

Swearing an oath of loyalty to PlayStation with the arrival of the PS4, our resident video-maker and Irn-Bru loving Scot prides himself as an expert in modern-day Sony. However, eight playthroughs of The Last Of Us later, and no Platinum to show for it, will forever be his greatest shame.

Seems I’ll stick with my Dualsense Edge, just can’t live without my rumble and haptics.
