Digital Asset Treasury Firms Continue to Buy Bitcoin and Ethereum. That's Why I'm Bullish on These Two Cryptocurrencies.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

These big buyers have slowed their purchases, but they haven't stopped.

Even after many of their smaller peers gave up over the past 12 months, the biggest digital asset treasury (DAT) companies are still buying Bitcoin (BTC -2.20%) and Ethereum (ETH -5.85%). When they do, it means there are fewer coins available for everyone else who might want to buy them. In the week ending Nov. 1, Strategy (MSTR -0.29%) bought 811 BTC, and Bitmine Immersion Technologies (BMNR -1.26%) bought 8,573 ETH.

Their pace of buying is down to a trickle compared to a year ago, but for the coins themselves, that doesn't stop me from being bullish about how they'll perform. Here's why.

DAT companies sell their stock, preferred shares, or debt to the market to raise capital that they then use to buy and hold crypto. When the market caps of these crypto treasury stocks fall beneath the value of the coins on their balance sheets, that plan stops working, and they often need to sell. Per Bitwise Asset Management's October 2026 report, at least a dozen businesses that launched Bitcoin treasuries in 2025 have since sold every coin, mostly to repay debt or return cash to shareholders.

Even Strategy, the pioneer of this entire business model, had to sell some of its Bitcoin to cover its needs.

In its third-quarter earnings report, it said it had sold $431.8 million of Bitcoin this year so far to fund the preferred dividends associated with some of its classes of shares, and also to fund stock buybacks for some classes of its shares. Strategy holds a total of 849,327 BTC as of Nov. 5, or about 4% of Bitcoin's 21 million coin cap. So far, it's still a net buyer despite making some sales.

Because it was still buying, the available supply of newly mined Bitcoin wasn't large enough to make up for the demand. Bitcoin mining currently creates 450 BTC daily. Strategy's hoard grew by 83,901 BTC in the second quarter, which was 205% of miners' output in that period. In Q3, its net additions shrank to 1,666 BTC, or about 4% of new supply, per Cointelegraph's tally of company filings.

Now, think about that classic diagram in every economics textbook which shows how (in theory) the price of an asset changes based on how much supply is produced relative to demand.

Strategy's purchases don't change anything about Bitcoin's fundamental properties, like its supply limit or how much miners can produce. However, it isn't the only buyer, and it controls a meaningful slice of the supply already. That means as Bitcoin's mining reward declines over time as a result of its halving, new buyers will need to compete with each other to secure a slice of their own. That's bullish.

Unlike Bitcoin, Ethereum has no supply cap, which means that DAT companies have a higher bar to clear in terms of exerting supply pressure before their efforts have a chance to translate into higher prices for the coin.

At the Ethereum network's current level of activity, the coin's supply increases by around 0.8% annually, which is approximately 2,764 ETH created on a daily basis. The network pays those new coins to stakers, while part of each transaction fee on the blockchain is destroyed to prevent the increases to the circulating supply from diluting holders much.

As of Nov. 5, Bitmine, the largest Ethereum treasury company, held 6,060,506 ETH, or 4.9% of the supply, and has bought more each week since June 30, 2025. In the last week of October, its purchases were worth 44% of Ethereum's net new weekly supply.

For Ethereum, the reason to be bullish is that Bitmine and other DATs buying the coin is just one avenue for it to gain in value. As its network activity increases, more Ether will be burned, putting upward pressure on prices. With an entire ecosystem of decentralized finance (DeFi) projects, services, and tokens that provide value, the chain has quite a few different avenues for growing network activity and subsequently boosting the price of Ethereum. That variety is one reason it can anchor a well-balanced crypto portfolio alongside Bitcoin.

Some of those projects in the ecosystem will inevitably flare out or become irrelevant, but even that process constrains the available Ethereum for buyers.

So, it makes sense to bet on the coin over the long term.

In Bitcoin news overnight, BTC USD price fell from $75,607 to $73,510 in ten minutes on Sunday evening, its lowest price since August 21. The worst of it came at 21:55 UTC, or 4:55 p.m. in New York. In that single minute, Bitcoin dropped 1.9% on Binance.

Most traders blamed Nvidia. Earlier that afternoon, CNBC reported that Nvidia’s board had opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang’s relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence agencies believe was passing information to Beijing (it’s just speculation for now).

So did the Nvidia scandal really trigger the dump, or did thin weekend trading turn an ordinary sell-off into a sharp one?

The timing does not fit a straight reaction to the news. CNBC published its report at 3:12 p.m. Eastern time on Sunday, which is 20:12 UTC. Bitcoin traded at $75,880 on Binance in that minute. For the next 90 minutes, almost nothing happened. At 21:44 UTC, BTC sat at $75,607, just 0.4% lower than when the story appeared.

Then the selling started, and it started in Ethereum. Between 21:50 and 21:55 UTC, ETH fell 6.9% on Binance, from $2,213 to $2,060. Bitcoin fell 2.4% over the same five minutes.

That pattern points to a liquidation cascade, a chain of forced sales. Traders who borrow to bet on higher prices get their positions force-closed when the price falls past a set point. Each forced sale pushes the price down to the next group of bets.

Weekends do not cause more crashes, though. Only 6 of the 29 drops came on a Saturday or Sunday, slightly fewer than the 29% of all hours that fall on weekends. What a quiet weekend changes is how far the price moves for each dollar sold.

Keno RTP by Number of Picks: What Changes and What Doesn’t

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Home » Blitz news » Keno RTP by Number of Picks: What Changes and What Doesn’t

This article explores how keno pick counts affect RTP, hit frequency, and volatility. Using probability calculations and simulated sessions, it shows why more numbers do not necessarily mean better returns, and how paytable structure determines your long-term expected payout.

Ask keno players which pick count “pays best” and you get strong opinions: one or two numbers for steady hits, ten for the big multipliers. Both camps are usually arguing about the wrong thing.

Return to player, or RTP, is the share of every dollar wagered that a game pays back over the long run. In keno, that figure is set by the paytable, and each pick count has its own paytable. So the honest answer to “which pick count has the best RTP” depends entirely on how those tables were built. On a modern crypto board, the answer is often “none of them,” and the more useful question becomes what the pick count does to everything else.

Below, the math runs on a 40-number board that draws 10, using openly published paytables plus a few hypothetical edits. Probabilities are exact; session figures come from 20,000 simulated runs.

Traditional casino keno uses an 80-number board with 20 numbers drawn. Each spot count gets its own paytable, and operators calibrate each one separately. Published estimates put the house edge in land-based keno somewhere between 20% and 40%, and it can shift by several points from one spot count to the next. That is why old guides compare 4-spot and 7-spot tickets so closely.

Online and crypto versions changed the design brief. Many operators now publish a single RTP for the whole game and then build every pick count’s paytable to land on that number. When that is the case, the pick count stops being a lever on expected return. A well-run bitcoin casino keno table that advertises 98% should return close to 98% whether you pick one number or ten. Rounding multipliers to one decimal means no table hits its target exactly.

Before any paytable comes into it, the board itself fixes the odds. With 40 numbers and 10 drawn, each number you pick has a 1 in 4 chance of landing. Your average number of hits is simply your pick count divided by four: 0.25 hits on one pick, 1.25 on five, 2.5 on ten.

The chance of catching at least one number climbs fast as you add picks:

The chance of hitting every number you picked collapses even faster. One pick is 1 in 4. Five picks is 1 in 2,611. Ten picks is 1 in 847,660,528. None of this says anything about RTP yet. RTP is the sum of probability times payout across every paytable row.

Here is what that sum gives for three paytables published on a live bitcoin keno page that states a 98% RTP:

The spread between them is 0.04 percentage points. Over $10,000 of total wagers, that is a difference of about four dollars in expected return. Pick count and risk setting do not move RTP here; the gap to 98% is rounding.

The same arithmetic tells you what a fair one-pick table would look like. One pick hits 25% of the time, so a 98% table has to pay 3.92x on a hit. If a one-pick ticket somewhere pays 3x instead, it returns 75%.

If RTP stays flat, pick count changes two things: how often you get paid and how hard the balance swings.

That last line is the surprise. Five picks on this table is almost nine times as swingy as ten picks on Low, even though both return the same 98 cents per dollar. More picks does not mean more volatility; the paytable’s shape decides it.

Breaking each table’s return down row by row shows where the money comes from.

On ten picks at Low risk, the 2-, 3- and 4-hit rows deliver about 90% of the entire return. The 1,000x prize for ten hits contributes roughly 0.0001 cents per dollar, close enough to zero that removing it would not change the RTP at the second decimal. Medium risk on ten picks moves the weight up a little: the 3- and 4-hit rows carry 74%, and the 5-hit row adds another 17%.

Five picks is different. The 2-hit row carries 42.5% of the return and the 3-hit row 34%, but the 300x prize for five hits carries 11.7% on its own, the same share as the 12x row below it. On a five-pick bitcoin keno game, the top prize is a real part of what you are being paid back. On ten picks, it is decoration.

Knowing which rows carry the return shows where a stingy operator could cut quietly. Two hypothetical edits:

On ten picks at Low risk, cutting the 2-hit payout from 1.1x to 1.0x would lower RTP by 3.1 percentage points, from about 98% to about 95%. Halving the 1,000x top prize to 500x would lower it by less than one ten-thousandth of a point. The jackpot is the row players look at, and the one that barely matters.

On five picks, the picture flips. Halving the 300x prize to 150x would cost 5.7 points of RTP, which is more than the 2.8 points lost by trimming the 2-hit row from 1.5x to 1.4x. So for high counts, check the small rows; for low and middle counts, check the top prize too.

Same RTP, very different evenings. Five picks on Low produces the worst typical session and the best lucky one, because so much of its return sits in a prize that turns up once every 2,611 rounds on average. Ten picks on Low is the slow grind. With crypto keno, where a round settles in seconds, 500 rounds can pass quickly, so choose the session shape you want before starting.

Check that your probabilities sum to 1 before trusting the answer. If totals across pick counts differ by more than a few tenths of a point, some counts are priced worse; avoid those.

On provably fair games, you can also verify each draw against the published seeds.
