Ten years after the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union, Brexit is back on the table. This time, though, London appears eager to reverse some of the barriers created by Brexit and bridge the decade-long divide between Britain and the bloc.

At a highly anticipated EU-U.K. summit in Brussels on Friday, British and EU leaders unveiled a modest deal to begin resetting the relationship. Under the agreement, the EU and United Kingdom will realign their carbon emissions trading systems; relax restrictions on food and agricultural trade—a major win for British farmers who have long complained about tedious export paperwork; and kick-start a “youth experience” program focused on travel, study, and work.

Although these are minor changes compared to the breadth of the 2016 Brexit framework, British Prime Minister Andy Burnham said on Friday that the deal could inject more than $22 billion into the U.K. economy in what he called the “single biggest growth measure we could take in this Parliament.”

“Today we draw the line on a decade of drift and division by reconnecting with old friends, by building a much more constructive relationship with our nearest neighbors and our largest market,” Burnham said.

According to a YouGov poll published in June, a majority of Britons believe that leaving the EU was a mistake and that London should move toward rejoining the bloc. Burnham has capitalized on this discontent, ignoring warnings from Conservative and Reform UK lawmakers who are against reopening the Brexit debate.

“It’s like the emperor’s new clothes—where no one is prepared to say the obvious truth,” Burnham said on Monday ahead of the summit. “We have lost more than we have gained in the last 10 years.” A National Bureau of Economic Research working paper found that by the end of 2025, Brexit had cut British GDP by 6 percent to 8 percent and investment by 12 percent to 13 percent.

Analysts suggest that global insecurity fueled by Russia’s war in Ukraine, the U.S. trade war, and energy market unpredictability have further convinced Britons that a post-Brexit world comes with greater instability.

“Our security and defence partnership, as well as our united support for Ukraine, are central to our commitment to address together the growing geopolitical challenges we face,” European Council President António Costa wrote on X on Friday, adding that the “EU and the UK are both stronger when we work together.”

It is unclear how much closer the bridging of ties will go. For every step toward dismantling Brexit, Burnham is expected to face backlash back home. “This is a total betrayal of Brexit Britain,” far-right Reform UK leader Niger Farage, a chief architect of the 2016 vote, said last month after Burnham floated restoring freedom of movement with the EU.

On Brussels’s side, though, the EU remains open to having “the closest possible relationship” with the United Kingdom, Costa said.

Missile incursions. Two Russian cruise missiles and a suspected drone entered Poland on Friday, adding to the growing number of reported Russian incursions into NATO territory in recent months. The incidents occurred while Russia was carrying out a massive overnight bombardment on Ukraine.

The intrusions coincided with European military exercises in Poland that began on Nov. 9; one Russian missile reportedly crashed only 22 miles from where the drills were taking place. No injuries were reported; however, Polish Defense Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz said it was “not accidental” that Russia’s incursions and the European military drills occurred in tandem. “We are dealing with an intentional provocation” by Moscow, Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk added. Moscow has yet to comment.

According to Ukrainian Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov, Russia’s nighttime barrage also triggered emergency outages across 11 Ukrainian regions as strikes on critical energy infrastructure forced three nuclear power plants to reduce generation. Russian attacks on Friday also reportedly hit apartment buildings, medical facilities, and bridges. Moscow’s Defense Ministry said it targeted sites where weapons were being manufactured.

Genocide ruling. Myanmar’s military-run government on Friday denounced an International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruling that found the country’s security forces guilty of genocide against its minority Rohingya Muslim community. The landmark decision, issued on Thursday, marked the first time that the court has found a country to be directly responsible for the crime.

“The court finds that Myanmar’s security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence, as well as acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to flee in large numbers,” ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa said during a readout of the 9-6 ruling.

Myanmar’s Foreign Ministry called the decision a “great disappointment” and noted that the judgment was not unanimous. It also said that the roughly 1.3 million Rohingya refugees in neighboring Bangladesh will only be allowed to return “when situations permit.”

However, Tom Andrews, the former U.N. special rapporteur on human rights in Myanmar, told Foreign Policy that a 9-6 result is still a “strong majority.” But he said that the international community must take additional steps to ensure that the country’s ruling junta faces real repercussions for its actions. “Because the thing is, if we establish that there is no consequence for genocide, then we can be guaranteed that genocide is going to happen again,” Andrews said. “And it’s only by imposing consequences that we can begin to see justice prevail, not only in this case but in future cases.”

Resetting relations. Chinese President Xi Jinping celebrated a “comprehensive turnaround” in his country’s relationship with Australia on Friday. Speaking on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, Xi said that Beijing is eager to set aside the two nations’ differences and expand cooperation in several critical sectors, such as artificial intelligence, energy, agriculture, and mining.

China is Australia’s top trading partner. However, ties between the Indo-Pacific powers began to deteriorate in 2020 after Canberra called for an inquiry into the origins of the COVID-19 pandemic, prompting China to impose punitive tariffs on Australian goods. In recent years, though, Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has sought to restore bilateral relations, especially as the future of his country’s critical iron ore industry comes under threat.

For decades, Chinese iron ore purchases have invigorated Australia’s economy; the vital resource was exempt from China’s 2020 duties. But with Beijing diversifying its partnerships in Africa and Latin America, where iron ore can be supplied more cheaply, as well as exercising increased leverage over iron ore pricing, Canberra is worried that its export earnings will take a major hit.

While Trump can brag about Xi meeting him on the tarmac in Shenzhen, China, this week, Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum has something she can brag about from her trip, too: going viral on Chinese social media. After spending the roughly 21-hour trip from Mexico City to Shenzhen (with a stopover in Tijuana, Mexico) sitting in a middle seat on an economy flight, Sheinbaum was greeted at the airport by a military guard and a dancing troupe. But they didn’t meet her at her plane—instead, she arrived to the welcome ceremony via airport bus.

Her down-to-earth travel style seems to have appealed to many Chinese netizens: A hashtag about her arrival was seen 10 million times on the Chinese social media platform Weibo, according to Bloomberg.

Microsoft ‘To Do’ users make a big to-do over popular task app’s pending retirement

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Todd Bishop on Nov 20, 2026 at 2:06 pmNovember 20, 2026 at 2:07 pm

Microsoft acquired the popular Wunderlist app in 2015, shut it down in 2020 and told its loyal users that Microsoft To Do would be their new home. Now they’re being asked to pack up again.

Microsoft plans to retire the To Do apps on June 30, 2027, moving personal task management for work and school accounts into Planner, its team project-management app. Microsoft disclosed the change Wednesday in a long Planner blog post amid the company’s Ignite announcements, and Windows Latest flagged the change later that day.

The reaction was swift and mostly unhappy. Tom Warren of The Verge summed it up on X: “Microsoft is killing its great tasks app in favor of its worse tasks app.”

That’s not entirely fair to Planner, which is built for a different job. But it captures the gripe: To Do was simple, free and worked across Windows, Mac, iOS, Android and the web, and nothing Microsoft is offering replaces all of that.

For work and school accounts, Microsoft says Planner “will be the destination for managing personal work tasks,” but “existing To Do tasks and lists will not migrate to Planner.” It says it will offer a way to export To Do data before the app shuts down.

Tony Redmond of Office365itpros, who tracks Microsoft 365 closely, cataloged the gaps: no file attachments on private Planner tasks, no equivalent for To Do’s list groups, no transfer of shared-list membership, assignments or permissions, and no native Planner apps for Windows or Mac.

He also noted the irony that Outlook’s old Exchange-based tasks will outlive To Do.

Why Microsoft is doing it: The company said putting individual and shared work on the same foundation lets it deliver more consistent features, and lets people connect their own tasks to Copilot and other AI agents. A new Planner agent, available with a Copilot add-on license, can take on tasks and generate status reports.

The Register’s Richard Speed put it less charitably, saying Microsoft had slipped the news into “this week’s Ignite AI puffery.”

Users weigh in: Brad Silverberg, the former Microsoft executive who led Windows 95 development and later became a Seattle venture investor, posted on X that he has used To Do daily, and Wunderlist before it.

“Very disappointed now,” he wrote, adding that he’s looking at TickTick and Todoist.

Similar posts piled up on LinkedIn from Microsoft 365 consultants and everyday users, some of them describing Planner as clunky by comparison.

Patrick Husting, a Seattle-area entrepreneur and software developer whose Ghostwriter add-in brought AI to Microsoft Office before Microsoft announced Microsoft 365 Copilot, offered on LinkedIn to maintain To Do for Microsoft for free.

Rivals smell an opportunity: Competitors wasted no time. TickTick posted a migration guide for To Do refugees. Todoist CEO Amir Salihefendic wrote on LinkedIn that the shutdown was “pretty unexpected” and pointed out that his app has outlasted competitors for 20 years.

The most pointed pitch came from Superlist, which called itself “the natural successor” and said it already has a tool for importing To Do lists. Wunderlist founder Christian Reber started the company in 2020.

Reber and his co-founders sold Wunderlist’s parent company, 6Wunderkinder, to Microsoft in 2015 for a reported $100 million to $200 million. In 2019, as Microsoft prepared to shut the app down, he publicly asked Satya Nadella to sell it back. Microsoft kept it, then retired it in favor of To Do.

Now some of those former Wunderlist users may end up back with the people who built it.

Russian strikes damage historic buildings in Kyiv and Odesa, including World Heritage sites

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

St Volodymyr’s Cathedral in Kyiv Photo by Wavemeart, via Wikimedia Commons

Multiple heritage sites in Ukraine were damaged by Russian strikes on 19 and 20 November, including the Unesco-listed St Michael’s Golden-Domed Cathedral and Monastery complex and St Volodymyr’s Cathedral in Kyiv, known for its frescoes, as well as 12 cultural heritage sites in Odesa, the strategic port city on the Black Sea, which was hit a day earlier. The entire historic centre of Odesa was added to Unesco’s list of endangered World Heritage sites in 2023.

At least nine people were killed in Kyiv and other regions of Ukraine during the hours-long attack on 20 November.

“Today, Russian drones damaged one of the most famous monuments of our country, the Volodymyr Cathedral,” Tetyana Berezhna, Ukraine’s deputy prime minister for humanitarian policy and minister of culture, wrote in a Facebook post on 19 November after the cathedral in Kyiv was hit, breaking windows and frames and causing “some damage to the paintings and structural elements”.

The neo-Byzantine St Volodymyr Cathedral, built in the 19th century, is under the jurisdiction of the Orthodox Church of Ukraine (OCU), which broke with the Moscow Patriarchate and was granted autocephaly by Constantinople in 2018. Three of Russia’s most famous artists of the 19th century and early 20th century—Viktor Vasnetsov, Mikhail Vrubel and Mikhail Nesterov—created religious art for St Volodymyr’s, as did the Ukrainian artist Mykola Pymomenko, a teacher of Kazimir Malevich.

The Austrian embassy, located next-door to St Volodymyr's Cathedral, was also damaged. Kyiv's municipal cultural heritage department published an inventory of damage from the attack, which also includes the embassy of the Netherlands.

A Russian drone was shot down over St Michael’s, which is the headquarters of the OCU, on 19 November.

Berezhna’s post cites the cumulative damage from Russia’s accelerating attacks: “Since the beginning of this year, the number of monuments damaged by Russia has increased by more than a third, and by the end of October it was almost 2,200. Almost 2,700 cultural institutions were also affected.”

The minister was in Lutsk, a historic city in northwestern Ukraine, this week to plan for a conference marking the 600th anniversary in 2029 of the Congress of Lutsk, which brought together the regional monarchies of the time. A Russian strike on Lutsk in October damaged Ukraine’s largest painting, Cosmogony, a 2,000 sq. m work by the contemporary artist Petro Antyp.

5 Evidence-Based Treatments for Borderline Personality Disorder

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

For a long time, borderline personality disorder (BPD) was considered one of the most difficult mental health conditions to treat. Some therapists even believed that people with BPD couldn't get better.

Fortunately, decades of research have demonstrated otherwise. Today, several treatments can help people with BPD manage intense emotions, build healthier relationships, and reduce impulsive behaviors.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) is probably the best known, but it's not the only option. Here are five approaches worth considering if you're looking for care for yourself or a loved one.

Developed by psychologist Marsha Linehan, DBT was the first treatment for BPD to demonstrate effectiveness in a randomized clinical trial.

The results were incredibly promising. Among chronically suicidal women with BPD, those receiving DBT engaged in less self-harm, had fewer psychiatric hospitalizations, and were more likely to stay in treatment than those receiving usual care.

Since then, DBT has been evaluated in dozens of randomized clinical trials. Its strongest evidence is for reducing suicidal and self-injurious behaviors, although promising findings extend to substance use, eating disorders, and depression.

DBT is based on the idea that people with BPD experience emotions intensely and may rely on coping strategies that provide temporary relief but create problems in the long run.

According to the treatment manual, comprehensive (full model) DBT programs include several components: weekly individual therapy, weekly skills group, and between-session phone coaching. In individual therapy, it is common to review difficulties that came up during the week and explore alternative responses. In skills group, patients receive didactic (class-like) instruction in four skill areas: mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness. Between-session phone coaching helps people apply these skills when they need them most.

Initially, Marsha Linehan recommended that people with BPD make a year-long commitment to their DBT program. However, many DBT programs now offer care for shorter durations (e.g., 6 months).

Developed by psychologist Peter Fonagy and psychiatrist Anthony Bateman, MBT has demonstrated improvements in BPD symptoms, self-harm, and interpersonal functioning in randomized clinical trials.

MBT focuses on improving mentalizing, our ability to understand our own thoughts and feelings and those of others. Rather than explicitly teaching coping skills, as in DBT, therapists help patients strengthen this ability through interactions during therapy.

For example, imagine leaving a session convinced your therapist was annoyed with you. Rather than simply reassuring you, an MBT therapist might explore what happened and what each of you might have been thinking or feeling.

The therapeutic relationship becomes a place to practice navigating emotionally charged interactions. Treatment typically includes weekly individual and group sessions over 12–18 months.

Developed by psychiatrist Otto Kernberg and colleagues, TFP has demonstrated improvements in BPD symptoms and interpersonal functioning in randomized clinical trials.

Like MBT, TFP focuses on relationships rather than teaching specific coping skills. However, TFP emphasizes difficulties integrating positive and negative perceptions of ourselves and others.

For example, someone with BPD might see their partner as loving and trustworthy one moment, but cruel and uncaring after a disagreement. TFP helps patients recognize that positive and negative qualities can exist in the same person.

Treatment typically involves twice-weekly individual sessions lasting a year or longer.

Developed by psychiatrist John Gunderson, GPM takes a different approach; the goal is to make effective BPD treatment accessible through generalist mental health clinicians.

Rather than requiring extensive training in specialized psychotherapy, GPM provides a practical framework for understanding BPD, managing crises, and improving everyday functioning.

Particular attention is given to relationships, work, school, and other meaningful activities. Treatment typically involves weekly appointments, with flexibility based on patients' needs.

Importantly, a randomized clinical trial comparing general psychiatric management with DBT found substantial improvements in both groups, including reductions in suicidal and self-injurious behaviors.

This work challenged the assumption that effective BPD treatment necessarily requires a highly specialized, intensive program.

BPD Compass is a newer cognitive-behavioral treatment my colleagues and I developed to make evidence-based BPD care more efficient and accessible.

We were intrigued that existing treatments all produce meaningful improvements, with no single approach consistently outperforming the others. This suggests that each addresses something important, whether that's emotion regulation (DBT), relationships (MBT, TFP), or building a meaningful life (GPM).

Yet many patients continue experiencing symptoms after treatment. We wondered whether nonresponse might reflect a mismatch between patients' difficulties and the treatment they receive.

We designed BPD Compass to address three broad vulnerabilities associated with BPD: emotion dysregulation, relationship difficulties, and impulsivity. In our clinical trials, treatment begins with an assessment to identify which patterns are driving a particular patient's symptoms, followed by an individualized plan emphasizing the most relevant modules.

BPD Compass takes 18 sessions, considerably shorter than many established BPD treatments. Like GPM, it was designed for generalist clinicians without requiring extensive specialized training.

In an initial randomized clinical trial, participants receiving BPD Compass showed large improvements in BPD symptoms. Subsequent research also documented improvements in emotional, relational, and impulsive functioning, as well as symptoms of co-occurring conditions.

BPD is an incredibly heterogeneous condition. Some people experience frequent suicidal crises and need intensive, long-term support. Others may make meaningful progress with a shorter intervention.

As a BPD treatment developer, my goal isn't to replace existing treatments. It's to expand the options available. Not everyone with BPD needs year-long therapy, and we should have effective, personalized alternatives for those who don't.

Ultimately, the best treatment is one that addresses your difficulties, provides the support you need, and is accessible enough for you to receive it.
