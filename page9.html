I’m a 70-Year-Old Woman. So What's Ahead?

Posted November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

I turned 70 in October. My best friend, who is two months older than me, kept telling me it is okay and that once I decide how I’m going to mark the day, my life will get back on track. I knew she was right, but as the weeks approached, I started to think about what 70 meant for me. None of the other decades had hit me in this way.

Is this moment a reminder? An opportunity to think about how to make meaning and how I want to live my life from now? Do I want change?

I wondered why I was reflecting on this, anyway. I figured that by thinking about it intentionally (that new overused word that I love) it must mean that there is some sort of change happening. An awareness of another passing.

I'm taking emotional stock of where I am.

I have a full life with my husband of 48 years, and we are both healthy. Big tick.

I have three children who are all partnered, settled, and have worthwhile creative lives. I have five beautiful grandchildren, from 15 to almost 3, and another due next year. They light up my life, and I love spending time with them. They are growing up quickly. Even the 3-year-old already has a full calendar.

I have friends and family in Australia and in the U.S.

So the question is, why would I want to change anything?

Then I realised that I was on the precipice of a new phase of letting go. My playbook, guided by the work of Edna Furman, to let go of my children at each stage holds, and I know at 70 I have yet a new bridge to cross. A vital one.

But what is this bridge? My children don't need me on a day-to-day basis; they are all separate, self-sufficient, and inhabit their own orbits.

Seventy is a recalibration. There is a sort of tussle with my own and my husband’s mortality. Not in a morbid way, but in a real way that I hadn’t thought about before.

It's a recognition that while we are okay now, a time will come when we go to the doctor and… I am constantly aware that many other people, including those much younger than us, must manage this reality daily.

I go back 12 years to when I single-handedly took my twin grandchildren to the park and chased them both as they went in different directions. I am very active, but could I do this now? I have trouble safely keeping up with a 3-year-old running down the street.

I need to reconsider how to find my new meaning. I turn to role models, those women who are the same stage as me. I find comfort once again in the words of my best friend, who has been a major part of my life for 58 years. She is also in the process of letting go of her children; we have talked about it incessantly for the last 10 years. While she has three daughters that she is very close to, and is available when really needed, she made the intentional decision not to be “the sleepover Nanna." Her artwork nourishes her, as does her time with her family. She refuses to rush, and she has found peace. Most of the time.

I think about how this can apply to me. I know I’d like to read more because it feeds my soul. I have a particular volunteer programme that I am going to embark on.

And I want to continue my work on letting go, mothering, and maybe some literature and film. I’m not sure yet.

The reality is my disquiet will lead me somewhere. It always does.

As always, I go to psychological theory to help me.

The words of Edna Furman come rushing back into my mind. They have guided me to let go, that others are "there to be left." I am faced with yet another new adjustment. The post I wrote last April needs an update.

Then I go to respected psychoanalyst Erik Erikson, whose developmental life stages offer guidance for psychosocial growth. He presents life clearly as a struggle between major turning points which need to be resolved before moving forward. According to this, I’m moving from middle adulthood, generativity vs. stagnation, toward later adulthood, which occurs at 65-plus, marked by integrity vs. despair.

How does this apply to me? I take this as my need to be generative, productive, and useful, while I move toward wisdom and fighting off the negativity about mortality and decline. I recall the mantras that I repeat daily: don't be a victim, no shoulds, it’s not about me, adapt and harness positive energy.

Then I imagine what journalist Abigail Shrier, in her “Tough Love” advice column in Free Press, would say to me in her loving, chiding way.

And just today I got the biggest reminder, which I’m going to bottle.

My daughter is spending the day with me as we both sit on the couch, do some writing, eat doughnuts, and laugh together.

Then my son drops in to do a call and comes into the kitchen and gives me a warm hug.

Then my other son texts me. Do you want tickets for a show tonight?

I feel so full and back on track for today.

Tomorrow I will be struggling again, but that’s okay.

The Golden Scale is a unique item in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake that allows Link to dive deeper. However, unlike the Silver Scale, you can only get the Golden Scale after becoming adult Link.

If you would like to explore the depths of Hyrule and hunt for all the treasures hidden underwater, you should learn how to get the Golden Scale in the Ocarina of Time remake.

You obtain the Golden Scale by catching a fish longer than 60 cm in the Fishing Pond. To complete the challenge, you must travel south of Hyrule Field to Lake Hylia as adult Link. You will see that the lake is dry, and you won't be able to reach the Fishing Pond, located on the right side of the map, on your own. However, if you get a fish that meets the requirements, keep it, and then go talk to the Fishing-Pond Man. Select the "I'm done" option and he will examine your fish, and you will receive the Golden Scale.

Is Your Sodium Intake Impacting Your Hiking Performance? A Dietitian Explains Why It Matters.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Take a look at the nutrition label on a freeze-dried backpacking meal, and you might be surprised by how much sodium it contains. Some meals pack more than 1,500 mg into a single pouch, roughly two-thirds of the recommended daily limit of 2,300 mg for most adults. That’s a lot for one dinner, but when you’re spending hours sweating your way up steep climbs, you’re also losing sodium along with water.

Sodium helps regulate fluid balance and supports normal nerve and muscle function. Yet there’s a contradiction in how you might think about it on the trail: you question the sodium in your freeze-dried dinner while adding electrolyte powder to your water bottle and snacking on pretzels, jerky, and cheese.

So, are you consuming too much sodium or not enough? The answer depends on what’s already in your food, how much you’re sweating, and how you’re replacing fluids.

You can pack nearly identical amounts of food and end up with dramatically different sodium intakes. I compared two realistic days of backpacking food, each containing commercial meals, protein bars, snacks, and other familiar trail staples. Both provide roughly 3,150 calories and weigh about 23 ounces dry, yet one contains 5,465 mg of sodium while the other contains just 1,975 mg.

That’s nearly 3,500 mg of difference before you add a single electrolyte packet. Some of the variation comes down to individual products: Mountain House Beef Lasagna contains 1,180 mg of sodium, compared with 340 mg in Good To-Go Pad Thai. Even switching pretzel flavors or protein bar brands can make a noticeable difference. Neither food bag is automatically better, but the comparison shows why it’s worth checking what you’re already carrying before deciding to supplement.

Health experts recommend limiting sodium for a reason. Consistently consuming more than your body needs can raise blood pressure, increasing your risk of heart disease and stroke over time. A particularly salty meal can also leave you thirsty or temporarily bloated as your body regulates its fluid balance. If you’ve been advised to limit sodium because of high blood pressure, kidney disease, or another medical condition, those recommendations still matter when you’re backpacking.

But eating a high-sodium diet day after day isn’t necessarily the same as eating salty meals during a strenuous backpacking trip. When you’re sweating heavily, you lose sodium you wouldn’t lose while sitting at a desk. That doesn’t mean every milligram of sodium in your food is necessary, or that you should add electrolytes without thinking about what you’re already eating. It simply means your activity and conditions matter when interpreting those numbers on the label.

Even if you’re hiking alongside someone on the same trail, you may be losing very different amounts of sodium. Sweat losses depend on two things: how much you sweat and how concentrated that sweat is. Research has found sweat sodium concentrations ranging from roughly 230 to 1,600 mg per liter. Temperature, exertion, clothing, heat acclimatization, and individual physiology all affect your losses, which means mileage alone tells you very little about how much sodium you’ve lost.

Here’s another wrinkle: losing 800 mg of sodium through sweat doesn’t automatically mean you need to consume 800 mg to replace it. Your body regulates sodium and water balance, and research suggests targeted sodium replacement matters more under certain combinations of prolonged exercise, substantial sweat losses, and fluid replacement. That’s why taking an electrolyte packet every hour isn’t necessarily appropriate. A packet doesn’t account for how much you’re sweating, how much you’re drinking, or how much sodium you’re getting from food.

Sodium doesn’t have to come from an electrolyte supplement to count. Salty snacks, meals, and electrolyte drinks all contribute to your intake, although when you consume that sodium can matter during long, sweaty days. Imagine spending ten hours hiking in warm weather, sweating heavily and drinking water but eating very little. At camp, you prepare a freeze-dried dinner containing 1,500 mg of sodium. That sodium helps replace losses and retain fluids during recovery, but it wasn’t available during the preceding hours of hiking.

Eating salty snacks throughout a prolonged, sweaty hike provides some sodium along the way instead of waiting until dinner. That doesn’t mean you need electrolyte powder in every water bottle. If you’re regularly eating pretzels, cheese, jerky, or other salty foods, you’re already consuming sodium during the day. On the other hand, if your trail menu contains relatively little sodium or you struggle to eat while hiking, an electrolyte drink may be a convenient alternative. What matters isn’t whether the sodium comes from a packet or a snack, but whether you’re adding something your food isn’t already providing.

It’s easy to assume that replacing the sodium you lose through sweat will protect you from exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH), a potentially dangerous drop in blood sodium. But according to the Wilderness Medical Society, most cases occur primarily because you drink too much fluid relative to your losses, often combined with your body’s tendency to retain water during prolonged exercise. Sweat sodium losses can contribute under some circumstances, but taking electrolyte supplements won’t prevent hyponatremia if you’re overdrinking.

The guidelines recommend avoiding overdrinking, generally by drinking according to thirst or using fluid needs you’ve estimated from experience in similar conditions. They also recommend having salty foods or drinks available during long, hot activities. Headaches, fatigue, nausea, and muscle cramps aren’t reliable indicators that you need more sodium because they can have several causes. If you or a hiking partner develops worsening confusion, repeated vomiting, seizures, or collapses during or after prolonged exertion, seek urgent medical help rather than assuming more water or electrolytes will solve the problem.

There’s no simple answer. Your sweat losses vary with the conditions and your individual physiology. Even knowing exactly how much sodium you lose wouldn’t tell you how much to replace. For context, 1,500 mg per day is considered an adequate intake for most adults under ordinary conditions, while 2,300 mg is the general recommended daily limit. Neither is a backpacking-specific target. A food bag containing 5,465 mg isn’t automatically excessive for a strenuous hiking day, just as one containing 1,975 mg isn’t necessarily inadequate. And since research hasn’t consistently shown that extra sodium improves endurance performance, there’s little reason to chase a precise replacement target.

The bottom line? Hike your own hike. If you’re curious about your sodium intake, track what you eat and drink, the conditions you’re hiking in, and how you feel during and afterward. You might notice patterns that help you plan future trips, without getting caught up in replacing every milligram of sodium you sweat out.

Googlebooks are a bit starved of desktop-class features at launch, but one of Google’s pitches around this platform is how apps can be built for it, and the company’s own employees are showing off what that looks like with some clever apps that just so happen to add features that feel a lot like they’re replicating macOS staples.

In a Reddit thread, three Googlebook apps built by Googlers through Antigravity are shown off, and they’re pretty cool, regardless of how you might feel about the platform itself.

“BentoBox” is a utility that gives you control over the status bar on Googlebook, enabling you to fill the status bar with shortcuts, mini-apps, and more. It’s full of glancable information such as weather conditions, battery charging speeds, and more, but also includes useful tools such as being able to access a clipboard history, set a timer, and more. The app requires permissions including accessibility overlays, but it works like a charm.

I had no idea the status bar on Googlebook could even be used like this, and it really makes that space a lot more useful. Of course, you have to draw a comparison here to macOS, which also uses this space for app shortcuts and tools, though BentoBox offers a different type of customization.

BentoBox is available for download via Google Play for free.

The other Mac-like app here is “Booklight,” a launcher that’s basically Apple’s Spotlight, just for Googlebook. It has a transparent design and lets you search “apps, settings, and the web.”

Technically, Googlebook already can do this in its built-in search bar (in the app drawer), but Booklight has more customization and functionality, including being able to write out shorthand commands all in one sentence with the app then completing the command in the appropriate apps, as well as having a clearer UI for experiences such as typing in quick math equations. It’s also good at finding symbols (so you don’t have to remember the U+ codes that carried over from ChromeOS). Not one I found myself wanting to use, but the potential is there.

Booklight is also supposed to be on the Play Store, but it’s disappeared in recent days.

Both BentoBox and Booklight were developed by Alexander Kuscher, VP over Laptops & Tablets at Google.

The other app highlighted in Google’s thread was Wayfinder, a file manager app described as a “desktop-grade file manager.” The big bonus here is network file access for NAS boxes and other uses, but the app also just has a more advanced UX compared to Googlebook’s built-in Files app.

Googlebook’s app gap is a real problem at launch, but these apps do show there’s a lot of potential in the platform, it’s just a matter of getting developers on board – which itself will require Google and its partners to actually sell some of these laptops… classic chicken-and-egg situation.

Csquare Reports Third Quarter 2026 Results and Business Highlights

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DALLAS, Nov. 9, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Csquare, Inc. (NYSE: CSQR) ("Csquare" or the "Company"), a leading provider of carrier-neutral data center solutions, today reported financial results for its fiscal third quarter ended September 30, 2026.

"Our third quarter results demonstrate strong and disciplined execution by our team and continued momentum across the business," said Spencer Mullee, Chief Executive Officer of Csquare. "We delivered strong revenue growth, record bookings and meaningful operating leverage while continuing to benefit from strong demand for our carrier-neutral digital infrastructure platform."

"These results reflect the solidity of our recurring revenue model and low net revenue churn, the strength of customer demand across our markets, and the operating leverage inherent in our business as Adjusted EBITDA margin expanded to 47.8% from 40.5% in the prior year period. Following our initial public offering in July, which resulted in $1.1 billion of debt reduction, our business is well positioned to continue executing on our strategy to deliver high-quality digital infrastructure across our urban core-focused portfolio."

"Demand for our offering remains strong and continues to accelerate. The strength of our platform is resonating in the market across traditional enterprise and higher density deployments as new and existing customers deploy and expand workloads on our platform. We believe our diversified portfolio, disciplined capital allocation strategy, and deep customer relationships position Csquare to continue capitalizing on a strong demand and pricing environment.  We remain focused on targeting high-quality customers, a diverse set of customers spanning multiple industries, and contracts that provide long-term, predictable cash flow."

RevenuesThird quarter total revenue increased 25.6% year-over-year to $302.3 million, compared to $240.8 million in the prior year period. Growth was driven by continued demand for the Company's colocation platform, customer deployments across existing and newly acquired facilities, and expansion of recurring infrastructure services. Revenue excluding metered power increased 22.1% to $276.2 million, underscoring the continued strength of the Company's recurring revenue base and customer expansion activity.

Commercial ActivityCsquare posted bookings of $66.1 million in the third quarter, representing the Company's 14th consecutive quarter of sequential bookings growth. The quarter was characterized by continued expansion of the commercial pipeline, strong pricing, and customers increasingly committing capacity further in advance of deployment requirements. Management believes these trends reflect ongoing supply constraints in many markets and growing customer focus on delivery certainty and long-term infrastructure planning.

Bookings were generated across enterprise customers, network providers, cloud-related deployments and higher-density infrastructure requirements. In addition, a significant portion of third-quarter bookings are expected to be deployed on shorter timelines than historical averages due to the utilization of existing capacity and targeted infrastructure upgrades rather than large-scale shell expansion projects.

Net Loss   Net loss for the third quarter of 2026 was $88.1 million, compared to a net loss of $29.8 million in the third quarter of 2025. The increase in net loss was primarily driven by higher year-over-year interest expense, IPO-related transaction costs, post-IPO stock-based compensation expenses, and costs associated with terminating certain pre-IPO compensation arrangements.

Adjusted EBITDAAdjusted EBITDA increased 44.3% year-over-year to $132.1 million, compared to $91.6 million in the third quarter of 2025. Adjusted EBITDA margin expanded to 47.8%, compared to 40.5% in the prior-year period, reflecting continued revenue growth, disciplined operating execution, and the inherent operating leverage of the Company's highly recurring business model.

A reconciliation of Adjusted EBITDA to Net Loss is included in the financial tables accompanying this release.

For the full year 2026, Csquare expects total revenues between $1.14 and $1.16 billion, Adjusted EBITDA between $490 and $500 million, recurring capital expenditures between $50 and $60 million, and non-recurring growth capital expenditures between $620 and $640 million.

The Company does not provide a reconciliation of forward-looking Adjusted EBITDA because certain items that affect the comparable GAAP measure cannot be reasonably provided without unreasonable effort.

IPO HighlightsDuring July 2026, Csquare successfully completed its initial public offering of 50.0 million shares of common stock at a public offering price of $21.00 per share and commenced trading on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker symbol  NYSE: CSQR.

On July 27, 2026, the underwriters exercised their option to purchase an additional 7.499 million shares at the initial public offering price. In aggregate, the offering generated approximately $1.16 billion in net proceeds after underwriting discounts and commissions.

The IPO represents a significant milestone in the Company's evolution and strengthens its ability to execute its long-term growth strategy. As a public company, Csquare believes it is well positioned to benefit from enhanced access to capital markets, increased visibility among customers and investors, and greater strategic and financial flexibility.

The Company used the net proceeds to repay outstanding indebtedness, significantly reducing leverage and strengthening its balance sheet to support future investment opportunities.

Adjusted EBITDAWe define Adjusted EBITDA as net (loss) income, excluding (i) income taxes, (ii) interest expense, (iii) depreciation and amortization, (iv) gain on lease modification, (v) loss on extinguishment of debt, (vi) bargain purchase gain, (vii) stock-based compensation, (viii) other income (loss), net, and (ix) transaction and other costs. The employee bonuses and loan extinguishment costs are recorded within Selling, marketing, general and administrative expenses in our Condensed Consolidated Statements of Operations. Management uses Adjusted EBITDA as a key measure of our operating performance and to assess the results of our business excluding certain items that we believe are not indicative of our core operating results. In addition, we believe Adjusted EBITDA is frequently used by securities analysts, investors, and other interested parties in the evaluation of data centers and other real estate companies. However, because Adjusted EBITDA is calculated before recurring cash charges, including interest expense and income taxes, which represent significant recurring cash charges necessary to operate our business, and is not adjusted for capital expenditures or other recurring cash requirements of our business, it should not be considered a measure of liquidity or an indicator of our cash flows and its utility as a measure of our performance is limited. Further, Adjusted EBITDA does not reflect our cash requirements or our ability to generate cash to meet those obligations. Funds from OperationsManagement uses FFO, which is a non-GAAP financial measure commonly used in the real estate industry. This measure is used by management to evaluate performance corresponding to the retail colocation data center industry which has similarities to other real estate type companies. FFO is calculated in accordance with the standards approved by the Board of Governors of the National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts. FFO represents net (loss) income (calculated in accordance with GAAP), excluding, when applicable (i) loss or gain from the disposition of real estate assets, (ii) depreciation and amortization and (iii) impairment write-downs of real estate assets and investments in entities when the impairment is directly attributable to decreases in the value of depreciable real estate held by the entity.

Management uses FFO as a supplemental performance measure because, in excluding the items identified in the calculation, it provides a performance measure that, when compared year over year, captures trends in utilization rates, pricing and operating costs. In addition, we believe FFO is frequently used by securities analysts, investors, and other interested parties in the evaluation of data centers and other real estate companies. However, because FFO excludes depreciation and amortization and does not capture the changes in the value of our data centers that result from use or market conditions, or the level of capital expenditures necessary to maintain the operating performance of our data centers, all of which have real economic effect and could materially impact our financial condition and results from operations, the utility of FFO as a measure of our performance is limited. Other companies may calculate FFO differently than we do and, as a result, FFO may not be comparable to other companies' FFO. Accordingly, FFO should not be considered in isolation or as a substitute for net (loss) income or any other performance measure calculated in accordance with U.S. GAAP.
