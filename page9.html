Here’s How the Porsche 718 Boxster EV’s Fake Flat-Six and PDK Work

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Porsche chose the 981-generation Boxster GTS model as its sound reference.

The upcoming Porsche 718 Boxster EV hops on board the growing simulated-internal-combustion-engine-and-transmission electric car trend. We just drove a prototype, and while there’s a lot to discuss about the car in general, it’s also worth honing in on the sound aspect. As you might expect, it’s rather pivotal to the experience behind the wheel.

The system that debuted in the Taycan is still called “E-Shift” here in the Boxster, and you can turn it on or off via a button on the steering wheel. Porsche chose the 3.4-liter naturally aspirated flat-six from the 981-generation Boxster as its muse. It’s a good choice, but also a bit of a surprise considering the spectacular sound out of the 4.0-liter flat-six found in the previous 718 Boxsters. But Porsche has its reasons, as Dr.-Ing. Sebastian Lefevre, team lead advanced driver assistance systems 718, tells us.

“It was a big discussion in our company,” Lefevre said. “From our point of view, the 4.0 engine is a little bit too soft in the sound. In our company, the 981 GTS sound, six-cylinder, was a little bit louder, and it would also feel more popular and familiar with our old customers.”

Once you hear the sound, there’s a good chance you’ll end up agreeing with Porsche. Lefevre mentioned the possibility of using the four-cylinder from the previous 718, but just as quickly shot the idea down referencing critique of the flat-four’s sound. How it reproduced the engine and exhaust via speakers is similar to how other OEMs with simulated internal combustion engines in EVs do it.

“A lot of microphones outside, inside,” Lefevre said. “And then we measured it all over the complete rpm range. And then we put it here, in this car, and measure it again. With the speakers, which are installed here. We have two.”

The most controversial speaker is bound to be the exterior-positioned one. More than anything, however, this exterior speaker exists as a sound maker for occupants to enjoy when the roof is off. It tries to emulate a mid-engine powertrain arrangement by way of the sound emanating from behind the driver and passenger; Porsche increases the volume inside the car with the roof removed, too. That’s a neat idea, but one that struggles to work in practice; I could hear some faint backfires on “downshifts” like Porsche’s ICE cars produce, but the experience is far from that of a real exhaust and engine echoing and reverberating off mountainsides or buildings.

Mercedes-AMG has the best ICE simulation around right now, with its AMG GT 4-Door Coupe going so far as to induce proper NVH characteristics by using exciters in the seats. The Boxster EV Prototype we drove relies entirely on the speakers to induce its hypnosis. But E-Shift brings a few theatrics to the table.

When lining up for a launch control start, Porsche pulses the torque of the electric motor to give a sense of an impending launch. It also lets you run the car into “redline,” tossing you forward with brake torque, just as it would in a manually-shifted gas-powered car. That said, Porsche took some liberties with the 8000-rpm redline depicted in this prototype; the 981 Boxster GTS instrument cluster shows a redline of 7600 rpm, so you have an extra 400 fake revs to play with in the EV.

Not as though this extra headroom matters, however, as Porsche chose to not simulate the torque curve of the flat-six. Instead, peak performance from the single electric motor is always available, and the car shuffles through its fake seven gears as speeds rise and fall. How it shifts is dependent on drive mode per usual with Porsche PDKs, and you can choose between manual or automatic shifting. You’ll even hear a tone difference in the engine noise over the speakers depending on whether you’re on- or off-throttle, just as you would were the real 3.4-liter be gulping in air behind your head.

For our full first impressions from behind the wheel of the 718 Boxster EV, make sure you read our prototype drive review.

Building a Yield Estimate You Can Actually Audit

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A pool’s displayed APR is a summary. It doesn’t tell a depositor everything they might earn. To estimate yield in code, first define what you’re measuring. Keep the inputs traceable and show the time window. This walkthrough covers a simple calculation and the checks that make it easier to test and explain.

“Yield” can mean different things. Pool-level fee activity isn’t the same as the return on a particular position. Incentive tokens may add value, and changes in the prices of deposited assets can affect a position’s value too. A headline number is hard to interpret without a clear scope.

Choose what you’re measuring: a pool, a strategy, or a defined position. Then choose a unit of account and an observation interval. Record whether the calculation includes fees, incentives, asset-price changes, deposits, withdrawals, and transaction costs. These definitions are part of the metric, not just documentation around it.

Neither label is enough on its own. A position-level result still needs a valuation method. Pool activity, by itself, doesn’t describe an individual depositor’s outcome. Keeping these concepts apart prevents a pool statistic from being presented as a realized position return.

For one observation interval, calculate a simple annualized rate from the period return and elapsed time:

Give the inputs explicit units. If both durations are in seconds, their ratio is unitless. The result scales the observed period return linearly; it doesn’t predict that the same rate will continue.

Here’s a small Python helper for that transformation. It expects the period return to have already been calculated using a documented method.

The helper doesn’t calculate the period return. That depends on what’s being valued and how cash flows are handled. Keeping the steps separate makes it easier to see whether an unexpected result came from the accounting or the annualization.

Keep the unannualized return and elapsed duration alongside the annualized result. Someone using an API or dashboard should be able to see the observed period, not just a rate that looks comparable across different windows. Set a policy for data that’s too sparse, stale, or incomplete to support an estimate. Return a clear status instead of a precise-looking number.

A yield pipeline is easier to debug when it keeps source observations apart from derived metrics. Store observations as received, then calculate estimates in a separate step. If the valuation method changes, you can recompute the estimate without rewriting the original observations.

For each observation, retain fields that show where it came from and what it means: the pool or position identifier, timestamp, valuation unit, values used, and the method or version that produced the estimate. If you track fees and incentives separately, say whether they’re already included in the position’s end value. Otherwise, the calculation may count the same value twice.

The names here are illustrative. What matters is that the estimate can be reproduced from its inputs and method. Store missing or stale data as an explicit condition, not a plausible-looking substitute. That way, downstream users and monitoring systems can see the gap.

Trading activity can help explain fee generation, but turnover alone doesn’t establish the return a position receives. Fee rules, position exposure, asset-price changes, and entry or exit costs all matter. Treat activity as a signal to investigate, not as a conclusion about yield.

A quoted fee rate and fees actually accrued during an observation interval are different measurements. Identify which one the calculation uses. Also account for the possibility that the price available for a hypothetical entry or exit differs from a displayed reference price because of market depth or price impact.

When ranking pools, don’t collapse these inputs into an opaque score too early. Keep component values such as observed fees, incentive valuation, costs, and elapsed time. If a combined score is useful, document its weighting and show the underlying components. When a ranking changes, developers can inspect the inputs instead of guessing what the score represents.

A comparison only means something when candidates use compatible conventions. Apply the same observation-window policy, valuation approach, and treatment of incentives. If a candidate lacks an observation for the chosen window, label that limitation rather than quietly switching to a different interval.

Version the calculation method and retain the raw observations. If a reported estimate changes after a code deployment, the method version helps show whether the underlying data changed or the calculation did. This is useful when reviewing historical values or investigating a dashboard change.

Use the same principle for monitoring. Alert on important input changes, such as a missing observation, a change in valuation source, or a shift in a component—not just on the final annualized rate. An alert that names the changed input gives a developer a place to start investigating.

A useful yield estimate doesn’t need to imply certainty. It needs to show what was measured, what was derived, and which assumptions shaped the result. For more background on Solana pool research, see the WealthVille Learn section.

Start with the definitions, then build the calculation. Keep observations and estimates distinct, show the observation window, and treat activity and quoted rates as inputs to investigate, not final answers. That makes yield estimates easier to compare, test, and explain.

Spring Boot 4.2 Is Out. What Will Change If You Just Bump the Version?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 24 with Mikhail Polivakha • Originally published at axelix.io

Hi everyone! My name is Mikhail Polivakha, and I am the technical lead of the Axelix project, an AI Native platform for Java development.

On November 19, Spring Boot 4.2.0 came out. AMQP 1.0 has appeared in it, executable JARs now start faster, and RestTemplate has officially begun its path to removal.

Imagine that you have a service on Boot 4.1 that works with RabbitMQ. You upgrade the Boot version, open your pom.xml, and there is still the same spring-boot-starter-amqp. It would seem, what is there to check? Well, in 4.2 this starter brings in support for a different protocol, and this way you can break production.

Let's figure out where this came from, what else may change during an ordinary upgrade, and which features in 4.2 I would try separately. We will take the official release notes as the basis.

P.S: As part of Axelix, support for the Spring Boot 4.2 release line will be added in the 1.4 minor release and will be backported to the 1.3.x release line, according to the support policy (a curious fact - ours is more similar to the Quarkus support policy than to that of Spring Boot, although we don't support Quarkus yet).

There has long been a funny situation with RestTemplate. If you were writing a new synchronous HTTP client, you were already advised to use RestClient. At the same time, an existing project could have ten integrations on RestTemplate, and the team had no particular reason to rewrite them en masse.

In Spring Framework 7.1, RestTemplate was marked as deprecated with the intention of removing it. Following it, Boot 4.2 deprecated its own infrastructure, including RestTemplateBuilder, RestTemplateCustomizer and the corresponding auto-configurations. If you have a client for some hypothetical catalog, the basic migration looks roughly like this.

Then, in place of a hypothetical getForObject, you will have something like catalogClient.get().uri("/products/{id}", id).retrieve().body(Product.class). But during the migration I would first open the configuration of the old builder. Where are the timeouts set there? Who adds the headers? Is there a custom error handler?

If the common settings used to arrive through a RestTemplateCustomizer bean, they need to be moved to RestClientCustomizer. Rewriting a single call is easier than later finding out why the new client behaves differently on a 404.

Separately, TestRestTemplate was deprecated. For tests of the application itself, there are RestTestClient and @AutoConfigureRestTestClient. Depending on the type of test, the client works with a mock transport or goes to an actually running server. And if you already have @RestClientTest with MockRestServiceServer for outgoing requests, use RestClient.Builder when migrating. HTTP client migration notes.

Here we need to take a step back and say how an ordinary executable JAR from Boot is structured, since not everyone knows.

By default, your classes are in BOOT-INF/classes, and the dependencies are in separate JARs in BOOT-INF/lib. The Launcher must be able to search for classes and resources inside these nested archives (essentially, a custom ClassLoader of its own is used there).

So, previously, during such a search, a URL connection was opened for each nested JAR being checked. In 4.2, the search goes directly through the nested archives. Plus, reading of compressed entries and resolution of cached resources were optimized. Less work and fewer allocations at startup.

Separately, sorting of auto-configurations and DevTools restarts were sped up (this won't affect production, but local development will be a bit snappier). Startup performance changes.

If you want to understand the effect in your own case, compare two built JARs via java -jar on the same JVM and with the same profile. Running from an IDE usually puts the dependencies directly on the classpath, so the path through nested JARs that we are interested in may simply be bypassed there.

In general, the first thing you should remember when upgrading Boot (and Spring Framework modules too) is that a minor release can and often will carry breaking changes. I wrote about this in detail in this article. There are specific reasons for such behavior, and a number of large projects that you know do this, from Axelix to K8S - all of them can break you in a minor release.

With AMQP, everything was simple for a long time. You added spring-boot-starter-amqp and got RabbitMQ support via AMQP 0-9-1. Now Boot can also do AMQP 1.0. And here the numbers are a bit confusing. 1.0 looks like the next version of the same protocol, to which the client will move by itself. In practice, these variants have different messaging models and different clients. RabbitMQ, meanwhile, has support for both variants.

Here is what this turned into in Boot 4.2.

If the task sounds like "upgrade Boot, leave our work with RabbitMQ as is", you need the first row. The spring.rabbitmq.* configuration remains the same. But if you imported Boot auto-configuration classes, for example in your own starter, check the imports. The spring-boot-amqp module for the old RabbitMQ integration moved to spring-boot-rabbitmq, and its classes moved from org.springframework.boot.amqp to org.springframework.boot.rabbitmq. Check the test dependencies too, because spring-boot-starter-amqp-test now also relates to AMQP 1.0. AMQP migration details.

And if you are deliberately going for RabbitMQ with AMQP 1.0, there it already has its own namespace spring.amqp.rabbitmq.* and its own health indicator amqprabbit. And one more little thing that is easy to get caught by locally. Without an explicitly set login and password, the client tries to connect anonymously. AMQP 1.0 settings.

Boot 4.2 introduced two settings that are easy to mentally merge into one. Let's start with transport. If metrics, logs and traces already go via OTLP to a single Collector, the common address can now be set once.

For HTTP, Boot itself will add the path of the corresponding signal, for example /v1/traces. An endpoint set directly for a signal takes priority. With headers, it's a bit more interesting. Common and signal-specific headers are merged, and if a key matches, the signal's setting wins. The common setting itself is used as a fallback for configured exporters. Common OTLP configuration.

Now imagine that the Collector receives data both from Java services on Spring and from services on other stacks. You want the same HTTP request to be described in telemetry by the same rules. For this, Boot introduced the opt-in management.observations.conventions=open-telemetry. Stable OpenTelemetry semantic conventions are supported.

In practice, I would first open the queries that dashboards and alerts live on. If they refer to old metric or attribute names, they may have to be rewritten. For example, when OpenTelemetry conventions are selected, the management.observations.http.server.requests.name setting stops affecting the metric name. So I would enable the common endpoint in one PR, and the change of conventions, together with the changes to monitoring queries, in another. Semantic conventions in Boot 4.2.

Let's say a service is available externally at https://example.org/billing. The proxy strips /billing before sending the request to the application, and passes this prefix in X-Forwarded-Prefix. The application itself handles a hypothetical /orders, but when it builds an absolute link to an order, an address with /billing/orders is needed on the outside.

And this very X-Forwarded-Prefix is ignored by default in Boot 4.2. Your test goes directly to the application, gets its 200s, and everything is wonderful. And the user behind a real proxy gets a link without /billing.

If the application really depends on the prefix, for Tomcat this can be configured like this.

At the same time, the new namespace is visible here. The server.forward-headers-strategy property was deprecated in favor of server.request.header.forwarded.strategy. The native value is called web-server in the new setting. If you leave both properties in the configuration, the application will crash at startup. In web-server mode, Tomcat and Jetty cannot apply use-prefix, which is why framework is chosen in the example.

One more point. Spring Framework 7.1 requires choosing a family of headers. By default, Boot uses X-Forwarded-*. If your proxy sets the standard Forwarded, choose server.request.header.forwarded.naming=rfc-7239. I would go and check this directly through the ingress, on some redirect and on a link that the application itself generates. Documentation on running behind a proxy.

I am almost 100% sure that there will be few users of any buildpacks among the readers, but I will still say a couple of words about them too.

Often in CI, a new runner is brought up for each build (this is not about the slave node for execution). Local Docker volumes work perfectly as long as the next build finds the same volume. If the runner is disposable, the next run starts with a cold cache.

In Boot 4.2, buildCache can be stored in a container registry as an image. One runner publishes the cache layers, the next one picks them up from there. There is support in both Maven and Gradle. buildWorkspace and launchCache still have only local options. Buildpacks cache in a registry.

And here are two changes that will also affect those who were not going to configure any new cache.

By default, the builder is now paketobuildpacks/ubuntu-resolute-builder. The base Ubuntu in the images moves from 24.04 to 26.04. If reproducibility is important to you, pin the versions of builder and runImage separately.

Paketo has an integration with Spring Cloud Bindings, which the buildpack adds automatically. At the time of the release, it can break the startup of an already built image with Boot 4.2. The reason is quite specific. Bindings looks for EnvironmentPostProcessor in the old package. The release notes suggest setting BP_SPRING_CLOUD_BINDINGS_DISABLED=true at the image build stage until compatibility is fixed. If the application relies on bindings, simply disabling them and considering the migration complete will not work. Buildpacks changes when upgrading.

If I were reviewing a PR in which the Boot version was simply bumped, I would first look at these three places.

RabbitMQ. If spring-boot-starter-amqp remains in the project, Boot will no longer pull in the previous RabbitMQ auto-configuration through it. In a typical application, where it creates RabbitTemplate, you can get an error at startup because of the missing bean. If the project directly uses classes that previously came transitively, compilation itself may already fail. For AMQP 0-9-1, replace the starter with spring-boot-starter-rabbitmq. AMQP migration.

An application behind a proxy. If the ingress passes the external prefix via X-Forwarded-Prefix, after the upgrade the service may start without any trouble but generate links and redirects without this prefix. Check the real Location through the ingress. If the application needs the prefix, enable use-prefix. For Tomcat and Jetty, use the framework strategy. Separately, check the settings from environment variables. If both the old and the new strategy keys remain, the service will crash at startup. Forwarded headers changes.

An image built by Paketo. Maven or Gradle can successfully build an image that will then crash at startup because of incompatible Spring Cloud Bindings. Therefore, in CI I would run the built image itself and wait for a response from the health endpoint. If the release incompatibility reproduces and the application does not use bindings, set BP_SPRING_CLOUD_BINDINGS_DISABLED=true during the build. Compatibility with Spring Cloud Bindings.

There are several more changes where everything depends on which settings you already have.

WebFlux and file uploads. A request with 51 parts is now rejected by default. The same will happen if a single part requires more than 1 MB on disk. Previously, both limits were unlimited. If you have file uploads on WebFlux, run a typical large request and, if necessary, raise spring.webflux.multipart.max-parts and spring.webflux.multipart.max-disk-usage-per-part. Multipart limits.

Redis master/replica. With spring.data.redis.masterreplica.nodes set, database 0 was previously always used, even if a different database was specified in the settings or the URL. Now the application will actually start reading from and writing to the specified database. If the usual keys suddenly "disappeared" after the rollout, check the database number. To keep the previous behavior, specify 0 or remove the number from the connection settings. Redis changes.

Tomcat and redirects. server.tomcat.servlet.use-relative-redirects is now true by default. If a client or a test expects an absolute URL in Location, check the response after the upgrade. The previous behavior can be restored with the value false. Tomcat settings.

Micrometer. If a dashboard shows the duration of operations that are still running, check it after the upgrade. Boot stopped creating LongTaskTimer for active observations by default. The previous behavior is restored via management.metrics.observations.include-active-long-task-timer=true. Micrometer changes.

Old deprecations. APIs and properties deprecated back in Boot 4.0 have been removed in 4.2. This is already a reason to check compilation and environment settings. If you use spring-boot-properties-migrator, it can now be configured so that the application crashes when errors or warnings are detected. List of changes when moving from 4.1.

By the way, two more items from the release notes. For embedded LDAP, enabling SSL without a bundle now leads to a startup error. And the Apache Pulsar starter is deprecated for now, as is RestTemplate support. For Pulsar, there is a guide on moving to the new project's starter.

Boot itself moved, among other things, to Spring Framework 7.1.0, Spring Security 7.2.0, Spring Data 2026.1.0 and Micrometer 1.18.0. If your build pins its own versions of these libraries, compare them with what is now managed by the Boot BOM. Updated dependencies.

As for me, 4.2 has a fairly characteristic set of changes. Of the new things, you can take the common OTLP endpoint, get the startup speedup together with the upgrade, and calmly do the migration to RestClient one client at a time. But in the version upgrade PR itself, I would first of all check what already works in production: which auto-configuration the AMQP starter brings, where the redirect behind the proxy leads, and whether the image that CI built starts. For each of these three scenarios, a green local test may say nothing at all.

Hackers are trying to get GTA 6 running on PC, Mac, and Linux

Grand Theft Auto 6's official release seemed like the end for apparent hacker Cyberleek, who raised millions of dollars in cryptocurrency by sharing unauthorized video of the open-world game ahead of launch. Rather than fading into obscurity, Cyberleek upped the ante by releasing an apparent build of GTA 6 onto the internet. And now, hackers, vibe coders, and programming enthusiasts are rushing to try and make GTA 6 playable on PC before Rockstar does.

Those following along might recall Cyberleek as the hacker who spent weeks provoking Rockstar Games. In addition to supplying the internet with leaked footage of the unfinished version of the game, Cyberleek also critiqued Rockstar and parent company Take-Two Interactive's business practices. The leaker claimed they objected to GTA 6's digital-only release as well as its pre-order bonuses. Cyberleek encouraged Rockstar and Take-Two to change course by dangling the possibility of sharing the files they were using to play the game to the public.

This suggestion was particularly shocking since Rockstar has not made the game available outside of console platforms. The game also doesn't exist on a disc, meaning that players don't have access to the game files. Without such data, players can't peek under the hood of the game — and they definitely could not tinker with it in unintended ways, like forcing the characters to appear without clothes in the overworld.

Cyberleek's motives seemed questionable, especially since the hacker gated the release of the alleged files behind monetary milestones. One of those early milestones included nude gameplay featuring the game's co-protagonist Lucia which successfully rallied fans to donate money. Once Cyberleek shared the footage, however, the hacker lost their leverage and the public moved on.

More recently, the hacker reappeared with a more appealing proposal: they would drop the apparent files free of charge. "The torch passes to the open source recomp and emulation projects," Cyberleek said on their website.

Polygon cannot confirm the veracity of these files; Rockstar Games and Xbox did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Yet even as some hoaxes related to Cyberleek seem likely, the larger claim about the game files appears to be legitimate. Since Cyberleek dropped the alleged executable, savvy GTA 6 fans gathering on Discord have shared steady progress on making the game accessible on Linux, Mac, and Windows PC. These initial attempts don't seem impressive, especially when the game barely functions, stutters, or displays rudimentary graphics without textures​​​​​​​ and lighting.

It would be a mistake to dismiss the reverse engineering projects though. The first big hurdle — getting the game to run at all — has already been overcome​​​​​​​. A few hours after entering the game for the first time, hackers managed​​​​​​​ to load some of GTA 6's shaders, which help the game get closer to its full visual fidelity.

Under normal circumstances, before artificial-intelligence coding assistance became readily available,​​​​​​​ incremental progress like this could have taken years for a game as complex as GTA 6. To be sure, it may still be weeks, if not months, before​​​​​​​ an unauthorized PC port of GTA 6 seems plausible. Given the enormous interest in the game — and the PC gaming community feeling slighted​​​​​​​ for being left out — a port may just be a matter of time. If it comes to pass, fans may also be able to access the game without paying for it.​​​​​​​

Rockstar and Take-Two, it seems, are determined to stop that from happening. At least one leading Discord server attempting to recompile the game has been banned, and the project leader has called it quits.

"I’m sorry," the former port overseer wrote on X.com. "I really want to make cool stuff but every single time shit happens that I can’t control so I’m stepping away."

Grand Theft Auto 6 is now available on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X. An official PC port has not been announced.

Dybala ‘proud’ of Roma’s Champions League return and claims: ‘I want to end my career in Italy’

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Paulo Dybala says he is proud to have helped bring Roma back to the Champions League, and hopes to finish his career in Italy.

Speaking to UEFA’s official channels, with quotes via CalcioMercato, the Argentine was speaking ahead of Roma’s league-phase trip to Paris Saint-Germain on Wednesday. Kick-off at the Parc des Princes is 21:00 CET [20:00 GMT].

The Giallorossi go into the game with four points after their defeat to Manchester United, while the holders have won all four of their matches so far.

“In my first years here, it was obviously a disappointment not to be able to bring Roma back to the Champions League. I also felt responsible,” Dybala said.

“In my first year, we came close by reaching the Europa League final, but in the years that followed we didn’t do enough to deserve it. Last year, though, I saw a truly determined team. We showed that by keeping clean sheets in the last few games, because we knew qualification didn’t depend only on us until the final matchday.

“It was a moment of great pride for me, because I could see how happy the fans were to be back in the Champions League, just like we were. We want to test ourselves against the best teams in Europe, and now we can.”

Dybala has stayed fit far more often under Gian Piero Gasperini, and he credited the coach’s approach.

“Physically, knee operations aside, I’ve felt very good over the last year, especially muscularly,” he said.

“The coach and his staff, including the fitness coaches who arrived this year, gave me a limited but appropriate workload in pre-season so I could always be available. So far this season, I’ve felt in good shape and I’m very happy.”

Dybala signed a one-year contract until 30 June 2027 in July. Now in his 15th year in Italy, after spells at Palermo and Juventus before moving to Roma in 2022, he made clear he has no intention of leaving.

“I never imagined I’d have a career like this, let alone spend 15 years in Italy. I’ve lived in the south, the north, and now the centre of the country. I’ve been here for almost half my life and I’m very happy,” he said.

“Italy welcomed me with open arms. My daughter was born here, so she’s Italian. Yes, I hope to stay in Italy for the rest of my career. It would be very special for me to dedicate an entire career to Serie A, as well as the year I spent in Serie B, which was very important for me.”
