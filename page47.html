Bitcoin bounces from 7-week low under $76K as Democrats sweep Congress

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin price downside reached a seven-week-low of $75,618 as 24-hour crypto long liquidations neared $300 million.

Bitcoin (BTC) sought to erase 3% overnight losses on Wednesday as crypto markets digested the Democratic sweep of the midterm elections.

Data from TradingView showed BTC/USD falling into Tuesday’s daily close as election results came in, with Democrats going on to win control of both the House and Senate. The pair dropped to a low of $75,618 on Bitstamp, a level last seen on Sept. 16, before rebounding as high as $78,039 over the next three hours.

BTC/USD one-hour chart. Source: Cointelegraph/TradingView

Data from CoinGlass showed the price dip liquidating late BTC long positions below $76,000, while a wall of asks kept upside contained below $78,000. Total crypto long liquidations over the past 24 hours stood at $288 million.

BTC liquidation heatmap. Source: CoinGlass

In comments sent to Cointelegraph, Kelly Ye, head of research at institutional digital asset trading platform Talos, said that markets had largely priced in the election outcome.

“Bitcoin moved about 1.7% after the polls closed, close to the roughly 1.8% options had priced, and there was no major leverage flush. The election was the main event, but markets had largely prepared for it,” she said.

US stock futures showed similar moves, initially falling before recouping some losses. S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 futures were both down by about 0.3% at the time of writing.

S&P 500 futures one-hour chart. Source: Cointelegraph/TradingView

Late Tuesday, Anthony Saglimbene, chief market strategist at financial services company Ameriprise Financial, told CNBC that concerns over data-center and broader AI capital expenditure were producing an adverse market reaction to the Democratic gains.

“If you get some governorships that are Democratic, what does that mean for the data center buildout, which has already seen a lot of skepticism over the last couple weeks in terms of the hyperscaler capex spending?” he asked.

In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, US President Donald Trump, who previously said he could become “Mr. Veto” to block Democratic policy initiatives, struck a conciliatory tone.

“I think we’ll work with the Democrats, and we’ll get along very well with them,” he said.

Bitcoin’s drop to $75,618 coincided with its 50-week simple moving average (SMA), a trend line that has held as support since mid-September.

In an analysis posted on X, Keith Alan, co-founder of crypto trading suite Material Indicators, identified the 50-week SMA as a line in the sand for Bitcoin bulls. Failure to defend it, he argued, would open the door to a “deeper correction ahead.”

BTC/USD one-day chart with 50 SMA. Source: Cointelegraph/TradingView

Crypto trader and analyst Rekt Capital, who had forecast BTC/USD dipping below $76,000 after a failed breakout attempt above $78,000, continued to flag overhead resistance on the three-day chart.

“If this continues then these newfound 3-Day Lower Highs may guide price back towards the ~$75.5k level below over time,” he said on Wednesday.

INTERVIEW: Carsten Cramer relaxed about Dortmund's transfer practices despite failing to sign Köln's Said El Mala

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MÜNCHEN (Bulinews): In Munich last weekend, Borussia Dortmund CEO Carsten Cramer stated the Westphalian club have not blown their cover after last summer’s prolonged transfer saga for 1.FC Köln star Said El Mala.

“From our perspective, we made a huge offer to them [Köln for El Mala] which maybe five years ago we wouldn’t have paid that much for a player,” Cramer explained at a round table event for which Bulinews.com was present.

“Numbers [in transfer fees] are changing at the moment, but we won’t pay ridiculous or stupid [prices] because we want to be a solid and serious club. No financial risks will be taken for successes.

The 20-year-old was heavily linked with BVB who, according to Sky Germany and BILD, offered fees of up to €50million but were rejected. In response to this, Cramer has no fears that the club’s historically cost-effective transfer methods have been exposed.

“We never mentioned the fee [for Said El Mala] publicly, so that [€50 million offer] is still a rumour. We just said we were interested to spend a huge amount of money,” he pointed out, casting doubt over their actual offer to the Cathedral City outfit.

“It can happen that [clubs will know Dortmund can now pay more for players] but that will not influence our way of negotiating. Everyone knows we are a powerful club, and we are a financially very healthy football club.

“We don’t need to hide our strengths. If we have an opportunity to spend money on a talented player which might sound a bit too expensive, if we are convinced, we might do it. I’m not concerned about [clubs knowing we can pay more],” he added.

Late last August, Köln would agree a contract extension with their talisman El Mala until June 2031. Could Dortmund plan another approach in the future? Cramer was quick to suppress the idea and avoid further rumours developing.

“We are happy with our squad; everyone is fully convinced with it. We decided to stop our negotiations with Köln because we had good ideas in mind and not only going down the El Mala route.

“It showed everyone that Dortmund is interested to make some special signings. What we have done with [Giannis] Konstantelias, [Kostas] Karetsas and [Joey] Veerman was really progressive from our perspective.

“We spent a huge amount of money for them, and don’t forget we have [Joane] Gadou who we hired very early from Salzburg. He may be cheap given his potential, but he cost money as well.

“No decision is done regarding how we proceed in the next transfer windows. We watch the market and there is no further comment regarding any player including El Mala, however his quality still exists,” Dortmund’s CEO concluded.

Manchester United came back from 2-0 down to beat AS Roma 3-2 in the Champions League on Tuesday night.

The Red Devils looked set for yet another defeat as Michael Carrick faced the pressure.

A second-half turnaround would ultimately take plenty of heat off the Englishman, although his future still remains in question at least for now.

The comeback could prove to be a significant turning point for Carrick, particularly after United's recent struggles.

But one result will not remove any concerns that have been had over the manager's abilities.

Especially as referee Jesus Gil Manzano may have had quite a role to play by not quite covering himself in glory at Old Trafford.

The start of Manchester United's comeback emerged from Matheus Cunha going down in the box, and a penalty being awarded.

The Brazilian looked to race through on goal, with defender Gianluca Mancini deemed to have fouled him.

However, on replays, while there was a hand on his back, the contact was not enough for the forward to have hit the floor.

And as you continue to watch the incident, you do then notice that Cunha had actually seemingly tripped himself up.

The hand on the back may have meant the incident did not meet the threshold for VAR intervention, but it should never have been given as a penalty in the first place.

The next blunder would not come too long after the decision to award Manchester United a penalty.

As Bruno Fernandes stepped up to take the spot-kick, the Portuguese midfielder would bluff his lines, with goalkeeper Devis Vazquez saving the attempt.

Manchester United would then find a stroke of luck, with the penalty ordered to be retaken due to encroachment.

However, encroachment rules state there must be a material impact on the penalty for it to come into effect.

In this incident, those who had entered the box early were on the opposite side of the box to where the penalty was saved.

And were nowhere to be seen when the ball was cleared following the save.

They had no impact on the outcome, meaning the first penalty attempt should have stood.

Man United probably should have had another penalty late on as well, with Shea Lacey fouled inside the box.

As the United academy product attempted to cross the ball, a late challenge wiped him out inside the area by Roma defender Wesley.

The incident was completely waved off, with the score at the time being 2-2.

United would eventually end up getting that much-needed third goal anyway.

Harvey to Bring Jus Mundi’s Arbitration Agent Into Legal Workflows

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Harvey announced on November 4, 2026 a partnership to integrate Jus Mundi’s Jus AI arbitration agent into its Assistant, Agents, Vault and Workflows products. The integration is intended for international arbitration and cross-border disputes teams, with global availability to joint customers planned by the end of 2026 at no additional cost.

The integration is designed to place specialist research beside the matter record. Once activated, a lawyer evaluating a client’s position could draw on applicable awards, treaty practice and institutional rules, then test the resulting research against documents in the matter’s Harvey Vault. The matter remains in Harvey, and findings can be carried into a memo, pleading or witness-preparation outline. Each authority links back to its source document on Jus Mundi’s platform.

Those results can be reviewed in the context of the matter’s pleadings, exhibits and transcripts. Pinpoint citations support checking the underlying material before an answer is presented to a tribunal. Recurring disputes work covered by the integration includes preparing a Request for Arbitration, checking authorities while drafting, comparing exhibit bundles with precedent and identifying clauses that rely on superseded international rules.

Jus Mundi’s source base spans investment and commercial awards, decisions, treaties, procedural materials and publications. Content relationships include the IBA and arbitral institutions including the ICC, AAA-ICDR, LCIA, SIAC, HKIAC, DIAC and Court of Arbitration for Sport. Secondary materials include the ICC Dispute Resolution Bulletin, ICSID Review and Redfern and Hunter on International Arbitration.

Jus Mundi’s Jus AI product page says Jus Mundi Data contains more than 125,000 case documents, awards and leading publications through more than 110 exclusive institutional and publisher partnerships. Its My Sources feature can search and analyze up to 10,000 user documents, with files secured and encrypted in a private Vault.

Jus AI runs on Jus AI Tenet V5, which Jus Mundi describes as a proprietary large language model trained on arbitration and international law. Users can choose four reasoning modes — Auto, Light, Think and Deep — and inspect reasoning steps, check citations and follow links back to sources.

The integration will operate within Harvey’s existing security, confidentiality and data-governance controls. Jus Mundi separately lists ISO 27001 and ISO 42001 certifications and a SOC 2 Type II attestation for Jus AI. The security provisions are central to a workflow that combines outside research with confidential case materials and private user documents.

Harvey Chief Business Officer John Haddock said the partnership responds to clients seeking deep domain expertise inside the platform where they manage important matters. Emmanuel Jacomy, a partner at A&O Shearman, said bringing the tools together would give arbitration teams a new way of working, with more time for client advocacy and strategic tasks.

For arbitration teams, the key change is where the work happens: source research, citation checking and drafting are designed to share one matter context, reducing the handoffs between a specialist research platform and the case file.

Hill-Climbing Agent Quality with Agents CLI Evaluation

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A live walkthrough of the Agents CLI eval loop, from a first run to regression tracking, model-backend experiments, and automated alerting

Building an agent that works on a demo is a different problem from building an agent that stays working across model upgrades, tool changes, and new conversation shapes. The first takes a weekend; the second takes a loop. A team writes test cases, runs them, grades them, reads what failed, changes the agent, and runs the cases again. If the score goes up, the change was good. If it goes down, revert. Over weeks of this, the agent inches toward the ceiling of what the model, tools, and prompt can do together. The quality-engineering name for this is hill-climbing.

The Agents CLI eval namespace was built around this loop. The commands are small and framework-agnostic: eval generate runs the agent against a dataset of cases, eval grade scores the generated traces, eval compare diffs two run results side by side, eval analyze clusters failures into themes, and eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt.

Below: baseline → regression → model swap → scheduling and alerting.

The scaffold ships with an eval dataset at tests/eval/datasets/basic-dataset.json, a config at tests/eval/eval_config.yaml, and a custom LLM-as-judge metric at tests/eval/response_quality.py. The config declares the metric to run:

The metric grades each response on a 1-to-5 scale for accuracy, relevance, and clarity, using gemini-3.8-flash as the judge. The default dataset has a single trivial case, which is not enough for a hill-climb. The first thing we do is expand it to four cases that probe different behaviors:

Four cases: a greeting, a weather question the mock tool handles with the hardcoded foggy response, a weather question for a city the tool defaults on, and an off-topic question that the agent has to decide how to handle. This is a small dataset by design; the hill-climbing loop pays off when the dataset is small enough to run on every change.

With the dataset in place, we run eval generate to drive the agent against each case, then eval grade to score the resulting traces:

Mean 4.0 out of 5 across four cases, with a standard deviation of 1.41. That stdev tells us immediately that the cases are not scoring uniformly, which is a healthier starting point than a flat 5.0 across the board. A flat 5.0 either means the agent is perfect (unlikely) or the dataset is too easy to show variation. 4.0 with real spread is a hill to climb.

To demonstrate the loop, we introduce a realistic regression. A developer working across several agent projects accidentally pastes the wrong system prompt into our weather agent: a chef persona meant for a different codebase. In app/agent.py:

Mean 2.75, down from 4.0. The judge agrees with our intuition that a chef persona on a weather agent is a worse product than a general-purpose helpful assistant. The stdev also tightened from 1.41 to 0.96, consistent with the chef prompt pushing most cases toward the same mediocre range.

A 1.25-point drop on a 1-to-5 scale is substantial, and the kind of regression that can slip through code review when the diff looks small ("one string literal changed"). The loop surfaced the change at all, with a specific number attached to it, before the agent reached production.

Mean scores are a start; the per-case diff is where the hill-climb happens. eval compare reads two result files and produces a structured JSON diff:

The differences object has the full per-case breakdown with the baseline and candidate values side by side. For a hill-climbing workflow, we typically pipe the output through jq or a small Python script to extract the signal; the raw JSON is the complete evidence.

Walking the two result files and lining up the scores per case shows where the regression landed:

Three cases dropped, one held. The most important of the three is sf_weather: the chef persona pulled the model's attention away from the registered get_weather tool, so the agent stopped calling it and started making up weather values. Unit tests can't catch this: the code runs, the tool is still registered, but the model's behavior around it has shifted. Only an end-to-end eval that scores the response in context surfaces this.

The mean drop by itself (−1.25) is a clear signal, but the per-case breakdown is what tells the developer where to look. A one-line system-prompt change propagated through three different cases in three different ways: greeting lost a point on tone, sf_weather lost two on tool usage, off_topic lost two on scope creep.

A common hill-climbing question is whether a cheaper or faster model would do the job acceptably. For the LangChain template, the model is defined in app/agent.py:

We swap gemini-3.8-flash for gemini-2.5-flash (restoring the helpful system prompt first), re-run eval generate plus eval grade, and compare the two result files. The mean drops from 4.00 to 2.75, the same magnitude as the chef-prompt regression, but the per-case breakdown tells a completely different story:

The cheaper model matched 3.8-flash exactly on the greeting and rome_weather cases and lost a point on sf_weather (slightly less polished phrasing). The whole visible drop traces to off_topic, where the cheaper model refused with a hedge about the event "not having happened yet" (a training-cutoff artifact), and the judge marked it harshly.

The lesson is that the summary number is misleading. Look only at the mean and you'd call gemini-2.5-flash as bad as the chef prompt. Look at the per-case breakdown and you'd call it a reasonable swap for most of the agent's workload, with one case (handling out-of-scope questions about recent events) exposing a real limitation. If the production distribution of user questions matches the first three cases more than the fourth, the swap is worth considering. If off_topic-style questions are common, it is not.

Two caveats on this experiment. First, model swaps interact with the prompt: a prompt tuned for a stronger model often leaks capability when ported to a weaker one, so the right comparison is "weaker model with a prompt tuned for it" versus "stronger model with its own tuned prompt." Second, the Vertex AI Eval Service calls a judge model to grade the traces, and the judge is held constant across the comparison. Changing the judge is a different experiment and should be done separately.

Agents CLI does not ship a scheduler, but the eval commands are designed to drop into standard infrastructure. The pattern we can use here is a cron job or Cloud Scheduler trigger that runs eval generate plus eval grade on a cadence and writes the results to a persistent location:

A nightly run against a stable regression dataset catches drift from model updates, data changes in the agent's tools, and dependencies that shift under the agent's feet. For a production agent, the scheduled run also captures the model vendor's quiet upgrades, which can move the agent's behavior without any change on our side. The dataset for the scheduled run is typically larger than the one used in the inner loop (dozens to hundreds of cases rather than a few), since it only runs once a day.

The scheduled run produces a result file every day, but a file nobody looks at is not an alert. The pattern here can be a wrapper script that compares each new run's mean against a baseline (the previous run, or a committed known-good) and exits non-zero when the drop exceeds a threshold. The non-zero exit is what triggers the alert from the surrounding scheduler.

And against an identical pair (self-compare):

The non-zero exit code is what a cron job, Cloud Scheduler, or GitHub Actions step picks up to send the alert.

For ADK projects, eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt using the GEPA framework (not currently supported for the LangChain extension). The command takes a dataset and a target metric, proposes prompt variants, grades each one, keeps the variants that improve the metric, and iterates. The output is a set of candidate prompts ranked by their eval score, which a human then reviews and promotes to the agent definition.

Two things to understand about eval optimize. First, it is scoped to prompt optimization, not to arbitrary agent changes: the agent's tools, structure, and model stay fixed. Second, GEPA sometimes finds genuine improvements and sometimes finds prompt variants that score higher on the specific metric by overfitting to its wording. The reviewer is the control. For projects where the loop applies, the discipline is to run eval optimize on the regression dataset (where improvements are measurable) and to review the candidate prompts carefully before promotion.

A few patterns and anti-patterns from running this loop ourselves and watching teams do the same.

Lead with a small, representative dataset, not a comprehensive one. The temptation when starting an eval is to try to cover every edge case the agent will ever see. The result is a dataset that takes an hour to run and that nobody runs on a change. Four cases that run in under a minute, as above, exercise the main behaviors and get run on every change. A regression dataset of three hundred cases that runs for an hour becomes a release gate at best and shelfware at worst.

Snapshot result files, not scores. The score is a summary; the result file is the evidence. When a regression surfaces three weeks after a prompt change, having the full result JSON from before and after the change (not just the mean score) is what lets eval compare show the specific case that moved. The per-case breakdown in Step 4 above would not have been possible from scores alone.

Separate the dataset from its expectations. The dataset is the inputs. The eval config is the expectations. They evolve on different cadences: the dataset grows as new real-world cases surface in production, and the config evolves as the team's definition of "good" matures. Keeping them in separate files, both committed to version control, makes it clear which half changed when a score moves.

Treat summary metrics with suspicion. The model-swap experiment in Step 5 is the clearest example. The mean dropped by half a point, but the per-case breakdown showed the drop was concentrated in one case while the others held or improved. A team that only looked at the mean would have made the wrong call.

More evals do not automatically lead to more fixes. A recent controlled study we ran found that coding agents using agents-cli run four times as many evals as coding agents without it, but change the agent after an eval about as often as the baseline. The behavior gain comes from what the skills put into the build at the start, not from the inner iteration. Evals are necessary for quality, but the quality comes from acting on what the evals show.
