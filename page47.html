GTA 6 On Switch 2? Digital Foundry Weighs In On The Possibility

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Could Grand Theft Auto 6 come to Switch 2? That has been the big question ever since Nintendo officially revealed its console last year, and it's something that we have mused over ever since (as unlikely as it may seem at an initial glance). Now that the game is out, the tech experts over at Digital Foundry have weighed in on the port possibility, and, perhaps unsurprisingly, they don't rate its chances.

In the outlet's latest rundown, it touches on the "compromised" Xbox Series S performance. It isn't all that, with DF noting "frame-rates routinely lingering in the mid to high 20s during ordinary traversal and combat," but it's the system that regularly lands the most comparisons to Switch 2, so DF says that it would be "interesting" to see if it would be "possible for a further cut back version to ship on Nintendo's hybrid console".

However, the experts point out that a Switch 2 port "feels extremely unlikely at this stage".

"Unless there are massive changes made," DF's John Linneman says around the 53:24 mark in the above video, "I don't see how this could possibly work on Switch 2".

The main reasoning seems to mostly come down to the sheer size of the damn thing. This is a game that is pushing even the highest-end consoles to their limit — "the huge CPU burden that restricts GTA 6 to 30fps on even PS5 Pro" — so how would the comparatively little guy Switch 2 ever stand a chance?

Now, we're never willing to rule anything out completely. Switch 1 got its fair share of 'impossible ports' in its lifetime, and Switch 2 has already landed a handful of beefy boys that we previously never could have imagined making the jump intact. However, this one might be a step too far. Please do prove us wrong, Rockstar!

It's a shame, because by all accounts, the game is fantastic. Our sister sites Push Square and Pure Xbox both gave it 10/10 reviews, praising it as one of the greatest open worlds of all time. It shot right to the top of Metacritic's ratings, too, tying with Ocarina of Time as the best of the best.

‘Aurora’ Short Film Team Angad Aulakh And Guy Kent On Tapping Into The Emotional Weight Of Self & Outer Space

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Angad Aulakh’s lifelong fascination with space inspired Aurora, a sci-fi short about an astronaut left stranded after a mission goes catastrophically wrong. Freddie Ballard (Guy Kent) is sent to investigate a mysterious signal, but an accident makes him the sole survivor, with no way home and growing doubts about what his superiors have told him.

Shot in black and white, the film draws on German Expressionism and retro-futurism to create its unique visual world. Aulakh and Kent talk to Deadline about shaping Freddie’s journey, building the film’s cosmic scale on a short-film budget and their plans for what lies beyond the vast reaches of the galaxy they created. Related Stories News Actor Russell Tovey Dismantles The Manosphere In His Award-Winning Performance In 'I Am The Prize' News 'Vultures' Swoops To Top Award At Oscar-Qualifying HollyShorts Film Festival: Winners List

DEADLINE: How did you come up with this concept? Watch on Deadline

ANGAD AULAKH: It really started with a lifelong obsession with space and the idea that we are not alone in the universe. It’s one of the most compelling ideas for storytellers and people in general. When we look at how short our lives are, how small Earth is and how vast the universe is, the possibilities for what’s out there are so exciting. It’s the idea that we might not even be asking the right questions yet. We don’t know which questions to ask because there are concepts so grand and profound. Exploring them through storytelling felt like a wonderful opportunity.

With short films, you have more leeway to be a little avant-garde. I often liken short films to haikus, while features are full-fledged novels. So I wanted to focus on the emotion, the moment, the visuals and the wonder of space. Technology has evolved so much that it’s why we can make a film like this on our budget today.

I’m happy to say we didn’t use any AI on the film. I’m not throwing shade at AI, by any means, but I know it’s a big part of the conversation right now. It started with looking up at the stars and wondering what’s out there and what stories I could explore in the vastness of space.

DEADLINE: Guy, I read that the two of you have partnered on several projects. Is this one the most ethereal? When you heard about the idea, what went through your mind?

GUY KENT: I was thrilled because I also have a lifelong fascination with space and our place in [the cosmos]. As Angad said, we have so many questions and no answers. Finding some faith that there’s meaning despite not having the answers is profound to me. When this idea came up, it was an exciting adventure to embark on.

Angad and I have been deliberate about budget and scope, especially with short films. We prioritize quality and want to understand what we can and can’t achieve. With this film, the next question was, “How are we going to pull this off? How do we make it feel like the work we want to do, while making sure it works, resonates and lets the emotional moments land?”

Our pre-production work and the team we’ve worked with in the past gave us a built-in shorthand. That was especially true with our cinematographer, but finding a stage where we could create a set and build on it was also a huge part of making this feasible.

DEADLINE: The short film rests on you, Guy. You have a lot of scenes on your own. Your character is not only lost in space, but lost to himself, or somewhere in between. How did the two of you work on that story? What would you like audiences to think about through that juxtaposition?

KENT: Angad and I had a lot of discussions that began with isolation. Isolation isn’t something you show; it’s something you sit in. It’s revealing to see what happens when all distractions are removed and there’s no barrier between you and your own thoughts.

For Freddie, there’s a chance to find himself again. He’s an astronaut trained for crisis, someone who understands how to solve most of the problems he faces. Here, he’s confronted with something very personal and existential that he has no answers for.

It would be interesting if audiences were left wondering not only how they would react to meeting something beyond Earth, but also considering the hope that there’s something greater. As Angad said, even if we don’t have answers to these questions, there can be meaning in an unknowable answer. That makes you feel found in a way.

DEADLINE: Angad, what was the hardest thing about putting this story together?

AULAKH: The biggest challenge was looking at short films as a whole and thinking about how much time we had and how long the film would be. There are practical considerations, too. A lot of festivals won’t accept a short film longer than 15 minutes.

AULAKH: There are practical considerations around the film’s life after it’s released, but audiences also expect a short film to be short. Ideally, you’re not making a 30- or 40-minute short, because then you might as well make a feature. We had to think about the real estate available to us and the most effective way to make audiences leave having felt something and gone on an emotional experience. That’s what I’m interested in as a filmmaker: moving people. A big part of that is considering how the music, sound design, performance and production design work together to convey something cinematic and effective.

You also want to tell a compelling story. Weaving Freddie’s journey into a cohesive narrative in 15 minutes was always going to be a challenge, but I’m pleased with how it turned out. We focused on the emotion and on the fact that a mission has gone wrong. This astronaut is either going to be killed aboard the spacecraft or find his way home. I hoped the quieter moments would sustain the audience because they’d want to stick around to see whether Freddie makes it.

DEADLINE: Let’s talk about the scope of the film. The cinematography is great. Why black and white as your color scheme?

AULAKH: German Expressionism was on my mind as I wrote the screenplay and thought about the aesthetic I wanted. The other reason is that we don’t see many black-and-white films set in space or black-and-white science-fiction films. More than anything, I think it looks gorgeous on screen. The light on the set, shining through the top grates, was beautiful.

We had scouted locations beforehand, so we knew what the set would look like. But the first image that came to mind was Freddie Ballard lying on the floor, screaming and tumbling. I saw it in black and white. I hadn’t shot a black-and-white film since film school, and I thought this was the perfect opportunity. The stars look beautiful in black and white. There was also a practical reason. Black and white is more forgiving when you don’t have a huge budget for production design. You can hide things in the shadows, and it worked well with our special effects and the 3D modeling. We also made sequences in Unreal Engine with Stuart Lippincott, who’s an absolute genius. His work was a huge inspiration to me. I thought the lighting, images, and compositions would be tremendous in black and white.

KENT: On an emotional level, I think black and white subconsciously creates a sense of melancholy. That’s the basis of German Expressionism, at least in my experience. There’s an emotional texture it brings.

AULAKH: Yeah, it’s very melancholic. Black and white also has inherent cinematic associations. Audiences think of films from the 1930s and ’40s, most of which were made in black and white. I love the idea that Aurora can feel like a product of the past, something you might have seen in those bygone eras. That was exciting because retro-futurism, which is used extensively in science-fiction films and video games, is powerful and interesting. I gravitate toward it, so I loved being able to use that aesthetic in Aurora.

DEADLINE: What were the biggest technical challenges, and where did you have to get especially inventive? You’ve talked about using black and white, but is there another example? How did you get inventive with the production design?

AULAKH: A lot of the credit goes to our visual effects artist, Ian Mills Miller. The entity you see in the film was originally going to be an actor, Ethan [Daniel Corbett], wearing a latex bodysuit. We were going to use his movements and digitally map them onto a being, the way they did with Gollum in The Lord of the Rings. Instead, we used Ethan as a reference, and Ian created the magnificent 3D being you see in the film.

That’s a hard shot to pull off because it’s expensive and time-consuming, and you have to be careful about how much you show. Black and white allowed us to hide the entity in the shadows. He’s a being of light at the end of the film, but throughout it he looks dark and mechanical. He’s influenced more by machinery than by alien life.

It was tricky. Having the ability to hide him in the shadows and take a less-is-more approach was effective. If you hold an effects shot too long, the audience may start to look at it differently. Even in major motion pictures, it can be disconcerting and create an uncanny valley effect. We wanted to avoid that with our digital effects.

DEADLINE: Talk a bit about your experience on the film festival circuit.

AULAKH: I’ll take the first part, and Guy can talk about where we’ve been and what’s coming up. We premiered at HollyShorts this August, which was a lovely experience. I’m a small-town kid from Mountaintop, Pennsylvania. We didn’t have a movie theater in town, and we still don’t. Getting to screen a film at the historic Grauman’s Chinese Theatre was an honor.

The festival was so well put together, and the audience response was wonderful. There were events throughout the week, so you had a chance to meet other filmmakers and talk with people watching your film. As a filmmaker, you get real-time feedback from the room. Unfortunately, we can’t attend many festivals because they’re all over the world. The few opportunities we get to sit with an audience and see the film on the big screen are phenomenal. It’s been wonderful so far. Our last few shorts had great festival runs, and we hope Aurora will top both of them. We’re just getting started. Guy can tell you more about where we’re headed next.

KENT: We’re going to FilmQuest. We are nominated for Best Sci-Fi Short and Best Visual Effects, which is fantastic. We’re thrilled to be part of that festival and what it represents. They’ve been wonderful. We’ve also done Anomaly Film Festival and Toronto Arthouse, and we’re going to Festival of Fantastic Films in the U.K. We have some other festivals to be announced toward the end of the year and early next year.

The greatest thing, as Angad said, is connecting with audiences and getting the film’s message out there. I think it’s a special piece, and there’s more to this world that we hope to build beyond the short. The more audiences see it, the more we’ll be able to go down that road.

AULAKH: We’re developing a television series based on Aurora and its world. If we can build some excitement throughout the festival circuit, it’ll make getting the series off the ground that much easier. We hope people respond to the film. After the festival circuit, we’ll release it online; we’re working on distribution for that, too.

These festival runs can last upwards of a year and a half, if you’re lucky. We’re excited to get the film out there around the world, and it has officially gone international. Sometimes things can go right in life, and this is luckily one of those situations. I don’t want to knock on wood as I say that. We’re thrilled and honored to be selected.

Stephen Colbert Signs With UTA

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Multiple Emmy Award winner Stephen Colbert and his production company Spartina Industries have signed with United Talent Agency for representation across film, TV, publishing, and more.

Colbert will continue to be managed by James ‘Baby Doll’ Dixon of DixonTalent, whom he has worked with for over 20 years.

The long-time late-night host, writer, and executive producer recently concluded his top-rated CBS series The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, which ran for 11 seasons. The series finale reached 6.74M live + same-day viewers, per preliminary panel-only data from Nielsen, becoming the most-watched weeknight episode ever, as Deadline reported in May. The series-high ratings were reached with a little help from his friends Paul McCartney, the Strike Force Five: Jimmy Kimmel, Jimmy Fallon, John Oliver and Seth Meyers, Jon Stewart, Andy Cohen, as well as Bryan Cranston, Paul Rudd, Tim Meadows, Elijah Woods and Ryan Reynolds. “We are thrilled to be working with Stephen Colbert. The Lord of the Rings: Shadow of the Past, as the project is tentatively titled, will be based on “Fogs on the Barrow-downs,” the eighth chapter of The Fellowship of the Ring, where Hobbits are trapped by a Barrow-wight in an unnatural fog, as Deadline reported in March. Richard Powell Unsettles Race and Color Theory

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Drawing on the work of artists and theorists alike, the art historian traces how generations of Black painters have deployed color and how color, in turn, shapes our understanding of Blackness.

Sometime in the 1980s, when I was still a fledgling curator of 20th-century art at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, I was invited to the studio of a rather prominent artist. During our conversation, the work of Jacob Lawrence came up somehow, and the artist mentioned that he was impressed by what he described as Lawrence’s use of “Black” colors. Did I mention this artist was White?

I was gobsmacked by the comment. Not that I wasn’t attuned to stereotypical societal associations of color — most notably, of bright, loud colors with Black people. In the 1950s, a full three decades before Nancy Reagan practically made red her official color, my Aunt Ruth was appalled that my mother made red coats for my sister and me, rather than using more acceptable pastel colors. Then a friend who grew up in Northern Ireland told me that Protestant Irish factions associated Catholic Irish with loud and bright colors.

But how did such racially associative ideas translate to the realm of the so-called “fine” arts? I was aware of the place of color theory in the practice of art and knew the names of important theorists, from Johann Wolfgang von Goethe to Johannes Itten to Josef Albers. But otherwise, I presumed artists’ palettes were the result of personal predilection, technical explorations (i.e., the opening up of brushstrokes and form in the 19th century and the introduction of a wall of color in the 20th century), or cultural orientation. So it was therefore with great interest that I approached reading Richard Powell’s latest publication, Colorstruck!: Painting, Pigment, Affect (2026).

He’s equally comfortable at the negotiating table or on skates in an ice rink.

He doesn’t do it himself but he knows principal media is part of the business.

He’s said to have once humbled the CEO of a major cable network group by holding back $200 million of his clients’ media spend when that CEO tried to bully him into big price increases.

He’s the longest-serving head of investment in media. And now, John Muszynski, legendary media investment executive with Publicis Media, plans to retire.

After 45 years at one holding company and negotiating nearly as many upfronts over that span, the chairman of Publicis Media Exchange (PMX) U.S., is stepping down from a full-time role this year, although he plans to continue to consult with the holdco.

Chicago-based Muszynski, who oversees decision-making on what Publicis estimates to be 40% of the U.S. video marketplace inventory, is arguably the last of the old-school heads of investment that have seen the upfronts morph into the byzantine bazaar it is today.

“For nearly five decades, I’ve had a front row seat to the industry’s transformation,” Muszynski told Digiday. “It’s gone from three networks to always-on content spanning the entire digital ecosystem. It’s been fascinating to be a part of it all.”

The avid ice hockey fan, hunter and fisherman (when he’s not negotiating) got his start in 1981 as a planner for Leo Burnett’s burgeoning media unit, handling longtime Publicis clients including Kimberly-Clark, McDonald’s and Kellogg’s. That unit eventually became standalone media agency Starcom, for which he rose to CEO of Starcom USA from 2005-2009. He then helped to build Spark Foundry from a 65-person boutique into a 3,000-person media agency.

In 2016, Publicis formed Publicis Media Exchange, which harnessed the collective buying clout of what was then Starcom, Mediavest, Spark, Zenith, Optimedia and Blue 449 media agencies. He was the lead negotiator when that formed, and became chairman of the group in 2022.

The execs who sat across from him at the negotiating table said they always felt they got a fair shake — in part because Muszynski always came prepared, was knowledgable and true to his word.

“John has been a tremendous influence on generations of marketers, agency team members and publishers,” said Mark Marshall, chairman of global advertising and partnerships at NBCUniversal. “He has always put his clients’ objectives at the forefront and pushed all of us to deliver for those clients. He is the epitome of ‘a handshake is his word’.”

Rita Ferro, president, global advertising, disney, was even more personal in her assessment of working with Muszynski. “John is a legend in this business and from the moment we met, he became a trusted partner and more importantly a dear friend,” she said.

Sean Moran, COO at Evergreen Trading agency, who negotiated with Muszynski during his 24 years as an ad sales exec with Viacom until 2020, thinks of Muszynski as a contemporary of the old-school buyers of the TV business, from Grey Advertising’s Jon Mandel to UM’s Donna Wolfe, and from Omnicom’s John Swift to GroupM’s Rino Scanzoni.

“When I was running MTV and we had a big upfront event, and had to get people there, if I could get John Muszynski to fly in and come to my event, that was a huge win,” said Moran. ”He represented so much gravitas. Obviously he controlled a lot of budgets, but it was also because he was so smart and yet had such empathy for good people. You couldn’t bullshit him. He was a good Midwestern guy who could talk about a great deal over over a steak, but he did his homework and knew his numbers.”

Moran said Muszynski often set the marketplace as “the main controller” of the upfronts. “One of his negotiating tactics was, ‘Yeah, I haven’t seen everything, but boy, it looks down’,” said Moran. “If he respected your inventory, you could do a deal with him fairly quickly and cut through the chaff. He was known to pull that with one or two people that were at the front of the line, and protect his clients. He would therefore set the marketplace for himself. It was very smart.”

Although Muszynski will retire from his full-time position at PMX, he will stay on to consult through 2027, working with Joel Lunenfeld who leads PMX globally and Mark Griffin, who oversees all investment in the U.S. as chief investment officer.

What stands out, however, is that crypto users appear to keep buying them through the selloff. Demand for 24/7 trading and onchain access to US equities appears to have found strong product-market fit as they are holding up even as the rest of the crypto market weakens.

In September, rising share prices drove most of the growth in value of tokenized stocks. Since mid-October, new minting has driven the growth in value even as share prices dropped. Over the eleven weeks to Nov 13, minting added $1.12 billion while price moves took away $339 million. Binance’s bStocks accounted for about four fifths of that minting, led by NVIDIA, SpaceX and Circle.

Market value has stalled over the past two weeks. We expect minting to pick back up again with the launch of OKXICE on Nov 23 opening large-cap tokenized stocks to US retail.

Securitize reported $11.5 million in tokenization revenue in Q3, its highest on record and 28% more than Q3 2025. Securitize's average tokenized assets reached a record $5 billion as of Q3 as BlackRock, Ark Invest and Neuberger brought new products onchain.

Tokenization revenue is more variable because it rises and falls with issuance, while asset servicing has settled around $7 million a quarter. Securitize's acquisition of River One could help smooth out those revenue swings and diversify its top line. Lending against tokenized assets adds recurring income on top of issuance. We expect credit to show up as Securitize's third revenue line within a year of the deal closing.

Most of the roughly $4 billion of tokenized stock sits in wrappers that US investors can't buy. Only about 10% is open to American investors, and almost all of that is crypto companies including Securitize, Figure, Exodus and Forward Industries. NVIDIA, SpaceX and the other large names are effectively offshore only.

That could change on Nov 23, when OKXICE opens to US customers who have onboarded through its CeDeFi Wallet. It’s the first venue built for American retail at scale, so the onshore share should grow post-launch. We expect issuer-backed listings to drive it, with 67 companies having opted out.

MegaRobo raises nearly $200M to let AI run real lab experiments

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Oman's sovereign wealth fund joins a round for robot labs that test what AI designs.

Chinese lab automation company MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M in a round joined by the Oman Investment Authority and CMB International. It builds systems that let AI design experiments, run them in robotic labs and learn from the results, and plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

China’s MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M to build labs where AI can run its own experiments, the company announced on Thursday. The Oman Investment Authority, the country’s sovereign wealth fund, joined the round, along with new investor CMB International.

Existing backers Asia Investment Capital, Granite Asia and Sinovation Ventures also put in more money. MegaRobo did not disclose its valuation.

AI can read papers and predict molecules, but science still needs real tests. MegaRobo links AI models to robots and lab tools. A model can plan a test, run it and use the results to pick the next one.

“That shift changes AI from a system that interprets the world into one that can help create new knowledge, materials, processes and products,” said founder and CEO Daniel Huang.

MegaRobo says it has more than 500 customers, mostly drug makers, biotech firms and research labs. Its Megalaxy Lab lets scientists and AI agents send tests to robot labs, and has served more than 200 of them.

It has also launched Poreva Open, a protein design contest with Tencent Cloud and DNA maker Twist Bioscience. Megalaxy Lab will test more than 10,000 AI-designed proteins in a real lab, to set a shared benchmark built on actual results. Anthropic, for one, had outside labs check Claude’s protein designs in August.

Money is pouring into AI drug design. Alphabet’s Isomorphic Labs raised $5B on Tuesday, and Chai Discovery raised $400M in July. In Berlin, Dunia Innovations is building a €280M robotic lab to test what AI designs.

MegaRobo says it has raised about $600M in total, including a $300M Series C backed by Goldman Sachs in 2022. It plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia, and to use the same technology beyond science, much like Jeff Bezos’ physical AI lab.
