Opinion: Liverpool defender who is 'so impressive' given last chance to save his career

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Liverpool's first-team squad have been under a magnifying glass for some time now.

Ever since they finished fifth in the Premier League last term, questions have been asked about whether Liverpool actually have what it takes to start challenging for silverware again.

At the moment, it really doesn't feel like they do as they've managed to lose three games across all competitions thus far with one of those defeats coming against Fenerbahce in the Champions League.

This lack of form has forced fans to question whether the over £150 million that was spent on improving the Reds' ranks was worth it or not.

In fact, some have even started to ask for new players to be brought in to try and help them out.

However, since the club still have so many players among their ranks, they have one or two decisions to make before anything concrete can be done.

So, the fact that this Liverpool star is now down to his last chance at saving his career is rather intriguing.

Conor Bradley given six months to prove his worth to Liverpool

At the moment, Ronald Araujo has Liverpool's right-back position locked down.

The Uruguayan's natural position is as a centre-back but, thanks to the Reds' lack of depth at full-back, the 27 years old's main position has been at right-back.

This obviously isn't the first season that Araujo has played in that position as, there have also been several occasions when he was used there by Barcelona.

And, since Jeremie Frimpong is performing rather poorly, Araujo has no competition, which makes his job much easier.

However, in the New Year, Conor Bradley is expected to return from his injury which will add an extra option for Liverpool in defence.

And, since Spanish outlet Diario Sport have now confirmed that Liverpool will wait until the end of the current season to make a decision on Araujo, Bradley now only has around six months to prove why he should be the Reds' starting right-back.

"At Anfield, they are very happy with the Uruguayan, but it is a significant financial undertaking, so they don't intend to make a final decision until the end of the season, despite reports that they would like to finalize the deal now," they wrote.

"If it were today, that would be the feeling, but there's still a long season ahead and no one wants to rush into anything.

"Let's not forget that Van Dijk and Joe Gomez could leave this year, leaving the center-back position, a crucial one in England, very vulnerable. In fact, he was voted Liverpool's Player of the Month for October.

"The player is regaining his confidence thanks to consistent playing time and has scored important goals for his new team."

Conor Bradley has to take this chance and run

If Bradley wasn't constantly side-lined due to injury, Liverpool would be having no problems at right-back.

At his best, the Northern Irishman is easily one of the Premier League's best attacking right-backs with Pep Guardiola even confessing his love for him in the past.

The defender, who has been described as "so impressive" by Chris Sutton is always one of the first names on Liverpool's team sheet when available.

However, with his contract set to expire in 2029, he's got some serious work to do if he wants to extend his stay.

And, the next six months could be crucial to that.

By June 2027, Liverpool will have needed to make a decision on Araujo and whether they want to make his deal permanent or not.

If they do, there's no chance of Bradley making his way back into Iraola's plans but, if, upon his return he can outperform the Uruguayan, he could find himself back on track to getting offered a new deal.

Nobody knows what the future has in store for Liverpool but, whenever Bradley returns to action, he needs to make the most of it and save his career.

College Football Playoff will expand to 24 teams starting next season

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

College football will expand its playoff to 24 teams beginning next year in a move that resets decades of tradition surrounding a once all-important regular season and dumps conference championship showdowns that used to be among the sport’s marquee events.

The announcement from the College Football Playoff and ESPN on Monday made official a deal that’s been in the works for months. The network reported it would pay an additional $1.7 billion over five years on top of the original $7.8 billion contract that runs through the 2031 season.

The new format will grant the Power Four conference champions automatic spots and give one more to the best team from the Group of Six. But that slot and the other 19 will be decided by a selection committee that will serve as the latest set of decision makers in college football’s long history of polls and opinions having lasting impacts on teams’ fates.

Beginning next year, around 30% of college football’s 68 power-conference schools will have a chance to play for the title, drawing the CFP closer to the NFL, which allows 43% of its teams into the playoffs. It leaves behind the last vestiges of yesteryear, when between four and eight teams entered December with a legitimate shot to win it all.

It’s a seismic shift that even ESPN conceded comes with some question marks. The network pays billions to televise regular-season games that might — or might not — have more meaning under the new system.

“In all honesty, it’s impossible to know for sure,” ESPN senior vice president Nick Dawson said in a conference call.

The revenue generated from the playoff expansion will presumably make up for the estimated $345 million the Power Four conferences generated in TV money for their title games. ESPN would then not be on the hook for $140 million of the extra money due to cancellation of the title games, the network reported.

How an expanded CFP will work

The top eight seeds will receive first-round byes in the new format and the playoff will start on the second weekend in December, with the first two rounds of games on campus. The traditional Army-Navy game will be played on the first weekend in December, which used to be reserved for conference title games.

Both the Southeastern and Big Ten conferences, whose approval was needed to adopt this change, and their Power Four brethren — the Big 12 and Atlantic Coast Conference — are banking that most of the additional spots will go to their members so their second-tier teams will have new motivation to keep spending big in an era of NIL payments and $50 million football rosters.

“I think it will be healthy for teams like our current situation, building to try to get to that point,” said coach Barry Odom, who is trying to rebuild at Purdue.

The first final for the 24-team playoff remains set for Jan. 24, 2028, in New Orleans. Traditional bowl games will still host the quarterfinal and semifinal rounds.

As for the rest of the bowls, most of them operated or televised by ESPN: “No decisions have been made at this time,” Dawson said.

As part of the expansion, the CFP is requiring Power Four teams to play at least 10 regular-season games against conference foes, other Power Four opponents and Notre Dame. Group of Six teams will have to play 10 games against conference opponents.

In theory, these requirements will keep everyone in the same ballpark when it comes to strength of schedule, which figures to play a huge role in the selection committee’s considerations.

Special treatment vanishes for Notre Dame

Notre Dame didn’t receive any guarantees, the way it did this year when, if it’s ranked in the top 12 by the CFP for what will be the last year of the 12-team postseason, it will get a spot.

Coach Marcus Freeman said he looks at the change as a net positive, in part because a team like his can now presumably afford more than one loss, and in part because of the new scheduling requirements. Notre Dame is one of only two independents left in major college football.

“I don’t want this to be like March Madness and have 60-something teams making the playoff,” he said. “But the more opportunities we have to make the playoff, it helps us.”

The new schedule also guarantees two off weeks during a regular season that will begin the week before Labor Day.

A bigger playoff changes the nature of the college regular season

The move to 24 teams marks the second expansion of the playoff since it began in 2014 as a four-team affair. Right after it moved to 12 in 2024, most thought it would eventually max out at 16, which was the SEC’s preference.

But the Big Ten wanted to go bigger, while the SEC balked at that, concerned about the league title game — a property it invented in 1992 and made billions from over the decades — along with the marketability of CFP games featuring third- and fourth-place finishers in power conferences.

ESPN took care of the money problem. Now, the question is whether fans will embrace an NFL-style free-for-all that could include about one-third of the Power Four’s 68 teams, depending on what happens with Group of Six schools whose role in these big-money power grabs seems to diminish with every move.

It’s the latest change for a sport that, for decades, built its distinct character and generated controversy by leaning on media and coaching polls to hand out national titles.

Like then, critics see a risk in giving more power to a selection committee that hasn’t really mastered four- or 12-team brackets. The exclusion of Florida State in 2023 and Notre Dame last season stand out as the best examples, and the first rankings this season left people scratching their heads again.

Skeptics also view the expanded playoff as another dilution of what is largely seen as one of the most important regular seasons in sports, replacing potential matchups between, say, Alabama and Georgia in an SEC title game with a 13 seed vs. 20 seed game that would pit, say, Kentucky and South Carolina.

Fan reaction “certainly weighed into the decision,” Dawson said.

“Ultimately, we just need to make a spectacular playoff,” he said. “The CFP has a responsibility to our fans, they’re the lifeblood of college football. We owe them to make this great. That really is our intent.”

As Big Ten commissioner Tony Petitti argued from the beginning, a postseason that gives bubble teams hope and keeps others vying for home-field advantage or byes will bring more eyes — not fewer — to the end of a regular season that sometimes boils down to only three or four traditional rivalry games with meaning.

Of course, nobody can be certain how things will play out. The only sure thing is that college football will never look quite the same.

“If they think it’s a good thing for college football, then I’ve got to believe that this is the route we’re headed,” Wisconsin coach Luke Fickell said, “and we’re going to make it a great thing for college football.”

James Gunn's DC Universe Is Being 'Dismantled' As DC Studios Prepares Another Reset (Report)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

These DC reboots are starting to become a bit of a tradition. In the wake of James Gunn and Peter Safran "stepping down" as DC Studios co-heads, it seems the new guard is ready to tear down almost their entire cinematic project. At least, according to a new report.

Puck's Matthew Belloni claims to have heard that "plans are already afoot" for new DC Studios head Jeremy Latcham — who steps into the role of president and chief creative officer of DC Studios as of December 1, 2026 — to scrap almost everything Gunn and Safran had planned post-"Man of Tomorrow," "The Batman: Part II," and an unnamed HBO series. As Belloni put it, "So DC goes back into reboot mode ... again."

This is the second major reset for DC's cinematic enterprise (third if you count the pre and post-Walter Hamada era), and it frankly seems like a dire state of affairs. When Gunn and Safran were brought in to head up the newly-christened DC Studios back in 2022, they had grand plans to revive the franchise following a disappointing end to the DC Extended Universe or "Snyderverse." This was supposed to be the moment DC turned things around and finally matched Marvel's cinematic success with an ambitious shared universe. Now, it's all come to a premature end as Skydance CEO David Ellison (who now oversees the newly merged Paramount and Warner Bros.) seemingly prepares to take a wrecking ball to Gunn and Safran's still fledgling DC Universe.

In his article, Matthew Belloni noted how James Gunn and Peter Safran reported to the Warner Bros. Discovery CEO, a post previously held by David Zaslav. The erstwhile head of Warners seemed perfectly happy to let his newly appointed DC Studios co-heads take full creative control. But, as Belloni noted, "Maybe it was too much freedom... hence 'Supergirl.'"

This is, of course, a reference to the box office flop that was this year's "Supergirl," a solo outing for Milly Alcock's Kara Zor-El that brought in just $126.5 million at the box office against a $170 million budget. And while /Film's own "Supergirl" review argued the movie has an aspirational and uplifting message and is nowhere near as bad as its box office returns may suggest, Belloni opined that David Ellison no doubt viewed the failure of "Supergirl" as a result of Gunn and Safran being given too much creative power. Hence, that simply won't be the case with Ellison now in charge.

This also means that while Jeremy Latcham is replacing the former DC Studios co-bosses, he will have nowhere near as much freedom and instead report only to Skydance studio heads Dana Goldberg and Josh Greenstein. Depending on how you felt about Gunn's creative decisions, then, this is either a good or disappointing development. But even if you like the idea of Ellison and his studio heads calling the shots and keeping DC on a tight leash, none of this bodes well for the brand's cinematic fortunes moving forward — especially if Ellison and co. really do intend to discard Gunn's entire DC roadmap and start from scratch.

When James Gunn and Peter Safran were appointed co-heads of DC Studios, the franchise desperately needed a hard reset. A string of flops in 2023, which included the box office bomb of superheroic proportions that was "The Flash" (a movie which, as it happens, Gunn once called "probably one of the greatest superhero movies ever made"), marked an ignoble end for the DC Extended Universe, and it was Gunn's job to reset, regroup, and revitalize the entire thing. Instead, he opted for a soft reboot that retained several elements of the DCEU. Even at the time, it seemed misguided, and it feels like all the more of a misstep in hindsight.

Now, David Ellison and his executives are, according to Puck, rebooting the entire thing. But it seems like it's all too late. Audiences were never going to be won over by animated "Creature Commandos" series or a potential Mister Miracle animated show. By all means, take a few risks, but maybe save them for after DC has regained some respect among mainstream viewers. Instead, we're at a point where the whole thing is being scrapped yet again, and most people simply do not care about these behind the scenes developments. They just want good movies (which, at this point, have been in short supply).

As Matthew Belloni observed in his report, the last decade has really "only solidified Kevin Feige's run at Marvel [Studios] as all the more remarkable" when compared to DC's struggles. Of course, with Jeremy Latcham having spent many years as a Marvel Studios producer, there's a chance he might turn things around. But by now, audiences are surely tired of having to keep up with these reboots, soft or otherwise.

Bandai Namco Shuts Down Unknown 9: Awakening Developer Reflector Entertainment

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bandai Namco has announced it is shutting down Reflector Entertainment, the Montreal studio behind 2024’s Unknown 9: Awakening.

Reflector, founded in 2015, is a subsidiary of Bandai Namco that was acquired in 2020. Unknown 9: Awakening is the studio’s only released title, which was first revealed at Gamescom 2020 and released on October 17, 2024. The third-person action game is set within the broader Unknown 9 transmedia franchise, which includes a book trilogy, comics, and a web series, and stars Haroona, a woman able to traverse a separate dimension called the Fold. Haroona, played by The Witcher actress Anya Chalotra, uses this ability and other supernatural powers to battle a hostile secret society across India and other global locations.

Upon release, Unknown 9: Awakening didn’t make much of a critical splash; it currently has a 59 Metascore and "Mixed" Steam reviews. In November 2024, a month after Unknown 9's launch, Reflector laid off 18 percent of its staff.  More layoffs were announced in January 2025 as Reflector canned an unannounced project while confirming Unknown 9 failed to meet expectations. With Reflector’s dissolution, Bandai Namco expects to post “an extraordinary loss” of approximately €21 million on its Q3 financial results for the fiscal year ending March 31, 2027.

by Kurt Schlosser on Nov 10, 2026 at 10:28 amNovember 10, 2026 at 10:28 am

Plaid is opening an engineering office in Seattle’s Westlake neighborhood in January and plans to double its local workforce to about 80 people by next summer.

The San Francisco-based fintech company says the new 11,466-square-foot space will serve as a hub for its technical teams, with most open Seattle roles focused on machine learning, data science and engineering.

Plaid said in a blog post that it chose Seattle because much of the cloud and AI technology behind modern financial infrastructure was built in the region. The company is developing AI models trained on its network to understand patterns in how money moves.

Plaid already has about 40 employees in the Seattle area, who currently work remotely, The Seattle Times reported. “There’s just great talent in Seattle, so it’s a little bit of a no-brainer for us,” Meghan Welch, Plaid’s chief people officer, told the Times.

Plaid’s careers page lists more than 50 open roles that include Seattle as a possible location, though most are also open in San Francisco, New York or other cities. About half are in data, engineering or security, with the rest spread across sales, legal, finance and other teams.

Founded in 2013, Plaid was valued at $8 billion in an employee share sale earlier this year, down from a 2021 peak of $13.4 billion. The company says more than 150 million people have used its technology to connect their financial accounts.

Plaid is led by co-founder and CEO Zachary Perret.

The new office will have views of Lake Union, an outdoor deck and event space, along with three catered meals a day, according to the company.

Unai Emery's actions after Aston Villa drew 4-4 with Manchester United on Sunday were disrespectful.

Billy Joel Is Returning to the Stage—Months After Selling His Long Island Estate and Revealing Brain Disorder

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Billy Joel is returning to performing—months after he offloaded his Long Island estate and nearly one year after revealing a brain disorder diagnosis.

The hitmaker, 77, revealed in a Nov. 9 social media post that he would be making his on-stage comeback.

“Florida Atlantic University will welcome singer, songwriter, and composer Billy Joel for Billy Joel: An Evening of Questions & Answers…And a Little Music,” he penned on Instagram, alongside a snap of himself singing.

The post revealed that he would be performing on Dec. 14, adding that tickets would be exclusively available to currently enrolled students at FAU.

Data center developers brace for new reality as election backlash sinks in

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Challenges, breakthroughs and big ideas in the race to protect our planet. SEE MORE

by Lisa Stiffler on Nov 10, 2026 at 10:25 amNovember 10, 2026 at 10:26 am

Data center developers already knew they had a public relations problem. This month’s midterm elections showed how much of a political problem it has become.

Voters approved data center restrictions in 44 of 45 local measures across a dozen states. Candidates who support stronger regulation of the facilities won 32 of the 34 gubernatorial races called so far. And political campaigns reportedly spent more than $380 million on TV ads referencing data centers, according to the Associated Press.

The results capped a year of accelerating resistance as tech giants plan to spend hundreds of billions of dollars on new facilities.

So what comes next? More outreach, more transparency and the expectation that states and local governments will pass data center rules, said leaders in the Pacific Northwest.

“We’ve been working and adapting our approach to meet the moment,” said John Wilson, chief financial officer of Sabey Data Centers, in an interview. “There used to be a time where we would say that our track record could speak for itself, and that’s clearly no longer the case.”

Communities nationwide have raised concerns about the facilities’ effect on rising electric bills, their water use for cooling electronics and the noise they generate.

Broader fears about artificial intelligence are also stoking the pushback, including worries that it will replace workers and appropriate human-created content, and that it can provide erroneous information, foster unhealthy relationships with users, or facilitate scams or terrorist attacks.

A renewed effort to regulate data centers is expected in January in Washington state — home to the headquarters of Sabey, Microsoft and Amazon.

Earlier this year, state Rep. Beth Doglio, D-Olympia, pushed legislation that included ratepayer protections, transparency on environmental impacts and clean energy requirements. Microsoft and the national Data Center Coalition opposed the bill, which failed.

Doglio has been meeting with stakeholders, including tech companies and data center developers, and will try again in the coming legislative session under noticeably different conditions. Fellow lawmakers, she said, will take on AI’s intersection with privacy, education, labor and other issues.

“It’s going to be a very tech-heavy session,” Doglio said.

Before voters weighed in this month, tech companies launched efforts to assuage public fears.

In January, Microsoft released its “Community First AI Infrastructure” initiative, which includes promises to pay the company’s full power costs, reject local property tax breaks, replenish more water than it uses, train local workers and fund AI education and community programs.

Last month, Amazon announced its “Built Together” program, pledging to invest $1 billion over five years in the U.S. communities where it operates data centers, including support for education and civic projects. The initiative offers similar commitments to Microsoft’s on transparency and covering its energy costs.

Wilson said Sabey also works with communities to understand and meet their needs. When a proposed campus in Indianapolis triggered significant pushback, the company brought community members to Quincy, a central Washington data center hub, to tour a Sabey facility and hear from locals about their experience.

“It requires a lot of patience. It requires a lot of transparency in our communication,” Wilson said. Sabey’s Decatur Technology Park in Indianapolis is moving ahead, and the company aims to bring it online in 2030.

But the even bigger challenge now is statewide moratoriums and data center bans — a scale of resistance that is difficult to address even when there is local support.

Oregon Gov. Tina Kotek in October directed a state agency to pause processing and issuing permits for new and expanding data centers until July 2027. Umatilla, where Sabey is developing its first Oregon campus, declared a local state of emergency over the potential loss of investment and revenue, the Oregon Capital Chronicle reported.

Even with the surge in resistance, “we’ve been able to find our way through on every project that we’ve had,” Wilson said. “I’m knocking on wood as I say that.”

Corporate commitments alone may no longer be enough to win public confidence, said Brad Smith, Microsoft vice chair and president, in a GeekWire interview. Communities increasingly want laws requiring all data center developers to build responsibly — a shift he called a “watershed” moment, “but one we anticipated.”

“[P]eople want to see everyone who builds a data center do it in a responsible way,” Smith added, “and they feel that the only way that one can obtain that result is to have some new laws that are put in place.”

Come next year, that could include Washington and impact local tech giants, Sabey and others. Baxtel, a data center industry research firm, lists 29 Microsoft facilities operating in Washington and four under construction, with Sabey operating eight facilities.

Amazon plans to build its first large-scale data centers in Washington, with proposed campuses in Walla Walla County and the Tri-Cities. Amazon did not respond to repeated requests for comment on this story.

Doglio said the next version of her bill will be more stringent. “We have to have a strong bill,” she said, adding that the public and members of her party are calling for it. Microsoft did not publicly specify which provisions triggered its opposition to the previous bill.

Wilson hopes any legislation, in Washington or elsewhere, allows flexibility and recognizes that data centers vary widely in size, impact and application. Sabey provides data center space and infrastructure for customers ranging from major cloud companies to corporations and community organizations.

“Describing all data centers as one thing is just not accurate,” Wilson said, “and it doesn’t understand the realities of the industry itself.”

Smith expressed optimism that U.S. lawmakers can find a middle ground that meets the needs of communities and tech companies.

“There’s plenty of room to draft laws that enable people to go forward and build responsible data centers without chilling the economic contributions these data centers make, especially to local communities,” he said.
