A2UI 1.0: Build delightful, fast, reliable, agentic experiences

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

User interactions with agents today are where computers were in the terminal era: capable, and answering almost everything in text. Users can ask an agent for anything by typing or talking, and what comes back is usually a wall of text, a poor way to compare three products or edit and approve a detailed plan.

Today, we're releasing A2UI 1.0, the first stable version of our open protocol for generative UI. It lets AI agents answer with interactive screens, such as a form to confirm or a chart. Developers can use it now with open-source renderer SDKs for the web, Android, and iOS and agent SDKs for Python, TypeScript, and Dart. Companies such as Atlassian, Alation, Bloomberg, and ByteDance are already building with it.

With A2UI, the agent describes what to show, and every app draws the UI with its own native components. The application receives A2UI messages and each app renders the content securely and at native speed. Users get the same low latency, high trust, and complete native fidelity whether they reach a company's agent in its own app or in a third-party AI assistant that hosts MCP apps, such as Gemini or Claude.

You wake up sick. Getting through the morning takes six apps: book a video visit, move your meetings, tell your team, turn on an out-of-office reply, ask your partner to do school pickup and order soup. Ask a text-only agent instead, and you get a numbered wall of text, then round after round of “which ones?”. A2UI won't handle your sick day, but it gives developers the framework to build an agent that can. That agent could answer with one view of all six apps. Changing your plan is a couple of taps instead of another round of messages, and nothing is sent until you confirm.

The same agent output works on any screen. Ask an agent to “move dinner with Priya to Friday”, and it can answer with one view that lines up your calendar, open tables from a restaurant booking app and prices from a rideshare app, so the time that works for everyone is obvious. The developer builds that view once with A2UI, and it renders natively on your phone and on your laptop.

I built a room design app where the floor plan drives the AI render

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A lot of AI room apps take a photo and hand back a pretty render. I wanted the boring part too: will the furniture actually fit, and where does it go. So I built Nookflip, which runs inside Anna, an AI app marketplace, with no backend of its own.

You upload a room photo and pick a style. The model returns a plan: furniture with real dimensions, paint colors with hex codes, materials, lighting. Then every piece lands on a to-scale floor plan you can rearrange, and when the layout looks right, it renders the makeover onto your own photo.

The plan model places each piece in a shared frame: the far wall in the photo is the top of the plan, and the camera side is the bottom. The app draws it as an SVG at real scale, and dragging snaps to a grid and stays inside the walls.

Every move runs a live check, all plain JavaScript, no AI:

If the model's own placements overlap, the app falls back to its own arrangement, trying the model's spot first, then the wall the plan's text mentions, then free spots that leave a walkway. The layout logic lives in one pure module with no DOM and no network, so it's covered by a node:test suite.

Two things this week came straight from strangers who tried it. A shopping list that keeps each piece's size next to its price, marks prices as estimates until you type the real one, and flags the piece where the total goes over your budget, in any currency. And a "render anyway?" check, because someone asked whether the render follows a layout that still has clashes. It can't follow one exactly, so now the app asks first.

The list also got budget, mid-range and premium estimates for every piece, with a line on why it costs that, and a lookup for shops that sell it in your country. The model can't browse, so it never makes up product links: the shop lookup is one web search, and it shows the shops' addresses instead of opening anything.

The AI Code Maintenance Myth: Why 'Unmaintainable' Isn't the Real Problem

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at tamiz.pro

Somewhere between the first 'AI writes a sorting algorithm' demo and today's million-line codebases, a comforting narrative hardened into conventional wisdom: AI-generated code is unmaintainable. It's too clever, too dense, too inconsistent. Reviewers dread it. Junior engineers can't read it. It's a ticking debt bomb.

This narrative is wrong — not because AI code is maintainable, but because the diagnosis is. 'Unmaintainable' is a label we apply when we can't find the intent behind a change. AI doesn't fail at producing maintainable code; it fails at producing code that carries intent. Those are different problems, and confusing them has led teams to waste enormous effort reviewing line-level style when they should be fixing the architectural and organizational gaps that AI exposes — a distinction with real consequences for how we build and review AI-assisted codebases.

The claim usually takes one of three forms:

The core error is treating maintainability as a property of individual lines of code. It isn't. Maintainability is a property of the system — the relationships between code, tests, documentation, architecture decisions, and the humans who hold context about why things are the way they are.

When an engineer says 'this AI code is unmaintainable,' they're usually describing a specific experience: they open a file, see 400 lines of plausible-looking logic, and cannot answer the most basic question — why does this branch exist?

That's not a code-quality problem. That's a context problem. The code is fine; the intent is missing. And intent is missing because the AI never had it in the first place.

A human writing code is constantly negotiating with the system: the module's boundaries, the invariants that must hold, the edge cases the tests don't cover, the three prior attempts that failed. That negotiation leaves traces — a comment here, a defensive check there, a slightly awkward function that signals 'this was written around a constraint.' AI, by default, generates the average implementation. It produces the most statistically likely code for a prompt, stripped of the scars that encode a system's history.

So the real question isn't 'is this code maintainable?' It's 'does anyone know what this code is supposed to do, and why?'

Here's the uncomfortable truth: AI-generated code often scores better on mechanical maintainability metrics — cyclomatic complexity, duplication, adherence to style guides — than the human code it replaces. Models are trained on vast corpora of well-structured code, and they reproduce its shape faithfully.

What they can't reproduce is the rationale. The decision to use a particular algorithm, the awareness of a platform quirk, the knowledge that a certain code path exists only to handle a legacy data format. These aren't in the training data; they're in the heads of the engineers who built the system.

This is why 'just review harder' fails as a strategy. When you review AI code, you're not reviewing a diff; you're reviewing a claim — 'this code does what the prompt asked.' But the prompt rarely captures the full set of constraints the code must satisfy. The gap between prompt and constraint set is where maintainability dies.

Here's the part that should worry teams more than 'unmaintainable code': AI doesn't create technical debt from nothing — it amplifies and conceals what's already there.

Consider a codebase with weak test coverage and muddled architecture. A developer prompts an AI to add a feature. The model produces code that fits the surface of the codebase — it uses the right imports, matches the naming conventions, slots into the existing patterns. But it can't know that the existing patterns are themselves the problem. It can't see that the module boundary it just crossed was already leaking abstractions. It happily extends the wrong architecture, making it more entrenched.

Worse, the code looks clean. It's formatted, it's type-safe, it has no obvious smells. So it sails through review, and the team's technical debt grows by a few hundred lines without anyone feeling the pain. The debt is invisible because the code is too plausible.

This is the real maintenance crisis: not that AI writes bad code, but that it writes good-looking code that quietly deepens systemic problems.

The most important shift AI has caused is in where engineering effort goes. For decades, the bottleneck in software development was writing code. Now it's reading it — specifically, reading code you didn't write and don't understand.

When AI generates 80% of a feature, the human's job changes from author to curator. Instead of expressing intent through code, they must:

This is a fundamentally different skill set. It's closer to code review of a stranger's pull request than to writing your own. And most teams haven't retrained for it. They're still applying review heuristics designed for diffs where the author had full context.

The result is a paradox: AI makes code generation nearly free, but makes comprehension the bottleneck. Teams that don't adapt will find that their velocity is capped not by how fast they can write code, but by how fast they can understand and trust it.

If the problem is missing intent, then the fix isn't better prompting or stricter linting. It's restoring intent to the system. Four things matter more than anything else:

1. Architectural contracts, not code. Before generating anything, define the invariants, the boundaries, and the interfaces the code must respect. AI is excellent at filling in a well-specified slot and terrible at inventing one. The more you can move intent into explicit contracts — types, interfaces, architectural tests — the less you depend on the AI to guess it.

2. Tests as the carrier of intent. Tests are the most reliable way to encode what a system is supposed to do. A strong test suite doesn't just catch regressions; it tells the AI — and the next engineer — what the behavior must be. Teams with deep test coverage will find AI-generated code far more trustworthy, because the verification loop is automated.

3. Ownership and context. The single biggest predictor of maintainable AI code is a human who owns the module and holds the context. 'AI wrote it' is fine; 'no one owns it' is fatal. Assign a named owner for every generated block, and make context transfer — docs, ADRs, design notes — part of the workflow, not an afterthought.

4. Review for intent, not style. Stop reviewing AI code for formatting or duplication. Review it for why: does this code belong here? Does it respect the module's purpose? Does it handle the failure cases the system actually has? This requires reviewers to think about architecture, not just the diff.

'AI code is unmaintainable' is a convenient myth because it lets teams off the hook. It says: the problem is the tool, not the process. But the evidence points the other way. AI produces code that is mechanically clean and semantically hollow. The maintenance problem is that we've been treating code as the thing to maintain, when it's actually the intent — the architectural decisions, the tests, the ownership, the context — that needs constant upkeep.

AI didn't create this problem. It just made it impossible to ignore, because now we can generate code faster than we can generate understanding. The teams that thrive won't be the ones that review harder. They'll be the ones that build systems where intent is explicit, tests are deep, and every line has a human who knows why it exists.

That's a much harder problem than 'unmaintainable code.' But it's the real one.

Could Firenze Lai's first UK solo show signal a shift in global market recognition for Hong Kong artists?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Firenze Lai’s A Second Storm (2025) from her White Cube show Photo: © White Cube/Theo Christelis; © the artist

In mid-October, coinciding with Frieze London and nearly four years after she relocated from her native Hong Kong to London, the artist Firenze Lai opened her first UK solo exhibition at White Cube Mason’s Yard. Featuring new works created in her adopted home, North and True North (until 14 November) is not just a milestone for her career, it also signals a potential shift in major galleries’ approach to Asia.

Lai is among a handful of Hong Kong artists represented by major Western blue-chip galleries today. Born in 1984 and educated in Hong Kong, the artist has exhibited internationally, including in the main exhibition of the 57th Venice Biennale in 2017. Her current show marks the first solo exhibition White Cube has staged by an artist originating from Hong Kong since it opened its outpost in the city in 2012.

The shift suggests the maturity of this generation of mid-career artists from Hong Kong, several of whom have relocated abroad fairly recently. It also raises hopes that Western blue-chip galleries are finally adopting a less siloed approach to Asia, in which global art markets were approached from a simplistic geographical perspective. This mindset of “keeping Asians in Asia” kept transnational relationships rare, the cultural strategist Adeline Ooi notes, but this is starting to change.

“People are much more cosmopolitan and international today. They are not just rooted within their region and bounded by their geographies,” says Ooi, the former director for Asia at Art Basel.

Major international galleries are taking a more sophisticated approach, rather than simply placing Asian artists in front of Asian collectors, says the Taipei- and New York-based art adviser Gladys Lin. “It’s about integrating that artist into the wider language of the gallery programme, so that collectors from different backgrounds can understand the work in relation to other artists, histories and conversations.”

Hong Kong has long been Asia’s biggest art market hub, with headline-grabbing auction sales for over half a century joined, since the 2000s, by a lively landscape of galleries and fairs catering to local collectors, and those from mainland China and the wider region. The city has hosted Art Basel Hong Kong since 2013, and Asian outposts of blue-chip galleries proliferated after the launch of the ArtHK fair in 2008. Artists from the city, however, have struggled to receive much attention in the global market, or even at home.

Leading multinational Western blue-chip galleries such as White Cube, Hauser & Wirth, David Zwirner, Gagosian and Pace have shown Asian artists internationally, but they tend to concentrate on bolstering their rosters with established names from Asian cultural powerhouses like mainland China, South Korea and Japan.

Firenze Lai, who has lived in London since 2022 © White Cube/Ollie Hammick

Hongkonger artists are increasingly exhibiting abroad, but primarily with smaller galleries. The Taipei-based artist Lee Kit works with galleries including Massimo de Carlo in London and ShugoArts in Tokyo. The UK-based Chris Huen Sin-kan showed with the now-defunct Simon Lee in New York and London, and more recently with Matt Carey-Williams.

While diaspora and émigré Hongkongers have more opportunities to cultivate overseas networks, artists based in the city are also widening their reach. Chang Ya Chin held her first solo show with Perrotin in New York in 2025, while Stephen Wong Chun-hei exhibited with Unit London in 2022 and Mother’s Tankstation in 2025, which also represents Lee Kit. The sound artist Samson Young performed his work Canon at Art Basel Unlimited in 2016, presented by Cologne’s Galerie Gisela Capitain and the now-closed Team Gallery. Wong Ping held a solo show at Tanya Bonakdar Gallery in New York in 2021, while Mak2 had her European gallery solo debut in 2023 with the now-defunct Peres Projects in Berlin.

However, representation by one of the global “big five” galleries remains rare for Hong Kong artists. White Cube began working with Lai in July 2025. “Her paintings have a singularity that speaks to the human condition, a universal theme that audiences everywhere can appreciate,” a spokesperson for the gallery says.

Lai was born and raised in Hong Kong, and has lived in London since 2022. Her visual language nonetheless remains rooted in her lived experience of her densely populated hometown. “We are not 100% Chinese, nor are we 100% British,” Lai says.

Moving to London changed how she approaches the canvas. The painter recalls that during her time in Hong Kong—where she built a solid career and a following for her dreamlike figurative paintings through a long-term and continuing collaboration with Guangzhou gallery Vitamin Creative Space—she tended to work on smaller canvases.

“After moving to London, I felt that I needed a bigger canvas. I have a lot more to say,” Lai says. Much of her poignant new work draws from deep emotional responses to her changing environment before and after relocating.

Lai says the current timing for global market recognition for Hong Kong artists makes sense. Those of her generation, born between the late 1970s and 90s, needed time to establish themselves and explore which galleries would be a good fit for their career. “I am proud of the fact that Hong Kong has produced a generation of artists who can have solo shows in London,” she says. “I’ve been told to give up many times. But I can now tell a 15-year-old [aspiring artist] that this is possible.”

It remains unclear how much long-term benefit a solo exhibition at a global gallery can bring to an artist’s career. “Who are the people paying attention to Firenze right now? If they are private collectors, who are they and what are their demographics? If it’s an institution, who are they?” Ooi asks. Expanding the following for her work will take time as much as exposure.

Lin says Lai’s move to London put her into a different art world, where she offers galleries a “compelling combination”. “She already comes with a collector base in Asia, an institutional history and more than a decade of critical exposure,” Lin says. “It’s not simply about bringing in Asian collectors, but about adding an artist who connects different collectors, institutional and geographical systems. For a global gallery today, geography can still be a useful entry point, but it cannot be the final framework.”
