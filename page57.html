SHoP Architects designs Novak Djokovic-backed Miami supertall skyscraper

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New York studio SHoP Architects has released designs for a ribbed supertall skyscraper in Miami, announced alongside the launch of a luxury brand for which Serbian tennis player Novak Djokovic is developing amenity facilities.

SHoP Architects, who are behind some of New York City’s tallest buildings, designed the 1,049-foot-tall (320-metre-tall) skyscraper around ATMA, a luxury brand being launched by hospitality operator Aman Group.

If completed at the stated height, it would be taller than the city’s current tallest skyscraper, and have the same projected height as the in-progress Waldorf Astoria Hotel & Residences Miami, which has been cited as the city’s future tallest.

The skyscraper will include a hotel, residences and a members’ club. According to Aman Group, ATMA’s “advanced wellness programming and world-class sport facilities” will be developed “exclusively” with Djokovic, who is credited as a global partner of the new brand.

Designs for the skyscraper itself show a ribbed form of balconied terraces organised around a central void that runs from the crown to the podium.

It is planned for Miami’s Brickell neighbourhood, and Aman Group said that the floor-to-ceiling windows of the skyscraper will merge the “futuristic architecture with the bay”.

Toronto-based interiors outfit Studio Paolo Ferrari has been chosen as the interiors lead on the project. It will include condos with one to five bedrooms, with six-bedroom penthouses pushed to the top of the skyscraper.

The structure will have setbacks at the upper floors, presumably to create more panoramic views for residences in the penthouses.

The ATMA concept is based around what Aman Group calls “performance-driven lifestyle”.

“Monolithic architecture meets biomorphic design, furniture is shaped naturally to the human form and ambience adjusts sensitively to residents’ and guests’ circadian rhythm,” said Aman Group.

Djokovic’s programme, meanwhile, will include a 120,000-square-foot spa and wellness centre.

“ATMA Miami will sit at the pinnacle of what performance living can achieve,” said Djokovic.

OKO Group and Oak Row Equities were announced as developers for the project.

SHoP Architects is no stranger to supertall luxury residences. It designed New York’s skinniest supertall skyscraper to have single-floor apartments on the city’s so-called Billionaires Row. It also designed the Brooklyn Tower, the tallest structure in the borough.

Cut your wedding music bill by hiring a DJ instead of a band and booking the DJ for the reception hours only. Bring in a music student or two from a local college to play the ceremony, and run a playlist through the venue’s speakers for the cocktail hour.

A reception DJ cost $1,800 on average at 2025 weddings, against $4,500 for a band, according to The Knot’s Real Weddings Study, which surveyed 10,474 couples who married that year.

Mortgage Rates Surge Again as Stubborn Inflation Ends Brief Reprieve

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mortgage rates are rising again after four consecutive weeks of declines, as hotter-than-anticipated inflation data sets the stage for another interest rate hike, putting upward pressure on Treasury yields.

The average rate on 30-year fixed home loans jumped to 7.28% for the week ending Nov. 19, up 17 basis points from 7.11% recorded the week before, according to Freddie Mac. For perspective, rates averaged 6.26% one year ago.

"While prospective borrowers often shop around for a home, they may not always think to do the same for their mortgage, as comparing multiple rate quotes can potentially save them thousands over a loan's lifetime," says Sam Khater, Freddie Mac's chief economist.

The October Consumer Price Index (CPI) report came in above expectations last week on both the headline and core measures, all but guaranteeing that the Federal Reserve will raise its benchmark rate to curb inflation at the next Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting on Dec. 8.

The looming hike, combined with the persistence of the war in Iran, pushed the 10-year Treasury yield back toward 5% this week, putting mortgage rates on an upward trajectory once again.

"The debate has shifted from whether the Fed hikes in December to how aggressively it moves," says Realtor.com® senior economist Anthony Smith. "That is a tough backdrop for mortgage rates heading into what is already a seasonally slow stretch for the housing market."

Markets are currently pricing a 93% chance of a 25-basis-point increase next month, according to the CME FedWatch tool.

Meanwhile, pending home sales fell to a record low in October, and existing home sales hit their lowest level since 2010, as the surge in mortgage rates through mid-October took its toll on buyers and sellers.

Earlier this week, the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) reported builder confidence falling to a new 14-year low, even as permit activity dropped to its lowest level since the early days of the pandemic, signaling that the supply pipeline will face continued pressure in the months ahead.

For buyers who remain active on the market, current conditions offer some meaningful advantages, including less competition from other shoppers, inventory climbing 8.6% year over year, and sellers increasingly willing to negotiate on price.

This week, the median listing price fell 2.4% from a year ago to $405,000, matching its steepest decline since early October, according to the latest Realtor.com weekly housing market update.

"The challenge is that until inflation cools enough to put mortgage rates on a durable downward path, affordability will remain the dominant constraint on the market," says Smith.

Your credit score plays a role when you apply for a mortgage. A credit score will determine whether you qualify for a mortgage and the interest rate you'll receive. The higher the credit score, the lower the interest rate you'll qualify for.

The credit score you need will vary depending on the type of loan. A score of 620 is a "fair" rating. However, people applying for a Federal Housing Administration loan might be able to get approved with a credit score of 500, which is considered a low score.

Homebuyers with credit scores of 740 or higher are typically considered to be in very good standing and can usually qualify for better rates, which can reduce monthly payments.

I Built More Trust in 10 Minutes in a Sauna Than in Months of Coffee Meetings

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ten minutes in a sauna can do the work of months of coffee meetings. Here’s what the research says about why, plus tips for getting started.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

I recently met a venture fund founder in the sauna at the Ritz-Carlton in Dallas. We spent about 10 minutes together and got deep into science and tech in the wellness world. We also spoke about taking care of our aging parents. We got further in those 10 minutes than most people do in months of coffee meetings, and we’ve stayed in touch ever since.

I don’t play golf, but I know plenty of business still gets done on the course. For me, the most honest business and personal conversations happen half-naked, sweating and uncomfortable. It’s the truth, literally.

In 2023, I wrote here that a sauna session could leave you primed to negotiate or close a deal. I’ve since changed my mind about the order of operations. The sauna doesn’t help you close. It helps you trust, and the deals follow.

CNN recently named a sauna as Barcelona’s hottest new nightlife spot, with a spa turning into a rave for 100 people built around wellness and sobriety. London’s ARC opened with a 65-person sauna and DJ sets. In New York, The Altar planned a 50-person sauna on Fifth Avenue.

Clubs aren’t disappearing, but the job they did for us, getting adults to drop their guard together, now has a new home. Only 54% of U.S. adults say they drink, matching the lowest level Gallup has recorded since 1939. I’ve written about what that means for beverage founders and why recovery has become a KPI. What interests me now is what happens inside the room.

There’s a reason for what Gabby Reece called “the truth barrel,” the name of an older podcast she recorded in a sauna. It’s hard to perform when you’re half-dressed and dripping. There are no phones and nothing to hide behind. Everyone in the room is equally uncomfortable, and nobody’s title matters much after five minutes.

When I sat in the most spacious sauna I’ve ever seen, at Love.Life in Los Angeles, the benches were full of pro athletes, coaches, trainers and founders comparing notes on our days and how we try to balance it all. More recently, I spent time in a 175-degree sauna at The Assembly Retreat, getting to know familiar faces and new connections alike. I’m a member of The Assembly, a network of investors, founders and operators in health and wellness, and those were some of the most intimate business and personal conversations I’ve had. No selling, no suits — except bathing suits. People help each other and tell the truth, and deal flow follows because you want to work with people you trust.

The research points the same way. A 2026 paper covering 1,907 sauna users in the U.K. tied weekly sauna use to better self-reported physical and mental health, and belonging explained part of that link. The authors also linked ritual and shared emotion to stronger bonds. It’s self-reported data from people already into sauna culture, but it matches what I’ve experienced.

I’ve learned that trust rarely gets built across a table. Sit somewhere hot with someone, or sweat with them, and don’t sell them anything.

The science is part of why I love the sauna and make it part of my routine three to four times a week. In a Finnish study of 2,315 men followed for about 20 years, those using a sauna four to seven times a week had a 50% lower risk of fatal cardiovascular disease than once-a-week users. A later study of 1,688 adults, about half of them women, found that the most frequent users had roughly a quarter of the rate of fatal cardiovascular events seen in once-weekly users. It’s not proof, but it’s enough to keep me on the bench.

It can also help you get more out of your workout. In an eight-week randomized trial of 47 inactive adults, adding a 15-minute sauna after exercise improved fitness, systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol more than exercise alone. Blood pressure fell an additional 8 mmHg. Exercise physiologist Dr. Stacy Sims writes about why heat training matters for women in particular.

For mental health, the evidence is still early but promising. A small sham-controlled trial found that whole-body heat held promise for depression, though it used a medical heating setup, not a sauna. I don’t treat it as a cure. As I’ve written before, wellness shouldn’t feel like a second job. I keep the sauna because it’s one of the few health habits that also gives me time with people.

I prefer a traditional barrel sauna over infrared. Infrared warms up faster, but sessions run longer, and the long-term mortality research centers on traditional saunas. Saunas have been around for thousands of years, and I like that this one still works much the way it always has.

I keep mine at about 175°F and stay for 20 minutes. The workout trial above used about 175 to 179°F, and the Finnish data favored sessions longer than 19 minutes. If you’re new, start shorter and build up. Most important, listen to your body.

Wear a hat to keep your head cooler so you can stay longer, and stick with natural fibers. The truest way to sauna is “no textiles,” but that’s tough to pull off with other people. Drink water, skip alcohol and cool down at your own pace. Check with your doctor first if you have a heart condition or any other health concern.

If you’re sauna-curious, start with your gym or a community sauna. Studios are spreading too, including SweatHouz, with more than 100 locations, though its private suites, each with its own infrared sauna and cold plunge, are built for privacy more than community. If you are considering purchasing a sauna for your home, SISU is known for design and function, and we have a Redwood Outdoors barrel.

The conversation that matters most to me happens in my own backyard. My husband and I have about two sauna dates a week. Along with our non-negotiable long walk on Sundays, it’s where we connect and catch up.

The nightclub gave us a place to lose ourselves. The sauna gives us a place to be found out, and that turns out to be the better deal.

A mainstay of the GTA series has been the presence of sex workers, but Rockstar Games has dropped this aspect for the newly released GTA 6.

As reported by Shack News, unlike most other games in the long-running franchise, you cannot hire a sex worker in GTA 6.

The outlet asked Rockstar about this decision, and received the following response:

"GTAVI is a game about a couple in a bind, lovers on the run. While we have taken a lot forward from previous GTA titles, everything we have implemented in VI has been done to support the spirit of the story we wanted to tell, and the gameplay systems we wanted to expand upon - things like wanted system, the radio, robberies and car theft. Many aspects of previous titles are not present in GTAVI, but the elements we have we believe are all necessary and complementary to the feeling and motivations we wanted to emphasize as you play through Jason and Lucia's story."

So, in other words, sex workers aren't included this time around because it wouldn't make sense for Jason or Lucia to pursue that, as they're already in a relationship — and that's a big part of the story.

As mentioned, they have been present in the series since 2001's GTA 3, and this will be the first 3D game to exclude them.

GTA 6 is also the first game that centres on a couple, however, with the relationship between Jason and Lucia interwoven in the gameplay as well as being a key tenet of the narrative. It seems Rockstar didn't think sex workers would be conducive to this goal.

In 2025, "Hunger Games" author Suzanne Collins released one of the most long-awaited stories in the entire franchise — the backstory of District 12 victor Haymitch Abernathy, "Sunrise on the Reaping." A film adaptation titled "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping" immediately took shape, and after Joseph Zada was cast as a young version of Haymitch — before he's an aging, depressed, drink-swilling lout portrayed by Woody Harrelson in the four original films — the rest of the cast began to round out.

Immediately after I read the book, I wondered who might be cast as Lenore Dove Baird, the beautiful and wild girlfriend Haymitch is forced to leave behind in District 12 when he's essentially chosen to participate in the Games by mistake. I was then extremely pleased when Whitney Peak was cast in this important part, who's not only Haymitch's love interest but a vital link to the other prequel "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes" ... in that Lenore Dove is distantly related to that film and novel's heroine Lucy Gray Baird (Rachel Zegler).

So where have you seen Peak before she took on the role of Lenore Dove? I should say that, in general, Peak's star is still on the rise, but there's a reason she earned this role; not only is she extremely talented, but she's appeared in some pretty high-profile projects in recent years. From a major HBO reboot (that did fail, by no fault of Peak's) to some independent films, here's where you've seen Whitney Peak, who plays Lenore Dove in "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping," before.

After small roles in films like "Molly's Game" and TV shows like "The Chilling Adventures of Sabrina," Whitney Peak booked her first lead role courtesy of the "Gossip Girl" reboot that aired on HBO Max from 2021 to 2023.

As a huge fan of the ridiculous campfest that is the original "Gossip Girl" — which was adapted, very loosely, from a novel series written by Cecily von Ziegesar and ran on The CW from 2007 to 2012 — I was excited for this reboot, but sadly, it was a largely toothless and lackluster impersonation of the original. Still, Peak was very good! She plays Zoya Lott, a newcomer to the high-status New York City preparatory school who finds out that her late birth mother had another daughter. After Zoya and her half-sister, the wealthy and popular Julien Calloway (Jordan Alexander), make plans to meet up, they end up attending Constance Billard together ... but Zoya, who doesn't come from extreme privilege, struggles to fit in with Julien and her high society friends.

Again, this version of "Gossip Girl" is entirely too concerned with social justice crusades, whereas in the original show, the 20-somethings did insane stuff like sell each other for hotels. (Social justice is ... just not the point of a show like "Gossip Girl!") It was ultimately canceled after its Season 2 finale aired, leaving the story unfinished ... but it also freed Peak up for bigger and better opportunities.

In terms of her big-screen work, Whitney Peak has appeared in a solid mix of independent project and a few bigger movies — aside from "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping." In 2022, Peak showed up in the supporting role of Becca in the legacy sequel "Hocus Pocus 2," and I already mentioned that she was in "Molly's Game," which marked her film debut.

Elsewhere, Peak led films like the independent horror movie "Eye for an Eye," directed by Colin Tilley and adapted from Elisa Victoria's book "Mr. Sandman" — in that, Peak plays a young woman grieving the tragic loss of her parents who finds herself newly traumatized by a shocking, violent incident in her new home in Florida. Peak also appeared in the action thriller "Trap House" alongside Sophia Lillis, Inde Navarrette, Jack Champion, and Bobby Cannavale, as well as Tommy Wirkola's survival film "Thrash" with Phoebe Dynevor and Djimon Hounsou.

You can see Peak as Lenore Dove Baird in "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping" in theaters now.

‘Dig’ Review: Amy Poehler’s Talent-Rich Peacock Comedy Barely Scratches the Surface

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The great benefit of having clout in Hollywood — aside from fame, fortune, and a standing reservation at any restaurant in town — is being able to say “no.” You don’t have to take any job that pays. You don’t even have to work year-round. And you certainly don’t have to chase silly trends, like, say — I don’t know — making a comedy that’s also a mystery that’s also a drama? Beloved, funny folks like Amy Poehler and Mike Schur, who earned a huge chunk of their influence through the indelible sitcom, “Parks and Recreation,” should be able to make anything they want! Any way they want! They earned it!

Far be it from me to know what exactly went wrong with Schur and Poehler’s new series, but something did, unfortunately, go wrong. Each 32-35 minute episode strains to wrap up plots that limp to the finish line. Narrative momentum only exists from the minor twists revealed in the final seconds — then the credits roll, the “up next” button lights up, and your natural curiosity over what happens next wins out over your desire to scroll for something better. Worst of all, everything is “light”: The tone is light, the jokes are light, the mystery is light, the drama is light, even the lighting is light. (It’s so bright, all the time, even at night!) Watching “Dig” is like bearing witness to dwindling interest: Whatever originally compelled the “Dig” team to adapt Kate Myers’ 2023 novel, “Excavations” gets spread so thin across the disparate parts of this broad adaptation that there’s almost nothing left.

Nothing, that is, but the stars. Poehler leads an affable cast (astutely assembled by casting directors Allison Jones and Emily Buntyn) as lead archaeologist Elise, a devoted digger (no, not that kind) whose decades in the dirt have earned her respect and admiration but also turned her cynical and closed-off. She’s an accomplished expert in the field who’s been waiting to run her own site for years, working diligently under the tutelage of beloved British professor Neville Wycombe (Hugh Laurie), but events have conspired to keep her from the top job.

The most obvious issue is also the simplest: Neville has repeatedly promised to retire, only to rescind that promise when it comes time to make it official. This year he swears he’ll step down for real, but Elise knows better than to buy-in, especially when her informal audition (giving the welcome speech to a group of undergrads they call Ugs) exposes her greatest weakness: She’s not an inspiring leader. “I know your default setting is ‘tough love,'” Neville tells Elise, “but please learn to give people some grace.”

That advice is put to the test when Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), Elise’s flaky former protégé, shows up at the dig site after abandoning her post five years prior without so much as a goodbye. Returning with a questionable back tattoo that reads “a traveler on the adventure of life” and without any luggage, directions, or direction (as in, for her life), Dylan is a messy 29-year-old trying to sort things out as she turns 30. Luckily, she meets the too-put-together-for-her-own-good Clare (Antonia Thomas), a 39-year-old head preservationist who’s secure in her calling, engaged to Dylan’s ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), and always dressed to the nines. (Surprise, surprise: Clare comes from money.)

The two will learn from each other, just as they’ll each learn from Elise, but they should all take more cues from Patty Borkman (Fina Strazza), the 19-year-old intern who’s assigned to be Neville’s assistant. That’s, in part, because Patty is the youngest and eagerist of a central foursome neatly divided by decades (did I mention Elise is 49?), but more so because Patty — as you may have guessed by her last name — is a fizzy vat of quirky energy that Strazza impressively dissipates into a charming, fresh, coming-of-age character.

Patty’s voice sounds like a shy version of Cristin Milioti’s adult baby from “30 Rock.” She wears silly hats, can’t drive, and struggles to stick up for herself. She kinda, sorta believes she’s a demigod (after reading one of Neville’s books on Greek mythology as a kid), and she’s always been an outcast (for these reasons and more). Watching Patty come into her own — in ways both delightfully weird and universally endearing — is the most rewarding and compelling aspect of “Dig”; a strange, distinctive spark that could grow into a roaring fire if the series continues (and learns from its strongest asset).

But that’s a big “if.” The first season (consisting of 10 episodes) pivots around the four women finding a rare statue and making the questionable decision to keep it secret from Neville. At the time, they say it’s because their boss takes credit for everything, and they want something that’s just their own, but that contention doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses because Neville doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses. Neville is whoever he needs to be in any given episode — a mentor, an oppressor, a goofball, a stick in the mud, a nice old man, and a villainous monster — and while Laurie can pivot with the best of them, his inconsistent character development betrays a show that’s hesitant to commit to much of anything.

The love triangle between Gary, Dylan, and Clare fizzles out without any substantial conflict. Elsewhere, an inappropriate crush is introduced and tossed away without consequence. Great guest stars like Jason Mantzoukas (a local looter) and Max Greenfield (as a sketchy art dealer) feel like they were hired for arcs that ended up getting cut, while regulars (like Gary) can feel adrift in their ample, listless screentime. Premises primed for comic hijinks never get there, mysteries teed up for suspense are left slack, and relationships that should create a little friction when brought together instead find a way to slide off each other without anything sticking.

Poehler, at the center of it all, is a problem. While it’s theoretically interesting to see the former Leslie Knope take the opposite tact — playing another hard-working public servant (every artifact they uncover remains in Greece) only without the unbridled optimism — Elise’s glum demeanor limits her ability to crack jokes, stir passion, and generally light up a scene. Patty is the only outsized character in a bunch of overly composed normies, and while everyone gets a moment or two to strut their silly stuff, it’s not enough in a series where the humor is already spread so thin.

“Dig” has all the talent it needs to be whatever show it wants to be. The most obvious option would be a comedy, but I have faith in the cast and creatives to excel in other genres, too. They just need to pick one and, when they do, dig a whole lot deeper than this.

“Dig” premieres Monday, November 23 on Peacock. All 10 episodes will be released at once.

Film Independent’s New President Wants to Build an L.A. Powerhouse — and Shake Up the Spirit Awards

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Major change is afoot at Film Independent, the Los Angeles nonprofit that supports indie filmmakers. Not merely a restructuring of departments over the coming years or a revamping of its centerpiece offering, the four-decades-old Spirit Awards, but something more expansive, place-based, and integrated.Since the tragic passing of Josh Welsh, a beloved figure in the indie community who served as Film Independent’s president for 12 years, many felt a leadership vacuum at a time when the L.A. film industry was in severe crisis, from productions rapidly departing the city to studio mergers.

Juan Devis, appointed the new president in April, now envisions Film Independent to become something akin to a nonprofit studio, and equally, a pivotal cultural institution in Los Angeles, to be considered in the same breath as LACMA, the guilds, or the Academy Museum. A day after announcing John Early and Kate Berlant as the new hosts of the Spirit Awards, Devis, in his first public interview since his appointment, spoke to IndieWire about his all-encompassing vision and gave exclusives on the latest promotions following a significant restructuring.Devis’ office sits in the same corner of Film Independent’s headquarters in Miracle Mile as Welsh’s, and though it shares a similar calm, shaped by majestic 270-degree, ninth-story views of the Hollywood Hills to South L.A., Devis has made it bespoke with mementos from his childhood. His father’s paintings circa 1979 hang on the back wall. In Bogotá, Colombia, where he grew up, his father was a painter, his mother a social worker, and Devis a Star Wars buff. Gleefully showing me miniature slides of scenes he recreated from the franchise’s toys he owned or borrowed, Devis reminisced about how, as an eight-year-old, he’d direct his family to reenact the movie from a script he recorded on a vintage audio cassette. He cast his father as Darth Vader, his sister as Princess Leia (before the revelations of “The Empire Strikes Back”), and himself, of course, as Lucas Skywalker.This obsession with film would lead Devis to major in it at Emerson College in Boston, return to Colombia to shoot his thesis, and find himself working in Los Angeles for New Regency on the Warner Bros. lot, driving up and down Barham Blvd and taking meetings at the Peninsula. He didn’t connect with the glamour of it all, and was eager to reconcile his “indie kid” notion of film with what he was seeing in L.A. In the early 9’0s, that meant the race uprising, the Northridge earthquake, O.J. Simpson, and studios eager to replicate the success of Robert Rodriguez’s “El Mariachi.”

Devis would spend 15 years at PBS, connecting, in his words, “storytelling with placemaking” and pioneering interactive documentaries, while serving on the boards of nonprofits like Arts for LA and as a California Arts Council member. When he got the phone call about his potential interest in becoming president of Film Independent, he saw a deeply “civic” role for storytelling that Devis felt was missing in the turbulent media landscape.Prior to accepting, Devis did his homework. He spoke to board members whom he is friends with, looked at their social media, and asked about the branding and positioning of the Spirit Awards (which he has never attended) vis-à-vis all the other “amazing programs supporting independent filmmaking both locally and globally.”Devis said he ran a collaborative workshop with staff leadership, asking them to break down everything they do. Film Independent does everything from development, funding, and production to distribution, education, and celebration, but these activities weren’t quite talking to each other.

For instance, the organization provides more than two dozen grants and donor-named fellowships across many scopes and areas. The #FiPresents folks offer an array of programming, including live reads such as the recent “Secretary” led by Jane Schoenbrun, suggesting the org’s expertise in exhibition. Numerous labs, from the Screenwriting, Fiction Producing, and Episodic Labs to robust international initiatives such as Global Media Makers, reach far to find underrepresented artists, taking them through development and mentorship. Fast Track is a four-day finance market that connects the film teams to funders. And the annual Forum and the triennial Sloan Summit convene folks from all aspects of the industry, making them key events on the nonprofit’s calendar.“I realized that Film Independent actually supports the entire life cycle of independent makers,” said Devis, “but they don’t see their programs and activities in that way. [Everything] is very siloed. So I started to really litigate that. For example, the [Sundance 2026 feature] ‘Union County’ found two-thirds of its funding through Fast Track. Project Involve’s shorts have turned into features and shows. Yet we don’t see ourselves as holding value in that regard. We simply create the conditions for these [films] to happen, and they go off on their own.”His solution? “If we are able to accordion out these [activities], to start to look at a vision of an interconnected organization that is supporting the entire life cycle, to look at how we can actually serve the filmmaker intentionally in each of these areas, then we are creating a different proposition. That is one of the main shifts we are doing right now. It’s looking at ourselves much more as a potential nonprofit studio.”Becoming something akin to a nonprofit studio is a huge cultural and institutional shift, so surely there must be resistance, since the staff has been functioning efficiently in their silos. Devis suggested that, generally, everyone is on board. “Staff needs to understand that we need to operate in a different way. As I talk to independent filmmakers, they [already] need the support in these areas, and they’re not getting it. It is a shift away from the [individual] lab mentality to the [full] life cycle. It is a shift in the culture of how we see the value we provide to the filmmakers, to our partners, and to the ecosystem in general.”The Humana Foundation grant is a pilot program to operationalize this shift. Five shorts will be selected from a submissions call, and each will receive $100,000 to develop stories about seniors’ contributions to society. Devis said, “We could have easily said, ‘Why don’t we create a lab to develop intergenerational stories about aging?’” But the goal is for Film Independent “to produce these shorts and other films down the line, identify director-producer teams, develop the IP, and figure out how to distribute it.” That’s how Devis positions the proposal to funders.In the competitive landscape of similar organizations like Sundance, the American Film Institute, or Gotham Institute, Devis argues Film Independent is singular in its goal of becoming like a nonprofit studio. Maybe ITVS, he suggests, but that is tied to documentary making and distribution via PBS. “Our landscape is completely open.”In this regard, IndieWire can exclusively reveal a series of promotions resulting from the three-to-five year restructuring strategy Devis has initiated.Kate Walker D’Angelo, previously senior director of Development, has been upped to executive vice president of Strategy and Institutional Advancement, which Devis said is a crucial top-level position, in part because of all the work Film Independent is doing with the government right now, and how it will continue to collaborate on the OTIS College Report on the Creative Economy with the goal of ultimately influencing policy. Walker D’Angelo will continue to lead Philanthropic Fundraising and Partnerships, with new responsibilities including oversight of Community and Membership, and Marketing and Communications.Returning to the idea of “life cycle,” Devis also applies it longitudinally. Proud of Film Independent’s Youth Program for middle and high schoolers, Devis said, “Youth are such an integral part of the industry because that’s where filmmaking begins.” Devis wants to propel an integration not just across the vertical — i.e., from development and financing to producing, distribution, exhibition and celebration — but also across filmmakers’ lives. They already host labs for emerging filmmakers, which for many is around age 27 or 28. “We are helping these filmmakers enter the ecosystem. So it doesn’t make sense to me to separate our youth lab programs from what we used to call artist development.”One implication of this shift is a greater emphasis on curation. “Can we identify projects that are ready to be moved to other areas, those which need packaging, additional funding?” Devis asked rhetorically. To this end, another major move will combine what were previously siloed as “artist development” programs and labs, “international” programs, “education” initiatives, and “youth” programs into one department headed by Angela Lee, who has been promoted to vice president of Creative Development and Production.This is a remarkable integration, given how unique (and relatively less publicized) Film Independent’s international programs have been. Initiatives like the Global Media Makers (GMM) Residency are funded by a State Department grant (e.g., the 2026 Residency, which concluded in October, invited nine filmmakers from Malaysia, plus folks from other Asian countries). Devis acknowledged that “we are very lucky to have this grant. It is very unusual. But those things are very volatile, right? I have to think about the sustainability of these programs. I have been in the nonprofit sector for a very long time. The worry is when you rely on one source to keep you going.”

The big question: Where do the Spirit Awards fit into this grand reconfiguration?Devis is very excited and optimistic about this prospect, since for him, the annual event, which will enter its 41st year in 2027, is “the best place to tell our story. I don’t want to separate the Spirit Awards from Film Independent. They are the head of the sphere of so many things we do, and they should be a reflection of our values, programs, intentions, and aspirations.”“And they should not be just an awards show.”In their first move from the legendary beach tent in Santa Monica to the Hollywood Palladium about a mile away from where the Oscars are held, the 2026 Spirit Awards show garnered tepid to more harshly critical reviews, not just because of the venue. Devis is aware of the reception, even though he hadn’t yet been appointed president. The problem, he said, is that the Spirit Awards ended up being “just another stop” on the “Lazy Susan” that is the “exhausting” awards circuit, losing everything that made them unique.“I think we need to return to when [the Spirit Awards] were a party, a true celebration. This involves a healthy dose of disruption. I actually think we shouldn’t fight the fact that we’re at the Palladium. We’re in the heart of Hollywood. The film and TV business in L.A. and in California has taken a very big hit. We need to celebrate our resilience in the place it all began. Everybody was very sad we moved from the beach and that people could bike to get there. Luckily enough, I never went to any of those and don’t have that reference. But I can imagine what this party could be at the Palladium.”Here, Devis spoke with the pride of being an Angeleno of more than three decades. “One of the first things is to embrace the idea of LA. I truly believe independent films are small businesses. We [the Spirit Awards] are the small business of the ecosystem, right? We’re in the neighborhood where the cool restaurant just opened up, with the bookstore, the bodega, and the Armenian-owned [enterprise]. That’s job creation. That’s entrepreneurship. That’s community building.”Devis has already begun implementing this vision for the Film Independent Spirit Awards. “One of the first things I did when I got the job was to call the city and say we want to close Sunset Boulevard. We want to have fans, and the city be a part of the celebration. Then, I invited Roman Coppola to be a fresh editorial voice, and it has been a super fun journey. I also think John [Early] and Kate [Berlant] are just the perfect hosts of the moment. We want to do something spectacular, even if it’s not perfect, right? We want to ‘undust’ ourselves from expectations we shouldn’t hold, that we should be a ‘proper’ awards show. What made the Spirit Awards shine is the opposite,” bringing up the example of the 2020 segment featuring Gay Men’s Choir of Los Angeles from Jordan Firstman that ended in an obsessed crescendo to queer icon Laura Dern.

Earlier this summer, fresh off the news of Film Independent’s biggest grant ever — $2.55 million from the Perenchio Foundation — the organization announced two new nomination categories: Best International First Feature, which Devis said reflects his commitment to capture the immense breadth of international cinema, and a new Digital Creator Award as a part of its Emerging Filmmaker Grants. “New formats and platforms extend the definition of “independent,” he said, claiming that the sensation of “Obsession” was just coincidental to the announcement of this award.Moreover, the Berlin Film Festival was added to the list of qualifying festivals, and the Robert Redford Environmental Vision Award was announced in June to recognize a narrative film or TV work engaging with environmental issues in an innovative way. Not announced yet because he’s still pondering it, Devis also wants to celebrate below-the-line workers at the Awards, since they are pivotal to L.A.’s film ecosystem.In fact, Los Angeles is the true cornerstone of Devis’ overarching vision, connecting, it would seem, his past to the organization’s future and fueling his dreams to foster integrated life cycles for local and global filmmakers across their life spans.The way to do that? Here, Devis thumped his desk with his knuckles, speaking with excitement and pride.“I want Film Independent to become a really important cultural institution for the city. Going back to this idea of small businesses, creatives, and the economy it generates, [we need to] celebrate who we are. MOCA celebrates contemporary artists. LACMA has a potpourri of things. So does Lucas. The Academy Museum does their thing. We need to move away from seeing ourselves as a trade organization and claim our space as a cultural institution.”Big, bright dreams. Devis’ repositioning of the nonprofit to function as a studio, along with the major restructuring and promotions, forms the basis for drawing a line from a shift in management strategy to a stake in cultural and institutional influence. The LA Film Festival may not come back, as some still hope it does, but Film Independent already has the Forum, the Changemaker Awards, youth screenings, prestige screenings, live reads, filmmaker conversations, and, of course, the centerpiece Spirit Awards. Devis said they are exploring how to bring these different live events under one tent, so to speak.To that end, another major promotion involves appointing Rachel Bleemer and Shawn Davis (the longtime Spirit Awards EP) as joint vice presidents of editorial and cultural experiences. Devis said they’ll be brilliant because they complement each other, and also signals a plan to reestablish their former partnership with LACMA.Finally, Valecia Phillips will lead Film Independent’s finance and operations department, even as Devis foreshadows a “digital ecosystem and innovation department” to leverage their existing streaming service, Vision Media, with potentially an app and a digital marketplace for the public. The goal is to hire somebody by 2027 Q1.Indie film as a category is famous for supposedly dying multiple deaths, with folks clutching pearls or taking to the soapbox with each new cycle of systemic change. Devis, as a Film Independent newcomer, wants to armor it with generative life cycles — entire movies, entire careers — while embedding the force of his staff, partners, and stakeholders in the cultural ecosystem of Los Angeles. His vision makes for a compelling case study of how to adapt, lead, and reposition during a prolonged industry crisis.Thursday, November 19, would have been the late Josh Welsh’s 64th birthday. One can imagine Welsh, remembered fondly for his hobby as a guitarist in his band Meatyard, signing off and even stringing along to Devis’ tunes.

Halide camera app just got even better for iPhone Duo

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Halide 3.4 is out today as the latest update to the pro camera app. The update adds several new features, including a larger viewfinder for iPhone Duo and more.

The new Halide update also lets you use the outer screen on iPhone Duo as a live preview or a fill flash. It brings back a few camera tools from Halide Mark II as well.

Lux Optics first added iPhone Duo support in October with Halide 3.3. With the phone fully open, the initial iPhone Duo update let you review your most recent photo alongside the live viewfinder.

Today’s new XL mode gives you the option to swap the photo preview for a larger viewfinder. Controls on both sides enable two-handed shooting.

While the Duo is open, the outer screen can mirror Halide’s camera controls or show a full-screen viewfinder without controls. The second option lets the person you’re photographing see how you’ve framed the shot.

In low light, Halide can use the outer screen as a fill flash. That gives you a softer source of light for portraits, and Halide’s developers recommend turning the screen brightness all the way up before taking the photo.

Halide also now places the histogram along the Duo’s crease. The update revises the Photo Lab interface for editing with the Duo half open as well.

On other iPhones, Halide 3.4 restores the color and luminance histograms and the shutter timer from Mark II.

Halide Mark III launched back in May with a redesigned camera and the new Photo Lab editor. Recent updates added RAW-only capture in 3.1 and Aperture Priority for iPhone 18 Pro in 3.2. Halide Mark III version 3.4 is available now on the App Store.

Who stays and who leaves Manchester United under Mauricio Pochettino, Gary Neville reveals list

Manchester United hired Mauricio Pochettino as their next manager to replace Michael Carrick, who managed just one win in 10 games.

His appointment makes him Manchester United’s eighth permanent manager since Sir Alex Ferguson retired in 2013.

Pochettino takes charge of Manchester United sitting in an unfamiliar 18th in the Premier League table with only one win from 10 matches. He has a massive job ahead of him, with many pundits and former players terming his situation the toughest in the Premier League.

Gary Neville believes the Argentine will need several transfer windows to get the squad where he wants it, if he gets that much of time at Manchester United.

The former captain shared his views on which players he expects Pochettino to keep, and who could be heading for the exit.

Neville believes Senne Lammens should remain part of the plans, although he expects Pochettino to bring in another goalkeeper to give him some competition for his place. At the back, he backed Luke Shaw, Harry Maguire and Leny Yoro to stay, while suggesting Ayden Heaven needs a loan move to the Championship.

“Heaven, I’d loan him out, get some experience in the Championship. He’s nowhere near it, it’s unfair to put him in the situation he’s in,” Neville said on Stick to Football.

In midfield, Neville wants Pochettino to build around Bruno Fernandes, Kobbie Mainoo and Youri Tielemans. He also believes Andrey Santos could have a future at the club because of his age, mobility and aggression.

Mason Mount, however, is not part of his plans. Neville said: “Mount would have to go” and identified him as the player to leave from that midfield group.

Neville also has his doubts on the forward line, with much of the front line seeing a change. Gary Neville expects Joshua Zirkzee to leave and believes United cannot continue paying Marcus Rashford’s wages.

“Zirkzee definitely to go. I think Marcus 100 per cent to go because he’s on massive money and that story has been well told. They can’t afford to pay him that money, you have to get rid of that wage at the earliest opportunity.”

He expects Pochettino to work with Amad Diallo, Bryan Mbeumo, Matheus Cunha and Benjamin Sesko. Patrick Dorgu could also be useful in the short term, although Neville thinks he may leave within the next couple of years. Manuel Ugarte, meanwhile, was another player he was certain would go.

Talking about the exits, Gary Neville has singled out many United stars to be shown the door in the first wave of squad clearance by Mauricio Pochettino. Neville identified Lisandro Martínez, Matthijs de Ligt and Noussair Mazraoui as further candidates to leave in the first wave of departures, alongside Mount, Rashford and Zirkzee.

Neville believes another group of exits could follow a year later, with Shaw, Maguire, Diogo Dalot and Fernandes among the players whose futures may not be at the club in long term. Pochettino would also have to decide whether Santos and Carlos Baleba fit into his long-term plans.

Neville does not expect United to rebuild the squad in a single summer, even if Pochettino makes a positive start.

“But the earliest Mauricio Pochettino can unravel this squad is two seasons if he does well. It could take three to get this squad unravelled.”

Pochettino’s first game comes against Liverpool at Anfield on Sunday. His immediate priority will be to get some results for Manchester United but beyond the immediate games and results, Manchester United are expected to make multiple exits from the club as Pochettino settles into the job.

Dig review: Amy Poehler and Parks and Recs Mike Schur have done it again

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The first time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Parks and Recreation, a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love. The second time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Peacock's new series Dig, which is — you guessed it! — a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love.

Story-wise, the two shows are miles apart. Literally. The former takes place in the decidedly unglamorous town of Pawnee, Indiana, while the latter, based on Kate Myers' novel Excavations, transports us to a sun-bleached archaeological dig in Greece. Yet despite the flashy location and a new mystery twist, Dig retains the core formula that made Parks and Recreation so irresistible: investing deeply in a lovable new crew of unlikely friends.

Kicking off that group is Elise (Poehler), the lead archaeologist on a long-running dig in southern Greece. She's qualified and passionate, yet has a hard time connecting with the undergraduates (or UGs) participating in the dig. Because of her more guarded exterior, she's consistently second fiddle to longtime dig leader Neville (Hugh Laurie). Perpetually on the brink of retirement, Neville seems unable to cede control of the dig to Elise, both because of his love for archaeology and the ego boost his role gives him. He's the Lucy to Elise's Charlie Brown, constantly yanking the football away at the last second.

So, when Elise discovers a perfectly preserved statue of Hera at an older dig site, the last thing she wants to do is tell Neville and give him an opportunity to claim any shred of credit. However, hiding any cultural artifacts from Neville and the Grecian government at large is illegal, so Elise must assemble a crack team to help her cover up her covert dig of the Hera statue, whom they nickname "Pretty Woman." First up is Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), an always flaky wild card who has unfinished business at the dig. Joining her are Clare (Antonia Thomas), a prim and proper conservator born with a silver spoon in her mouth, and Patty (Fina Strazza), a sheltered farm girl with a deep passion for Greek mythology.

Like with most sitcoms, the characters begin as the broadest versions of themselves before revealing their deeper nuances. At first, Elise is prickly to a fault, allowing Poehler to sidestep any preconceived comparisons you might want to draw to Leslie Knope. Elsewhere, Dylan is the hottest of hot messes, Clare is a haughty queen bee, and Patty is an out-of-her-depth klutz. They border on parodies instead of full-bodied characters, making Dig's earlier episodes a bit lukewarm.

But as Dig's title suggests, this is a show that won't be satisfied by sticking at surface level. As the show delves into what makes its characters tick, it becomes a heartwarming tale of found family, not unlike many other beloved Schur shows. Suddenly, character traits that felt one-note to start are revealed to be defense mechanisms or misconceptions, adding entirely new dimensions to Dig's core four.

Dig works best when that core works together in earnest, which is another reason why the show's early entries feel like slower burns: They're doing the groundwork to loop every woman into the Pretty Woman dig. Once they're all on the same page, Dig gets off and running — even more so once Pretty Woman mysteriously vanishes, leaving Elise and her motley crew to do some boots-on-the-ground detective work. Thus begins a madcap caper involving stakeouts, undercover identities, and the black market.

Along the way, Elise, Dylan, Clare, and Patty are allowed to be wonderfully messy and vulnerable, all while staying refreshingly supportive of one another. For example, a love triangle burbles up between Clare, Dylan, and Clare's fiancé/Dylan's ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), yet the storyline never devolves into cat fights or possessiveness. Instead, Dig focuses more on what these relationships mean to Clare and Dylan beyond romance. For Clare, her engagement is a sign of stability and true adulthood, whereas Dylan is looking for closure, something she notoriously can never get.

Dig also dabbles in intergenerational conflict, highlighting the differences between each of its leads, all of whom are at very different stages in their lives. Again, Dig doesn't take a hackneyed "Gen Z versus millennials versus Gen X" route. It's more interested in showing the different wants that motivate us. Patty yearns for belonging and friendship, while Elise wants to cement her legacy and make sure she's done something big enough to call her own, no Neville required.

Once Dig's ensemble truly comes together, they mesh wonderfully. Poehler brings a weary exasperation to Elise that slowly gives way to warm leadership, a transformation that's a joy to witness. Thomas and Viswanathan are perfect foils, the former a picture of poise, the latter completely uninhibited. Watching them feed off each other offers up a perfect comedic contrast. Finally, there's Strazza, whose endearingly goofy Patty becomes the beating heart of the show. Laurie, too, is a joy, blending Neville's smarminess with a genial charm that makes him infuriatingly tough to hate.

In focusing on archaeology, Dig emphasizes the importance of excavating ancient objects and interrogating the stories of those who once used them. In many ways, Dig is doing the same thing with its characters: peeling back their layers to get at their core truths. Only instead of using trowels and brushes, Dig uses the power of the workplace sitcom. It's a power Schur and his collaborators know well, and the result is a show that will leave you feeling like you've just uncovered something great in a dig of your own — with far less dirt on your face and clothes, that is.

Former Alibaba exec’s Everful raises $48.5M to connect retailers with Chinese factories

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Everful, a San Francisco- and Singapore-based startup that connects smaller retailers and brands with Chinese factories and manages the process from order to delivery, has raised $48.5 million in Series A funding.

The round was led by Vertex Ventures Southeast Asia & India and January Capital, with participation from existing investors Square Peg and Lightspeed.

Everful is on track to reach $100 million in annualized revenue by the end of the year, about two and a half years after its launch, co-founder Sisi Song said. The company ships over 5 million items a month to customers in more than 60 countries and works with over 30,000 factory partners, she added. Its online wholesale catalog includes more than 20 million products.

Song, a former Alibaba executive, and serial entrepreneur Kevin Han, who sold a previous company to Amazon, co-founded Everful in 2024. The company began as a jewelry wholesale business before evolving into a multi-category, factory-direct sourcing platform.

Retailers and brands often rely on intermediaries, face high minimum order requirements, and experience long sourcing cycles, which tie up capital in inventory before sales trends are clear. Song noticed the growing gap between fast-changing consumer trends and traditional wholesale sourcing models while working as a venture investor at Bessemer Venture Partners, where she mostly focused on cross-border sourcing and meeting numerous startups in the sector. That same gap shaped Everful’s goal to simplify buying directly from factories through an AI-enabled platform.

The B2B sourcing industry is highly competitive. Big tech giant Alibaba connects international buyers with manufacturers and suppliers, while Faire, a San Francisco-based wholesale tech platform, enables retailers to purchase wholesale inventory directly from brands. According to Everful’s co-founders, its key differentiator is responsibility for the sourcing process, rather than simply facilitating transactions.

“We take care of everything,” Han said. “We handle sourcing, order consolidation, quality control, and logistics. That’s something the marketplaces don’t offer.”

Everful is built for smaller retailers and brands without dedicated sourcing teams, Song said, and it also verifies the factories it works with. While peers also offer services such as quality inspection, logistics, and AI-powered sourcing, Everful aims to integrate these capabilities into a more streamlined purchasing experience, creating an end-to-end process that reduces the work retailers must handle themselves.

Han added that Everful uses AI agents to automate labor-intensive sourcing tasks, including tracking consumer trends, identifying suppliers, coordinating orders across factories, and managing logistics.

Because Everful sources from Chinese factories, ongoing U.S.-China trade tensions and tariffs bear directly on its business. Song said the co-founders have been thinking about geopolitical issues since day one of building the company, and that this has shaped Everful’s strategy from the outset, leveraging her experience at Alibaba and Bessemer Venture Partners.

She mentioned the Trump administration’s suspension of the de minimis exemption, which had allowed eligible shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the U.S. duty-free, as one example of a shift in trade policy.

Song explained such changes could favor a model that combines overseas sourcing with local retail sales. “We’re empowering local retailers and brands,” she said. “Long-term cross-border commerce will be cross-border B2B sourcing plus local selling.”

Everful plans to use the Series A round to expand its presence in the U.S. and Europe, particularly the U.K., France, and Italy, while investing in AI to automate product customization and other sourcing workflows. It also aims to deepen integrations with e-commerce platforms, building on its Shopify integration as it works toward becoming an end-to-end infrastructure platform for global sourcing.

The startup employs about 70 full-time staff globally, including 45 in research and development. It has also established an AI operations team to connect engineering and business functions.
