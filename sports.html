Where Burnley v Blackburn ranks in the EFL's top 8 fiercest rivalries

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The passion and intensity of supporters is what makes British football such a good watch, and derby games are particularly special.

There are some fantastic rivalries up and down the divisions, and whilst those in the EFL may not be able to match the media hype that the likes of Arsenal vs Tottenham or Man Utd vs Man City generate, there’s no denying that some of the tastiest derbies in Britain are outside the top flight.

FLW ranks the eight most famous footballers to have played in the Championship at some point in their career.

One of those is Blackburn vs Burnley, with the East Lancashire rivals set to meet at Turf Moor on Sunday in what will be a ferocious contest.

With that in mind, FLW has ranked the top EIGHT fiercest rivalries in the Football League…

With Bristol Rovers in League Two, and Bristol City in the Championship, this may be something of a forgotten rivalry, as the two clubs haven’t actually met since 2013.

That was a Johnstone’s Paint Trophy clash, with the Robins coming out on top at Ashton Gate, in what was a brilliant atmosphere. Things did spill over on the night, with home fans invading the pitch, but it also demonstrated how much the game meant.

Unfortunately, we could have to wait some time for the next Bristol derby, but the two clubs not playing for so long will only add to the animosity when they do meet again.

It’s a similar story between Stoke and Port Vale, although you have to go back to 2002 for the last time the two clubs met.

But, the Potters’ U21 side did face Vale in the Checkatrade Trophy back in 2018, with police criticising the ‘despicable’ behaviour of the supporters on the night.

Again, the absence of this fixture on a regular basis will only add to the hatred that develops between the two clubs, and whilst it’s another fixture that may not be played for a long time, it’s still one of the fiercest in the EFL.

The Steel City derby is another fixture that we won’t see this season, but it is a game that has been played regularly over the years, and it’s always a great occasion, whether it’s at Hillsborough or Bramall Lane.

Owls supporters won’t have fond memories of their last encounter, as their relegation was confirmed with a defeat to the Blades, in what was a very tough time for the club.

But, they’re under new ownership now, and they will be determined to get revenge for that when they do meet again, which could be as soon as next season.

Even though Wrexham have forced their way into the conversation about who is the best club in Wales, there’s no denying that the biggest rivalry is between Swansea and Cardiff.

The two clubs have a real hatred for one another, and it’s the first fixture that both sets of supporters look for, and the rivalry resumes at the end of November.

Despite relegation in the 2024/25 campaign, Cardiff hammered Swansea 3-0 in the last meeting, and with both teams doing well in the Championship, it should be a tense occasion.

Next up, we’ve gone for the East Lancashire derby, and neutrals who don’t know what this fixture is all about will get to see it on Sunday.

It’s a fixture that Burnley have ruled in the last decade or so, with Rovers having failed to beat their rivals in 11 attempts, which stretches all the way back to 2010.

But, with the Clarets having not won at home in over a year, and Blackburn in good form, they will believe that Sunday is the perfect opportunity to inflict more pain on Burnley.

The south coast derby is another fierce rivalry in the Championship, and it has already produced drama this season, with Saints beating Pompey on their home patch.

After years apart, the two sides met last season, and whilst the games weren’t the best for neutrals in terms of action, the atmosphere made them a gripping watch.

Portsmouth are having a good season so far, and they will be desperate to get one over their bitter rivals in 2027 when they meet at Fratton Park.

This is another brilliant derby in the Championship right now, with Wolves and West Brom meeting for the first time in front of fans since 2012 earlier this season.

A Fer Lopez goal was enough to give Wolves the bragging rights, with the game played out in front of an electric atmosphere at Molineux.

With Albion and Wolves in the mix for promotion, this rivalry could go up a level throughout the season, and the clash at The Hawthorns later in the season has the potential to be epic.

Finally, the number one rivalry in the EFL has to be West Ham vs Millwall, and the meeting back in September was proof why this rivalry is seen as one of the best in the country.

An exciting game helped, and The Den was rocking when the Lions went two goals up, before West Ham pulled it back to nick a point.

This is another fixture that had been absent for so long, so hatred had been brewing, and most would agree that it is a great addition to the Championship.

Prediction: Aston Villa vs Sunderland

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Aston Villa welcome Sunderland to Villa Park on Saturday afternoon with both sides seeking to build momentum after the international break.

While Villa are aiming to rediscover their winning touch after four matches without a victory, Sunderland arrive buoyed by an important success over Chelsea that moved them off the foot of the Premier League table.

Aston Villa’s campaign has stalled somewhat in recent weeks after a strong spell earlier in the season.

Unai Emery’s side enjoyed a run of six wins and one draw from seven matches in all competitions, but they have since failed to win any of their last four games. Their most recent outing was a dramatic 4-4 draw against Manchester United, a result that left Emery furious after watching his team squander two separate two-goal leads and concede twice in stoppage time.

Villa’s Premier League record currently stands at three wins, four draws and three defeats from 10 matches, leaving them 10th in the table and five points adrift of the top four.

Defensive issues have become increasingly apparent. Since the beginning of October, no side has dropped more points from winning positions, while Villa have conceded in 13 of their 16 matches across all competitions this season.

There have been positives at home, however. Villa are unbeaten in their last five matches at Villa Park, winning three and drawing two, while scoring 14 goals during that period. They also boast a strong recent record against Sunderland, having lost only one of their last 12 league meetings with the Black Cats.

Amadou Onana and Matteo Ruggeri remain unavailable through injury.

Aaron Wan-Bissaka and Johan Manzambi will both need late fitness assessments, while Matty Cash is expected to miss out after suffering concussion while on international duty with Poland.

Taylor Harwood-Bellis could therefore continue at right-back after making his full Premier League debut against Manchester United.

Nicolas Jackson, John McGinn and Alysson all scored in the draw at Old Trafford and are expected to retain their places. Leon Goretzka is also competing with Joao Gomes for a midfield role alongside Boubacar Kamara.

Sunderland head to the Midlands in far better spirits after ending a difficult run with a crucial win before the international break.

Regis Le Bris’s side defeated Chelsea 1-0 at the Stadium of Light thanks to a first-half penalty from Enzo Le Fee, snapping a seven-match winless run in the Premier League.

The victory lifted Sunderland off the bottom of the table and moved them within three points of 13th place, highlighting how quickly the situation could improve with another positive result.

Despite their recent success, away form remains a major concern. The Black Cats are bottom of the away standings, having collected just one point from five road fixtures while conceding 13 goals, a total matched by no other side in the division.

Sunderland are also attempting to end a long run without success at Villa Park. Their last victory there came in January 2011, and they have failed to win any of their last seven away matches against Villa in all competitions.

Sunderland have several injury concerns heading into the match.

Captain Granit Xhaka has been ruled out with a calf injury, while striker Brian Brobbey is unavailable due to a thigh problem. Romaine Mundle, Daniel Ballard and Habib Diarra also remain sidelined.

There is better news defensively, with Omar Alderete returning from suspension and potentially replacing Luke O’Nien in central defence.

Chemsdine Talbi will hope to keep his place after making his first Premier League start of the season against Chelsea, while Wilson Isidor is expected to lead the attack. Isidor scored both home and away against Villa last season and will be looking to repeat that feat.

Aston Villa’s inability to protect leads has become a recurring problem, and Sunderland should believe they can create opportunities given the hosts’ recent defensive record.

However, the visitors face this contest with significant absences and continue to struggle away from home. While Sunderland will be encouraged by their victory over Chelsea, Villa’s strong home form and greater attacking depth should give them the upper hand.

I can understand the Devil’s annoyance. Pizza is one of the greatest pleasures on Earth, which means that pizza is absolutely disqualified from being served in Hell, even though there’s a perfect-looking pizza oven literally everywhere you look.

A recurring source of humor in Gary Larson’s comic strip was to take a common phrase or idiom and interpret it as literally as possible. In one comic from 1992, he picked apart the common phrase “ride off into the sunset” by depicting a burnt-up cowboy that had literally ridden into the sun. In another comic from 1985, he played on the phrase “dressed to kill” by showing two sharks in fancy evening attire.

In this comic from 1982, Larson dissected the phrase “To Hell and back” by turning Hell into a mere tourist destination. In it, a man is showing off slides from his vacation, which include a picture of his wife standing with the Devil. Especially fun here is how happy the Devil is, while the man’s wife looks bored by the encounter.

While Hell showed up in several Far Side comics, it wasn’t always the same Hell, or at least it didn’t appear to be. Several comic strips depicted very specific, different forms of Hell, each of which was specifically designed to torture a particular resident of the underworld. For example, this comic from 1990 has the caption “Bowler’s Hell.” It shows the bowler having made an impossible-to-hit split, with the Devil saying “Whoa! Another split?... What a bummer!” Clearly, endless splits like this would be the worst possible punishment for a bowler.

If a bowler’s version of Hell is endless splits, a classical conductor's version of Hell would have to be an orchestra filled only with banjos. Larson seemingly found banjos to be obnoxious instruments as a number of his comic strips feature them in a less-than-flattering light. In another, more Earthly Far Side comic, a different conductor is horrified by the sound of a banjo in his orchestra as he shouts “Stop! Stop! What’s that sound? What’s that sound?”

Still, it seems that, for Gary Larson’s musical tastes, banjos rank higher than accordions, which received even harsher treatment.

This Far Side comic contrasts Heaven and Hell. In Heaven, you receive a harp when you die, but in Hell, the instrument given out to all new inhabitants is an accordion. I seriously wonder if any accordion or banjo players were ever offended by Gary Larson’s repeated attacks on their instruments.

In yet another specific version of Hell, dogs who have been sentenced to eternal damnation are forced to work as their arch-nemesis: mailmen. Originally, I’d assumed that this comic strip was a parody of the movie All Dogs Go to Heaven, a truly beautiful animated film directed by Don Bluth. But the years don’t add up as this comic strip comes from 1986 while All Dogs Go to Heaven is from 1989.

And while I’m not the kind of person to get into a theological debate between any two parties, I think Don Bluth is right about where dogs go in the afterlife, whereas Larson is totally wrong about any dog going to hell.

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern. Truly growing beyond alcohol often requires us to develop new skills and tools to navigate the challenges we once relied on alcohol to handle. For the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop, learning to say no is one of those skills.

Fortunately, like most skills, saying no is something we can learn through practice. Personally, I've found that having a few ready-to-use ways to say no makes a world of difference. That's why I've put together 7 of my favorite scripts for saying no with grace.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Alabama is 8-2. UCLA is 10-0. And in the latest College Football Playoff (CFP) rankings that were released on Tuesday night, the Crimson Tide came in one spot (No. 3) ahead of the Bruins (No. 4) for a second straight week.

That is just one of the many things that has fueled debate the last couple weeks about whether the CFP committee favors the SEC over the Big Ten, Big 12 and ACC. I don't believe the answer is as simple as them being biased, but I also understand why people could think they are.

Because much like when Nate Bargatze was asked how many liters are in a gallon, or how many yards are in a mile in the "Washington's Dream" sketch on Saturday Night Live, "nobody knows" what the CFP committee values when it comes to their rankings.

The fix for this should be simple: tell everyone how you go about determining the rankings. Or, more bluntly, which advanced metrics are used to help rank the top-25 teams.

When asked about that very thing earlier this week by The Athletic, CFP executive director Rich Clark danced around the question a bit and ultimately said this:

"Just to let you know, we have not provided those publicly. Basically, there's so many metrics to really give that out publicly with an explanation of them isn't really feasible right now. It is something that we're considering. We're looking at this in the offseason to see what metrics and how we would present those potentially in the coming seasons."

The committee is not hiding the fact it uses metrics to help set their rankings, but why not tell fans how many, and which, metrics are used? And which ones are the most important? And how were those metrics determined to be more important than the others?

And how is it not "feasible" to simply say which metrics you use? Perhaps Clark means it isn't feasible to show every metric in a clean, TV-friendly graphic. Maybe not. But with ESPN holding the CFP's media rights through the 2031 season, it's fair to wonder how much input the network has in what the public sees.

One metric we know the CFP values is strength of schedule. Committee Chair Hunter Yurachek — the athletic director for Arkansas — has defended Alabama's ranking due to the Crimson Tide's schedule. They rank sixth in strength of record and sixth in strength of schedule on ESPN's Football Power Index (FPI).

That may be a fine and dandy explanation to a casual college football fan, but ESPN owns the media rights to the CFP, so using ESPN's own metrics is really suspect.

Other than that, what else does the committee value? Head-to-head results don't really appear to be one of them. CBS Sports noted the committee has valued head-to-head results in some cases, like Alabama over Kentucky, but not in others.

One prime example is…you guessed it…Alabama being six spots ahead of Tennessee despite the Volunteers beating the Tide by 16 points this season. Yurachek explained that decision by citing Alabama's three wins over teams in the committee's top-25.

Now, to be fair, the metrics do back up the strength of schedule debate in Alabama's favor. UCLA is first in strength of record, but just 67th in strength of schedule. With a non-conference schedule that featured teams like San Diego State (96th in FPI) Cal (97th) and Nevada (98th), that isn't incredibly surprising.

Alabama's non-conference schedule wasn't much better. Florida State (No. 40) is a shell of what it once was and is in the midst of a coaching search, and East Carolina (No. 89) and FCS Chattanooga don't move the needle either. At least not to the point where they should definitively be ahead of the Bruins with two losses.

If the strength of schedule and strength of record metrics are what the committee favors more than others, just say it. A published methodology protects the committee more than this current approach does. Instead, every close call we get currently in the rankings can look like a favor to ESPN and the SEC.

But perhaps it isn't the strength of schedule that matters; perhaps it's strength of schedule within a team's conference. ESPN's Heather Dinich said on Tuesday night's show that there is a "new metric" the committee is using this year to help them rank each team. And the metric certainly seems to favor schools where football "just means more."

So the reason No. 21 South Carolina is still ranked — even after losing last week, 34-6, to an Arkansas team (Yurachek's Arkansas team, just to remind you) that was 3-6 and had lost four of its last five games before that game — is because…the SEC's schedule is tougher? According to who, exactly? Who determines how difficult a conference's schedule really is?

Nobody knows, but it must also be the reason three other four-loss teams — No. 22 Oklahoma, No. 24 Florida, No. 25 Ole Miss — are also ranked.

Ultimately, the CFP Committee needs to be transparent before the college football season even begins. Members of the committee change every year, so the way they go about developing the rankings should be made available every offseason so we all know exactly what to expect. Then, if the committee changes their criteria midseason, those changes should be made public as well, along with legitimate reasons for why those changes were made.

Show your work, CFP Committee. Fans can handle it.

A Pop-Star Acting Newbie, the Afterlife and “Dirty Energy”: The Making of ‘La Bola Negra’

Directing duo Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo stretch a love story across the Spanish Civil War, three generations and.the great beyond.

Javier Calvo was traveling with his filmmaking partner, Javier Ambrossi, when he read Alberto Conejero’s play La Piedra Oscura (The Dark Stone) and the idea for La Bola Negra (The Black Ball) took hold. Conejero’s drama, set in a military hospital during the Spanish Civil War, imagines the last night of Rafael Rodríguez Rapún — a Republican lieutenant and the real-life lover of playwright Federico García Lorca — in conversation with Sebastián, the young Nationalist soldier who is to execute him at dawn.

“I imagined this little gay boy having to leave his town, all this tragedy, and how this happened to my country only 90 years ago, and having a gay character in the civil war,” says Calvo. “We never talk about that. Where were we? What happened to us? It hit me, and I started to cry. The film, co-written with Conejero, braids three timelines. In 1932 Granada, Carlos (Milo Quifes), a young upper-class Andalusian, is blackballed from an exclusive casino because he’s gay — the premise of Lorca’s unfinished play La Bola Negra, of which only four pages survive. In 1937, Sebastián (singer- songwriter Guitarricadelafuente) keeps watch over Rafael (Miguel Bernardeau). And in 2017 Madrid, Alberto (Carlos González) learns that he is Sebastián’s grandson. Sebastián has left him the rest of Lorca’s play — pages the film imagines into being — which he had promised Rafael he would save. Those pages — verified by Glenn Close, who plays a Spanish-speaking American scholar named Isabelle Durand — lead Alberto and his relatives to discover that Sebastián spent most of his life secretly living with another man.

“This is a movie about what we pass on to the next generation,” Ambrossi says. “It’s about inheritance.”

The co-directors, known as Los Javis among their collaborators because of their shared first names, reassembled their usual crafts team to begin shaping each timeline: cinematographer Gris Jordana, editor Alberto Gutiérrez, production designer Roger Bellés, costume designer Ana López Cobos, composer Raül Refree and casting directors Eva Leira and Yolanda Serrano.

The directors were already keen on Guitarricadelafuente and Bernardeau for their respective roles, as well as González for the part of Alberto, which Ambrossi calls “a creation.”

“He’s so comedic and flamboyant, but we wanted to give him a role where he can prove the actor that he is because sometimes queer people are given the same [stereotypical] roles,” Ambrossi says.

Quifes, who like Guitarricadelafuente makes his screen debut in the film, was found through an open casting call. “He has a sense of melancholy about him, but it’s not about hopelessness; there’s a sense of hope and beauty,” says Leira. “And he has such a beautiful gaze. It’s a gaze that comes from ancient times.”

Los Javis also knew they wanted to shoot in 35mm from the outset, which aligned with DP Jordana’s approach. “You don’t have to fight the format to get images,” she says of 35mm film. “If you use the right light direction, the rest just comes together. It’s like magic.”

Light also drove the design of the opulent casino in Granada, the only set built from scratch. Everywhere else, the production restored existing buildings, hiring local artisans who still work with prewar methods. “At first glance, it looked like something crazy and unnecessary when we built it in the middle of Spain, but when you open the windows, you see Spain, not a green screen,” says production designer Bellés, who commissioned artist Ignasi Monreal to paint the mural on the ceiling in the style of Joaquín Sorolla, “one of the great painters of Spanish light,” he explains.

The windows were made of sugar glass so that they could shatter safely when the casino explodes near the end of the film after being overtaken by a mob as Carlos makes a final case for admission. The remaining structure was then filled with fake snow as the scene transitions to Carlos lying on a mountainside, realizing he has died from the bomb’s explosion. The shot, captured at an altitude of 3,000 meters (more than 9,800 feet high) in the snowy region of the Sierra Nevadas, brought to life elements described in Lorca’s poem — like the skiers on the slopes — which also inspired Guitarricadelafuente’s original song, “La Nieve (The Snow),” that plays in the background.

“With Federico García Lorca, there’s a lot of powerful imagery and symbolism, like in the beginning of the film, the horse running past Sebastián is a representation of untamable desire,” Guitarricadelafuente explains. “ ‘La Nieve’ symbolized the end of a journey and approaching death. I had to leave the emotional baggage of Sebastián to fully understand what he was carrying to be able to write that.”

Refree took a hands-on approach with Penélope Cruz, who portrays Nené Romero, a cupletista cabaret singer. “She told me, ‘I’m not a singer,’ but as soon as I heard her singing … I fell in love with her singing,” he says. “The problem was that she hadn’t created the character yet.”

Over the course of several days, Refree went to Cruz’s home for rehearsals, and she began to craft the persona of Nené, who serves as a portal to a world in which Sebastián can be free to be his true self when he sees her perform “Las Tardes del Ritz (Afternoons at the Ritz)” and “Batallón de modistillas (Seamstress Battalion)” on the base.

“My goal was to have this kind of music that she could be influenced by when talking to the soldiers and to create this evocative thing, like making two men play together as a couple,” Refree explains of the first number. “The second one needed a dirty energy that would make the soldiers lose control and want to touch her. So we created something very rhythmic and feminine at the same time.”

Wardrobe also helped shape Nené. Cobos drew from research on 19th century cuplé singers, like María del Consuelo Vello Cano, to sketch Cruz’s feathery, embellished ensemble.

“When she saw the white dress, she said, ‘This is it; she has to be wearing white,’ and Gris also said it would be helpful because we don’t have a lot of lights in the theater,” says Cobos. “[For the second number,] because I know Penélope’s a big fan of Mugler, I went through their images and found these overalls and dresses that reminded me of military costumes. So I did my own interpretation of them, and it worked really well because it helped her move and it was very comfortable for her to do the choreography with the tank and all of the other dancers.”

In the final scene of the film, Sebastián reads a poem with the words, “Mira, que nos acechan todavia (Beware, they still haunt us),” after being forced to shoot Rafael. It’s the final verse of “Love Sleeps in the Poet’s Breast,” one of the Sonetos del Amor Oscuro (Sonnets of Dark Love), written by Lorca for Rafael, expressing his forbidden desire for him amid the looming threat of societal persecution.

For Calvo, the warning is just as relevant today. “We had it on the whiteboard, and it was kind of a guide [for us],” he says. “Putting it [at the end of the film] was perfect and so significant because they do still haunt us.”
