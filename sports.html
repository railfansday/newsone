Harry Kane believes Manchester United will gain a ‘fantastic manager’ in Mauricio Pochettino if Michael Carrick is sacked.

United are expected to dismiss Carrick during the international break following the team’s worst-ever start to a Premier League season.

Sunday’s 4-4 draw at home to Aston Villa has left United 18th in the Premier League with just one win in 10 games. Carrick’s side are also out of the Carabao Cup following a 3-2 defeat at home to Brighton in September.

Kane worked alongside Pochettino for five years at Spurs where they reached a League Cup and Champions League final and has endorsed the 54-year-old’s likely move to United.

Manchester United are closing in on a deal to appoint Mauricio Pochettino before their next game against Liverpool with Michael Carrick set to be sacked.

United must strike a deal with US Soccer, with a solution required over the United States’ next two international games in particular, while the club’s hierarchy is meeting at a Monaco summit to finalise a decision over the club’s manager.

Compensation, worth an initial $7m (£5.3m), will be a formality due to the clause in Pochettino’s existing contract, that runs until the 2030 World Cup.

While additional compensation will be required for Pochettino’s trusted coaching staff, including long-time assistant Jesus Perez.

The United States face Concacaf Nations League fixtures against Haiti during the current international break, with an away match on 14 November and a home match in Cincinnati on 18 November.

US Soccer are currently planning for Pochettino to be in charge of both games, though United will push to have Pochettino available sooner, with their next Premier League match against Liverpool at Anfield on 22 November.

Another option is potentially for Darren Fletcher to be handed an interim role once more, should a deal be unable to conclude in time.

While losing Pochettino would be a blow for the USA, The Independent understands that US Soccer has been prepared for the prospect of his exit at some stage before the 2030 World Cup, with a succession plan already in place.

Pochettino’s move back to England would be a major shift in his plans after committing to a four-year build to the 2030 World Cup, having overseen an encouraging, yet ultimately unsuccessful home World Cup this summer, ending in a heavy defeat to Belgium in the last 16.

While Pochettino admitted in September that he was “excited” for the journey to Spain, Portugal and Morocco, he conceded four years is a “long time” and that this commitment was “different”.

"We are serious people,” Pochettino told The Independent and other outlets in a wide-ranging interview. “Now it's a different thing. When we gave our word to the federation, it was about starting and finishing, after the World Cup. It's different, maybe, if you ask me now. Because now we commit to four years.

“Always it's different. Before, it was very easy to say 'OK, we start now, we prepare the team for the World Cup, we play the World Cup, and then we'll see.' [Leaving] before that, it was difficult. Because we committed before. For us, it was the moment to say 'if some club came, it's impossible'. Now it's another question.

“For us it was impossible to think about coming back, or going to another club. Because we received some calls from different clubs. But we cannot go away, first, because we didn't want to go. Always you can break a contract, say you stop, and that's it, you agree something. But in the moment I think it wasn't right for us to create all these situations and expectations.

"And now it's a four year contract, it's different. We can next season lose the Copa America or the Gold Cup, and they're not happy with us, they can sack us, or we can say, 'ok it's not in the way that we think'. Four years is different. Maybe now if someone calls in the future, it's a different, different type. We are excited, we are thinking to spend four years, I think to the next World Cup. But now it's a long period."

Fantasy Football Updated Rookie Report: Fernando Mendoza gets his shot

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Nine weeks into the NFL season, we've seen enough from this rookie class to know who's for real, who's still finding their footing and who could make some noise down the stretch. From Carnell Tate's impressive start in Tennessee to Fernando Mendoza getting his opportunity in Las Vegas, here's my midseason check-in on 15 rookies, and what I'm expecting for the rest of the season.

Carnell Tate has been everything we hoped he would be — and then some. Through seven games, he's hauled in 34 receptions for 413 yards and a touchdown despite playing in one of the more inconsistent passing offenses in football. Cam Ward and the Tennessee Titans haven't exactly made life easy for their rookie receiver, but Tate continues to produce.

The talent is undeniable, the opportunity is there and the chemistry with Ward continues to develop. I expect Tate to build on what's already been a successful rookie campaign. He's a weekly fantasy starter with plenty of room to grow.

Jonah Coleman might be the rookie running back I'm most excited about for the second half of the season. He's rushed for 191 yards and three touchdowns while adding 13 receptions for another 128 yards. More importantly, look at how Denver is using him. He handled 21 touches in Week 9, and the coaching staff clearly trusts him.

With R.J. Harvey on injured reserve and J.K. Dobbins getting older, the opportunity is sitting right in front of Coleman. He's already shown he can contribute in the passing game and handle meaningful carries. I wouldn't be surprised if Coleman finishes as one of the most valuable rookie running backs in fantasy.

Omar Cooper Jr. missed time early in the season, but the moment he got healthy, he made his presence felt. Through four games, Cooper has 20 receptions for 306 yards and two touchdowns, including back-to-back 100-yard performances. In Week 9, he followed those outings with eight receptions for 58 yards while Garrett Wilson was sidelined.

What I've really liked is the rapport he's developing with Geno Smith. Cooper looks comfortable; he's earning opportunities and he's showing he can win in different areas of the field. Wilson's return could impact his volume, but I'm buying what we've seen. Cooper has legitimate weekly flex appeal moving forward.

KC Concepcion Jr. has quietly been one of the most impressive rookie wide receivers in football. He's sitting at 37 receptions for 437 yards and a touchdown, including 251 receiving yards over his last three games. What jumps off the screen is his ability to create after the catch. Get this young man the football in space and let him work.

The Browns haven't exactly provided consistent quarterback play, but Concepcion continues to find ways to produce. He's dynamic, explosive and already showing flashes of a player who could eventually become a Pro Bowl-caliber receiver. For fantasy purposes, he's worked his way into weekly flex consideration.

And how about Denzel Boston? The rookie has 507 receiving yards and three touchdowns on 32 receptions. Boston gives me some young Mike Evans vibes with his size, downfield ability and willingness to go make contested catches. He's already producing explosive plays despite the limitations of this Browns offense.

What's encouraging is that both Boston and Concepcion are earning significant opportunities without cannibalizing each other's production. Boston has legitimate big-play upside every single week and continues to look like one of the steals of the rookie class. I love what we've seen and expect a strong finish.

Germie Bernard's rookie season has been a little up and down, but I've seen enough to remain encouraged. He's caught 32 passes for 284 yards and a touchdown, highlighted by a phenomenal 11-reception performance in Week 6. The following two games weren't nearly as productive, but I wouldn't let that discourage you.

Bernard has established himself as a trusted option for Aaron Rodgers in a Pittsburgh offense that's been better than advertised. His role makes sense. Get him the football quickly, let him operate in space and allow his natural ability to take over. Bernard feels like a buy-low candidate with a solid second-half outlook.

I can't believe I'm putting Fernando Mendoza on this list, but here we are. The No. 1 overall pick is taking over as the Raiders' starting quarterback after Kirk Cousins was traded to Minnesota. Mendoza has attempted just eight regular-season passes, completing six for 48 yards, so we have almost nothing to evaluate.

But here's what we do know: Cousins threw 20 touchdowns in nine games and Mendoza gets to operate in that same Klint Kubiak offense featuring Brock Bowers. Listen, any quarterback throwing to Brock Bowers gets a touchdown bonus in my book. Add Ashton Jeanty, Trey Tucker and a potentially more favorable schedule down the stretch, and there's legitimate streaming appeal here. I'm not expecting Mendoza to be a league winner, but don't be surprised if he becomes fantasy relevant sooner than people think.

Jadarian Price is one of those players where the box score doesn't tell the entire story. He's rushed for 172 yards and added 83 receiving yards on 10 catches without scoring a touchdown. The production hasn't been there consistently, but every time he touches the football, you see the explosiveness. Unfortunately, injuries and ball-security issues have limited his opportunities.

With Zach Charbonnet still working his way back from a knee injury, there's a path for Price to earn a larger role. Seattle's offensive line should also improve as it gets healthier. I'm cautiously optimistic, but I need to see him string together consistent performances before trusting him in fantasy lineups.

Makai Lemon has been better than his rookie numbers might suggest. Through nine games, he's caught 37 passes for 312 yards and one touchdown, including 19 receptions for 185 yards over his last three games. The Eagles offense has been difficult to watch at times, and Lemon has dealt with inconsistent quarterback play while occasionally being asked to operate as the team's top receiver with DeVonta Smith unavailable.

What I've loved is his ability to separate, particularly against zone coverage. He's consistently finding openings and earning targets. The big fantasy performances haven't arrived yet, but the underlying usage is encouraging. There's more here than the box score suggests.

We finally got our first look at Jordyn Tyson, and while it wasn't perfect, there were encouraging signs. In his NFL debut, Tyson caught four passes for 30 yards but also lost a fumble on his first reception. I'm not worried about that. What matters is that New Orleans immediately looked to get him involved.

This Saints offense ranks among the league leaders in neutral-situation pass rate, meaning the opportunity should continue to be there. Getting Calvin Banks back should help Tyler Shough operate more comfortably behind that offensive line. The biggest question remains Tyson's health. If that hamstring holds up, I'm expecting his role to expand significantly.

Mike Washington Jr. gave us one of the more surprising rookie performances of the season when he handled 25 carries for 83 yards. Then he turned around the following week and rushed six times for -3 yards. That's been the problem. Washington has 275 rushing yards and a touchdown this season, but his role remains difficult to project.

Neither Washington nor Ashton Jeanty has consistently found success running behind this Raiders offensive line. Washington clearly has some ability, but can this coaching staff establish a defined role for him? Until that happens, he's difficult to trust. He's an interesting stash with upside if Jeanty misses time.

There's no bigger fantasy disappointment from this rookie class than Jeremiyah Love. And before anybody gets upset, I'm not putting all of that on the player. Love has rushed for 268 yards, added just 36 receiving yards and scored two total touchdowns in six games. We've seen flashes of the talent that made him such a highly regarded prospect, but the situation in Arizona has been a disaster.

The offense has struggled, the quarterback situation has deteriorated and now Love is sitting on injured reserve. If you invested a second-, third- or fourth-round fantasy pick in him, there's no way around it — it's been disappointing. The talent hasn't disappeared, but the fantasy return simply hasn't been there.

Antonio Williams is one of those rookies who could become much more relevant over the second half of the season. He's caught 26 passes for 284 yards and a touchdown, including eight receptions for 79 yards over his last two games. The problem hasn't necessarily been Williams. It's been the Commanders' offense.

Jayden Daniels continues to deal with a significant elbow injury and Washington hasn't been particularly creative offensively. We've also seen Williams have some unfortunate plays wiped away. With Terry McLaurin out, there should be additional opportunities available. He's not a weekly fantasy starter yet, but there's a path for Williams to become a useful flex option.

Emmett Johnson has quietly put together 348 yards from scrimmage and two total touchdowns, including that ridiculous 128-yard receiving performance against Denver in Week 8. We saw a 73-yard touchdown reception and a player who can absolutely make things happen with the football in his hands.

The problem? Kenneth Walker III has been phenomenal since arriving in Kansas City.

Johnson isn't taking that starting job anytime soon. But what if the Chiefs can turn him into a Jerick McKinnon-type receiving weapon? There's potential for standalone fantasy value in that role. And if Walker were to miss time, Johnson immediately becomes one of the most valuable backups in football.

Kaelon Black has 303 rushing yards and a touchdown this season, and I don't think enough people are paying attention to what he's doing. That's more rushing production than Jeremiyah Love, Mike Washington Jr. and Jadarian Price. The biggest obstacle is obvious: Christian McCaffrey.

But we've seen San Francisco utilize multiple backs at times, and keeping McCaffrey fresh has to remain a priority. Black has demonstrated that he can be an effective runner when given opportunities. The receiving game hasn't been a major part of his role, so there are limitations to his standalone fantasy value. Still, this is a player I want rostered heading into the second half of the season.

Jon Scheyer Was Blunt About Duke’s Michigan State Loss

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Duke head coach Jon Scheyer did not reach for an excuse after his No. 2 Blue Devils lost to the No. 5 Michigan State Spartans by a final score of 79-73 in the Champions Classic on Tuesday night at the United Center in Chicago. He handed the credit to the Spartans and put the blame on his own team.

Michigan State jumped out to an 11-2 lead at the 17:30 mark of the first half, with guard Jeremy Fears Jr. assisting on three straight baskets, and the Spartans never looked back. It was the first loss of the season for Duke, which fell to 2-1 while Michigan State moved to 3-0.

Nov 10, 2026; Chicago, Illinois, USA; Duke Blue Devils head coach Jon Scheyer gestures to his team against the Michigan State Spartans during the first half at United Center. Mandatory Credit: David Banks-Imagn Images Credit: IMAGN IMAGES via Reuters Connect (Reuters)

Scheyer opened his postgame press conference with the Spartans. "Congrats to Michigan State. They 100% deserved to win. I thought they outcompeted us. They played more together," he said, per Spartans Wire. He added, "Credit all the world to Coach Izzo and Michigan State." Tom Izzo had an admission of his own after the win.

On Fears, Scheyer said, "I think you've got to tip your cap to him. He controlled the whole game." He went on: "He plays with amazing control over the game, and it's hard to stop a guy if you give him 17 free throws."

Scheyer was harder on his own group. "That wasn't Duke basketball. We did not play together," he said. "Bottom line, we weren't connected, and we're gonna fix it, I can promise you that much," he told reporters, per CBS Sports. He also said Duke's fans "deserve better."

It was very hard for the Blue Devils to contain Fears all game long. He scored 18 points, grabbed seven rebounds and recorded 11 assists in 33 minutes. He dictated the game from start to finish for this Spartans basketball team.

He did it without needing his jumper, going 3-of-9 from the field and getting to the free-throw line 17 times, where he made 12. Fears was also a factor on the defensive end, blocking one shot and recording one steal.

They are a deep bunch, and the talk coming in was about how deep Duke was.

From the field, Michigan State was 25-of-56 (45%) and 7-of-20 from three-point land (35%). As for Duke, the Blue Devils shot 20-of-60 (33%) from the field and only 7-of-29 from three (24%). Duke being thrown off in this game was about more than just Fears. The Blue Devils offense was out of rhythm because of how well the Spartans defense played.

World Children's Day 2026: 6 ways to help kids feel heard

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The backpack hits the floor, and "How was your day?" gets a one-word answer: "Fine." Kids don’t always say what’s on their mind, but a little time and attention may help them open up.

World Children’s Day is a good moment for parents, caregivers, and teachers to ask kids how they’re feeling and listen without trying to fix anything. The day honors children’s rights, including the right to be heard.

Here’s a look at what the day is about, plus a few activities that can help kids feel understood, at home or in the classroom.

World Children's Day is a global day of action for children, run by UNICEF. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 20. This year’s theme is “My day. My rights. My future.” It invites children to tell world leaders what kind of future they want.

The day celebrates children and the rights they’re born with. In many places, children “take over” high-profile roles in media, sports, and government for the day. Landmarks light up blue to show support for children's rights.

November 20 marks two milestones for children’s rights. On this date in 1959, the UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of the Child, a statement of shared ideals. Thirty years later to the day, it adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1989, a treaty that nations can commit to follow.

The Convention sets out the rights every child has, including the right to be safe, to play, and to have their opinions taken seriously. Nearly every country has formally agreed to follow it, and it’s the most widely ratified human rights treaty in history.

Countries around the world mark World Children’s Day. Many also celebrate a separate Children’s Day, most notably June 1, International Children's Day.

A Trip Through Toyota’s New GR Garage Experience Center

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

From Ivan Stewart’s Baja-winning off-road trucks to the monstrous Toyota Eagle MkIII, this collection chronicles nearly 70 years of Toyota racing history.

Toyota’s motorsports resume is a lot longer than you might expect. Long before Gazoo Racing was stamping GR badges onto Corollas, Supras, and the occasional RAV4, Toyota was racing across the Australian Outback, battling American muscle cars in IMSA, and building some of the most successful prototype racers ever to cross the finish line.

Near the Toyota Experience Center in Plano, Texas, the automaker has gathered an impressive collection of vehicles from its racing past. There’s a little bit of everything here: a 1957 Toyopet Crown that helped launch Toyota’s international racing ambitions, a legendary desert-racing pickup, a Le Mans prototype packing 1000 horsepower, and even a few cars from more recent spec series.

Here’s a look at some of the machines that helped build Toyota’s motorsports legacy at the new GR Garage Experience Center.

From the moment you walk into Toyota’s GR Experience Center, it’s obvious nearly every inch of this place has been curated to celebrate the brand’s motorsports history. Racing memorabilia, suspension components, and die-cast cars decorate the walls and many office rooms at the unassuming facility. It’s equal parts museum and shrine that honors the Japanese company’s obsession with racing.

Toyota’s international motorsports career began not with a World Rally car, but with this. In 1957, a Toyopet Crown entered the Mobilgas Round Australia Rally, an extraordinarily demanding endurance event covering thousands of miles of Australia's unforgiving landscape. The Crown finished 47th overall and third among foreign entries. They didn’t take home the trophy for first place, but surviving the 19-day endurance trial was plenty to brag about. More importantly, the experience exposed weaknesses Toyota would address in future vehicles.

Long before the Supra, there was the 2000GT. Toyota enlisted legendary American racer and car builder Carroll Shelby to develop the beautiful sports car for SCCA competition in 1968. Only three were prepared for the program, with Scooter Patrick driving the No. 33 car pictured here. The 2000GT’s 2.0-liter inline-six produced roughly 200 horsepower in racing trim, and the Shelby-prepared Toyotas proved surprisingly competitive against Porsche. Patrick finished second in the C-Production championship standings.

Don’t let the Celica badge fool you. This is about as closely related to a production Celica as a NASCAR stock car is to the sedan it resembles. Developed by Dan Gurney’s All American Racers, the IMSA GTO Celica used a tube-frame chassis, rear-wheel drive, and a heavily turbocharged inline-four engine producing as much as 600 horsepower. In 1987, Chris Cord and Toyota captured the IMSA GTO drivers’ and manufacturers’ championships. Not bad for a highly dramatized economy car.

Few vehicles are more immediately recognizable in off-road racing than Ivan “Ironman” Stewart’s red-and-white Toyota trucks. Built by Precision Preparation Inc. (PPI), these machines helped Stewart become one of the most successful drivers in desert racing history. Across his career, Stewart accumulated 84 victories and 10 championships, including 17 Baja 500 wins and three Baja 1000 victories. His nickname came from his willingness to tackle grueling desert events without a co-driver. Just imagine spending hundreds of miles bouncing around inside this thing. Sadly, most of us have only driven one of these via the Ironman “Ivan” Offroad Racing arcade game.

My absolute favorite in the room, and easily the most intimidating. Developed by Dan Gurney’s All American Racers, the Eagle MkIII competed in IMSA’s outrageously fast GTP category during the early 1990s. A turbocharged inline-four engine produced more than 800 horsepower, while its sophisticated aerodynamics generated enormous downforce. With Juan Manuel Fangio II leading the charge, the Eagle helped Toyota capture consecutive IMSA GTP manufacturers’ championships in 1992 and 1993. At one point, the Eagle won 17 consecutive races. It was so dominant that very little competition remained.

The Lexus LFA was already an engineering spectacle, thanks largely to its screaming 4.8-liter V-10. But Toyota wasn’t content to let its supercar spend its life sitting in collectors’ garages. The LFA became an important part of the early Gazoo Racing effort, participating in grueling endurance races at the Nürburgring. The program also helped Toyota develop its philosophy of using motorsports to improve road cars, with company chairman Akio Toyoda personally participating in racing and vehicle development.

Lexus decided that another 10 horses, sharper suspension, quicker shifts, and a gigantic fixed carbon-fiber wing were needed for the LFA. And they were right. The resulting Nürburgring Package was originally limited to just 50 examples. In 2011, one lapped the Nürburgring in 7:14.64, an astonishing time for a road car of its era. Just to the right of the LFA sits a Toyota Supra MkIV, the very one Toyota master driver Hiromu Naruse used to teach Akio Toyoda how to hustle a car.

Before Toyota finally conquered the 24 Hours of Le Mans, it built this legendary hybrid prototype. The TS040 combined a 3.7-liter V-8 with electric motors driving both axles, producing approximately 1000 horsepower. Rather than conventional batteries, the car used supercapacitors to store energy recovered during braking. Toyota won both the World Endurance Championship manufacturers’ and drivers’ titles in 2014, before introducing an extensively revised version of the TS040 for 2015.

The Lexus RC F GT3 seen here, yawning with its giant grille, has spent many years competing in motorsports. Its 5.4-liter V-8 engine helped Vasser Sullivan Racing become a formidable competitor in IMSA, despite facing newer, turbocharged rivals. The team captured the 2023 GTD Pro championship, with victories at Watkins Glen and Long Beach. It followed that up with a class victory at the 2024 12 Hours of Sebring. 2026 was the RC F’s final year in IMSA, and it will be replaced at the 24 Hours of Daytona in January by the GR GT3 race car.

The Supra’s return to the street also brought a new Toyota race car. Developed for customer racing in the GT4 category, the GR Supra GT4 shares its 3.0-liter turbocharged inline-six with the road-going Supra, but adds suspension, brakes, aerodynamics, and a stripped-out interior that’s only tolerable in race cars. Its initial specification produced approximately 430 horsepower, subject to racing balance-of-performance regulations. Unlike the wild prototypes elsewhere in this collection, the Supra GT4 remains closely related to something you might drive to work.

Think of the GR86 Cup Car as the affordable end of Toyota’s motorsports spectrum. Built for Toyota’s single-make GR Cup racing series, which began in 2023, the car retains the production model’s 2.4-liter flat-four engine but replaces its manual transmission with a six-speed sequential gearbox. The goal is to provide drivers with relatively accessible, closely matched machinery that emphasizes driving talent instead of checkbook size. Plus, it’s one of the few cars in the museum that you can also drive in iRacing.

Developed for SRO’s TC America touring-car championship, the GR Corolla TC starts with Toyota’s 300-hp, turbocharged 1.6-liter three-cylinder engine and retains the GR-Four all-wheel-drive system. Interestingly, it also uses the production car’s eight-speed automatic transmission. The transformation includes upgraded suspension, Alcon brakes, a roll cage, and aggressive aerodynamic bodywork.

The ‘Midlife Crisis’ Isn’t Real. The Actual Risk Is Believing the Myth.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Knowing the reality can benefit your physical and mental health.

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

If you ask someone what comes to mind when they hear the word midlife, 99.9 percent of the time the answer will be very specific: crisis. Ideas about middle age tend to skew negative, no matter the cause.

As a midlife researcher, this is worrying to me because I know just how pernicious these legacy beliefs can be. Yet the most prevalent negative interpretations of midlife are not supported by the research. The truth is so much more hopeful and, frankly, interesting.

While it’s hard to predict exactly who will experience this, it’s likely that 10 to 20 percent of people in midlife will go through some intense period of challenge and growth. For those folks, it’s important to remember that this isn’t a bad thing. First of all, the “crisis” is only part of the story, and not even the most interesting part. Whatever challenges you face are likely to be followed by changes to your life that will help you become freer, stronger, and more tuned in to what you really want for yourself in the second half of your life.

Debunking the myth of the midlife crisis isn’t just about correcting the record—it’s about harm reduction. Cultural notions have a real-time effect on our perception of our health and well-being in our middle years, and if the “information” is more myth than reality, that can have negative effects on our physical, psychological, and cognitive health.

Research done by Becca Levy, PhD, a professor of public health and psychology at Yale, and summarized in her book Breaking the Age Code, has shown the power of both negative and positive beliefs about aging to impact our healthspan and lifespan. Levy found that those who had more positive images of aging (e.g., being healthy and active) lived longer than those with the more stereotypical views about older adults as weak, forgetful, and slow. She also found that those with a more positive mindset about aging were healthier, with lower blood pressure and a lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease.

Beliefs about aging can eclipse the truth in many arenas. If a woman in her 50s whose children have just left the nest goes to see her physician about feeling excessively fatigued, depressed, and sleepless, the cliché of the midlife crisis makes it more likely that her concerns will be dismissed. This can lead to misdiagnosis for more serious conditions such as depression, autoimmune disorders, or hormonal deficiencies, which if recognized can be treated properly.

One of the best ways to set ourselves up for success when the sea of life starts to get choppy is to pay close attention to the way we are thinking about our story. Whether we know it or not, we are always telling ourselves stories in order to make meaning out of what happens to us and how it makes us feel. When we’re going through a rough patch, it helps immensely to take control of the narrative—moving from a negative frame of mind to a more hopeful one that emphasizes agency and opportunity. On the opposite end of the spectrum from folks who enter midlife ready for battle, many enter midlife on autopilot. Some assume they are stuck with the decisions made early in adulthood. Others want out but don’t imagine the alternatives or can’t see ways to improve circumstances.

Either way, it’s important to remember that it is possible to make tweaks or small changes in midlife; they don’t have to be radical ones. One of my colleagues on the MacArthur Foundation Research Network on Successful Midlife Development used to say, “Some people are so fed up at work that they quit their jobs in frustration, when all they really needed was a two-week vacation.” When we sleepwalk through midlife, at best it’s a missed opportunity for growth, and at worst it simply delays the challenges that will inevitably surface. What we know from the research is that middle age—whether or not you go through a “crisis”—is the time to reflect and make change.

I like to think of midlife as halftime in sports: Some of the important plays have been made, but not all. While a little discomfort with the poor choices of our past isn’t a bad thing, getting mired in regret isn’t something that helps anybody. You have the opportunity to breathe, examine what has come before, and start analyzing what you need to do next to reach your goals or modify them. There is plenty of time left after midlife, so reflection can help people see how they might move in different directions.

For those who don’t know where to begin when it comes to reflecting on their life story and even changing it, there is good research to suggest that a “life review” can help. When done well, a midlife review can help you make sense of the past and bring clarity to your goals moving forward (find out more about how to do it below).

One strategy is to do life reviews at the decade milestones (40, 50, 60) and possibly at the halfway marks (45, 55, 65). Another strategy is to do it when there are significant life events that might require some readjustments, such as going through a divorce, a job loss, or having a child leave home. Midlife is a great time to revisit old stories and even consider some rewrites and new beginnings.

It is important to keep in mind that it is unlikely that you will have accomplished everything you expected to by age 40, 50, or even 60. And it is unrealistic to think you will be happy with all areas of your life. If you think you are having a midlife crisis, ask yourself whether it’s really a crisis or if it’s just that you’re not happy with how some things are going. Maybe it’s just a time to make some changes. The life-shattering, ego-dissembling portrayal of the inescapable midlife crisis we have all become accustomed to is neither inevitable nor universal.

For the first time we saw a peek behind the mask, one that has very often been one portraying an optimistic and positive expression. Amorim knew there was no point attempting to lie to the supporters, the media but above all to himself.

What we know is that Amorim echoed the ferocity of these sentiments when speaking in the dressing room after full-time in what was likely the first dressing down that the team got. Six players were reported to have disappointed more than the others, too.

Above all, what stood out was the fact that the head coach opted to question whether his players wanted it enough. This was no longer a question of learning an identity, rotation or an injury crisis: it became a matter of heart.

Another intriguing extract from the post-Ferencvaros tirade that Amorim went on was about the squad’s ability to balance playing in two different competitions, namely Serie A and the Europa League.

“We’re not ready to be competitive on two fronts. We need to work on our way of looking at football and how we approach matches. There are no excuses tonight, there’s nothing I can say.

“I have to focus on the league, obviously, but we also have to do well in the Europa League. We must and can win games despite the rotation. We have to be ready – we’re not now – to play and win every three days.

Once again, Amorim offered a bit of a revelation: the league is the absolute priority for Milan this season. It may not exactly be revolutionary information, given that a top four spot remains the most accessible avenue to the main objective for 2026-27: securing a Champions League return.

And yet, in his emotional outburst, the former Sporting CP and Manchester United boss told us all in black and white that Serie A will be the focus. As a result, it’s right to expect more ups and downs in Europe, especially with such a depleted squad.

There is an element of observational realism about Amorim’s words; almost an admission of defeat in his attempts to spin two plates at the same time and get the most out of the resources at his disposal. Does it suggest a Europa League resurgence is coming? Not exactly.

Amorim has not made a habit of mentioning individuals ever since his arrivals, both in his pre-match words but especially after games in which emotions are running high. However, he brought up someone who was absent for the Ferencvaros game.

What Amorim seems keen to hammer home is that progress for this season will be judged on the ability to bring the ceiling closer to the floor. In other words, we have seen the potential of this team already with some impressive wins, yet the base level for performances remains frighteningly low.

Consistency is not just about being able to string together several positive results in a row, something the current Rossoneri do not seem capable of. It is about the repeated displaying of an idea and an identity every few days, to suggest the team are getting closer to the vision the coach has.

College Football Playoff will expand to 24 teams starting next season

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

College football will expand its playoff to 24 teams beginning next year in a move that resets decades of tradition surrounding a once all-important regular season and dumps conference championship showdowns that used to be among the sport’s marquee events.

The announcement from the College Football Playoff and ESPN on Monday made official a deal that’s been in the works for months. The network reported it would pay an additional $1.7 billion over five years on top of the original $7.8 billion contract that runs through the 2031 season.

The new format will grant the Power Four conference champions automatic spots and give one more to the best team from the Group of Six. But that slot and the other 19 will be decided by a selection committee that will serve as the latest set of decision makers in college football’s long history of polls and opinions having lasting impacts on teams’ fates.

Beginning next year, around 30% of college football’s 68 power-conference schools will have a chance to play for the title, drawing the CFP closer to the NFL, which allows 43% of its teams into the playoffs. It leaves behind the last vestiges of yesteryear, when between four and eight teams entered December with a legitimate shot to win it all.

It’s a seismic shift that even ESPN conceded comes with some question marks. The network pays billions to televise regular-season games that might — or might not — have more meaning under the new system.

“In all honesty, it’s impossible to know for sure,” ESPN senior vice president Nick Dawson said in a conference call.

The revenue generated from the playoff expansion will presumably make up for the estimated $345 million the Power Four conferences generated in TV money for their title games. ESPN would then not be on the hook for $140 million of the extra money due to cancellation of the title games, the network reported.

How an expanded CFP will work

The top eight seeds will receive first-round byes in the new format and the playoff will start on the second weekend in December, with the first two rounds of games on campus. The traditional Army-Navy game will be played on the first weekend in December, which used to be reserved for conference title games.

Both the Southeastern and Big Ten conferences, whose approval was needed to adopt this change, and their Power Four brethren — the Big 12 and Atlantic Coast Conference — are banking that most of the additional spots will go to their members so their second-tier teams will have new motivation to keep spending big in an era of NIL payments and $50 million football rosters.

“I think it will be healthy for teams like our current situation, building to try to get to that point,” said coach Barry Odom, who is trying to rebuild at Purdue.

The first final for the 24-team playoff remains set for Jan. 24, 2028, in New Orleans. Traditional bowl games will still host the quarterfinal and semifinal rounds.

As for the rest of the bowls, most of them operated or televised by ESPN: “No decisions have been made at this time,” Dawson said.

As part of the expansion, the CFP is requiring Power Four teams to play at least 10 regular-season games against conference foes, other Power Four opponents and Notre Dame. Group of Six teams will have to play 10 games against conference opponents.

In theory, these requirements will keep everyone in the same ballpark when it comes to strength of schedule, which figures to play a huge role in the selection committee’s considerations.

Special treatment vanishes for Notre Dame

Notre Dame didn’t receive any guarantees, the way it did this year when, if it’s ranked in the top 12 by the CFP for what will be the last year of the 12-team postseason, it will get a spot.

Coach Marcus Freeman said he looks at the change as a net positive, in part because a team like his can now presumably afford more than one loss, and in part because of the new scheduling requirements. Notre Dame is one of only two independents left in major college football.

“I don’t want this to be like March Madness and have 60-something teams making the playoff,” he said. “But the more opportunities we have to make the playoff, it helps us.”

The new schedule also guarantees two off weeks during a regular season that will begin the week before Labor Day.

A bigger playoff changes the nature of the college regular season

The move to 24 teams marks the second expansion of the playoff since it began in 2014 as a four-team affair. Right after it moved to 12 in 2024, most thought it would eventually max out at 16, which was the SEC’s preference.

But the Big Ten wanted to go bigger, while the SEC balked at that, concerned about the league title game — a property it invented in 1992 and made billions from over the decades — along with the marketability of CFP games featuring third- and fourth-place finishers in power conferences.

ESPN took care of the money problem. Now, the question is whether fans will embrace an NFL-style free-for-all that could include about one-third of the Power Four’s 68 teams, depending on what happens with Group of Six schools whose role in these big-money power grabs seems to diminish with every move.

It’s the latest change for a sport that, for decades, built its distinct character and generated controversy by leaning on media and coaching polls to hand out national titles.

Like then, critics see a risk in giving more power to a selection committee that hasn’t really mastered four- or 12-team brackets. The exclusion of Florida State in 2023 and Notre Dame last season stand out as the best examples, and the first rankings this season left people scratching their heads again.

Skeptics also view the expanded playoff as another dilution of what is largely seen as one of the most important regular seasons in sports, replacing potential matchups between, say, Alabama and Georgia in an SEC title game with a 13 seed vs. 20 seed game that would pit, say, Kentucky and South Carolina.

Fan reaction “certainly weighed into the decision,” Dawson said.

“Ultimately, we just need to make a spectacular playoff,” he said. “The CFP has a responsibility to our fans, they’re the lifeblood of college football. We owe them to make this great. That really is our intent.”

As Big Ten commissioner Tony Petitti argued from the beginning, a postseason that gives bubble teams hope and keeps others vying for home-field advantage or byes will bring more eyes — not fewer — to the end of a regular season that sometimes boils down to only three or four traditional rivalry games with meaning.

Of course, nobody can be certain how things will play out. The only sure thing is that college football will never look quite the same.

“If they think it’s a good thing for college football, then I’ve got to believe that this is the route we’re headed,” Wisconsin coach Luke Fickell said, “and we’re going to make it a great thing for college football.”
