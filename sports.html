Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern. Truly growing beyond alcohol often requires us to develop new skills and tools to navigate the challenges we once relied on alcohol to handle. For the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop, learning to say no is one of those skills.

Fortunately, like most skills, saying no is something we can learn through practice. Personally, I've found that having a few ready-to-use ways to say no makes a world of difference. That's why I've put together 7 of my favorite scripts for saying no with grace.

CFP committee needs to show its work after Alabama over UCLA ranking

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Alabama is 8-2. UCLA is 10-0. And in the latest College Football Playoff (CFP) rankings that were released on Tuesday night, the Crimson Tide came in one spot (No. 3) ahead of the Bruins (No. 4) for a second straight week.

That is just one of the many things that has fueled debate the last couple weeks about whether the CFP committee favors the SEC over the Big Ten, Big 12 and ACC. I don't believe the answer is as simple as them being biased, but I also understand why people could think they are.

Because much like when Nate Bargatze was asked how many liters are in a gallon, or how many yards are in a mile in the "Washington's Dream" sketch on Saturday Night Live, "nobody knows" what the CFP committee values when it comes to their rankings.

The fix for this should be simple: tell everyone how you go about determining the rankings. Or, more bluntly, which advanced metrics are used to help rank the top-25 teams.

When asked about that very thing earlier this week by The Athletic, CFP executive director Rich Clark danced around the question a bit and ultimately said this:

"Just to let you know, we have not provided those publicly. Basically, there's so many metrics to really give that out publicly with an explanation of them isn't really feasible right now. It is something that we're considering. We're looking at this in the offseason to see what metrics and how we would present those potentially in the coming seasons."

The committee is not hiding the fact it uses metrics to help set their rankings, but why not tell fans how many, and which, metrics are used? And which ones are the most important? And how were those metrics determined to be more important than the others?

And how is it not "feasible" to simply say which metrics you use? Perhaps Clark means it isn't feasible to show every metric in a clean, TV-friendly graphic. Maybe not. But with ESPN holding the CFP's media rights through the 2031 season, it's fair to wonder how much input the network has in what the public sees.

One metric we know the CFP values is strength of schedule. Committee Chair Hunter Yurachek — the athletic director for Arkansas — has defended Alabama's ranking due to the Crimson Tide's schedule. They rank sixth in strength of record and sixth in strength of schedule on ESPN's Football Power Index (FPI).

That may be a fine and dandy explanation to a casual college football fan, but ESPN owns the media rights to the CFP, so using ESPN's own metrics is really suspect.

Other than that, what else does the committee value? Head-to-head results don't really appear to be one of them. CBS Sports noted the committee has valued head-to-head results in some cases, like Alabama over Kentucky, but not in others.

One prime example is…you guessed it…Alabama being six spots ahead of Tennessee despite the Volunteers beating the Tide by 16 points this season. Yurachek explained that decision by citing Alabama's three wins over teams in the committee's top-25.

Now, to be fair, the metrics do back up the strength of schedule debate in Alabama's favor. UCLA is first in strength of record, but just 67th in strength of schedule. With a non-conference schedule that featured teams like San Diego State (96th in FPI) Cal (97th) and Nevada (98th), that isn't incredibly surprising.

Alabama's non-conference schedule wasn't much better. Florida State (No. 40) is a shell of what it once was and is in the midst of a coaching search, and East Carolina (No. 89) and FCS Chattanooga don't move the needle either. At least not to the point where they should definitively be ahead of the Bruins with two losses.

If the strength of schedule and strength of record metrics are what the committee favors more than others, just say it. A published methodology protects the committee more than this current approach does. Instead, every close call we get currently in the rankings can look like a favor to ESPN and the SEC.

But perhaps it isn't the strength of schedule that matters; perhaps it's strength of schedule within a team's conference. ESPN's Heather Dinich said on Tuesday night's show that there is a "new metric" the committee is using this year to help them rank each team. And the metric certainly seems to favor schools where football "just means more."

So the reason No. 21 South Carolina is still ranked — even after losing last week, 34-6, to an Arkansas team (Yurachek's Arkansas team, just to remind you) that was 3-6 and had lost four of its last five games before that game — is because…the SEC's schedule is tougher? According to who, exactly? Who determines how difficult a conference's schedule really is?

Nobody knows, but it must also be the reason three other four-loss teams — No. 22 Oklahoma, No. 24 Florida, No. 25 Ole Miss — are also ranked.

Ultimately, the CFP Committee needs to be transparent before the college football season even begins. Members of the committee change every year, so the way they go about developing the rankings should be made available every offseason so we all know exactly what to expect. Then, if the committee changes their criteria midseason, those changes should be made public as well, along with legitimate reasons for why those changes were made.

Show your work, CFP Committee. Fans can handle it.

Julius Randle on Nets' 'embarrassing' blowout loss to Bulls: 'One of the worst games I've ever been a part of'

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Brooklyn Nets wore red, white and blue alternate uniforms on Wednesday night, throwing it back to the franchise's ABA heyday when they welcomed the Chicago Bulls to Barclays Center. The evening featured an appearance by 10 members of the teams that won ABA championships in 1974 and 1976 — including the legendary "Dr. J," Julius Erving.

"I fully anticipated the Nets to be so fired up having us here, they would blow Chicago out," the Hall of Famer said during an appearance on the YES Network broadcast midway through the second quarter. "But I'm looking at 62-39, so … I was wrong."

The Nets scored Wednesday's first basket, with young guard Nolan Traoré getting a runout dunk after Egor Dëmin picked off a Josh Giddey pass. A minute later, Bulls guard Norman Powell knocked down a 3-pointer to put Chicago on top … and, well, that was all she wrote for Brooklyn.

The Nets never again led, or even tied, the visitors, trailing by as many as 37 points in a truly dismal performance that settled as a 137-107 blowout loss. At home. At the hands of what had been a three-win team, with the NBA's fifth-worst offense and absolute worst defense … and without exciting lottery pick Caleb Wilson.

It was a wretched outing — one that saw the Nets commit 20 turnovers leading to 27 Chicago points, and allow 15 first-half 3-pointers and 76 first-half points, the third straight game in which they conceded 70-plus before intermission. Brooklyn gave up 62 total points in the paint, and sat idly by as eight different Bulls scored in double figures.

In the midst of the depressing showing, courtside cameras caught Brooklyn forward Julius Randle lighting into his teammates on the Nets bench:

After finishing the night with 17 points, four assists and no rebounds in 27 minutes of work in the lopsided loss, Randle — who's soldiered through a 17-win season with the Lakers, the post-Anthony Davis doldrums in New Orleans, and the 21-win end of the David Fizdale era in New York — sounded a similarly dour note about the state of affairs for the 3-11 Nets in his postgame press conference.

"That was embarrassing," Randle said, according to Brian Lewis of the New York Post. "Been in the league for 13 years, and that's one of the worst games I've ever been a part of. And not because of the score … but it's how we did it. That wasn't cool. Nothing about it was cool. We've got to find a way to figure out the root of it or whatever, but that's just unacceptable. It's just a pride, an effort, the competitive spirit — all of that, it was nonexistent."

The Nets entered Wednesday intending to celebrate the high-water mark in franchise history, but wound up closer to a historic nadir. According to Erik Slater of Clutch Points, the last two losses "mark the first time they've given up 135 points in back-to-back games since April 1 [and] 2, 1969."

That's a far cry from where the Nets hoped to be after trading this summer for Randle, a three-time All-Star and two-time All-NBA selection who'd starred for the cross-town New York Knicks before helping the Minnesota Timberwolves make a pair of playoff runs, to pair with incumbent sweet-shooting forward Michael Porter Jr. in the frontcourt, in hopes of restoring a franchise that had the third-worst record in the NBA over the preceding three seasons to respectability.

But despite the presence of the 20-point-scoring veteran forwards and some exciting flashes from young guards Traoré, Dëmin and rookie Mikel Brown Jr., Brooklyn has remained mired in the muck, ranking among the league's worst teams on both ends of the court and looking like a team without much of a sense of who it wants to be, how it wants to play, and what its path to success might look like.

"Very disappointed, just starting with effort," head coach Jordi Fernández said after the game, according to C.J. Holmes of the New York Daily News. "We were not a competitive group. We were not a connected group. We were not a selfless group. I saw selfishness. I saw pointing fingers, and I saw guys that didn't always play hard."

That's not the brand of ball that Fernández has preached since arriving in Brooklyn in 2024. The team's desultory effort, though, suggests that maybe what he's preaching isn't quite getting across.

"I can try to say it in Spanish, because in English, they don't understand," Fernández quipped after the game. "So I'll keep trying and find whatever language works. But yeah, our effort was not good."

"I think when you win, you find ways to win. When you lose, you find excuses. Seems like a lot of excuses," Randle said, according to Collin Helwig of NetsDaily. "So, you know, for me, I've got to go back into work. I've got to go back into the film. I've got to figure out — I've got to look myself in the mirror tonight, too, like everybody else, and figure out what I can do better. Not point the finger or anything. Like, I got to figure out as a man [...] that has played this game since a kid and love it, and has given a lot to me, I got to figure out how I can help the team and be better for the team. A Pop-Star Acting Newbie, the Afterlife and “Dirty Energy”: The Making of ‘La Bola Negra’

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Directing duo Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo stretch a love story across the Spanish Civil War, three generations and.the great beyond.

Javier Calvo was traveling with his filmmaking partner, Javier Ambrossi, when he read Alberto Conejero’s play La Piedra Oscura (The Dark Stone) and the idea for La Bola Negra (The Black Ball) took hold. Conejero’s drama, set in a military hospital during the Spanish Civil War, imagines the last night of Rafael Rodríguez Rapún — a Republican lieutenant and the real-life lover of playwright Federico García Lorca — in conversation with Sebastián, the young Nationalist soldier who is to execute him at dawn.

“I imagined this little gay boy having to leave his town, all this tragedy, and how this happened to my country only 90 years ago, and having a gay character in the civil war,” says Calvo. “We never talk about that. Where were we? What happened to us? It hit me, and I started to cry. The film, co-written with Conejero, braids three timelines. In 1932 Granada, Carlos (Milo Quifes), a young upper-class Andalusian, is blackballed from an exclusive casino because he’s gay — the premise of Lorca’s unfinished play La Bola Negra, of which only four pages survive. In 1937, Sebastián (singer- songwriter Guitarricadelafuente) keeps watch over Rafael (Miguel Bernardeau). And in 2017 Madrid, Alberto (Carlos González) learns that he is Sebastián’s grandson. Sebastián has left him the rest of Lorca’s play — pages the film imagines into being — which he had promised Rafael he would save. Those pages — verified by Glenn Close, who plays a Spanish-speaking American scholar named Isabelle Durand — lead Alberto and his relatives to discover that Sebastián spent most of his life secretly living with another man.

“This is a movie about what we pass on to the next generation,” Ambrossi says. “It’s about inheritance.”

The co-directors, known as Los Javis among their collaborators because of their shared first names, reassembled their usual crafts team to begin shaping each timeline: cinematographer Gris Jordana, editor Alberto Gutiérrez, production designer Roger Bellés, costume designer Ana López Cobos, composer Raül Refree and casting directors Eva Leira and Yolanda Serrano.

The directors were already keen on Guitarricadelafuente and Bernardeau for their respective roles, as well as González for the part of Alberto, which Ambrossi calls “a creation.”

“He’s so comedic and flamboyant, but we wanted to give him a role where he can prove the actor that he is because sometimes queer people are given the same [stereotypical] roles,” Ambrossi says.

Quifes, who like Guitarricadelafuente makes his screen debut in the film, was found through an open casting call. “He has a sense of melancholy about him, but it’s not about hopelessness; there’s a sense of hope and beauty,” says Leira. “And he has such a beautiful gaze. It’s a gaze that comes from ancient times.”

Los Javis also knew they wanted to shoot in 35mm from the outset, which aligned with DP Jordana’s approach. “You don’t have to fight the format to get images,” she says of 35mm film. “If you use the right light direction, the rest just comes together. It’s like magic.”

Light also drove the design of the opulent casino in Granada, the only set built from scratch. Everywhere else, the production restored existing buildings, hiring local artisans who still work with prewar methods. “At first glance, it looked like something crazy and unnecessary when we built it in the middle of Spain, but when you open the windows, you see Spain, not a green screen,” says production designer Bellés, who commissioned artist Ignasi Monreal to paint the mural on the ceiling in the style of Joaquín Sorolla, “one of the great painters of Spanish light,” he explains.

The windows were made of sugar glass so that they could shatter safely when the casino explodes near the end of the film after being overtaken by a mob as Carlos makes a final case for admission. The remaining structure was then filled with fake snow as the scene transitions to Carlos lying on a mountainside, realizing he has died from the bomb’s explosion. The shot, captured at an altitude of 3,000 meters (more than 9,800 feet high) in the snowy region of the Sierra Nevadas, brought to life elements described in Lorca’s poem — like the skiers on the slopes — which also inspired Guitarricadelafuente’s original song, “La Nieve (The Snow),” that plays in the background.

“With Federico García Lorca, there’s a lot of powerful imagery and symbolism, like in the beginning of the film, the horse running past Sebastián is a representation of untamable desire,” Guitarricadelafuente explains. “ ‘La Nieve’ symbolized the end of a journey and approaching death. I had to leave the emotional baggage of Sebastián to fully understand what he was carrying to be able to write that.”

Refree took a hands-on approach with Penélope Cruz, who portrays Nené Romero, a cupletista cabaret singer. “She told me, ‘I’m not a singer,’ but as soon as I heard her singing … I fell in love with her singing,” he says. “The problem was that she hadn’t created the character yet.”

Over the course of several days, Refree went to Cruz’s home for rehearsals, and she began to craft the persona of Nené, who serves as a portal to a world in which Sebastián can be free to be his true self when he sees her perform “Las Tardes del Ritz (Afternoons at the Ritz)” and “Batallón de modistillas (Seamstress Battalion)” on the base.

“My goal was to have this kind of music that she could be influenced by when talking to the soldiers and to create this evocative thing, like making two men play together as a couple,” Refree explains of the first number. “The second one needed a dirty energy that would make the soldiers lose control and want to touch her. So we created something very rhythmic and feminine at the same time.”

Wardrobe also helped shape Nené. Cobos drew from research on 19th century cuplé singers, like María del Consuelo Vello Cano, to sketch Cruz’s feathery, embellished ensemble.

“When she saw the white dress, she said, ‘This is it; she has to be wearing white,’ and Gris also said it would be helpful because we don’t have a lot of lights in the theater,” says Cobos. “[For the second number,] because I know Penélope’s a big fan of Mugler, I went through their images and found these overalls and dresses that reminded me of military costumes. So I did my own interpretation of them, and it worked really well because it helped her move and it was very comfortable for her to do the choreography with the tank and all of the other dancers.”

In the final scene of the film, Sebastián reads a poem with the words, “Mira, que nos acechan todavia (Beware, they still haunt us),” after being forced to shoot Rafael. It’s the final verse of “Love Sleeps in the Poet’s Breast,” one of the Sonetos del Amor Oscuro (Sonnets of Dark Love), written by Lorca for Rafael, expressing his forbidden desire for him amid the looming threat of societal persecution.

For Calvo, the warning is just as relevant today. “We had it on the whiteboard, and it was kind of a guide [for us],” he says. “Putting it [at the end of the film] was perfect and so significant because they do still haunt us.”

While training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real plan, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. Steve Sarkisian shrugged off Arch Manning's leap into his arms late Saturday night in Baton Rouge, then put up two fingers. He should have instead formed a zero with his hand after Manning's game-winning touchdown pass to running back Raleek Brown.

The initial motion from Texas football's head coach signaled he wanted the Longhorns to attempt for a two-point conversion after taking a 22-21 lead over LSU with three seconds remaining. But his initial hand motion wasn't followed by a true two-point attempt. Instead, Manning took the snapfrom center Connor Robertson and immediately kneeled down, ceding any possible points in its eventual 22-21 victory.

How the hell did the Texas Longhorns win that? The hard way.

Texas could have lost to 23-22 if it turned the ball over and allowed an LSU defensive score on the two-point attempt. The chance of that event occuring was slim. But Sarkisian still opted to avoid the chance altogether, making a heady decision in the process. What changed Sarkisian's mind after his initial signal to go for two? He credited information from two Longhorns staff members in particular.

Video from Saturday night showed a man in a white hoodie and baseball hat speaking in Sarkisian's ear shortly after Manning's celebration. The man in question: John Michael Jones, Texas' director of player personnel. Jones also moonlights as an analytics expert, the on-the-sideline voice of a multi-member Texas analytics staff.

"(Jones) really manages a lot of the analytics for me," Sarkisian said Monday. "I get the information from analytics, I usually have a conversation with Mike, and then at times I may go converse with (defensive coordinator Will Muschamp). There's a lot of dialogue that goes into it, and then ultimately I make the call on whatever it is."

Sarkisian said Jones provides input on a variety of situations, including kneel downs, timeout usage, penalty acceptance and 10-second runoff decisions. The kneel down after Texas' last-minute touchdown was an especially prudent decision, one Sarkisian says came from Jones and Michael Bimonte, the Longhorns' assistant quarterback coach and co-passing game coordinator.

"I was excited, too, don't get me wrong. In your mind, we just won the game, but there are still three seconds," Sarkisian said as he explained his post-touchdown jubilation. "But below five seconds, with the lead, you take the knee to not even put the ball in harm's way at all. I thought both Mike Bimonte and John Michael (Jones) were very clear that this was a take-a-knee situation.

"I was already saying 'go-for-2', in my mind it was safe. They already went to, 'it's below five (points), this is a take-a-knee, be-safe-situation."

The Austin American-Statesman was unable to reach Jones or Bimonte for comment as of Monday.

Jones and Bimonte aid Sarkisian each Saturday. They also lead a key element of the Longhorns' weekly preparation.

Texas holds weekly "situational-masters meetings," per Sarkisian, a labor of love for Bimonte in particulaer. The offensive staffer "scours" the web to find examples of smart football decisions across the NFL, college football and high school football, and he then presents his findings to Texas coaches and players. There's also a presentation of "anti-situational masters," moments, in which Bimonte shows Texas players and coaches a collection of avoidable mistakes.

Sarkisian said Texas places an emphasis on raising the "football IQ" of its players. Jones and Bimonte appear to do exactly that for Sarkisian himself, who was convinced to make the proper decision on Texas' final offensive play at LSU.

Newcomers to the ballot are in bold.

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

We'll dive further into this group as we get closer to the Dec. 6 vote.

Giants legend Buster Posey joins the ballot, and he's one of those cases where I don't think a bunch of people need to delve deeply into his numbers. More than statistics, sometimes we get "feel" guys, as in, you just know he's a Hall of Famer. Posey qualifies there, I would think, for 75% of the electorate. We'll obviously do a deeper dive in the coming weeks, but even without the longevity component (Posey only played in parts of 12 MLB seasons), I think he's in.

As for the rest of the first-timers, the best case would be Jon Lester. Given the way a lot of similar starting pitchers have been treated in voting in recent years, my hunch is he'll be able to hang around on the ballot, but he has a major uphill battle in order to get in.

Then you're looking at Brett Gardner, Ryan Zimmerman, Kyle Seager, the short peak of Jake Arrieta and relief work of Andrew Miller. Good players worth mentioning, of course, but they won't sniff the Hall and it seems likely all behind Posey and Lester are one-and-dones on this ballot, as they'll fall short of 5% of the vote.

This is the sixth year for Alex Rodriguez on the ballot, which means it's the sixth time that I point out that we don't really need much time to go over his case. His numbers say he's one of the 10 best position players of all time, but his ties to PEDs are keeping him out to this point.

He did rise from 37.1% of the vote in 2025 to 40% last time, a new high-water mark. The progress is too modest to believe it's trending toward him getting in, but the number this year is certainly worth watching. If he somehow makes a leap to the 50% range, a deeper conversation might be in order.

What has changed in James Bond films over the decades

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In 1962, a martini-drinking womanizer saved the world for the first time in "Dr. No." As Robert Pattinson takes on the role of 007, here's how the films evolved.

After years of speculation, Amazon MGM Studios announced on Monday that Robert Pattinson would be taking on the role of James Bond following Daniel Craig's departure after five hit films in the spy series.

This year alone, Pattinson has starred in "The Odyssey," "The Drama" and "Primetime." He is also famous for his role as "Twilight" vampire Edward Cullen.

The upcoming Bond movie will be directed by Denis Villeneuve, an Oscar nominee known for movies such as the "Dune" trilogy and "Arrival."

Based on the spy novels by author Ian Fleming, the Bond franchise — about a British secret agent known for his charm and ⁠a ⁠fondness for martinis "shaken, not stirred" — is among the longest-running and highest-grossing in Hollywood history. ​​​​​​​

The first film in the series, "Dr. No," premiered in London on October 5, 1962.

Playing the leading role, Agent 007, was an unknown actor: Sean Connery.

In the film, we are first introduced to his character as he sits in a casino across from an attractive woman who is about to gamble away her fortune. When she asks 007 his name, the camera pans to Bond lighting a cigarette. With a smoldering look in his eye, he utters the legendary words: "Bond, James Bond."

Shortly thereafter in the film, 007 receives an assignment to fly to Jamaica and investigate the murder of another M16 agent. He eventually tracks down a Chinese-German criminal scientist who is bent on destroying the US space program and achieving world domination.

In principle, this story is the basis of all of 25 James Bond films. Whether the villain is Dr. No, Blofeld, Goldfinger or Le Chiffre, they all aim for one thing: world domination. But to do so, they have to get past James Bond whose gunfire and wild chase scenes inevitably spoil their plans.

Although many fan forums consider "Dr. No" to be one of the best Bond films, the movie would never be made in the same way today.

Firstly, one could write entire books about the omnipresent sexism and machismo that the character James Bond has displayed over the decades.

Certainly there would have to be a thick chapter dedicated to the women in his films, who are typically portrayed as naive, willingly flinging themselves into Bond's arms, seeking protection. Sometimes they are sophisticated female antagonists who lose control at the thought of hopping into bed with the secret agent.

Whether friend of foe, each film had at least one Bond girl — and often more: More than 60 love interests have appeared in the films. Their names, like "Honey" or "Pussy Galore" would never be acceptable today. In more recent decades, female roles in Bond films have changed — as has Bond himself.

In the last five films in which Daniel Craig has played Bond, he is seen as a man who is tough on the outside and soft on the inside — and who is not always lucky with women.

Aside from the portrayal of women on screen, there are also plenty of other details in "Dr. No" that haven't quite stood the test of time.

Another sign of the zeitgeist in the 1962 film is that everyone smokes, which is a no-no by today's Hollywood standards. Cigarettes are found everywhere in the old film, from the casino to the hotel bar — and even the bedroom.

Smoking was commonplace in the US in the 1970s, so naturally this came across on the silver screen. In the 1970s TV series "Space: 1999" people were even depicted smoking on the moon, but by the 2000s, smoking was largely banned in many parts of the world due to its health risks.

One can also note in "Dr. No" that few people are wearing seat belts. When Bond drives off in his sports car to visit a woman, he is followed and a chase scene ensues. Bond, notably, is not wearing a seat belt.

His pursuers eventually drive off a slope and die, of course — after all, they aren't wearing seat belts either and cars didn't have airbags at the time. The vehicle explodes, prompting 007 to say, "They were obviously in a hurry to get to their own funeral."

In the older Bond films, all secretaries and receptionists are played by women, which in the 1960s, were considered standard female jobs.

Whether redheaded, blonde or brunette, secretaries are Bond's favorite targets for womanizing and they are all susceptible to his charms.

Throughout most Bond films, the agent has a romantic tension with Moneypenny, his boss's secretary, something which was not described in the original books.

In the 2012 film "Skyfall" Moneypenny is given a first name: Eve, and we finally learn her backstory. In this film, she is no longer a hopeless secretary in love, but a top-trained agent who accidentally shoots Bond and is reassigned to desk duty as punishment.

In any case, today Moneypenny's job title today would probably be "Office Manager" or "Executive Assistant" rather than "Secretary."

In the film, Dr. No tells James Bond that he is the child of a British missionary and a Chinese woman from an upstanding family. However, the character is portrayed by Canadian actor Joseph Wiseman who is wearing makeup to make his eyes look more "Asian."

While it used to be the norm in such movies to have East Asian characters played by white actors, today these cases of "yellowface" are frowned upon — just as it would be to wear blackface, putting black makeup on one's skin to portray a Black person.

If there was a "Dr. No" remake in the future, a Chinese actor would be more likely to play this role.

Daniel Craig already paved the way in modernizing James Bond by stripping away campy gadgets, punchlines and invincibility, replacing them with a grounded, psychological and emotionally vulnerable portrayal of a post-9/11 spy.

Now it is up to Robert Pattinson to revisit the famous MI6 agent. Film critics believe his background will allow him to add dark sophistication and nonconformism to the iconic espionage role.

Throughout his career, Robert Pattinson has managed to combine roles in blockbuster hits and indie gems, from Bruce Wayne in "The Batman" to a disposable worker who is cloned every time he dies in "Mickey 17."

Pattinson already has experience with classic spy archetypes: As the mysterious Neil in Christopher Nolan's sci-fi action thriller "Tenet," he assisted the protagonist in a time-bending espionage mission. He is also known for his eccentric personality and deadpan humor — qualities that could be useful for a modern Bond.

Thomas Frank confirmed as Crystal Palace manager after Pierre Sage’s sacking

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Frank’s first job since Spurs dismissal in February

He has been promised funds for January signings

Thomas Frank has been confirmed as Crystal Palace’s manager after Pierre Sage was sacked last week. Frank, out of work since being dismissed by Tottenham in February, was quickly identified as Palace’s main target and has signed a two-and-a-half-year deal at the Premier League’s bottom club.

Frank spoke to Palace in the summer about succeeding Oliver Glasner but decided he was not ready to return to the dugout because he wanted to take a break from football.

It is understood that the Dane has also attracted interest from Newcastle and Fulham in recent weeks after difficult starts for Matthias Jaissle and Álvaro Arbeloa respectively. Palace also considered Sean Dyche and Vítor Pereira. The chair, Steve Parish, led negotiations with Frank and assured him funds would be available for players in January. Parish described Frank as “the perfect fit”.

“Crystal Palace is an iconic club in England and in the Premier League,” Frank said. “I will do everything I possibly can to help and support the players and everyone around the place, and I’m really looking forward to meeting them.”

Palace have yet to confirm Frank’s full staff but it is understood Justin Cochrane – an assistant in Thomas Tuchel’s England setup who worked with Frank at Brentford and Tottenham – is expected to join him. There are also talks with Chelsea over Bernardo Cueva, who worked under Frank as Brentford’s set-piece coach before Keith Andrews and is tipped for an assistant’s role at Palace, who said Paddy McCarthy and the goalkeeping coach Andy Quy were staying to work under Frank.

Sage fell out with the Palace hierarchy after a haphazard summer window during which the club signed 11 players but failed to replace Maxence Lacroix, the France defender, adequately. Palace agreed a deal with Club Brugge for Joel Ordóñez but had to pull out of the deal after the Ecuador defender failed a medical owing to a fractured foot. Ordóñez is yet to return to action so they may have to look elsewhere.

The decision to sack Sage was made on Sunday evening but there was no confirmation of his departure until Thursday.
