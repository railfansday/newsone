After 3 Marathons Without a Plan, I Finally Followed One. Here’s What Happened in NYC.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

My 3:45 goal didn’t go to plan, but the NYC Marathon still delivered a win.

The first time I entered the New York City Marathon drawing, I got in. I’m not sure what possessed me that day to apply, having never run another race in my life. I figured I’d never get in, so why not? I played soccer growing up, and when the world shut down during COVID-19, running became the easiest way to get out of the house and see my friends. That’s where my running journey began.

My NYC Marathon training also happened during my senior year of college, which brought its own challenges. While I was excited to run, I also didn’t want to miss out on any of the socializing that happened that year. I did my best to make it to every party, every club soccer game, and anything in between while running as much as possible. I was scared that running was going to infringe on my everyday life and I wouldn’t have time to keep having fun. Despite all of that, I had caught the marathon bug and after finishing the New York City Marathon in 2022, I went on to run the Paris and Chicago Marathons in 2024.

While training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real plan, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. In between, I pieced together training information from influencers on Instagram and Google searches (this was pre-ChatGPT!). Now that I’m a Runner’s World+ member, I have all those resources right at my fingertips.

Although I caught the marathon bug almost instantly, I also felt defeated after New York. I knew I could have done better. Between New York City in 2022 and Paris in 2024, I never ran more than 10 miles at a time, but I was running enough that I knew I could do it again if I really wanted. I followed a similar approach for Chicago in 2024: Build up to one long run about a month out, then fill the rest of the weeks with shorter runs and one medium-length run. It wasn’t much of a plan, but it worked for me. My times kept improving, and I broke four hours for the first time in Chicago.

Looking back, I realize that my no-plan approach was partly a way to protect myself. If I never said my goal out loud, I couldn’t feel disappointed if I missed it. Breaking four hours in Chicago changed that. For the first time, I wanted to see what I could do with a real plan.

I was now living in the city I had expected to call home ever since I left college, so I really wanted this race to feel meaningful. For the first time in my marathon career, I downloaded a Runner’s World training plan, the 16-week Break 3:45 plan to be exact, printed it out, and started tracking all of my runs. I wanted to beat my time in Chicago and get as close to 3 hours and 45 minutes as possible.

In the beginning, this felt super daunting. I had never done an interval run in my life nor had I ever intentionally sought out hills to make my runs feel harder than they already did. Nonetheless, I found my old Garmin Forerunner 55 and learned how to upload intervals to run up and down the West Side Highway. When the plan called for hills, I made my way back to the Queensboro Bridge for the first time since 2022, ready to face it with excitement instead of fear. Even while pretending to be excited, the incline is no joke!

For a while, I really felt like I had gotten the hang of things. I actually started to recognize what different paces felt like during the intervals, instead of sprinting and pulling back and looking at my watch while I ran. And, I could now confidently run over many of the bridges in New York City, notably the Williamsburg and Manhattan, even though they aren’t even part of the marathon course. I wasn’t following the plan exactly, but I was able to reorganize the days of the week to hit all the workouts.

Near the end of August (roughly week eight of the plan), it became hot all the time, I was constantly traveling for weekend trips, and I had started to lose the excitement of running. So while I was making sure to hit all of the long runs, the interval training fell off completely. I was a little disappointed in myself because the interval training runs were workouts that made me feel like I was getting stronger each week. I had never done this type of workout before, so I could tell I was getting better each time.

To find the joy of running again, I really started to lean on my friends. Every week, I’d send out a few texts to see who was available and wanted to go for a run with me. I ran the loop in Central Park almost every week and met up with my friends in Brooklyn to run every Friday morning. I started to explore parts of the city I had never been to before, which helped ground me in my training and remember that running is supposed to be fun and is an exciting way to explore the city.

From my previous experience, I knew that running the long runs was the most important thing for me to feel confident on race day. So much of marathon running is mental, so knowing that I could run 20 miles (and have run 26.2 before), was enough for me. So, while I started with a plan, I started reverting back to my tried-and-true method—the just make sure you run the long runs plan.

Outside of the training plan itself, I leaned on Runner’s World a lot. I didn’t have access to this in the past so I didn’t feel confident about planning for prerun meals or even how to take gels during the race. I also found that being able to read runner stories online made me feel more connected to the community, which helped me mentally with my training.

Plan or no plan, I’m proud of myself! My official time was 3:57:39, so while I did not achieve my goal of breaking 3:45, I did run a sub-4 race, and following the Runner’s World plan helped me get there. The last time I ran this race, I was a senior in college and thought I had it all figured out. I was going to move to New York City, get a real adult job, and start the life I had been imagining. Similar to my experience following the Runner’s World plan, this is not how everything went. Instead, I moved to a different city after graduating, eventually found my way back to New York, and my life is so much more full than I ever expected because of it. Maybe things aren’t always supposed to go to plan, and that’s a good thing!

The great work done by the likes of Matthew Le Tissier in keeping the club in the Premier League was undone, and it was up to Harry Redknapp to secure an immediate return the following season having failed to save the club from the drop.

As ever when a club suffers relegation, there were huge changes to the playing squad at St Mary's, with the likes of Peter Crouch, Kevin Phillips and Fabrice Fernandes departing, and those three were among 24 departures during the whole campaign.

With so many outgoings, it was clear that there would be a lot of incomings too, especially with Redknapp at the helm, and 15 players arrived at St Mary's to boost their promotion push.

Saints barely spent any money either as they counted the cost of relegation, with Ricardo Fuller joining from arch-rivals Portsmouth for a bargain £90,000.

On the same day, the south coast outfit also completed the signing of experienced Polish centre-back Tomasz Hajto, who moved to England after spells in Germany with MSV Duisburg, Schalke and FC Nurnberg.

The then 32-year-old had more than 60 caps for his country at that point, and his signing was seen as a coup for Southampton as they looked to achieve instant promotion back to the promised land.

Speaking on the day he signed, Hajto said:

Southampton are a big club. Over the years I have watched a lot of Premier League football and they have always been there and I hope to help them get back there.

"They are among the favourites to go up and they have a lot of ambition to do that. They had 27 years at that level and deserve to be there."

Strangely, despite being a central defender, Hajto was handed the number seven shirt by the club, which had famously been worn by Matthew Le Tissier, one of the greatest Premier League players of all time, just a few years before.

Video from Saturday night showed a man in a white hoodie and baseball hat speaking in Sarkisian's ear shortly after Manning's celebration. "I was excited, too, don't get me wrong. In your mind, we just won the game, but there are still three seconds," Sarkisian said as he explained his post-touchdown jubilation. There's also a presentation of "anti-situational masters," moments, in which Bimonte shows Texas players and coaches a collection of avoidable mistakes.

The ballot for the BBWAA portion of the 2027 Baseball Hall of Fame vote was revealed on Monday, featuring 13 holdovers and 14 newcomers.

The full ballot, in alphabetical order, is Bobby Abreu, Jake Arrieta, Jay Bruce, Mark Buehrle, Asdrúbal Cabrera, Starlin Castro, Wade Davis, Brett Gardner, Cole Hamels, Félix Hernández, Torii Hunter, Jon Lester, Andrew Miller, Dustin Pedroia, Andy Pettitte, Buster Posey, Alex Rodriguez, Francisco Rodríguez, Jimmy Rollins, Pablo Sandoval, Ervin Santana, Kyle Seager, Joakim Soria, Chase Utley, Omar Vizquel, David Wright and Ryan Zimmerman. Newcomers to the ballot are in bold.

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

We'll dive further into this group as we get closer to the Dec. 6 vote.

Giants legend Buster Posey joins the ballot, and he's one of those cases where I don't think a bunch of people need to delve deeply into his numbers. More than statistics, sometimes we get "feel" guys, as in, you just know he's a Hall of Famer. Posey qualifies there, I would think, for 75% of the electorate. We'll obviously do a deeper dive in the coming weeks, but even without the longevity component (Posey only played in parts of 12 MLB seasons), I think he's in.

As for the rest of the first-timers, the best case would be Jon Lester. Given the way a lot of similar starting pitchers have been treated in voting in recent years, my hunch is he'll be able to hang around on the ballot, but he has a major uphill battle in order to get in.

Then you're looking at Brett Gardner, Ryan Zimmerman, Kyle Seager, the short peak of Jake Arrieta and relief work of Andrew Miller. Good players worth mentioning, of course, but they won't sniff the Hall and it seems likely all behind Posey and Lester are one-and-dones on this ballot, as they'll fall short of 5% of the vote.

This is the sixth year for Alex Rodriguez on the ballot, which means it's the sixth time that I point out that we don't really need much time to go over his case. His numbers say he's one of the 10 best position players of all time, but his ties to PEDs are keeping him out to this point.

He did rise from 37.1% of the vote in 2025 to 40% last time, a new high-water mark. The progress is too modest to believe it's trending toward him getting in, but the number this year is certainly worth watching. If he somehow makes a leap to the 50% range, a deeper conversation might be in order.

What has changed in James Bond films over the decades

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In 1962, a martini-drinking womanizer saved the world for the first time in "Dr. No." As Robert Pattinson takes on the role of 007, here's how the films evolved.

After years of speculation, Amazon MGM Studios announced on Monday that Robert Pattinson would be taking on the role of James Bond following Daniel Craig's departure after five hit films in the spy series.

This year alone, Pattinson has starred in "The Odyssey," "The Drama" and "Primetime." He is also famous for his role as "Twilight" vampire Edward Cullen.

The upcoming Bond movie will be directed by Denis Villeneuve, an Oscar nominee known for movies such as the "Dune" trilogy and "Arrival."

Based on the spy novels by author Ian Fleming, the Bond franchise — about a British secret agent known for his charm and ⁠a ⁠fondness for martinis "shaken, not stirred" — is among the longest-running and highest-grossing in Hollywood history. ​​​​​​​

The first film in the series, "Dr. No," premiered in London on October 5, 1962.

Playing the leading role, Agent 007, was an unknown actor: Sean Connery.

In the film, we are first introduced to his character as he sits in a casino across from an attractive woman who is about to gamble away her fortune. When she asks 007 his name, the camera pans to Bond lighting a cigarette. With a smoldering look in his eye, he utters the legendary words: "Bond, James Bond."

Shortly thereafter in the film, 007 receives an assignment to fly to Jamaica and investigate the murder of another M16 agent. He eventually tracks down a Chinese-German criminal scientist who is bent on destroying the US space program and achieving world domination.

In principle, this story is the basis of all of 25 James Bond films. Whether the villain is Dr. No, Blofeld, Goldfinger or Le Chiffre, they all aim for one thing: world domination. But to do so, they have to get past James Bond whose gunfire and wild chase scenes inevitably spoil their plans.

Although many fan forums consider "Dr. No" to be one of the best Bond films, the movie would never be made in the same way today.

Firstly, one could write entire books about the omnipresent sexism and machismo that the character James Bond has displayed over the decades.

Certainly there would have to be a thick chapter dedicated to the women in his films, who are typically portrayed as naive, willingly flinging themselves into Bond's arms, seeking protection. Sometimes they are sophisticated female antagonists who lose control at the thought of hopping into bed with the secret agent.

Whether friend of foe, each film had at least one Bond girl — and often more: More than 60 love interests have appeared in the films. Their names, like "Honey" or "Pussy Galore" would never be acceptable today. In more recent decades, female roles in Bond films have changed — as has Bond himself.

In the last five films in which Daniel Craig has played Bond, he is seen as a man who is tough on the outside and soft on the inside — and who is not always lucky with women.

Aside from the portrayal of women on screen, there are also plenty of other details in "Dr. No" that haven't quite stood the test of time.

Another sign of the zeitgeist in the 1962 film is that everyone smokes, which is a no-no by today's Hollywood standards. Cigarettes are found everywhere in the old film, from the casino to the hotel bar — and even the bedroom.

Smoking was commonplace in the US in the 1970s, so naturally this came across on the silver screen. In the 1970s TV series "Space: 1999" people were even depicted smoking on the moon, but by the 2000s, smoking was largely banned in many parts of the world due to its health risks.

One can also note in "Dr. No" that few people are wearing seat belts. When Bond drives off in his sports car to visit a woman, he is followed and a chase scene ensues. Bond, notably, is not wearing a seat belt.

His pursuers eventually drive off a slope and die, of course — after all, they aren't wearing seat belts either and cars didn't have airbags at the time. The vehicle explodes, prompting 007 to say, "They were obviously in a hurry to get to their own funeral."

In the older Bond films, all secretaries and receptionists are played by women, which in the 1960s, were considered standard female jobs.

Whether redheaded, blonde or brunette, secretaries are Bond's favorite targets for womanizing and they are all susceptible to his charms.

Throughout most Bond films, the agent has a romantic tension with Moneypenny, his boss's secretary, something which was not described in the original books.

In the 2012 film "Skyfall" Moneypenny is given a first name: Eve, and we finally learn her backstory. In this film, she is no longer a hopeless secretary in love, but a top-trained agent who accidentally shoots Bond and is reassigned to desk duty as punishment.

In any case, today Moneypenny's job title today would probably be "Office Manager" or "Executive Assistant" rather than "Secretary."

In the film, Dr. No tells James Bond that he is the child of a British missionary and a Chinese woman from an upstanding family. However, the character is portrayed by Canadian actor Joseph Wiseman who is wearing makeup to make his eyes look more "Asian."

While it used to be the norm in such movies to have East Asian characters played by white actors, today these cases of "yellowface" are frowned upon — just as it would be to wear blackface, putting black makeup on one's skin to portray a Black person.

If there was a "Dr. No" remake in the future, a Chinese actor would be more likely to play this role.

Daniel Craig already paved the way in modernizing James Bond by stripping away campy gadgets, punchlines and invincibility, replacing them with a grounded, psychological and emotionally vulnerable portrayal of a post-9/11 spy.

Now it is up to Robert Pattinson to revisit the famous MI6 agent. Film critics believe his background will allow him to add dark sophistication and nonconformism to the iconic espionage role.

Throughout his career, Robert Pattinson has managed to combine roles in blockbuster hits and indie gems, from Bruce Wayne in "The Batman" to a disposable worker who is cloned every time he dies in "Mickey 17."

Pattinson already has experience with classic spy archetypes: As the mysterious Neil in Christopher Nolan's sci-fi action thriller "Tenet," he assisted the protagonist in a time-bending espionage mission. He is also known for his eccentric personality and deadpan humor — qualities that could be useful for a modern Bond.

Why Your Body Is Begging You to Run More Trails Instead of Roads

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

(Photo: Cavan Images / Brent Doscher / Getty)

Running a marathon beats up your body. Your leg muscles will be damaged on a cellular level, which will translate into soreness and loss of strength that will persist for a week or more. Now imagine how much worse it would be if you were running one of those crazy mountain marathons with rough terrain and steep climbs and descents. It would be awful… right?

Maybe not, according to a new study in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise led by Mélanie Metra and Guillaume Millet of France’s Université Jean Monnet Saint-Étienne. Millet, who once finished third at the absurdly difficult 200-mile Tor des Géants mountain ultra, is one of the world’s leading trail running researchers. I’ve previously covered his research on the differences between trail and road runners, how to run uphill, how to succeed at Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc, and other trail-centric topics.

This time, Millet and his colleagues explore the question of whether road or trail running beats you up more. It’s not a perfect study (for reasons we’ll come back to), but it has some very interesting—and somewhat surprising—insights.

The researchers asked runners to undertake one of the following three challenges: a simulated road marathon of roughly 26 miles on a treadmill; a simulated trail marathon on a treadmill; or an actual trail marathon on a trail outside the lab.

They took a whole bunch of measures of “neuromuscular fatigue” before and after the marathon, then again one day, two days, and seven days later. These involved various measures of leg strength, maximal jumps, questionnaires about subjective pain, and blood tests to assess markers of muscle damage. The objective was to determine whether the fatigue and recovery profile would differ after the various types of running.

To make the three challenges roughly equivalent, they used the following rule of thumb: every 100 meters of uphill climbing is equivalent to an extra kilometer of flat running. With that in mind, the two trail marathons involved a total distance of 28K (just over 17 miles) with 1,400 meters (4,600 feet) of vertical ascent and descent, for a total “equivalent effort” of roughly 42K, which is the standard (flat) marathon distance.

They recruited a total of 46 runners, of whom 16 were primarily road runners and were assigned to the simulated road marathon. The rest were primarily trail runners, and they were divided equally between the two trail marathon groups. The fitness, age, and training characteristics of the three groups were fairly well matched. Everyone was instructed to run the marathon as fast as possible.

The overall finding was that the road marathon beat the runners up far more than either of the two trail marathons.

The road marathoners lost more strength, and were still showing a deficit two days later. There’s a similar pattern, albeit less pronounced, in the subjective ratings of soreness and pain. (Calf muscles, on the other hand, didn’t show much difference between the groups.)

The simplest interpretation is that road running causes more muscle damage than trail running.

This could be because road running is more repetitive: every stride is the same, so the same muscle fibers are being activated over and over until they get damaged. Trail running has more variety, both in the “technicity” of the terrain and in the shifts between flats, uphills, and downhills. The lack of differences between the simulated and real trail groups in the new study implies that technicity doesn’t really matter (since the treadmill has a very regular surface while the outdoor trail has an irregular one), so it’s the uphills and downhills that matter.

Another possibility is that it’s not just the variability in muscle activation—it’s also that all those hills force you to slow down, and in some cases even walk, so the slower pace reduces the load on your muscles.

Both of these factors may help explain one of the big riddles in the results, which is why all the downhills in the trail runs didn’t cause more damage. After all, marathoners complain that the slight downhills in the opening miles of Boston wreak havoc on their legs. But Boston’s smooth and gradual descent lets runners lock into a fast (and damaging) pace, while the irregular ups and downs of the trail marathon lead to a slower pace and more variable stride.

There are also some possible explanations that have less to do with the inherent differences between road and trail and more to do with the study design. One is that the groups weren’t random: the road runners had trained primarily on roads, and the trail runners primarily on trails. Perhaps it’s training on trails that makes your legs more resistant to muscle damage, rather than racing on trails being less damaging. Or perhaps there are other general differences between people who choose road versus trail running. The only way to check that possibility would be to randomize people to different groups.

The more serious problem, in my view, is that the overall average completion time for the road marathon was 3:54, compared to just 3:02 in the simulated trail group and 2:59 in the outdoor trail group. This suggests that “100 meters of climbing is equivalent to an extra kilometer of flat running” is an overly generous conversion factor. It seems very likely to me that I’d be more sore after a four-hour run than after a three-hour run, regardless of what the terrain was like.

To be fair, it’s not automatically true that longer runs cause more soreness than shorter ones. Pace matters, too. Millet himself has previously found that leg muscles are less fatigued after 200 miles at the Tor des Géants than after shorter races, because you simply can’t sustain a hard-enough-to-be-damaging pace for that long. But the difference in durations between the trail and road groups raises questions about the conclusions.

One reason I’m tempted (for better or worse) to wave away those questions is that the results fit with my own intuitions and experiences. I’ve never been as sore as I was after a road marathon; I’ve often been pleasantly surprised at how quickly my legs bounce back after a long ramble in the forest or mountains. We can’t yet call it a scientifically proven fact, but call it a strong hunch.

A Realistic View of What Kirk Cousins 2.0 Brings to the Vikings

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Minnesota Vikings made the move they had to make after a half-season of underwhelming quarterback performance in trading for Kirk Cousins. He will be the starter as soon as possible, which is likely to be this Sunday against the Packers. His arrival has come with much fanfare as the savior of the Vikings offense who, combined with the Vikings dominant defense and good special teams, could position the Vikings for a deep run in the post-season.

But a quarterback switching teams in mid-season is not without its difficulties, even with Cousins' familiarity with Kevin O'Connell's scheme and some of the key players around him from Justin Jefferson, Jordan Addison, and T.J. Hockenson to Brian O'Neill, Blake Brandel, and Christian Darrisaw. It's also an advantage that Cousins has been playing the entire season, so he doesn't have to get acclimated to playing again, as a backup quarterback who hasn't played would need to do.

If Cousins plays on Sunday (as expected at this point), there could be some hiccups as he works out timing and other rapport issues in his first game back after three years. But Cousins has been playing in the same type of offense under Klint Kubiak in Las Vegas, and is probably the most well-versed quarterback in Kevin O'Connell's scheme in the league- and one of the most well-suited to execute it.

The first graphic compares Cousins' rate and EPA efficiency in the play-action passing game, which is central to KOC's offense. He's been one of the more effective quarterbacks out of play-action for most of his career, and has run it at a relatively high rate for most of his 15-year career. He's also played at a relatively high rate from under center, which KOC also likes to do to make the play-action game more effective. Cousins' career passer rating from under center is 107.2, and in play-action it is 110.0. This season, Cousins' passer rating with play-action is 123.4.

The Vikings have used play-action more than the Raiders have so far this season. Part of that is game situation- both down-and-distance and score/time remaining. You would think that with the Vikings dominant defense, they would continue to be able to run play action at a high rate to maximize Cousins' strength in that area.

The second graphic represents Cousins' passing metrics to each part of the field. The big takeaway is that the lion's share of Cousins' passing yards and attempts are between the numbers. He's also had good success throwing at intermediate depth (10-19 yards) this season. That's another important element of KOC's scheme-targeting the middle of the field. Both Kyler Murray and Carson Wentz have been reluctant to target the middle of the field, which compromises the effectiveness of O'Connell's scheme. One of the staples of O'Connell's scheme is the dagger route combination, which has largely disappeared from run or targeted routes this season. It's also one of the most effective route combinations for Justin Jefferson and Jordan Addison.

Bottom line, having Kirk Cousins at quarterback should open up the entirety of KOC's playbook, but it may take some time for Cousins to re-familiarize himself with the playbook and get on the same page again with receivers. Still, I don't anticipate Cousins hesitating to get the ball out to open receivers as Murray and Wentz have done this season.

Since the last time Cousins played for the Vikings- three years ago- he had his Achilles tendon repaired and spent most of his time in Atlanta recovering from that. At that point it looked like he was done. His passes lacked velocity and his movement was slower than it had been. Apparently, he couldn't really push off of his foot with the repaired Achilles and that was negatively affecting his game. But after signing with the Raiders, it appears that Cousins has fully recovered from the Achilles injury and no longer has the issues that plagued him in Atlanta. The velocity is back and while Cousins has never been particularly mobile, he is able to move like he used to before the Achilles injury. According to Albert Breer, when the Vikings were looking into trading for Cousins, after, "meshing the scouts' and coaches' analysis, they saw Cousins as still possessing a live arm and deadly accuracy and having returned to the baseline of athleticism he set before tearing his Achilles in 2024, evidenced on tape in the keeper game that's essential to Kubiak's scheme."

I've watched all of Cousins' throws with the Raiders this year, and it is reminiscent of his previous stint with the Vikings. I don't know that his arm strength has declined much- it doesn't look like it- but there is a caveat. He can make all the throws, as you would expect from a starting NFL quarterback, but he needs to step-in to deeper throws for them to have the needed velocity. That may have been the case before as well but it is something that stood out when watching his tape this season. He can't launch balls down field with velocity off his back foot like Jordan Love or Caleb Williams. This is the impact of being his being 38 years old. Additionally, he has not become any more mobile over the last three years, and the scampering of Kyler Murray to pick up first downs here and there is definitely not in Cousins' bag, if that wasn't obvious. He has run for five-yard gains on a couple occasions this season, but that's about it. He is able to maneuver within the pocket effectively and does fine on bootlegs, but he's not going to outrun many pass rushers for very long. He's also not afraid to deliver the ball downfield in the face of oncoming pressure- and pay the price for it. He's also not afraid of throwing into tight windows and does so with regularity.

Cousins has 8 interceptions so far this season, three of which came last weekend against the 49ers. A few of them came on throws left a bit short, a few in tight window throws where ball placement and/or route were not ideal. One his receiver fell down. And the last one came with 4 minutes left in the 49ers game and he was just trying make something happen down multiple scores. But for the most part Cousins' interceptions came while being aggressive and playing on time making throws that Murray and Wentz have generally avoided this season.

Cousins hasn't been sacked much so far this season- just 11 times in 9 games. He's also the only quarterback who has not been sacked on a blitz so far this season. So, he's generally done an excellent job of avoiding the rush and getting the ball out- maybe only one of sacks can be blamed on him. And whereas Murray or Wentz may look to scramble to buy time, Cousins is mainly focused on getting the ball out. He's very much a pocket passer in that respect, with his only move typically being to step up in the pocket to avoid the edge rush.

The Raiders offense has different strengths than the Vikings, and that has influenced Cousins' play. Their offensive line is similar strength as the Vikings, anchored by All-Pro center Tyler Linderbaum. But the strength of their receiver group is their tight ends- Brock Bowers and Michael Mayer- and they run double tight-end sets at the highest rate in the league. Their receivers are Tre Tucker and former Viking Jalen Nailor. The Raiders running game is terrible- last in the league- averaging just 2.9 yards per carry. Ashton Jeanty hasn't done much as a running back, but he is a good receiving back. So, roughly 60% of Cousins' targets with the Raiders were to tight ends or running backs- mainly Jeanty.

Cousins has the third highest PFF passing grade (91.6) when targeting intermediate (10-19 yard) routes this season among starting quarterbacks and has done so at the fifth-highest rate (24.8%). Most of those targets were to tight ends between the numbers. Cousins is likely to target wide receivers more with the Vikings- as he did in 2022-23- as that is the strength of the Vikings receiver group. But the route concepts the Vikings employ will likely have him continue to target intermediate routes over the middle at a fairly high clip, complemented by outside zone runs that can spread the defense out and make linebackers bite on play action.

In addition to having a poor running game, the Raiders also have the last ranked defense in both points and yards allowed. They've allowed at least 30 points in each of their last six games, which has put Cousins under pressure to keep pace. That has affected the game situation and play calling for the Raiders, which pass just over 60% of the time- one of the higher rates in the league.

Still, despite a poor supporting cast when it comes to the running game and defense, Cousins has managed a top ten ranking (7th) across a composite of key quarterback metrics this season. That's leaps and bounds higher than anything the Vikings have had so far this season.

By most metrics, Cousins' performance this season has been pretty close to his season average across five and a half seasons with the Vikings previously, so from an age standpoint there doesn't seem to be an impediment there to his achieving similar results with the Vikings this season compared to what he did with the Raiders and compared to what he did previously in Minnesota. The schemes in Las Vegas and Minnesota are similar- both from the Shanahan tree- and Cousins' spent a year and a half in O'Connell's system already and three and a half seasons with Justin Jefferson, a half season with Jordan Addison and a year and a half with T.J. Hockenson. It's really a question of how fast everything comes back to him. It wouldn't be surprising if there is a ramp up period, but from all accounts he's hit the ground running since he arrived on Tuesday. My guess is there will be hiccups on Sunday as he gets back into the swing of the Vikings offense- and maybe here and there for a few games- but I also expect him to make more throws than we've been seeing from Murray and Wentz this season. Whether that's enough to beat the Packers and 49ers remains to be seen, and once again the Vikings' defense will need to show up and be dominant, but Cousins should be able to allow the Vikings offense to possess the ball and put them in position to score more points than they have so far this season- even with a more limited playbook and kind of re-learning on the field as far as rapport with receivers.

How quickly Cousins can get up to speed with the Vikings offense could mean the difference between a division championship and potential first seed in the playoffs and a wild card spot, assuming the Vikings beat the weaker teams remaining on their schedule. But the next two games are crucial- the Packers and 49ers- in terms of keeping pace within the division and competing for the top seed in the postseason. If the Vikings can win them both, they move to 8-2 and have a much easier schedule the rest of the season and would hold a key tie-breaker against the 49ers. If they lose both games, they fall to 6-4 and if they can regroup from there to gain a wild card spot they will play on the road throughout the playoffs in all likelihood. That's a tough road to make a deep run. But if the Vikings and Cousins ramp up to play their best football in December- and beyond- they will be as formidable a team as there is in the league and not a team any division winner is looking forward to playing.

But a premium is on winning in November- beginning on Sunday- to best position the team for a deep run. Hopefully Cousins- and the rest of the team- is up to the task.

I Walked After Every Meal for Two Months, and My Digestion and Energy Levels Have Never Been Better

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Experts weigh in on why sitting is the worst thing you can do after eating

It’s almost 9:00 p.m., and my work dinner in Manhattan’s SoHo neighborhood is finally coming to a close. As the people around me start to get up and call their Ubers, I head out the door to go home—on foot. After a lavish dinner, most people’s last thought is to get a brisk walk in, but for me, it’s crucial—not to hit a step count or burn calories, but as my version of a digestif, if you will.

My idea to walk after dinner came from a video I saw months ago from a TikTok nutritionist who touted a brisk 10-minute walk after meals as the key to boosting metabolism, regulating blood sugar levels, and helping your stomach digest food. My two favorite pastimes are eating and walking. I never thought the two would go hand in hand.

“Sitting briefly after eating isn’t inherently harmful, but prolonged, uninterrupted sitting can lead to a higher post-meal blood-sugar response compared with breaking it up with short walks,” says Elizabeth Sharp Edens, an internal medicine and obesity medicine physician, functional medicine practitioner, and founder of HMW Method. A 2022 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine found that light-intensity walking reduced post-meal glucose by almost 17 percent. The same study found that standing breaks after meals reduced glucose spikes by 9.5 percent on average. The TL;DR: Research says that any type of movement can improve glucose levels.

We know walking can do wonders—it can clear the mind, help you lean out, and even boost dopamine—but why is it a solution for feeling less full and avoiding afternoon crashes? “When you walk, your muscles use glucose for energy,” Edens explains. “Muscle contractions move glucose transporters called GLUT4 to the surface of muscle cells, helping draw glucose out of the bloodstream through a pathway that doesn’t depend on insulin.” Since your legs, the vehicle for walking, contain some of the biggest muscles in your body, it makes sense that walking effectively stimulates muscle contractions that move glucose transporters. “Walking breaks are a simple way to support blood sugar management while getting your steps in,” she adds.

While many rely on peptides, green powders, and “magic” supplements, walking is an easy, mindless (and free) way to improve digestion and prevent blood-sugar crashes. “Even two to five minutes after a meal can help, ideally before the glucose peak, typically 60 to 90 minutes after eating,” Kiran Krishnan, a research microbiologist, the founder and chief science officer of the Biome Learning Center, and Dose key opinion leader, adds.

I often joke after large meals that I will have to be rolled home. The good news: Walking can also reduce the overwhelming feeling of fullness. “Walking also helps the gut physically,” Krishnan says, citing a study that found that a slow walk after a meal significantly shortened the time it took the stomach to empty half its contents. “For people prone to bloating, mild [walking] cut intestinal gas retention from 45 percent to 24 percent and eased symptoms,” he adds. Plus, Krishnan says studies show reduced LDL cholesterol levels and improved liver function in those who walked after meals.

Then there’s good old gravity, says Caitlin Hosmer, a Maine-based registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry. “Gravity helps too, which is part of why walking may help people who feel uncomfortably full or get reflux after meals,” she explains. “Gentle movement and staying upright help food move out of the stomach and along the digestive tract.”

Ten minutes isn’t long, but studies show it’s just as effective as a longer walk. A 2025 study published by PubMed Central found that a 10-minute walk lowered glucose measurements by 6 percent and a 30-minute walk lowered glucose levels by 5 percent, meaning just 10 minutes of walking is all you need to help reduce a post-meal glucose spike. If you can walk for 30 minutes, an hour, or even longer—that’s great. But just 10 minutes can work just as well.

What matters most is how quickly you get moving. “Glucose typically peaks 60 to 90 minutes after eating, and moving before that peak is what blunts the spike,” Hosmer says. “A walk puts your muscles to work just as glucose from the meal is flooding into the bloodstream.” At first, I questioned whether I had to walk after every meal. “Put your effort where it counts most: after your largest, most carbohydrate-rich meal,” Hosmer advises, adding that for most, that’s after dinner, but walking after all three meals is ideal.

My post-meal walks varied—some days I would walk for 30 minutes or even an hour, depending on how much time I had and where I was going next. But as I started incorporating them into my work schedule, especially on busy office days, I often had only 10 minutes to spare. When in a pinch, I would get up and walk around the office floor and, at home, do air squats, which engage the same large leg muscles as walking and can be just as effective as a post-meal walk.

It may feel strange to pencil in time to walk after every meal, but the benefits I have noticed surpass any inconvenience or awkwardness. I have fewer crashes after I eat, whereas I previously would feel lethargic after every meal. I’ve also noticed improved digestion. While this can be chalked up to a number of things (my beloved vibration plate, greens powders, or digestive enzymes), I always feel lighter and less full after a post-meal walk.

It’s also not meant to feel daunting or like a chore. “Your post-dinner walk isn’t a workout. It’s a way of helping your body handle the meal,” Hosmer says. “The one real ‘wrong way’ is skipping it because you think it has to be long or perfect. I often say something is better than nothing, and that’s especially true here. Even a few minutes of walking, started soon after you finish eating, can blunt the blood sugar rise, and it may help your digestion too.”

Arne Slot's time as Liverpool's manager was fairly mixed.

Most fans wanted his sacking to occur months earlier but, Fenway Sports Group chose to stick by him and, luckily, he managed to get his men to qualify for the Champions League.

His fifth-place finish was clearly miles away from where Liverpool actually needed to be which is why Andoni Iraola was brought in.

And, while a lot of people thought his time at Anfield would be smooth-sailing, there are still a lot of changes he needs to make before his team are properly ready to compete for silverware again.

The main issues that need to be rectified are the performances of some players with the January transfer window the best way for them to fix the issues that they're suffering with.

But, while Slot was in charge, he had the answer to one of Iraola's main problems but, instead of keeping him around, he chose to move him on with fans now wondering why that decision was ever made.

Liverpool must figure out what they're doing with their midfield

Liverpool's main problem area among their first-team squad at the moment is their midfield.

They currently have four options to choose from as their number sixes and, while Alexis Mac Allister and Trey Nyoni have put in some solid performances, Dominik Szoboszlai and Ryan Gravenberch have both failed to do the same.

The duo were the two midfielders offered long-term contracts by FSG last season with each of them being handed bumper new deals.

Clearly, Liverpool expected them to be their regular midfield duo but, that has been far from the case.

Mac Allister and Nyoni have both performed far better than their teammates and, it feels like they should have been the ones given new contracts instead.

However, that isn't what happened and now, Iraola and Julian Ward seem to be stuck between a rock and a hard place.

Tyler Morton would have been perfect for Andoni Iraola

Some people believed that selling players such as Luis Diaz, Tyler Morton and Jarell Quansah was the best thing to do.

And, of the lot, Morton is probably the one that Liverpool wish they didn't let go, especially since he's the perfect defensive midfielder for Iraola's system.

Not only can he win duels, keep his line and add extra stability to a team but, he can also create chances and score them if needed.

Alongside all this, he's also a really hard-working central midfielder so, the fact that each of these skills line up perfectly with Iraola's system is a real kick in the teeth.

Obviously, what's done is done and these things can't be changed now but, in hindsight, selling a Liverpool-born midfielder who wanted nothing else but to play for his childhood club was maybe not the Reds' best idea.

That is, especially when they already knew that their midfield was going to be a problem for them.

Wolfsburg’s Muhammed Damar reflects on spectacular Germany U21 return

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

VfL Wolfsburg attacker Muhammed Damar provided one of the genuine bright spots in Germany U21’s 2-1 friendly defeat to Belgium in Essen on Friday evening. The 22-year-old marked his return to Antonio Di Salvo’s starting XI with a sensational free-kick equalizer and an action-packed attacking performance that earned him Get German Football News‘ Player of the Match award.

As GGFN reported in its match coverage and player ratings, Damar covered virtually every blade of grass during his 63 minutes on the pitch. The Berlin native scored Germany’s only goal, struck the crossbar twice, and repeatedly caused problems for the Belgian defence with his movement, deliveries, and dribbling. Damar certainly followed through on his stated desire to make his return to the U21 ranks count.

Speaking to GGFN and other journalists in the mixed zone afterward, Damar offered an interesting account of his goal, the celebration that followed, and the pride he takes in representing Germany. The former Hoffenheim and Elversberg midfielder also discussed Germany’s preparations for next summer’s European U21 Championship and the competition for places in Di Salvo’s squad.

Damar’s equalizer in the 25th minute came from a free kick just outside the Belgian penalty area. Rather than attempting to curl the ball over the defensive wall, the Wolfsburg professional cleverly rolled his effort beneath the jumping Belgian defenders. The goalscorer subsequently revealed that the decision had been entirely spontaneous.

“It was all instinctive,” Damar told journalists. “First, I told the guys that I wanted to see how the wall was positioned so that we could agree on who would take the free kick. But I had a good feeling right from the beginning. I knew what I was doing. Then I saw how the wall was standing and thought, ‘Okay, I’ll just put it underneath.’ And it worked out pretty well.”

Damar also explained the significance of his subsequent goal celebration, during which he pointed toward his head. The gesture, according to the 22-year-old, was intended to highlight the intelligence behind his decision.

“That was just meant to say, ‘Clever, you sly fox,'” Damar explained. “I acted cleverly and then wanted to point toward my head to show that I was thinking with my head.”

Another element of the celebration had an interesting backstory. Damar ran toward Germany U21 assistant coach Ovid Hajou, who had apparently predicted that the attacker would contribute a goal from a set piece.

“Ovi is a great guy. I really like him,” Damar said. “We always practised free kicks and finishing during training sessions. Today, he told me that I would score a goal, either directly from a free kick or by setting one up. That’s why I ran over to him.”

Damar’s free-kick goal was hardly his only noteworthy contribution. He nearly doubled his tally with another dangerous set piece in the 38th minute, forcing Belgian goalkeeper Lucca Brughmans to tip the ball onto the crossbar.

Another effort struck the woodwork in the 58th minute. The Wolfsburg attacker nevertheless expressed frustration that he had been unable to make more of his opportunities, particularly given Germany’s eventual defeat.

“I’m really, really annoyed about it,” Damar admitted. “If I’d scored that second goal, the one that hit the crossbar, the game would have looked different. It really frustrates me. But that’s football. These things happen. You have to keep going.”

The Wolfsburg attacker also refused to allow his own performance to overshadow the disappointing team result.

“I certainly don’t like losing,” Damar explained. “Nevertheless, I have to say that we played well, or played well for long stretches. We conceded unfortunate goals. That mustn’t happen to us, but these matches are there for us to learn. Better now than next year.”

Damar’s outstanding performance came at a particularly meaningful point in his international career. The 22-year-old returned to the U21 squad this month after an extended absence, having rebuilt his confidence and rediscovered his attacking productivity at Wolfsburg.

As Get German Football News detailed this past summer, Damar endured a difficult period following his successful 2024/25 loan spell at SV Elversberg. Limited Bundesliga opportunities at Hoffenheim ultimately prompted a permanent move to Wolfsburg last summer.

The switch has helped revive his fortunes. Damar’s improved club form earned him another opportunity under Di Salvo, who maintained contact with the midfielder even during his absence from the national team. Following Friday’s match, the player reiterated just how much representing Germany means to him.

“I’m happy that I had a good game because I was really looking forward to it,” Damar explained. “I think I’ve already said in other interviews that I’m having a tremendous amount of fun being here. I’m genuinely happy to be here. I love wearing the eagle on my chest. It makes me proud.”

Despite the defeat to Belgium, Damar remained optimistic about the value of facing strong opposition in preparation for next summer’s UEFA European U21 Championship. The Wolfsburg professional emphasized that Germany would benefit from testing themselves against teams they could potentially encounter again during the tournament.

“It’s great to have matches like these because these teams aren’t bad,” Damar explained. “They could even be our opponents in the group stage. It’s good to have these matches now, and if you lose them, it’s not so bad because you can learn from them. The most important thing is being ready when it really matters next year.”
