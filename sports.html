Oregon star edge rusher Teitum Tuioti out for the season

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

EUGENE — One of Oregon football's most important defensive players is done for the season.

Ducks senior defensive end Teitum Tuioti suffered a season-ending knee injury in Oregon's 41-28 win over Ohio State, Dan Lanning announced on Monday.

"Teitum has been unbelievable for us this year, and every year he's been here," Lanning said. "Just watching him grow as a player and person has been a lot of fun for us. But unfortunately Teitum's done for the year, he had a knee injury, he'll end up having surgery."

Tuioti was among the Ducks' leaders in tackles with 44, and ranked second on the team in sacks with 5.5 on the season. The former Sheldon High School star sits at No. 8 all-time for Oregon with 22.5 sacks across his four seasons, providing steady leadership on a talented and experienced defensive front.

In the win over the Buckeyes, he also showed off his arm: Tuioti completed a pass to fellow defensive end Matayo Uiagalelei on a memorable fake punt which shifted momentum in the game.

"I hate it for him, because (the injury) was a routine, weird play that it happened on," Lanning said. "He left it on the line there on the field, and I don't know if he'll get another chance to play as an Oregon football player, but whoever gets to have that guy on their team in the future is gonna be really, really lucky."

Should Tuioti forgo his fifth year of eligibility and enter the NFL Draft, he is a projected second- or third-round pick according to some mock drafts.

A Trip Through Toyota’s New GR Garage Experience Center

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

From Ivan Stewart’s Baja-winning off-road trucks to the monstrous Toyota Eagle MkIII, this collection chronicles nearly 70 years of Toyota racing history.

Toyota’s motorsports resume is a lot longer than you might expect. Long before Gazoo Racing was stamping GR badges onto Corollas, Supras, and the occasional RAV4, Toyota was racing across the Australian Outback, battling American muscle cars in IMSA, and building some of the most successful prototype racers ever to cross the finish line.

Near the Toyota Experience Center in Plano, Texas, the automaker has gathered an impressive collection of vehicles from its racing past. There’s a little bit of everything here: a 1957 Toyopet Crown that helped launch Toyota’s international racing ambitions, a legendary desert-racing pickup, a Le Mans prototype packing 1000 horsepower, and even a few cars from more recent spec series.

Here’s a look at some of the machines that helped build Toyota’s motorsports legacy at the new GR Garage Experience Center.

From the moment you walk into Toyota’s GR Experience Center, it’s obvious nearly every inch of this place has been curated to celebrate the brand’s motorsports history. Racing memorabilia, suspension components, and die-cast cars decorate the walls and many office rooms at the unassuming facility. It’s equal parts museum and shrine that honors the Japanese company’s obsession with racing.

Toyota’s international motorsports career began not with a World Rally car, but with this. In 1957, a Toyopet Crown entered the Mobilgas Round Australia Rally, an extraordinarily demanding endurance event covering thousands of miles of Australia's unforgiving landscape. The Crown finished 47th overall and third among foreign entries. They didn’t take home the trophy for first place, but surviving the 19-day endurance trial was plenty to brag about. More importantly, the experience exposed weaknesses Toyota would address in future vehicles.

Long before the Supra, there was the 2000GT. Toyota enlisted legendary American racer and car builder Carroll Shelby to develop the beautiful sports car for SCCA competition in 1968. Only three were prepared for the program, with Scooter Patrick driving the No. 33 car pictured here. The 2000GT’s 2.0-liter inline-six produced roughly 200 horsepower in racing trim, and the Shelby-prepared Toyotas proved surprisingly competitive against Porsche. Patrick finished second in the C-Production championship standings.

Don’t let the Celica badge fool you. This is about as closely related to a production Celica as a NASCAR stock car is to the sedan it resembles. Developed by Dan Gurney’s All American Racers, the IMSA GTO Celica used a tube-frame chassis, rear-wheel drive, and a heavily turbocharged inline-four engine producing as much as 600 horsepower. In 1987, Chris Cord and Toyota captured the IMSA GTO drivers’ and manufacturers’ championships. Not bad for a highly dramatized economy car.

Few vehicles are more immediately recognizable in off-road racing than Ivan “Ironman” Stewart’s red-and-white Toyota trucks. Built by Precision Preparation Inc. (PPI), these machines helped Stewart become one of the most successful drivers in desert racing history. Across his career, Stewart accumulated 84 victories and 10 championships, including 17 Baja 500 wins and three Baja 1000 victories. His nickname came from his willingness to tackle grueling desert events without a co-driver. Just imagine spending hundreds of miles bouncing around inside this thing. Sadly, most of us have only driven one of these via the Ironman “Ivan” Offroad Racing arcade game.

My absolute favorite in the room, and easily the most intimidating. Developed by Dan Gurney’s All American Racers, the Eagle MkIII competed in IMSA’s outrageously fast GTP category during the early 1990s. A turbocharged inline-four engine produced more than 800 horsepower, while its sophisticated aerodynamics generated enormous downforce. With Juan Manuel Fangio II leading the charge, the Eagle helped Toyota capture consecutive IMSA GTP manufacturers’ championships in 1992 and 1993. At one point, the Eagle won 17 consecutive races. It was so dominant that very little competition remained.

The Lexus LFA was already an engineering spectacle, thanks largely to its screaming 4.8-liter V-10. But Toyota wasn’t content to let its supercar spend its life sitting in collectors’ garages. The LFA became an important part of the early Gazoo Racing effort, participating in grueling endurance races at the Nürburgring. The program also helped Toyota develop its philosophy of using motorsports to improve road cars, with company chairman Akio Toyoda personally participating in racing and vehicle development.

Lexus decided that another 10 horses, sharper suspension, quicker shifts, and a gigantic fixed carbon-fiber wing were needed for the LFA. And they were right. The resulting Nürburgring Package was originally limited to just 50 examples. In 2011, one lapped the Nürburgring in 7:14.64, an astonishing time for a road car of its era. Just to the right of the LFA sits a Toyota Supra MkIV, the very one Toyota master driver Hiromu Naruse used to teach Akio Toyoda how to hustle a car.

Before Toyota finally conquered the 24 Hours of Le Mans, it built this legendary hybrid prototype. The TS040 combined a 3.7-liter V-8 with electric motors driving both axles, producing approximately 1000 horsepower. Rather than conventional batteries, the car used supercapacitors to store energy recovered during braking. Toyota won both the World Endurance Championship manufacturers’ and drivers’ titles in 2014, before introducing an extensively revised version of the TS040 for 2015.

The Lexus RC F GT3 seen here, yawning with its giant grille, has spent many years competing in motorsports. Its 5.4-liter V-8 engine helped Vasser Sullivan Racing become a formidable competitor in IMSA, despite facing newer, turbocharged rivals. The team captured the 2023 GTD Pro championship, with victories at Watkins Glen and Long Beach. It followed that up with a class victory at the 2024 12 Hours of Sebring. 2026 was the RC F’s final year in IMSA, and it will be replaced at the 24 Hours of Daytona in January by the GR GT3 race car.

The Supra’s return to the street also brought a new Toyota race car. Developed for customer racing in the GT4 category, the GR Supra GT4 shares its 3.0-liter turbocharged inline-six with the road-going Supra, but adds suspension, brakes, aerodynamics, and a stripped-out interior that’s only tolerable in race cars. Its initial specification produced approximately 430 horsepower, subject to racing balance-of-performance regulations. Unlike the wild prototypes elsewhere in this collection, the Supra GT4 remains closely related to something you might drive to work.

Think of the GR86 Cup Car as the affordable end of Toyota’s motorsports spectrum. Built for Toyota’s single-make GR Cup racing series, which began in 2023, the car retains the production model’s 2.4-liter flat-four engine but replaces its manual transmission with a six-speed sequential gearbox. The goal is to provide drivers with relatively accessible, closely matched machinery that emphasizes driving talent instead of checkbook size. Plus, it’s one of the few cars in the museum that you can also drive in iRacing.

Developed for SRO’s TC America touring-car championship, the GR Corolla TC starts with Toyota’s 300-hp, turbocharged 1.6-liter three-cylinder engine and retains the GR-Four all-wheel-drive system. Interestingly, it also uses the production car’s eight-speed automatic transmission. The transformation includes upgraded suspension, Alcon brakes, a roll cage, and aggressive aerodynamic bodywork.

Three outside contenders who could replace Michael Carrick as Man United's next manager

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Ben Sully | 10 Nov 2026 23:44, Last updated: 10 Nov 2026 23:48

Manchester United are said to be on the brink of parting ways with head coach Michael Carrick, with the team languishing in the Premier League relegation zone after winning just one of their 10 matches.

While a final decision is yet to be confirmed, rumours are already swirling about who the club could pick as Carrick's replacement should they decide to change head coach over the international break.

USA boss Mauricio Pochettino is reportedly towards the top of the club's managerial shortlist, with his current contract believed to contain a release clause.

Meanwhile, the Red Devils have also been linked with former Newcastle United boss Eddie Howe, ex-Germany head coach Julian Nagelsmann and the vastly experienced Antonio Conte, who previously managed Chelsea and Tottenham Hotspur in the Premier League.

While the aforementioned quartet are believed to be among the main contenders, there may be alternative candidates who are at least on the radar of the Man United hierarchy.

With that in mind, Sports Mole highlights three outside picks the Red Devils could consider if they decide to replace Carrick.

According to the latest odds, Kieran McKenna is among the potential contenders for the Man United job, although he is seen as an outsider compared to the likes of Pochettino, Howe, Nagelsmann and Conte.

There is a temptation to say that McKenna ‘knows the club’, but instead of using that tired old cliche, it is perhaps better to say he is someone who will be well-known by many figures at the club, and with that, there is less uncertainty about the way he works as a coach.

McKenna spent a couple of years working as the Under-18 coach at Man United before he was promoted to the role of assistant manager, where he worked with Ole Gunnar Solskjaer, Jose Mourinho, Ralf Rangnick and Carrick.

The 40-year-old ultimately decided to forge his own path as a manager when he took the top job at Ipswich Town in December 2021, going on to lead the club to back-to-back promotions before suffering Premier League relegation in the 2024-25 campaign.

Ipswich stuck with McKenna and were rewarded with another promotion-winning season, but he ultimately decided against overseeing the club’s latest Premier League campaign, deciding to take a break from football management.

Considering it has been five months since he made that decision, it is unclear whether McKenna is now ready to jump back into football management, although the chance to manage a club of Man United’s stature would surely be a convincing factor.

There may be a tendency to think he would be a similar appointment to Solskjaer and Carrick due to his previous connections with the club, but he has already built a stronger CV than the aforementioned pair, and his willingness to use width as a means of attack would suit a philosophy that has previously been associated with Man United.

With Fabian Hurzeler and Marco Rose currently impressing at Brighton & Hove Albion and Bournemouth, Man United may want to look to Bundesliga’s exciting coaching talent for their next head coach.

Stuttgart boss Sebastian Hoeness is seen as one of the league’s best coaches, even though his side has struggled for consistency this season.

Hoeness got his first taste as a Bundesliga head coach at Hoffenheim, where he oversaw 11th and ninth-place finishes before taking a year out of management.

In April  2023, the 44-year-old got the call from Stuttgart, who were languishing at the bottom of the table at the time of his appointment.

Hoeness lifted the club out of the automatic relegation spots before he guided them to victory in the relegation/promotion playoff against Hamburg.

Stuttgart’s transformation under Hoeness was there for all to see when the club achieved a runners-up finish in 2023-24, their best league finish in 17 years.

While there was a drop-off in the next Bundesliga campaign, any frustration from a ninth-place finish was made to feel insignificant by Stuttgart’s triumphant DFB-Pokal, which ended the club’s 28-year wait for glory in the domestic cup.

Champions League qualification followed in the 2025-26 campaign, an achievement that means Hoeness is yet to experience an unsuccessful season at Stuttgart, the sort of consistency that Man United could certainly benefit from.

Hoeness has shown a willingness throughout his coaching career to select a formation that best suits the player profiles in his squad, while still having a clear philosophy to certain aspects of the game, including the willingness for his teams to push full-backs and wing-backs high in a bid to get in good crossing positions.

Out of the three options, Marcelino is the biggest curveball on this list, although he has much more senior management experience than McKenna and Hoeness.

While he may be relatively unknown to an English audience, he has a strong reputation in Spain as a wily operator who tends to command the respect of his players.

Marcelino has managed some of Spain’s biggest clubs, including Sevilla, Valencia and Athletic Club.

The 61-year-old guided Valencia to their first Copa del Rey triumph in 11 years in the 2018-19 season, while he beat Real Madrid for the Spanish Super Cup during his time in charge of Athletic.

However, he perhaps spent his best years in charge of Villarreal, first for a spell from 2013 to 2016 before spending three more years in charge from 2023 to 2026.

In his first stint in charge, Marcelino guided the Yellow Submarine to promotion before achieving three consecutive top-three finishes in La Liga and a run to the Europa League semi-finals in 2015-16.

After returning to the club in November 2023, the experienced coach steered the club to an eighth-place finish, before leading them to Champions League qualification in back-to-back seasons.

In fact, he guided the club to third spot last season, their best top-flight placing since finishing runners-up in 2007-08, but it proved to be his final campaign in charge after failing to agree terms on a new contract.

Inigo Perez’s struggle to find consistency since taking over the reins at Villarreal has only added to the sense that the club may have made a mistake by allowing him to depart.

However, Villarreal’s potential error may be an opportunity for another club, and while it would be a left-field decision to choose him as Carrick’s replacement, he possesses over 800 games of senior managerial experience, has already coached clubs with demanding fanbases and would not be fazed by the Red Devils’ precarious position in the Premier League relegation zone.

His pragmatism has sometimes been used against him as a criticism, but it may be just what Man United need after shipping 19 goals in 10 Premier League games this season.

Marcelino's keenness to play quickly through the lines and hit teams on the counter may also suit a team that is struggling for confidence in midfield and has players in advanced areas who can race in behind defences.

The ‘Midlife Crisis’ Isn’t Real. The Actual Risk Is Believing the Myth.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Knowing the reality can benefit your physical and mental health.

If you ask someone what comes to mind when they hear the word midlife, 99.9 percent of the time the answer will be very specific: crisis. Ideas about middle age tend to skew negative, no matter the cause.

As a midlife researcher, this is worrying to me because I know just how pernicious these legacy beliefs can be. Yet the most prevalent negative interpretations of midlife are not supported by the research. The truth is so much more hopeful and, frankly, interesting.

While it’s hard to predict exactly who will experience this, it’s likely that 10 to 20 percent of people in midlife will go through some intense period of challenge and growth. For those folks, it’s important to remember that this isn’t a bad thing. First of all, the “crisis” is only part of the story, and not even the most interesting part. Whatever challenges you face are likely to be followed by changes to your life that will help you become freer, stronger, and more tuned in to what you really want for yourself in the second half of your life.

Debunking the myth of the midlife crisis isn’t just about correcting the record—it’s about harm reduction. Cultural notions have a real-time effect on our perception of our health and well-being in our middle years, and if the “information” is more myth than reality, that can have negative effects on our physical, psychological, and cognitive health.

Research done by Becca Levy, PhD, a professor of public health and psychology at Yale, and summarized in her book Breaking the Age Code, has shown the power of both negative and positive beliefs about aging to impact our healthspan and lifespan. Levy found that those who had more positive images of aging (e.g., being healthy and active) lived longer than those with the more stereotypical views about older adults as weak, forgetful, and slow. She also found that those with a more positive mindset about aging were healthier, with lower blood pressure and a lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease.

Beliefs about aging can eclipse the truth in many arenas. If a woman in her 50s whose children have just left the nest goes to see her physician about feeling excessively fatigued, depressed, and sleepless, the cliché of the midlife crisis makes it more likely that her concerns will be dismissed. This can lead to misdiagnosis for more serious conditions such as depression, autoimmune disorders, or hormonal deficiencies, which if recognized can be treated properly.

One of the best ways to set ourselves up for success when the sea of life starts to get choppy is to pay close attention to the way we are thinking about our story. Whether we know it or not, we are always telling ourselves stories in order to make meaning out of what happens to us and how it makes us feel. When we’re going through a rough patch, it helps immensely to take control of the narrative—moving from a negative frame of mind to a more hopeful one that emphasizes agency and opportunity. On the opposite end of the spectrum from folks who enter midlife ready for battle, many enter midlife on autopilot. Some assume they are stuck with the decisions made early in adulthood. Others want out but don’t imagine the alternatives or can’t see ways to improve circumstances.

Either way, it’s important to remember that it is possible to make tweaks or small changes in midlife; they don’t have to be radical ones. One of my colleagues on the MacArthur Foundation Research Network on Successful Midlife Development used to say, “Some people are so fed up at work that they quit their jobs in frustration, when all they really needed was a two-week vacation.” When we sleepwalk through midlife, at best it’s a missed opportunity for growth, and at worst it simply delays the challenges that will inevitably surface. What we know from the research is that middle age—whether or not you go through a “crisis”—is the time to reflect and make change.

I like to think of midlife as halftime in sports: Some of the important plays have been made, but not all. While a little discomfort with the poor choices of our past isn’t a bad thing, getting mired in regret isn’t something that helps anybody. You have the opportunity to breathe, examine what has come before, and start analyzing what you need to do next to reach your goals or modify them. There is plenty of time left after midlife, so reflection can help people see how they might move in different directions.

For those who don’t know where to begin when it comes to reflecting on their life story and even changing it, there is good research to suggest that a “life review” can help. When done well, a midlife review can help you make sense of the past and bring clarity to your goals moving forward (find out more about how to do it below).

One strategy is to do life reviews at the decade milestones (40, 50, 60) and possibly at the halfway marks (45, 55, 65). Another strategy is to do it when there are significant life events that might require some readjustments, such as going through a divorce, a job loss, or having a child leave home. Midlife is a great time to revisit old stories and even consider some rewrites and new beginnings.

It is important to keep in mind that it is unlikely that you will have accomplished everything you expected to by age 40, 50, or even 60. And it is unrealistic to think you will be happy with all areas of your life. If you think you are having a midlife crisis, ask yourself whether it’s really a crisis or if it’s just that you’re not happy with how some things are going. Maybe it’s just a time to make some changes. The life-shattering, ego-dissembling portrayal of the inescapable midlife crisis we have all become accustomed to is neither inevitable nor universal.

Rio Ngumoha learned from an early age to experience the fast-track treatment in football and at first he didn’t exactly like it.

“I think I was six, maybe five,” recalls Ngumoha. “I was playing for a local team. I remember I got an assist and then next thing you know, I could hear my mum on the side saying well done, and then they started saying that I was playing with older players. I just panicked and I started crying and then I walked off the pitch because I was so scared because everyone was older than me.”

It took this shock to the system to understand the precocious talent that he was. “I realised after I could definitely have competed with them, so I’ve toughened up a bit.” Punching above his weight went on to become the norm for Ngumoha; it still is. At 18, he is the youngest player in the senior England squad, picked by Thomas Tuchel for what is already his third camp this month.

No longer is Ngumoha stunned by his rate of progress. It’s what the Liverpool starlet has come to expect from himself; he said last month that he “definitely had the confidence and the belief” to think he could “wear an England shirt at 18”. But such confidence didn’t come without his humble beginnings, grinding in the cages, where he would visualise himself one day representing his country. “I feel like if you don't visualise anything or you don't dream big enough, then you might not ever achieve it,” he asserts.

Made in the Manor (Park), Ngumoha grew up in a particularly rough part of east London. “Everyone knows it’s a tough area, Newham especially,” he says. There are darker avenues he could have ended up going down, but those around him – chiefly his mum Liz and his older brother James – ensured he stayed on the right track.

“I think the people around me and all my friends and family made sure I wasn't a part of anything that was too rough, or just to keep me on my toes really. And I had football, which is a top, top escape route for things like crime and stuff. So I think I'm just having my brother there, my mum, all my friends supporting me. They just knew to leave me out of all of that stuff so I can get to the top.”

James especially was integral to his footballing rise. “My brother, he's been a massive part of my journey,” Ngumoha adds. “He saw the talent in me from when I was like five years old, which is crazy to say. He’s my agent as well now. He just wants the best for me, and he's really supportive.”

There were things that family couldn’t necessarily help him with, though. His commute to and from Chelsea’s Cobham training ground, which before the days of the Elizabeth line required him to take two trains and two tubes – a journey that touched two hours – was a daunting and taxing one for a child to take on a near-daily basis.

“I think sometimes going from school to get the train is a bit tough, especially coming back late around 11pm. For a schoolkid, it isn't easy,” he reflects. “But that's a sacrifice you've got to make sometimes to reach the top.”

Chelsea were hoping that continued sacrifice would reap mutual rewards for both club and player. Academy coaches felt he could win the Ballon d’Or – à la Harry Kane, his international captain – wearing the blue shirt. In fact, a report from Sky backed this up with some interesting numbers, look specifically at data per 30 minutes of actual possession. Milan are first in Serie A for build-up actions, but they are 20th for entries into the final third and entries into the opposition box.

Amorim’s side are 19th for percentage of touches in the attacking third and 18th in percentage of touches in the opposition box. When the Rossoneri have the ball, it is rarely in a threatening area.

With numbers like these, there isn’t much for Ramos to get on the end of, and it makes the positive attacking numbers (23 goals in 11 league games) feel quite unsustainable.

The Portuguese striker meanwhile is first in the Milan squad in Serie A for interceptions, recoveries, touches in the middle third and touches in the opposition half. He is second for receptions, touches and duels, and third for passes.

Simply put, the No.9 is being drawn away from the area he should be operating in because the team are not capable of sustaining spells of possession further up the field, so he needs to drop deeper to feel involved.

This is something Amorim must work on, because while the goals are coming from other sources for now – in some cases due to the space Ramos helps create – it could all come tumbling down unless the attacking play improves more generally.

Some may argue that encouraging an ‘end of the world’ type mentality will not help the players play more carefree and will actually only stiffen them up. Yet, it could also provide the stimulus for the fight and the courage that Amorim clearly so desperately wants.

College Football Playoff will expand to 24 teams starting next season

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

College football will expand its playoff to 24 teams beginning next year in a move that resets decades of tradition surrounding a once all-important regular season and dumps conference championship showdowns that used to be among the sport’s marquee events.

The announcement from the College Football Playoff and ESPN on Monday made official a deal that’s been in the works for months. The network reported it would pay an additional $1.7 billion over five years on top of the original $7.8 billion contract that runs through the 2031 season.

The new format will grant the Power Four conference champions automatic spots and give one more to the best team from the Group of Six. But that slot and the other 19 will be decided by a selection committee that will serve as the latest set of decision makers in college football’s long history of polls and opinions having lasting impacts on teams’ fates.

Beginning next year, around 30% of college football’s 68 power-conference schools will have a chance to play for the title, drawing the CFP closer to the NFL, which allows 43% of its teams into the playoffs. It leaves behind the last vestiges of yesteryear, when between four and eight teams entered December with a legitimate shot to win it all.

It’s a seismic shift that even ESPN conceded comes with some question marks. The network pays billions to televise regular-season games that might — or might not — have more meaning under the new system.

“In all honesty, it’s impossible to know for sure,” ESPN senior vice president Nick Dawson said in a conference call.

The revenue generated from the playoff expansion will presumably make up for the estimated $345 million the Power Four conferences generated in TV money for their title games. ESPN would then not be on the hook for $140 million of the extra money due to cancellation of the title games, the network reported.

How an expanded CFP will work

The top eight seeds will receive first-round byes in the new format and the playoff will start on the second weekend in December, with the first two rounds of games on campus. The traditional Army-Navy game will be played on the first weekend in December, which used to be reserved for conference title games.

Both the Southeastern and Big Ten conferences, whose approval was needed to adopt this change, and their Power Four brethren — the Big 12 and Atlantic Coast Conference — are banking that most of the additional spots will go to their members so their second-tier teams will have new motivation to keep spending big in an era of NIL payments and $50 million football rosters.

“I think it will be healthy for teams like our current situation, building to try to get to that point,” said coach Barry Odom, who is trying to rebuild at Purdue.

The first final for the 24-team playoff remains set for Jan. 24, 2028, in New Orleans. Traditional bowl games will still host the quarterfinal and semifinal rounds.

As for the rest of the bowls, most of them operated or televised by ESPN: “No decisions have been made at this time,” Dawson said.

As part of the expansion, the CFP is requiring Power Four teams to play at least 10 regular-season games against conference foes, other Power Four opponents and Notre Dame. Group of Six teams will have to play 10 games against conference opponents.

In theory, these requirements will keep everyone in the same ballpark when it comes to strength of schedule, which figures to play a huge role in the selection committee’s considerations.

Special treatment vanishes for Notre Dame

Notre Dame didn’t receive any guarantees, the way it did this year when, if it’s ranked in the top 12 by the CFP for what will be the last year of the 12-team postseason, it will get a spot.

Coach Marcus Freeman said he looks at the change as a net positive, in part because a team like his can now presumably afford more than one loss, and in part because of the new scheduling requirements. Notre Dame is one of only two independents left in major college football.

“I don’t want this to be like March Madness and have 60-something teams making the playoff,” he said. “But the more opportunities we have to make the playoff, it helps us.”

The new schedule also guarantees two off weeks during a regular season that will begin the week before Labor Day.

A bigger playoff changes the nature of the college regular season

The move to 24 teams marks the second expansion of the playoff since it began in 2014 as a four-team affair. Right after it moved to 12 in 2024, most thought it would eventually max out at 16, which was the SEC’s preference.

But the Big Ten wanted to go bigger, while the SEC balked at that, concerned about the league title game — a property it invented in 1992 and made billions from over the decades — along with the marketability of CFP games featuring third- and fourth-place finishers in power conferences.

ESPN took care of the money problem. Now, the question is whether fans will embrace an NFL-style free-for-all that could include about one-third of the Power Four’s 68 teams, depending on what happens with Group of Six schools whose role in these big-money power grabs seems to diminish with every move.

It’s the latest change for a sport that, for decades, built its distinct character and generated controversy by leaning on media and coaching polls to hand out national titles.

Like then, critics see a risk in giving more power to a selection committee that hasn’t really mastered four- or 12-team brackets. The exclusion of Florida State in 2023 and Notre Dame last season stand out as the best examples, and the first rankings this season left people scratching their heads again.

Skeptics also view the expanded playoff as another dilution of what is largely seen as one of the most important regular seasons in sports, replacing potential matchups between, say, Alabama and Georgia in an SEC title game with a 13 seed vs. 20 seed game that would pit, say, Kentucky and South Carolina.

Fan reaction “certainly weighed into the decision,” Dawson said.

“Ultimately, we just need to make a spectacular playoff,” he said. “The CFP has a responsibility to our fans, they’re the lifeblood of college football. We owe them to make this great. That really is our intent.”

As Big Ten commissioner Tony Petitti argued from the beginning, a postseason that gives bubble teams hope and keeps others vying for home-field advantage or byes will bring more eyes — not fewer — to the end of a regular season that sometimes boils down to only three or four traditional rivalry games with meaning.

Of course, nobody can be certain how things will play out. The only sure thing is that college football will never look quite the same.

“If they think it’s a good thing for college football, then I’ve got to believe that this is the route we’re headed,” Wisconsin coach Luke Fickell said, “and we’re going to make it a great thing for college football.”

Donnie Dunagan, who voiced the young Bambi in Disney’s 1942 animated classic during a brief Hollywood career as a child actor, has died. He was 92.

Movie historian Tom Weaver said Dunagan died November 4 but did not provide details.

Born on August 16, 1934, in Texas, Dunagan moved with his family to Memphis, where he tap-danced his way to winning a talent contest in Memphis at age 3. He caught the eye of a talent agent, who had the boy and family come to Hollywood by train.

Dunagan’s screen debut came in the 1939 monster flick Mother Carey’s Chickens, leading to more than a half-dozen screen roles including Son of Frankenstein, The Forgotten Woman and Tower of London. As the United States was entering World War II, the youngster would land his most memorable role, playing the orphaned deer in the 1942 toon classic Bambi.

In a 2011 interview, Dunagan recalled meeting Walt Disney several times on the lot. “He was a leader; all over the place, helping, coaching, laughing, and even a kid like me then could sense that he was keenly respected by the air and production people. That was not always the case in other studios when the ‘boss’ came around. Mr. Disney was a class by himself.’

Dunagan was turning 8 as the film was released, but it would be his final screen credit. He played college football before join the U.S. Marines during the Korean War, serving in the Corps for the next quarter-century. He became the youngest drill instructor in Marines history and was promoted 13 times in 21 years, risign to the rank of major. He had multiple tours of duty in Vietnam, where he earned the Bronze Star, three Purple Hearts and other accolades. He retired from the service in 1977, never having told his fellow Marines about his Bambi role.

Inspired by Fall Marathon Season? Here’s What You Need to Do to Prepare for 26.2

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Follow these nine coach-approved training tips now for a strong finish later.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

The spirit of the fall marathon is undeniably contagious: the roaring crowds, the crisp autumn air, and massive community energy. If you spectated this year, watching the hundreds (if not thousands) of runners race toward the finish line might have you yearning to lace up your shoes and hit the starting line next season. But before you jump into training, hit pause and make sure you’re prepared for success.

“Training to run a marathon will revolutionize your life, but it will also test you in brand-new ways,” says Susie Crossland-Dwyer, an avid marathoner and ultramarathoner, coach, and founder of studio s in Cincinnati. “A lot goes into a healthy, successful training season and what you do leading up to it.”

Here are nine tips that coaches want every marathoner to know to get to race day healthy, strong, and fast!

You’re about to set out on four-plus months of intense weekly mileage. That’s really tough on your body. To sidestep an overuse injury, having a steady foundation is crucial. A review of research on runners’ injuries published in the Journal of Sport and Health Science found that the injury incidence rate among runners is 40.2 percent, and overuse from ramping up mileage or intensity too quickly causes more than 70 percent of the injuries.

“As you begin your marathon season, start with base building and don’t just jump right into doing all the miles and all the speed workouts,” advises Crossland-Dwyer, who recommends spending at least three months gradually working up to 20 to 25 miles per week before starting an official marathon training program.

“Strength training, specifically your core and hips, is the most effective way to prevent injuries,” says Jeff Gaudette, owner and head coach at RunnersConnect. “As you ramp up your mileage for the marathon, this will be essential to you staying healthy through the training segment.”

Studies have found that strength training can also boost your performance by improving your running economy. You don’t need a certain base of strength before starting your training plan, he says, but you should include two to three weight workouts each week.

Along with weight training, “try plyometrics to get your body used to absorbing impact, single-leg exercises to prevent muscle imbalances, and mobility drills to keep your range of motion and joints healthy,” advises Meg Takacs, NASM-CPT, a run coach and founder of the Movement & Miles app.

Carb-loading before race day is what most novice runners are familiar with when starting on their marathon-training journey, but it’s only one aspect of your diet that’s important.

“Nutrition is key for successful training and a successful race—from carbs to water, electrolytes, and salt capsules,” says Takacs. “The goal is to stay ahead of any deficits before they happen because once you’re in a hydration or nutritional deficit, it’s almost impossible to come back.”

Buy water bottles to carry with you on long runs (sip water or a sports drink every 10 to 15 minutes), plus gels or gummies to nibble on every 30 minutes to stay fueled and strong through your long runs ahead.

We’re not talking about the race itself—though, yes, you’re going to be on your feet for a long time for that, too. But it’s important to know the kind of time commitment you’re going to be looking at for the next 16 or so weeks leading up to race day.

You’ll be devoting a couple hours, at least, likely every Saturday or Sunday morning to a long run, plus your other weekly runs. But also, says Crossland-Dwyer, “Training for a marathon isn’t all about running. In fact, a big portion of your weekly training hours should be spent preparing, with prehab exercises and prerun activations, doing strength-training, and recovering with mobility work, adequate rest and refueling, and extras like compression and cold therapy.”

Yes, destination races are popular, and if you’re pretty sure this whole marathon thing is going to be a one-and-done experience for you, you might as well pick an epic course. But don’t ignore races in your backyard.

“It’s so fun to see your city streets opened up for you,” says Erin Beck, vice president of innovation, product, and experience at Xponential Fitness, who’s run 21 marathons. “Seeing your city from a different vantage point than usual can keep your mind off of the treachery of 26.2 miles.”

Yes, you have several months until race day. But don’t wait to find your favorite clothing and shoes. “Purchase the gear you’ll need now, including shoes for training, the shoes you’ll race in, your potential race kit, and options for different weather conditions,” Gaudette says. “That way, you can test everything out during a few training runs to ensure it’s comfortable.”

Don’t forget about accessories: Having the right details for you, from the fuel belt that bounces the least on your hips to the socks that help you sidestep blisters, will make training all the more comfortable.

If you’re not sure where to start, check out a local running store or online forums for recs from knowledgeable contributors.

Runners joke about bloody nipples for a reason. Rubbed-raw skin there—or your inner thighs or armpit or your upper arm where it shifts against your shirt or sports bra—is inevitable at some point (or several) during marathon training.

Blisters from ill-fitting shoes or the wrong socks could be in your near future too. “Unfortunately, the only way to really know what clothing will contribute to this to test it out, which is why it’s good to have everything you might wear early,” says Gaudette.

In the meantime, buy an anti-chafing stick or cream now and lube up before every long run ahead to protect your skin and slash the chances that you’ll be in a lot of pain later.

All the anticipation of gearing up for your race will likely be mixed with nervousness, too. Remind yourself that you can do this because so many people like you have done the same.

“You don’t have to be Eliud Kipchoge to run a race—and you don’t have to know who that is to run a race, either!” says Beck. “The only thing you have to be in order to be a marathoner is bold enough to try one.”

Every successful marathoner has a training plan to follow. So before you dive into marathon training, you’ll want to find a plan that spans at least 16 weeks and fits your fitness level and your goals.

Do you just want to finish? Are you looking to break three, four, or five hours? Can you meet the weekly mileage in week one of the plan? Think about these questions and find the plan that works to guide you to the starting line and finish line feeling strong.
