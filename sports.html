Manchester United are emerging as the Premier League club showing the strongest interest in Como centre-back Jacobo Ramón as they begin planning the next stage of their defensive rebuild.

The 21-year-old has enjoyed an outstanding rise under Cesc Fàbregas in Italy, establishing himself as one of Serie A’s most promising young defenders.

His performances have now earned him a first senior Spain call-up, while United, Chelsea and Liverpool are all monitoring his progress.

According to AS, Man United have been the most persistent English club in their pursuit of Ramón, although Chelsea and Liverpool are also keeping the defender on their radar. Read the AS report on Jacobo Ramón

Ramón is currently focused on Como and is not expected to leave during January, meaning any serious transfer battle is more likely to develop next summer.

The biggest complication for United is Real Madrid. Los Blancos retained 50 per cent of Ramón’s rights when selling him to Como and can reportedly bring him back for around €10million.

Madrid remain impressed by his progress and could eventually exercise that option before deciding whether he has a place in their first-team squad.

Fàbregas has previously made his admiration clear, describing Ramón as “a young player with a great future” when discussing the defender’s potential.

From a Man United perspective, the interest makes plenty of sense.

Ramón is young, physically imposing at 1.96m and comfortable building attacks from defence. He has also developed a goalscoring threat, contributing three goals and two assists for Como this season.

United have already seen his quality first-hand. Ramón scored during Como’s 4-1 Champions League victory over them in October, a performance that reportedly strengthened their interest.

United have increased their monitoring of the Spaniard as they assess potential defensive additions for 2027.

The difficulty is that United do not control the situation. Madrid’s inexpensive buy-back clause gives them a huge advantage, while Ramón is attracting interest from several major clubs.

Still, United should remain in the conversation. If they want to build a younger, more technically secure defence, Ramón fits that strategy extremely well.

November 15 is already set to be a somber day for Xbox Game Pass, so members should brace themselves for a massive hit to the subscription service’s catalog. The addition and removal of games from Microsoft’s service is part of its very structure, but removals tend to happen all together on a single day, while additions arrive spread out throughout the month. For example, five free games have been confirmed for Xbox Game Pass in November, though their additions will happen on different days, as part of the Wave 1 updates to the service this month.

This, however, is not the case with the games being removed from the service in November. According to a new report by CBR, several games are being removed from the service on November 15, including the classic GoldenEye 007. In total, members can expect 13 games to be removed at once from Xbox Game Pass, fully severing access to them for free via the service. If players intend to continue playing or start any of the removed games, they’d better start soon or consider purchasing these games – especially now, as they are at a discount.

The previous reports hinted at Xbox Game Pass losing 14 games this November, but one of the titles, Football Manager 26, is not being removed specifically on November 15. Instead, the game is leaving the service on November 10, the same day its sequel is being released. Even so, the majority of the loss is coming in bulk, and if you want to enjoy any of the games here, you’ll need to make choices.

Liverpool are reportedly keeping a close watch on Aston Villa goalkeeper Zion Suzuki as they begin planning for life beyond Alisson Becker.

The Japan international has impressed since moving to Villa Park in the summer and is already attracting attention from several of Europe’s biggest clubs.

Liverpool’s interest is particularly significant because Alisson is in the final year of his contract, leaving the Reds with an important decision to make over one of the most influential players of their recent era.

According to TEAMtalk, Liverpool have checked on Suzuki while assessing their long-term goalkeeping options.

The 24-year-old joined Aston Villa for around £30million in the summer and has quickly impressed with his shot-stopping, distribution and ability to play as a modern sweeper-keeper. Real Madrid, Paris Saint-Germain, Arsenal, Manchester City and Tottenham are also monitoring him.

Still, if Alisson does depart in 2027, Suzuki looks like exactly the type of goalkeeper Liverpool should be considering.

Jeremie Frimpong’s Liverpool future has suddenly become uncertain, with the Netherlands international reportedly open to a move to Saudi Arabia just 18 months after arriving at Anfield.

The 25-year-old joined Liverpool from Bayer Leverkusen for around £29.5million in 2025, but injuries and competition at right-back have prevented him from becoming an undisputed starter.

That has now led to surprising speculation that his stay on Merseyside could be cut short.

The Chicks Recall ‘the Incident’ and Compare Bush to Trump in ‘Taking the Long Way’ 20th Anniversary Shows: Concert Review

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

Natalie Maines does not normally talk a great deal in concert. This may sound counterintuitive to say, since objectively there is no person in music history more famous for talking in concert than Natalie Maines. But she has always been more of a quality than quantity gal when it comes to that, so her legendary 2003 comment about being ashamed to share a home state with then-president George W. Bush was more of an anomaly than an indicator that she or her bandmates are going to be prone to lengthy speeches at any given concert by the Chicks. Related Stories 'The Mosquito Bowl' Review: Football Heroes Take to the Battlefield in Peter Berg's Emptily Macho WWII Epic 'A Few Good Men' Review: Broadway Revival Breathes New Life Into Aaron Sorkin's Courtroom Classic

Fortunately, the short current tour the group is wrapping up was custom-built to feature a steady stream of personal asides, which are welcome, if you happen to be a fan of the trio as personalities and not solely as musical talents. The main reason for that is that the outing is officially a celebration of the 20th anniversary of “Taking the Long Way,” the 2006 record that won five Grammys in total, including a rare Grammys sweep of the top three categories, album, record and song of the year. Not much was revealed in advance about the exact format of the tour. As it turned out, the Chicks (nee: Dixie Chicks) were not only down for spending the first two-thirds of the concert performing “Taking the Long Way” in its 14-song entirety, but for providing between-song commentary on most of the tracks, along with background on the preceding events that ultimately led to this left turn of an album — including “the incident,” as the Bush flap has been euphemistically known in band circles for 23 years.

This made for a rare treat, as Maines, Martie Maguire and Emily Strayer sat on stools on a riser for an hour and a half and went deep into “Storytellers” mode, engaging in a mixture of somewhat pre-scripted and fairly spontaneous dialogue before nearly all of the songs, also sometimes putting up documentary film clips on the big screen to further illustrate what was going down in the studio in the mid-2000s. (Some of the clips were from their “Shut Up and Sing” documentary, but many were unreleased.) The show fell into that rare sub-genre many of us love from a veteran performer: the docu-concert.

But the other reason for some added chatter during the tour was the timing, which was coincidental (seemingly?), leading up to — and, in Los Angeles, overlapping — the 2026 midterm elections. Their three-night stand at the Dolby Theatre in Hollywood began Sunday, two nights before the nation went to the polls, followed by a show on election night Tuesday, with a wrap-up of the stint and the tour scheduled for Wednesday. (Wednesday’s show will be a make-up for the one that was supposed to happen Monday, but got canceled in the hours beforehand due to an unspecified band illness.)

Naturally, the topical events of this week were bound to inspire a few thoughts comparing the current climate with the heated temperature that resulted in a lot of attempted Chick-broiling in 2003-4.

“It was 20 years ago, and a lot has changed since then,” Maines told the Dolby crowd near the start of Sunday’s show. “Now I would give George Bush a big kiss. Now we’re ashamed the President of the United States from the United States.” (Maines had made similar remarks earlier in the tour, and noted that these had been publicized enough that the White House issued an official statement “calling me a peanut head… or peanut brain, I can’t remember.” For the record, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle said on Oct. 15, “Natalie Maines is a despicable nobody who clearly suffers from a severe case of Trump Derangement Syndrome that has completely rotted her peanut-sized brain.”)

The crowd of 3,400, skewed disproportionately toward progressive cowgirls, went nuts. As they did later in the first act, when, before singing “I Hope,” Maines shared that they had recently visited the Obama museum, which gave them hope of again seeing a country that is not “run by a deranged nutjob.”

The only other overt reference to current events came during the second act — the greatest-hits set — when Maines sang “White Trash Wedding” and followed the time-honored opening line “You can’t afford no ring” with the contemporary addendum: “Because gas prices are too high! And electricity. And medical!” They expounded upon this a little more during Tuesday night’s show, when all three members fanned themselves with large “Fuck Trump” fans.

There’s one word to describe the trio’s nearly quarter-century history of not backing down: Nice.

Not every artist who does a full-album throwback show plays the original record in order; some rearrange the sequence to put a big single at the end. But the Chicks adhered to original running order, which meant that “Not Ready to Make Nice” provided a natural climax to the show only three songs in. And anyway, sometimes gratification that isn’t delayed is the best kind. So when Maines broke from the visage of chagrin and anger that characterizes most of “Not Ready…” to bust into a defiant smile after admitting “I kinda like it,” we’d gotten the most cathartic moment of the show out of the way less than 15 minutes into a two-and-a-half-hour gig. And we kinda liked it, because after all, act-2 favorites like “Sin Wagon” and “Goodbye Earl” don’t exactly count as anticlimactic.

Any serious fan of the group might have heard those latter barnburners in concert enough for one lifetime… not that retributive murder and taunting fornication ever get too old. But the main draw of this show for a more hardcore Chick-aholic was as it usually is for veteran acts’ full-album gigs: the promise of long-ignored deep tracks as inherently part of the concept. And “Taking the Long Way” certainly has its recently unplumbed depths. Four of the songs from the 2006 album have remained staples of the group’s sets: the quasi-title track “The Long Way Around,” the balladic “Easy Silence,” the rocking “Lubbock or Leave it” and, of course, “Not Ready.” That left 10 the group has not gone back to in a show for at least 10 years and, in several cases, 20.

Or never. “Baby Hold On” is apparently the one track the group never performed live before this tour (at least according to setlists.fm). Four had not been played since the arena tour of 2016: “Everybody Knows,” “Silent House,” “Favorite Year” and “I Like It.” Another, “Lullaby,” had not mollified an audience since 2014. And four hadn’t been performed since the year the album came out, two decades ago: “So Hard,” “I Hope,” “Bitter End” and “Voice Inside My Head.” In fact, the last two of those had apparently only ever been performed live once. All to say: the Chicks were taking the long way back around to a huge part of this setlist.

How’s it holding up, anyway? Probably everyone can think of Grammy album of the year winners that seemed like a good idea at the time, to somebody, but haven’t dated well. And it would be wrong to imagine that “Taking the Long Way” made it into the winners’ circle solely on musical merits, when there was clearly an appetite to give the Chicks a medal of valor for all that they’d put up with on top of a slew of Grammys. Even I, not having listened to the full album in quite a while, approached this concert playback with a touch of trepidation: What if it didn’t live up to memories and it felt like the Recording Academy had given out all that gold as a consolation prize for their crucifixion?

So there was good news Sunday — not quite as good as Tuesday night’s national news, but still pretty good — in just how solidly “Taking the Long Way” stands up as not just some sort of political milestone but a superior collection of music. (I’d still give the nod to “Home” as their best, but this remains a strong second.) The dance the trio did in collaboration with producer Rick Rubin and their songwriting collaborators was a slightly tricky one, with enough themes of personal iconoclasm running through several key songs that it almost came to feel like a concept album, even if they were only being allusive to the controversy that continued to follow them… with the exception of “Not Ready,” which was pretty darned specific, given how only a minority of us can relate to receiving death threats.

That anthem was preceded by a film reel of right-wing commentators having their way with the Chicks in the immediate wake of the Bush controversy, including Fox host Bill O’Reilly famously opining that “these are callow, foolish women who deserve to be slapped around.” Maines recounted their first writing session with Dan Wilson, who co-wrote six of the album’s songs, where “he said, ‘So you probably wanna write about the incident… You wanna write like an apology song?’ We said, ‘Fuck that.’ And he was like, ‘OK, fine. How about “Not Ready to Make Nice”?'” The result gave songwriting blind dates a good name. “Even though we went through all the stages of grief, we landed on anger for most of it,” said Strayer, the trio’s banjo player, “and we thought it was so specific to our story that it wouldn’t necessarily relate to everybody else, but little did we know or anticipate that it would become such an anthem for everybody else’s story as well.” Now, maybe even more than then, there’s something about their righteous Troll Derangement Syndrome that rings a bell.

The members were able to point out instances in other songs that referred in some way to the controversy. While each verse in “The Bitter End” was about a different kind of parting, and in some cases a sweeter one, they confirmed that the final verse did indeed describe their immediate estrangement in 2003 from the country music establishment they’d spent years kissing up to, be it label execs or radio programmers or fellow country stars. The last verse rang afresh with a mixture of resignation and the triumphalism that is inherent in any of their three-part harmonies: “You had a good time / Drinking all of our wine / After the show / We all rode the wave / Of that crazy parade / Oh, where’d you go / What happened to / The ones we knew / As long as I’m the shiniest star / Oh, there you are… / We’ll still be here when you come round again.” That’s a family reunion that never quite came true, as the group came to be more accepted in rock and Americana circles than in mainstream country… though the number of hats and boots in attendance at the Dolby made it clear there was a huge part of the former audience that never got lost back when sides were taken.

There were some more amusing memories during this first section, as “Lubbock or Leave It” led to a discussion of whose parents were most supportive at the time. Maines’ father, famed steel player Lloyd Maines, “a father figure to us musically,” was seen in a film clip describing how he’d tossed off an angry letter to the hometown Lubbock country station that banned the Chicks — whereas Strayer and Maguire, who are sisters, had “our mom was saying things like, ‘I’m gonna put her (Maines) over my lap and spank her’ — stuff like that. So we had a little different dynamic going on at home.”

Some other family fighting came up. “I bet that’s the only time you’ve ever heard sisters fight about in vitro,” said Maines, following a film clip in which Maguire and Strayer got into it about the difficulties of scheduling dates on their European tour around her IVF procedures. “I had to go see a different gynecologist in a different country every other day,” explained Maguire, while her sister had already had success using the procedure. The resulting song, being performed on this tour for the first time in two decades, was “So Hard,” surely one of the first songs on a bestselling album devoted to in vitro fertilization. (Ironically, Sara Bareilles just performed a contemporary one on the same subject at the Dolby just a couple of weeks ago.)

And speaking of songs that very much capture a moment in time, Strayer pointed out that “Baby Hold On” referred to the three children under the age of 3 1/2 she and then-husband Charlie Robison had at the time, and the need to find marital intimacy amid that, when they sang, “Let’s kick the babies out of bed / How ’bout you and me instead.” Strayer and Robison would be divorced within a couple of years, which may account for why the song was never played live until this year. “I think I thought it was about hope, but it was probably more about desperation at that point,” Strayer admitted to the Dolby audience. “We’ve all been there,” responded Maines.

The album also had its share of less overtly autobiographical but still deeply personal-sounding songs. “Favorite Year,” co-written with Sheryl Crow, was one of these, a spirited but still haunting recollection of a past love affair that left a lot of “what if” questions behind, even though the singer has a husband and child. If this one had a personal meaning for one or more of the members, we didn’t learn, because there were a few numbers from “Taking the Long Way” when they did just shut up and sing. But “when we were together, I was at my best” is both a warm thought and a chilling one. And the song provided yet another example of how, on this album, the trio sometimes sounded a lot closer to the Bangles than anything out of Nashville or even Texas.

Come the end of the album playback, Maines said they would be “taking a brief intermission, and come back and play this whole album backwards.” Instead, the second act brought nine songs that are a part of any of their contemporary sets — including the mashup of “Long Time Gone” and “Daddy Issues” they’ve been including ever since they premiered it in collaboration with Beyonce on the 2016 CMA Awards. (Is it really the 10th anniversary of that controversy?) The only original song performed from the last 20 years came from their only album released in the last 20 years, the title track of 2020’s “Gaslighter” … a divorce song, not a Trump song, though it being played during the week that America effectively voted to divorce the president gave it a little extra resonance.

There was more rock and less talk in this final greatest-hits segment. Of course fans would hope that another album might be in the hopper from the music world’s least studio-prolific superstar act shy of Lauryn Hill. Perhaps collecting memories and overseeing the documentary footage for the “Long Way” retrospective has reminded them they like the recording studio? Or perhaps they will continue to lean on repertoire. They’ve got one of the greatest catalogs in contemporary music, so it’s hard to complain if they find a way to milk it every few years. They took the short way around to becoming instantaneous pariahs 23 years ago, but when it came to going from that panic-button moment to being transformed into heroines for the ages, it’s been a hell of a long tail.

Harriet Phillips Says Raising Her Daughter Solo Was “Painful.” But Now She Can Ask Kate Middleton for Advice

Harriet Phillips, the wife of Peter Phillips, son of Princess Anne, sat down with Hello! for an exclusive interview five months after her royal wedding, which took place at All Saints’ Church in Kemble, Gloucestershire, the village where she grew up. The ceremony was attended by King Charles III and Queen Camilla, as well as many other members of the royal family.

For Harriet, who until then led a life far removed from high society, her official entry into one of the world’s most famous royal families inevitably brought about some big changes.

Harriet and Peter Phillips at Royal Ascot

She and her daughter, Georgina, now live at Gatcombe, Princess Anne’s estate. Peter’s sister, Zara Tindall, also lives there with her husband, Mike, and their three children. After 24 years with the British National Health Service (NHS), where she specialized in child mental health and caring for the most vulnerable children, Harriet left the NHS to embark on a new professional venture at a private foundation, continuing to focus on early-childhood development.

She has also taken on her first official role as patron of Home-Start Cotswolds, an organization that supports families with young children. “Becoming a parent can be one of the most wonderful experiences in life, but it can also feel overwhelming and isolating,” Phillips told Hello! “We often assume that parenting comes naturally, but the reality is that everyone needs support at different points. Home-Start offers vital, practical, and nonjudgmental support. Just having someone to listen can make an enormous difference.”

For Harriet, the decision to leave the health service does not represent a break with the past: “It’s been an enormous privilege to spend 24 years working in the NHS, and I’ve loved my career. It has taught me so much, professionally and personally. Over time, I’ve become increasingly passionate about the importance of promoting healthy brain development. If we can support parents during this critical early period, we have an opportunity to make a lasting difference for children.”

The Phillips couple at the 2026 DMMI Royal Charity Polo Cup

Harriet then looked back on her love story with Peter, which began in an entirely unexpected way. The two first met properly in early 2022, when their three daughters were on the same hockey team. At first there was just some casual small talk between them, then walks with their dogs, followed by dinners at pubs away from prying eyes and, finally, evenings spent cooking together. A low-key courtship, away from the spotlight, blossomed into a love story.

When she met Peter, there wasn’t a “thunderbolt moment”; Harriet said she simply felt comfortable with him, enjoying an unspoken sense of calm. “I just felt incredibly at ease with him and experienced a lovely, peaceful, safe feeling when talking to him—like being with a really good friend,” she told Hello!

He was the one who made the first move: “Peter was the brave one in recognizing that there was something between us and acting on it. This time, I thought, If I’m lucky enough to meet someone again, I want things to move slowly and ensure that everything is entirely right.” Then she added, unable to hide her happiness: “He is very romantic, loving, kind, loyal, amazing, and my best friend.” Their early dates were simple and low-key. “He’s a great cook and I love cooking. He’s great at barbecues. It was wholesome and suited to who we both are as people,” she said.

On July 25, 2025, during a weekend in the south of England, Peter proposed on a beach. “We were on a weekend getaway in the south of England on a hot day during the heat wave. He asked me to marry him on the beach; I was completely overcome,” she said. “He had the ring made at Pragnell beforehand—it’s absolutely beautiful.” Neither of their families had been told about the surprise: “It was the best-kept secret ever.”

During the interview, Harriet also reflected on her childhood in Gloucestershire and fondly remembered her parents and their long marriage. “I was brought up in a village in the middle of fields. My parents celebrated an incredible 51 years of marriage, and sadly, my father died just before Peter and I got together.” It was an experience that left a deep mark on her life, and today, as she said in reflecting on her own love story, Harriet also sees it in the context of meeting the Princess Royal’s son: “I often think maybe that’s why Peter came along.”

The loss of her father, and the difficulties she faced in the years that followed, helped shape her outlook on life. Harriet explained: “There is a beauty that comes out of difficult situations. A beautiful jewel is formed under pressure. Difficulties in life enable you to relate to people and build empathy.”

Harriet and Peter with Princess Royal Anne

Harriet then talked about her first meeting with Princess Anne, her eventual mother-in-law, which was much more informal than she had imagined it would be. They met in the garden at Gatcombe, sitting on benches and drinking cups of tea. “There was always going to be initial nerves about meeting anyone’s family members, but I am quite a calm person,” she said. “It was really relaxed…It couldn’t have been nicer.”

Harriet has come to appreciate, above all, the princess’s dedication to her work and local charities: “She sets an amazing example with her work ethic. What I’ve seen is her tireless attention to local charities and her genuine interests. It’s important to do things that match who you are, where you live, and what your passions are.”

And their relationship already seems very strong. “She’s brilliant, really good fun, and straight-talking,” Harriet said. “She’s incredibly generous—we had our wedding reception at Gatcombe, and it was amazing and special for Peter and I to have our wedding where he grew up.”

Charles and Camilla also welcomed her warmly right from the start, as seen in the various photos taken at public events. Her first meeting with the king and the queen consort took place at Royal Ascot: “It was so lovely and relaxed and there was lots of laughter. You feel as though you’re meeting family, but at the same time, it feels otherworldly. Both the king and the queen have been so welcoming to me and Georgina. I can’t thank them both enough.”

The couple with the king and queen at Royal Ascot

Harriet also spoke with Hello! about Kate Middleton, including the admiration she feels for her and the bond they’ve formed. “I can look to Catherine for advice and guidance,” she said. She recalled a special moment during a vacation on the Isles of Scilly: The children (Georgina, George, Charlotte, and Louis) found themselves playing in the water together, in a spontaneous and informal atmosphere. “I’ve had an incredible experience being welcomed in. Her first stay at Balmoral with her daughter was also a special experience. Then her brother, Nicholas, arrived to drive her to the church. “It was quite wet and it had rained that morning, but the girls kept my train completely white and immaculate,” said said. “They did a really good job.”

In the church, decorated with floral arrangements that seemed to bring nature indoors, Harriet carried a bouquet with myrtle and lily of the valley: “All my family members have had myrtle in their wedding flowers, so I continued that tradition.”

Dirk Kuyt had plundered 71 goals in 101 games for Feyenoord before Liverpool pressed ‘buy’ in the summer of 2006. In theory, the Reds were getting a bargain at just £10million. A proven goalscorer and, those who had seen him in action, the very antithesis of a goal hanger.

Yes, Kuyt ran around a lot. He worked for the team. He put in a shift. Ironically, that was part of the reason why his career at Liverpool will be remembered as that of honest pro rather than superstar.

Kuyt, with those solid legs and neat mop of blonde hair, made his debut in a 2-1 home win over West Ham on August 26, 2006, coming on as a substitute to replace Peter Crouch.

That 2006-07 Liverpool team could be classed as being ‘between strikers’.

Crouch brought something different to the mix and Craig Bellamy was a classy pest with a goal or two in him, while Robbie Fowler had come back for a second stint but was more nostalgia act than genuine first-teamer so there was a goalscoring void to be filled. Was Kuyt the man?

This, of course, was the Rafa Benitez era at Anfield. His tactical wizardry had guided a genuinely mixed bag of a Liverpool team to Champions League glory in 2005, but now there was the Holy Grail of trying to win the Premier League.

Anyone and everyone can chip in on cup runs, but a title assault needs a genuine and regular goalscorer. A 20-goal haul from Kuyt and solid contributions from elsewhere could have been the winning formula.

The Dutchman took a little while to settle but scored twice in four days at the end of September, helping Liverpool to home wins against Newcastle and Spurs.

Reds fans were hopeful but cautious too. On the one hand, there was a feeling that could this be the start of something. On the other, there was a nagging feeling that he was good but not great. A seven out of 10 player.

Fans are greedy. They want superstars. They want No.9s and No.10s. Kuyt looked like what he was. A £10million signing from the Dutch league.

He scored 14 goals in that first campaign, a very respectable haul but not a game-changing one.

In the next, he managed 11 while a late flurry in Liverpool’s near-miss 2008-09 season yielded 15.

It was back to 11 in the following campaign but back up to 15 in 2009-10, a tally boosted by one very special afternoon against Manchester United.

One thing was very clear. Rafa loved him. Or rather, given that the Spaniard doesn’t really do love, Rafa saw how valuable he was to the cause. From being an out-and-out striker, Benitez turned Kuyt into the ultimate workhorse, ploughing a furrow up and down one side of a front three.

And Liverpool fans adjusted with him. The fanciful expectations of Kuyt being a world-beater had been downgraded to a different type of admiration. He wasn’t Pele, but good old Dirk would play his heart out for the club and die for the cause. And that goes a long, long way on Merseyside.

While other Reds flickered and flirted with greatness, Kuyt offered a rubber-stamped guarantee. Whatever the occasion, whatever the opponent, whatever the location, he would put in a blue-collar shift week in, week out.

That can sound patronising, but Kuyt had another trick up his sleeve which moved him into a category beyond honest trier. The man from the little coastal town of Katwijk aan See (Dutch for Clacton-on-Sea… well, not really) could score big goals in big games.

There were other important strikes against Arsenal, while he netted seven times in the 2007-08 Champions League campaign. Even the hat-trick against Manchester United had added cult value as they were all one-yard tap-ins. Those goalscoring instincts had never left him.

Every Video Game Character In Twisted Metal

📅 Jul 27, 2023  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Peacock's Twisted Metal incorporates characters from the video games while altering many of their backstories for the series. The original Twisted Metal game arrived for the PlayStation in 1995 and spawned a hit franchise. In each round, racers fight in custom vehicles with unique weaponry. The contest is usually held by the mysterious Calypso, who grants the Twisted Metal characters who win one wish. The series, which was renewed for a third season in late 2025, has been a fascinating way to explore a somewhat loose universe, pushing for more of a linear story for the characters to move through.

The Twisted Metal video games don't have a strict canon, with each entry essentially acting as a reboot. Peacock's Twisted Metal season 1 followed this approach, setting up the world and characters. Then, the tournament started halfway through Twisted Metal season 2. Throughout both seasons, the game's fan-favorite characters make appearances in major and minor roles. With Twisted Metal season 3 finally feeling like its in the works after an exciting update from star Anthony Mackie, seeing the tournament continue will be a highlight of what's to come, as will continuing to explore the characters that have made it from game to screen.

Anthony Mackie leads the cast of the Twisted Metal series as John Doe. The John Doe featured in Peacock's Twisted Metal might share the same name as the character from Twisted Metal: Black and they might both be amnesiacs, but that's where the similarities end. On the show, John originally came from San Francisco but lost his memory in a car accident. While Calypso isn't around to grant any wishes, his goal is to earn a permanent place in New San Francisco and become the first "Milkman" to retire. However, his wish changes when he's actually in the tournament, hoping to bring his sister Krista back to life. In Twisted Metal: Black, John is a person locked up in Blackfield Asylum, whose wish is to regain his lost memories. He believes he was once part of a gang that tried to blow up a Disease Control building. However, his ending revealed he was an undercover FBI agent who prevented the bombing from going ahead. While this revelation makes him one of the game's more heroic figures, his wish to learn who he was is still twisted ironically by Calypso.

Sweet Tooth is played by wrestler Samoa Joe (voiced by Will Arnett) in Twisted Metal. Easily the most ironic character from the games, this version of Sweet Tooth is a former child star whose jealousy over his co-star, a lovable dog, drives him into a murderous rage that gets him sent to an asylum. John Doe and Quiet first encounter Sweet Tooth in Vegas, and the psychotic clown later goes on a road trip across the Divided States of America, attacking various law enforcement outposts and building a cult.

Sweet Tooth's haunting clown mask and ice cream truck are staples of the Twisted Metal games, but his backstory has been reinvented many times. In Twisted Metal 4, he even became the organizer of the contest, having overthrown Calypso.

Still, in nearly every game, he's depicted as a serial killer with an appetite for chaos. In the most recent outing, 2012's Twisted Metal, Sweet Tooth's wish was to find the "One who got away," referencing the only one of his victims to escape with their life.

Neve Campbell plays Raven in Twisted Metal, the COO of New San Francisco. While technically one of the main villains of the series, Raven only appears in the first and last episodes, and her true plan is only revealed in Twisted Metal's final scenes. The character also has no similarities to the actual Raven featured in Twisted Metal: Black, who is introduced in season 2. Still, she's one of the many versions of the character who exist in New San Francisco in the second season.

The character Raven in the Twisted Metal games is goth, witchy, and fits much better with the tone of the franchise overall. When portrayed in the games, Raven is a teenager who enters the tournament to gain revenge on the boys responsible for her girlfriend's death. The car she drives is Shadow, which is a hearse, and the special weapon is usually an explosive shadow that launches from the car and explodes upon contact with a rival racer.

Thomas Haden Church's Agent Stone is the main antagonist of the Twisted Metal series on Peacock, and is responsible for the death of Quiet's brother. Stone was a mall cop who earned little respect before the apocalypse. When society breaks down, he becomes a ruthless enforcer of the law who punishes even the most minor offenses.

The original Twisted Metal game featured the first version of Agent Stone, a secret agent who drove the sleek Crimson Fury, while Black introduced the version the TV series is based on. The second Agent Stone in the Twisted Metal games is a strict lawman who wants to undo a past mistake. The first Agent Stone in the Twisted Metal games drove Crimson Fury, a red sports car, usually with a laser beam as its special weapon. The Twisted Metal: Black version drove Outlaw, a modified police car.

Jared Bankens plays Agent Shepard in the TV adaptation, who, besides having long hair and working in law enforcement, is an entirely different character from the version seen in the Twisted Metal games. Shepard, in the Twisted Metal Peacock series, is Agent Stone's cruel henchman and becomes one of the targets for Quiet's revenge for his brother's death.

In Twisted Metal: Head-On for the PSP, Agent Shepard is an upstanding FBI agent who becomes the new driver of the Crimson Fury, the car driven by the first Agent Stone in earlier Twisted Metal games. His wish is simple: if he wins the tournament, he'll bring down Calypso himself. In doing so, he hopes to end the Twisted Metal contest for good. He only appeared in a single Twisted Metal game, which explains why he only had a relatively minor role in the Peacock series, in which he died in the first episode.

Jamie Neumann plays Miranda Watts in the live-action Twisted Metal cast, the leader of a convoy of trucks that never stops moving. She's also in a relationship with Diany Rodriguez's florist, Amber. However, they aren't together when the Twisted Metal series begins, and she and her convoy become allies of John and Quiet during their road trip. In the finale, Miranda drives the Twister car, just like the character in the Twisted Metal games. While she was expected to return for the tournament in Twisted Metal season 2, she didn't end up competing.

Like other characters in Peacock's Twisted Metal series, the version from Twisted Metal: Head-On, is quite different. The game version of the character entered the race to track down her missing sister. Her twin sister, Amanda Watts, first appeared in Twisted Metal 2, but was also a playable character in Head-On, and was voiced by esteemed voice actor Tara Strong.

Diany Rodriguez's Amber appears in several episodes of Peacock's Twisted Metal series, and is one of the few characters with real similarities to their game counterpart. Amber appears to be based on Amber Rose, a character from Twisted Metal III.

Amber drove the Flower Power car and was an environmentalist who entered the competition to stop it from destroying the world. In the Peacock TV show, Amber is also an environmentalist who runs an apothecary using her plants.

Her car, Flower Power, also appeared in the Peacock series, parked outside Amber's house. Flower Power was one of the new vehicles introduced in the 3rd Twisted Metal game. Its special attack involved a flower emerging from the car to damage rival racers with a blast of pollen

Granny Dread is another obscure character from the Twisted Metal video game franchise, making her sole appearance in Twisted Metal III. Peg O'Keef is the character in the Twisted Metal series who is the original leader of the truck convoy. She makes a deal with John to collect something for her in exchange for weapons for his car.

In Twisted Metal III, Granny Dread entered the competition (driving the monster truck "Hammerhead") to end it for good, so she could watch daytime TV in peace. Her dislike of the Twisted Metal contest is incredibly personal, since a previous competition left her neighborhood in ruins. She's a character with an incredibly bombastic backstory, one that perfectly fits into the borderline-cartoonish tone of the Twisted Metal games. Like Amber Rose, Granny Dread only appeared in the third Twisted Metal game, making her an intriguing choice for the Peacock show.

In the Twisted Metal series, Bloody Mary, a former lover of John Doe, is played by Chloe Fineman. She is introduced when John and Quiet stop at a Milkman Pitstop, which features cameos from other Twisted Metal game characters, with Mary recalling their history together.

Mary's anger stems from her unrequited romantic feelings and the fact that he's set to retire thanks to his mystery package. Consequently, she decides to scheme against him.

In the games, Bloody Mary is one of the many drivers of Spectre, a car whose driver switches every game. She has quite a tragic backstory in the games. She confessed her feelings to her crush in school, and in response, he pushed her into some mud, making her the subject of ridicule. After a lifetime of romantic rejection, she enters the race to finally meet her one true love. Her car, the Spectre is renowned for its somewhat overpowered Ghost Missile ability.

Brother and sister duo Carl and Jamie Roberts were upstanding police officers in the original games, with Carl driving the Outlaw in the first Twisted Metal. However, Carl disappears due to Calypso sending him into outer space in his Twisted Metal ending.

Following his disappearance, Jamie enters the next tournament to find him. Carl is also known as Buzz Roberts in Twisted Metal 2 & 3, and following the second game, the duo drives together rather than being individual characters.

On the Twisted Metal show, Carl is played by Michael Carollo while Chelle Ramos plays Jamie. The two are still officers of the law, but sadly, that means obeying Agent Stone. The pair appear in multiple episodes of Twisted Metal, and are part of Stone's Lawmen faction. Unfortunately, the pair are deceased by the time series one reaches its conclusion.
