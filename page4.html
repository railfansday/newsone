Survival after sudden cardiac arrest didn’t improve despite training bystanders, emergency responders

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

CHICAGO — Sudden cardiac arrest statistics are chilling. In the United States, 350,000 people’s heartbeats, breathing, pulse, and blood circulation will all halt outside hospitals this year, seemingly out of the blue. These are not heart attacks. Only 1 in 10 people will survive them.

CPR and defibrillation can save their lives (and brains), but that depends on bystanders and emergency response systems being both nearby and prepared to step in — immediately. Survival chances drop by 10% for every minute that goes by without CPR.

Hints from observational studies suggested better, more comprehensive community training can help, which means educating 911 dispatchers, first responders, and potential bystanders. But results from a randomized controlled trial presented Sunday at the American Heart Association’s scientific sessions and published in the New England Journal of Medicine on Sunday show that approach has not worked, or at least not yet. But examples in Denmark may point the way forward.

“We performed an efficient, large, and high-quality trial engaging large segments of the community in emergency response, and in spite of this, survival remained low and unchanged,” Carolina Malta Hansen, the study’s lead author, said at a media briefing. “A multifaceted community and EMS implementation strategy did not improve survival with a favorable neurologic outcome for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. However, in the final year, there appeared to be an improvement in bystanders.”

Starting in 2021, researchers randomly assigned participants in 60 counties in North Carolina to receive intensive education on cardiac arrest intervention or to continue with standard practice. The intervention entailed training 911 dispatchers to improve their rapid recognition of cardiac arrest and better guide bystanders through CPR. First responders were educated on the use of automated external defibrillators before emergency services arrived. Community members in workplaces, religious groups, government offices, and other settings received training in CPR.

About 8.5 million people live in these counties. During the study’s five years, 22,459 adult residents experienced cardiac arrest outside the hospital. Their median age was 64; 37% were women, 33% were Black; and 29% lived in rural areas.

The numbers were split this way: 11,615 people experienced out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in counties that received intensive education, and 10,844 people had out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in communities that did not receive the intervention.

Amrish Deshmukh, a cardiac electrophysiologist who treats patients with heart rhythm disorders at the University of Michigan Health Frankel Cardiovascular Center, called the study results disappointing, but not surprising.

“Many interventions that have been studied in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and in post-arrest care unfortunately have not been shown to improve survival or neurologic outcomes,” he told STAT in an email. He was not involved in the new trial. “Prior studies have shown that mobile phone alerts, volunteer training, and increased automated external defibrillator (AEDs) availability can increase the performance of CPR and use of AEDs, but not all have shown improvements in survival.”

And yet, study author Malta Hansen, a cardiologist and clinical research associate professor at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital and Copenhagen EMS in Denmark, is hopeful. In the trial’s fourth year, an exploratory analysis showed the percentage of patients who underwent CPR performed by a bystander increased, suggesting that this component of the intervention was effective. More time may be needed to see which elements mattered and whether they affect survival.

“In light of the apparent improvement in CPR for the last year, further evaluation in the longer, more intense intervention is important,” she said.

Malta Hansen cites the case of Denmark, where rates of bystander CPR and survival are much higher than in the United States. The North Carolina study trained almost 5% of the population, at which point researchers started seeing a difference in delivery of CPR between the group that received training and the group that did not.

“Obviously, that’s not enough. We have observational evidence from other countries like Denmark, where 80% of the population is trained and 75% of patients receive high-speed care,” she said.

In Denmark, the defibrillation rate is 11%, compared to 2% in the U.S. In Denmark, survival is about 16%, compared to 10% in the U.S.

That CPR-trained rate in Denmark climbed from 20% in the early 2000s to 80% now, Malta Hansen said. “It’s not something that’s done in two or three years.”

Here’s what helps: To get your driver’s license in Denmark, you have to show that you have certification in CPR. “It’s just an incredibly efficient way to reach everybody,” she said.

Brahmajee Nallamothu, an interventional cardiologist and editor in chief of the AHA journal Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes, had another theory on timing. Starting the trial right as Covid-19 was emerging could have affected educational efforts and could explain why effects of the training were delayed. He also asked if the educational intervention was the right approach.

“I don’t think anyone would argue with the importance of early treatment in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. When we think about bystander CPR, early defibrillation with AEDs, the challenge is: How do you translate that?” Nallamathu, who was not involved in the trial, said. “I think we still struggle with what’s the best way to train individuals so that they have sustained ability to understand and then utilize that tool when that situation arises, that’s an area that’s still complicated and still unknown.”

Malta Hansen’s team will follow this population in North Carolina for at least another year.

“We have established a fantastic infrastructure and network and collaboration, and everybody’s very excited to keep working on this,” she said. “What we need are communities who are very engaged and very eager to keep working on this. So we believe that future efforts to guide us will be successful.”

That won’t be easy, Michigan’s Deshmukh said.

“Implementation science is challenging (as evidenced by this and other negative trials), but is what I suspect will move the needle the most,” he wrote.

Qalo SL-1 vs. Garmin Cirqa: How these minimalist wearables compare

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both screenless bands cost about $200 and work without a subscription, but their specs are pretty different.

Garmin and Qalo both launched screenless fitness bands just 10 weeks apart this year — and also priced them four cents apart at about $200 each. Garmin announced the Cirqa on July 21, and Qalo followed on September 29 with the SL-1. Neither of the bands has a display or requires a paid membership to work, separating them from Whoop, whose bands come with a membership starting at $199 per year.

Looking at their respective spec sheets, Qalo claims the longer battery life while Garmin lists the deeper water rating, more activity types and more training metrics. The Garmin wearable also has an optional paid software tier that Qalo doesn't. We've tested both in depth: our Garmin Cirqa review scored it 7.4 out of 10, while our Qalo SL-1 review gave it 6.8.

Qalo sells the SL-1 for $199.95 with a black silicone strap and a charging dock. Extra straps will set you back $34.95 apiece. No subscription is required to use the band, and the companion app is free.

Garmin's press release says the $199.99 Cirqa tracks its features without the need for a subscription. That said, there is an optional tier called Connect+, which costs $6.99 per month or $69.99 per year. Cirqa's manual lists things like AI-generated insights, nutrition logging, Live Activity Stats, training guidance and a performance dashboard among the features that need the subscription. It also says that guided workouts require a subscription, and that some activities may too. Both bands are eligible for HSA and FSA spending.

Qalo lists the SL-1 at 18 grams and 10.5mm thick but doesn't say whether that weight includes the strap. Garmin's Cirqa is 14.8 grams without the strap and 20.7 total grams with the smaller wristband.

The SL-1 is worn on the wrist, about an inch above the wrist bone, on a silicone strap with a woven one sold separately. Meanwhile, the Cirqa sports a fabric hook-and-loop band that comes in two wrist sizes and can also be worn on the upper arm with a long arm band. Garmin offers four colors, and the Cirqa has one side button, a multicolor status LED and vibration alerts for alarms and a Find My Band feature. Qalo's support pages describe only a charging status light.

Garmin has rated the Cirqa at 5 ATM, which its manual describes as being capable of withstanding pressure equivalent to a depth of 50 meters and includes a Swim activity. In contrast, Qalo's product page says the SL-1 is water-resistant to 10 meters and warns against scuba diving and deep-water pressure. Its support site gives it a IP68 rating. In terms of temperature, the Cirqa's stated operating range is -4 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit, while Qalo lists 14 to 113 degrees Fahrenheit.

Qalo advertises 12 or more days of battery life for the SL-1 and Garmin up to 10 for the Cirqa. In our SL-1 review, the battery still had over half its juice left after a whole week. The Cirqa, meanwhile, charges in up to 90 minutes over a proprietary Garmin cable, while the SL-1 uses a magnetic dock with a USB-C cable. Interestingly, Qalo's docs disagree on how long it takes the SL-1 to charge: the product page says 60 to 120 minutes while the support site says two to three hours.

Both bands come with an optical heart rate sensor and can measure blood oxygen, skin temperature, heart rate variability, stress and sleep stages, and both offer menstrual cycle tracking. Neither has built-in GPS, but the Cirqa can use a paired phone's GPS. Qalo rolls sleep, HRV, movement and stress into a daily "Q Score" from 1 to 100 while Garmin spreads its analyses across Body Battery, Training Readiness, training status, VO2 max and HRV status. The Cirqa lists more than 80 activity types against the SL-1's 54, and the Cirqa can broadcast heart rate to other devices. The SL-1 shares one profile with Qalo's smart rings, which the company also sells without a subscription.

Qalo's product page says the SL-1's 54 activity modes include automatic activity detection, but our review found that the band didn't pick up workouts by itself, not even walks. Meanwhile, the Cirqa's automatic detection worked well enough in our testing that manual recording was rarely needed. Qalo also says its movement tracking is up to 12.5 percent more accurate than "popular alternatives." While our SL-1 review didn't test accuracy, our Cirqa review did count Garmin's reliable accuracy among the band's strengths.

Beyond the specs, we found the Cirqa to be perfectly functional in our testing. That said, the single button was the main irritation, since dismissing the alarm with a mistimed press starts a workout instead. We also found the Garmin Connect app to be pretty heavy on data and light on interpretation, and it was hard to see why the Cirqa costs more than Garmin's own cheaper trackers. The SL-1 fared a little worse. The battery was excellent, still showing 59 percent after a full week, and there's no fee to worry about, but the magnetic clasp on the silicone strap can come loose if a sleeve catches it at the wrong angle, and the app does little beyond aggregating the data it collects.

With both bands at the same price, we'd lean towards the Cirqa, which gets the basics right. The SL-1 would make more sense as a first screenless tracker if it were cheaper.

Coping With an Acute Injury or Health Diagnosis

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Many clients who come to see me in therapy are coping with an acute injury or health change. For example, they may have sustained an injury while playing a sport or been given a new medical diagnosis. A sudden injury or health change can feel shocking and is often difficult to cope with. You may be trying to adjust to changes in mobility and functioning and worried about when and if you will return to your “normal” self.

Often, the most pressing concern is: Will I get better? And when? The uncertainty is difficult to tolerate. While some injuries or diagnoses have clear treatment plans and timelines, more often it is not crystal clear what someone’s recovery or medical journey will look like. The anxious brain likes to jump to worst-case scenarios when uncertainty exists. It takes a “better safe than sorry” approach, leading us to think about all the possible horrible outcomes so we can be prepared. However, these worst-case scenarios may never come to pass, and anxiety can start to take up more and more space in your daily life.

An injury or new diagnosis can feel like a never-ending emotional roller coaster ride that you never wanted to take. It can be challenging to acknowledge the reality of the injury or diagnosis, including sudden changes you may be facing, without thinking that your life is doomed. Below are some strategies for coping with an acute injury or health diagnosis.

It is helpful to be informed about your condition, as a general understanding can help you have effective conversations with health care providers. It is useful to use reputable sources for gathering information, and your medical team may be able to offer resources. Keep in mind that not all of your questions may have firm answers, and there may be limits to what you or your medical team may be able to know.

While it is difficult to tolerate uncertainty, be mindful of not going down an information-gathering rabbit hole that consumes too much of your time and life. If you notice that trying to gather information about your condition is taking over your daily life or that your anxiety is increasing, set time limits for gathering information. For example, 30 to 60 minutes a week.

A sudden injury or diagnosis can create a wave of emotions. You may feel scared, apprehensive, sad, angry, frustrated, etc. Trying to suppress your emotions can intensify them. Find an authentic way to express how you are feeling. For example, take five to 10 minutes daily to write out your thoughts or feelings, say them aloud on a voice memo app, or share how you are feeling with a trusted friend or family member. If it feels unfamiliar and anxiety-provoking to talk about your feelings, consider doing so in a less focused or pressured environment. For instance, you may talk about your feelings while traveling in the car with soft music playing.

It can be unfamiliar and challenging for some people to accept help from others. Maybe you are used to doing everything yourself, and accepting help leads you to feel incapable. However, accepting help from others allows people who care about you to be of assistance during a difficult time. This is beneficial for them as well as for you. Also, spend time with people you are comfortable with and enjoy. It is natural to feel down, withdraw from others, and isolate ourselves during stressful periods. Yet, isolation can worsen mood. Even if you don’t initially feel like it, carve out some time with others. Your desire to be with others may increase over time. Also, it may be helpful to seek out connection with others who have the same injury or diagnosis. Talking with others with a shared understanding and hearing about others’ treatments, recovery, and coping strategies can feel relieving and supportive.

It can be difficult to accept limitations or the reality that there are some things you may not be able to do or need to do in a different way. It is important to give yourself space and time to grieve any losses you are experiencing. Expecting too much of yourself or trying to ignore the current circumstances can lead to increased frustration, anger, and sadness. Also, trying to meet unrealistic goals may deplete you and lead to less functioning and ability than you would have if you set more realistic and sustainable goals.

Just because you have limitations or there are certain things you cannot do does not mean you are incapable. Non-judgmentally ask yourself what you can and want to realistically take on, and aim to set small, doable goals.

A new injury or diagnosis can be anxiety-provoking and scary, and can highlight what or who you care most about. Certain activities may be different now, yet there are likely things or people who continue to be meaningful to you. Carve out time for them. Stay connected, or reconnect, to what feels most important and fulfilling to you. You may find that many of these things are simple and joyful, such as spending time with loved ones or time in nature.

The anxious part of our minds equates uncertainty with bad based on self-protection and preservation. While a good outcome is not guaranteed, neither is a bad one. The future could be incredibly difficult, and it also could be better than you are thinking or neutral. When your mind wants to leap ahead to worst-case scenarios, pause, take a slow breath, and remind yourself, “Uncertain does not mean bad; it simply means unknown.” Then refocus on the present moment and a values-based action you can take today.

Kings send Nique Clifford to Thunder for draft assets: Trade grades

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Sacramento Kings have agreed to trade guard Nique Clifford to the Oklahoma City Thunder in exchange for an unprotected 2028 second-round draft pick via Utah and an unprotected 2030 second-round draft pick via Minnesota, the team announced Sunday, Nov. 8.

Clifford, 24, is a 6-foot-5, 210-pound guard out of Colorado State that was originally selected by Oklahoma City with the 24th overall pick in the first round of the 2025 NBA Draft. However, Clifford never logged a minute in a Thunder uniform following a trade to the Kings that sent Oklahoma City a protected 2027 first-round pick.

He appeared in 75 games for Sacramento during the 2025-26 season and started 28. Clifford averaged 8.6 points, 3.8 rebounds and 2.4 assists in about 25.1 minutes during his rookie campaign with the Kings, while shooting 41.8% from the field and 33% on 3-pointers.

Clifford was being molded into a perennial "three-and-D" type of player for the Kings. He saw a lot of playing time in his rookie campaign, in part due to the numerous injuries Sacramento sustained to its roster that year.

He scored a career-high 30 points in a 132-126 loss to the Cleveland Cavaliers on Feb. 8. Throughout the 2025-26 season, Clifford also spent some time down in the G-League, playing for the Stockton Kings, the Kings affiliate.

In his collegiate years, Clifford averaged 10.1 points and just under a steal. He shot 46.9% from the field and 36.4% on 3-pointers.

Clifford signed a four-year rookie contract with Kings worth up to $15.7 million with $6.3 million guaranteed, according to Spotrac. Clifford's average annual salary is about $3.9 million.

He'll earn another $3.2 million in base salary.

Clifford appeared to be a contributing piece to the Kings' rebuild. The consummate professional, Clifford came into work, did what he was asked and pushed himself to be the best version of himself.

There was an expectation that Clifford would take a leap into his second-year but instead it came with setbacks. Clifford saw his minutes diminish early in the season. In his last game against the San Antonio Spurs on Friday, Nov. 6, Clifford only logged 4 minutes, 30 seconds and scored five points on 2-of-2 from the field.

He only played more than 10 minutes once this season – against the Memphis Grizzlies on Oct. 31. Clifford appeared in five of their nine games, earning four DNPs by coach's decision.

It appeared that Clifford was on the opposite end of head coach Doug Christie's rotations and a change was warranted. The Kings get to open a roster spot while picking up assets.

The Thunder have had misfortune early in the season, facing season-ending injuries to key rotation players, and additional injuries that will keep guys out long-term. Jalen Williams suffered a fractured left fibula and a dislocated ankle that required surgery. He's out for the year.

Cason Wallace has a torn labrum in his left shoulder and he is also out indefinitely. Chet Holmgren was ruled out 8 to 10 weeks after a biological treatment for a small patellar tendon tear in his right knee.

Oklahoma City needs bodies. Clifford was being buried on the bench early this season, the Thunder believe there's a spark that he can bring to to the team.

The organization has built a reputation of maximizing reserves and role players getting the best out of them, the latest project will be Clifford.

United rescued a point right at the death on Sunday after Patrick Dorgu headed home a crucial equaliser that may have saved Carrick's job. His side drew 4-4 against Aston Villa at Old Trafford and he has maintained that he is looking forward to rather than contemplating whether he'll still be in charge beyond that game.

Carrick's men produced another comeback just days after a remarkable turnaround against AS Roma, coming from behind to beat the Italians 3-2. Many of the club's legends have had plenty to say about the dire situation they find themselves in, sitting in the relegation zone in November.

Hernandez never experienced such tough times during his United career, instead winning two Premier League titles under Alex Ferguson. The Mexican striker still plays his football at the age of 38 for US outfit Atlantic Dallas but his motivational pre-match talk with Sky Sports hints at a future job in management or as a director.

After sharing the pitch with Carrick throughout his spell at Old Trafford, he gave an interesting perspective on the current state of the club. He explained why he is backing his former teammate to turn things around:

"I am confident, I am a believer that sometimes these decisions cannot be in your own way, I'm not saying that because I am in completely favour, I'm saying it in the world. I am more in favour of if a decision is taken already, instead of staying and complaining, go with it and let's see if it was right or not."

Hernandez recalled Carrick's success as interim coach: "Last season he did an amazing job, he brought United back to the Champions League. Now the start of the season wasn't like what we expected, we are used to normally when you have a really good season to maintain that, but football is like that."

"The players need to perform over there, after this white line, when the whistle blows, there's nothing much more the manager can do. It can be one of your best or the best chapter in your career."

My favorite Stephen King adaptation is, without a doubt, Stand by Me. Almost two years ago, we here at CinemaBlend lamented that the Rob Reiner-directed movie was leaving Netflix, but now it’s back, and I definitely plan on watching it again with my Netflix subscription. It’s actually been a lot longer than two years since I last saw Stand by Me, and I’m definitely ready for a rewatch of the movie with a now legendary cast including Wil Wheaton, River Phoenix, Jerry O'Connell, Corey Feldman, Keifer Sutherland, and John Cusack. I have a strong emotional attachment to the movie that outshines any other of King’s book-to-screen adaptations.

I was ten years old when Stand by Me was released in 1986, and I was 11 when I saw it. While the kids in the movie were a touch old, as with any 11-year-old, I thought of myself as slightly older than I actually was. That made me right in the sweet spot for the movie. Sure, I was a child of the '70s/’80s, and the characters in the movies were my parents' generation, but I felt a kinship with them. Plus, because they were my parents' generation, I had a direct connection to the 1950s, having heard all the stories about my mom and dad’s childhoods.

That also meant that I connected with the music in the movie. Director Reiner and music supervisor Celest Ray picked some bangers from the early days of rock n’ roll for the soundtrack, and I loved it. I’m sure I heard the soundtrack before I saw the movie, much like another Boomer nostalgia movie, Good Morning, Vietnam. For kids in the 1980s, the soundtracks to movies like Stand by Me were often our first exposure to them.

The ‘80s were a different time, and seeing Rated-R movies way before we ever should have meant that I had seen a couple of King adaptations before Stand by Me. Movies like Firestarter and Cujo were staples of cable TV, and I’d seen both. I’d also been terrified of both. I didn’t even know Stand by Me was based on a King novella until I saw his name during the opening credits. It blew my mind. I thought he wrote horror stories. Stand by Me definitely wasn’t that, although the dog “Chopper” scared me as much as Cujo did.

Stand by Me is the first adaptation from a King story that showed that other side of the author. The one we all know now from movies and books like this one and The Shawshank Redemption. I had no idea he wrote such heartwarming stories to go along with the ones that kept me up at night, like The Shining and Carrie. Before Stand by Me, all the movies based on his books were like those and Maximum Overdrive, which was another movie I saw around this same time and really scared me, like, a lot.

Stand by Me has no horror at all, and nothing supernatural. Just a deeply personal story about friendship that kids of any era can connect with, just like I did. It's one of the best King movies, despite not being what he's most famous for. I can’t wait to watch it again this weekend.

7 Sleek Sports Cars Ranked Worst To Best By Drag Coefficient

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Equipping a sports car with a big and powerful engine is all well and good. But if the car in question is designed like a cinder block, then it's just wasting the engine's potential. As a car speeds up, the air resistance around it increases. And a car with a sleeker design can slip through the air with less resistance, thus granting it better performance. That, in a very basic nutshell, is why aerodynamics matter.

Now, drag coefficient (Cd) isn't the be-all and end-all when it comes to measuring a car's aerodynamics as a whole. Something like drag area, which combines that metric with frontal area measurements, provides a more complete picture. Nevertheless, drag coefficient scores still provide a solid impression as to just how slippery a car is — and such figures are more readily available from automakers, too.

Sleek designs certainly aren't reserved exclusively for performance models; economy cars like Honda's little hybrid that could, the Insight, can also be marvelously slippery. But this article will be focusing on some of the sleekest sports and performance cars out from across the last century, covering old-school favorites such as the Tatra T87 with modern marvels like Volkswagen's XL1.

This Czechoslavkian masterpiece is a genuinely fascinating piece of design. Before moving onto the aerodynamic aspects of the T87, it's first worth covering what else makes it such an intriguing automobile. Motivation is sourced from a rear-engined 2.9-liter V8, which kicked out an impressive-for-the-era 85 horsepower. Back in the '40s, that was a serious amount of power to be dealing with, and Tatra ensured its effectiveness by complimenting it with a seriously slippery design.

German Zeppelins provided plenty of influence behind the T87's iconic shape, and the end result was a drag coefficient of just 0.36 Cd. This enabled the 85-horse V8 to propel the T87 to an impressive 100 mph, although the handling characteristics of a rear-engined machine with independent swing-axle suspension resulted in a fair number of accidents. Rumor has it that, due to so many Nazi officers meeting their maker in T87 accidents, the car was actually banned for use – but the accuracy of these claims has been contested over the years. Still, it all adds to the fascinating history of Tatra's aerodynamic masterpiece.

Production spanned from 1936 to 1950, albeit with a gap during the war years. Despite such a wide production window, though, just over 3,000 T87s were ever built. It proved to be a significant influence to Ferdinand Porsche, and today, it's a cherished classic that attracts collectors willing to pay over $100,000.

The Huayra replaced the awesome Zonda in 2011, and it boasted a design that was just as striking. It was a million-dollar masterpiece at launch, packing a 720-horsepower twin-turbocharged 6.0-liter V12, capable of propelling it along to a top speed of 224 mph. Aerodynamic trickery helps the Italian to remain stable at speed, with front- and rear-mounted flaps that rise and fall to alter downforce levels.

It's important to remember that a supercar like this needs to be comfortable performing through the corners as well as the straight lines. Here's where that active aero really proves its relevancy. Those four flaps allow the Huayra to transform itself depending on the given requirements. While flat, the Huayra boasts a drag coefficient of just 0.31 Cd (as proven by a Mercedes wind tunnel). But they can also flip up to assist the car with braking and keep it planted through the corners.

Much like Pagani did with the Zonda, as the Huayra began to age, the automaker began to produce numerous special editions. Among them was a rolling $7.3 million air piece dubbed the Codalunga. The svelte model sports various ducts and diffusers, plus a longtail design – which is a known method of reducing the amount of drag a car's body produces. However, the Codalunga has not been individually tested in a wind tunnel, so whether it beats the standard Huayra's 0.31 Cd isn't officially known.

While on the topic of longtail designs, it seems appropriate to mention the McLaren Speedtail. This car categorically proves the effectiveness of such a design, with a drag coefficient of just under 0.29 Cd. The British firm from Woking knows a thing or two about churning out slippery supercars, but this Speedtail is something of a caricature; it sits 3 feet longer than the F1, and it's even longer than the F1 GTR Longtail. Helping it cut through the air as precisely as possible is a narrow frontal area, numerous concealed ducts, a complete lack of rear-view mirrors, and front wheels that are almost entirely enclosed.

Of course, a sleek design still needs to be complimented with a mighty power train if impressive performance is the end goal. And with a turbocharged 4.0-liter V8 that combines its efforts with a single electric motor to produce 1,035 horsepower, the Speedtail recipe is certainly a comprehensive one. Combined with a dry weight of just 3,153 pounds, that's enough to see the Speedtail achieve a top speed of 250 mph. Plus, it'll crack 186 mph in just shy of half the time required by its forefather, the mighty F1.

As impressive as the McLaren Speedtail's design is, what's arguably far more impressive is that a $44,245 Corvette from 2005 can match the $2.25 million British hypercar's drag coefficient. With a drag coefficient of just 0.28 Cd, the C6 Chevy is surprisingly slippery. This wasn't a one-hit-wonder for Chevrolet, either, as previous generations had also proven to be remarkably aerodynamic as well. But the C6 manages to outdo them all (albeit only marginally). The C4 boasted a drag coefficient of 0.34 Cd, while the C5 sat at 0.29 Cd. It's worth noting that later generations – including the current C8 — also sit behind the C6's rating.

It's no wonder that people think the C6 is still the best Corvette to date. Regardless of whether you agree or not, it's hard to find real fault with the C6. It combined a brilliantly efficient design with supercar-rivaling performance; the most powerful iteration was the 638-horsepower ZR1, which could top 200 mph. Even the base models were serious performers thanks to an LS1 lurking beneath that low-slung hood. These cars were affordable when new, and they're still great bang-for-buck choices today, with entry-level coupes and convertibles both available for sub-$25,000 – proving that slippery sports cars don't exclusively belong in six-figure price brackets.

Electric cars and hybrids pay a lot of attention to aerodynamics, as being able to slip through the air with minimal resistance is a particularly effective way of increasing a vehicle's range. That's led to buyers of electrified vehicles becoming understandably obsessed with realistic and effective ranges. It should come as no surprise, then, that BMW's bold hybridized i8 sports a drag coefficient of just 0.26 Cd.

That drag coefficient proves that the i8's striking design isn't all about stealing the attention of passers by, in fact, it's really quite clever. The various slices and slashes between those layered body panels help to direct airflow into the car's various radiators, which allows the i8 to disregard active aero. Even the cockpit itself is teardrop-shaped to reduce drag, while the flared rear pillars help keep the visuals sweet without damaging any aerodynamic benefits.

With a turbocharged 1.5-liter inline-three engine and single electric motor tackling the task of motivation, the i8's power train recipe could not be more different from that of the aforementioned Corvette. However, just like the C6, the BMW i8 now finds itself at a very tempting price point, with early examples now changing hands for sub-$40,000.

This one's probably something of a surprise, as unlike so many of the other mentions on this list, the Nissan GT-R doesn't exactly look immensely slippery. It's not particularly low-slung, it doesn't incorporate an obvious longtail design, nothing else about it is clearly geared toward aerodynamics — plus, there's a great big spoiler adorning the trunk. However, that hasn't stopped the GT-R from being a technological marvel with a drag coefficient of only 0.26 Cd. Yep, this chunky model with heaps of boost and gaping grills matches the rating of BMW's slippery little eco-minded coupe.

The secrets of the GT-R low drag coefficient are actually somewhat hidden. It sports an almost completely flat floor, a functional diffuser which helps to channel airflow efficiency, and a slightly tapered rear overhang. Also helping to direct the air in an effective manner are what Nissan describes as "aero-blades" adorning the front fenders, while the narrow fender vents help to provide valuable downforce at the front, too.

Throughout its impressive 18-year tenure, the R35 GT-R saw the power from its twin-turbocharged 3.8-liter V6 rise to genuine supercar levels. And the clever aero tweaks it received over those years helped drivers to make the most of that strength. It came incredibly close to cracking the 7-minute barrier on the Nurburgring, peaking with a time of 7:08.679 in its 2013 Nismo guise — breaking the world record for fastest volume production car in the process.

All of the cars featured upon this list are aerodynamically impressive, and they certainly sit a long way removed from the least aerodynamic cars ever made. However, the Volkswagen XL1 stands in a class of its own. The sole purpose of this striking little hybridized-diesel inline-three-powered sports car was to be as mightily efficient as possible.

The headline number here is a drag coefficient of just 0.19 Cd. To get this number down so far, it combines many of the other engineering techniques used by other cars on this list. It very obviously sports a teardrop profile like the i8, allowing the smoothest possible path for airflow. Like the GT-R, the car's floor is completely flat, preventing the underside from pushing against the wind. And — going above and beyond the Speedtail in this regard – covered rear wheels assist the XL1 in achieving its remarkably low drag coefficient.

It wasn't just aerodynamic efficiency that Volkswagen was chasing with the XL1. It was also designed to be immensely frugal on fuel, to the point where a Honda Insight or Toyota Prius might look like a big-block Chevy from the '70s in comparison. On the European combined test cycle, Volkswagen managed to achieve a frankly mind-boggling 313 mpg, a feat helped by the model's incredibly low curb weight (courtesy of magnesium wheels and a carbon-fiber monocoque chassis).
