Right now is an exciting time to be a God of War fan with the many projects the franchise has in the works. Prime Video is hard at work on a new God of War TV series, but there’s still no word yet on when it will be released. Ryan Hurst was originally tapped to play Kratos in the show, but after he suffered an injury that would have forced production to pause for months, he was recast with Dave Bautista, who is already almost ready to slot back in as cameras are preparing to roll once again. God of War mastermind Cory Barlog has also confirmed that Santa Monica is working on a new God of War game that will be centered around Kratos, but the franchise has another story to tell before it returns to its original protagonist.

We’re talking, of course, about Laufey, wife of Kratos who was first introduced into the franchise in God of War in 2016. The entire journey of the first God of War reboot game centers on Kratos and Atreus taking Faye’s ashes to the highest peak in all the realms, which they first think is in Midgard before later discovering she was talking about Jotunheim. Earlier this year at one of PlayStation’s State of Play events, Santa Monica took the stage to debut over 20 minutes of uninterrupted gameplay footage from God of War Laufey, and the studio later confirmed that the game would be released in February 2027. However, the official Santa Monica X account confirmed yesterday that the game has since been delayed to May 20, 2027, to give the team time to give it its finishing polishes.

iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF vs. State Street SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF: These Defense ETFs Have a Surprising Difference Investors Should Know for 2027

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

These two aerospace and defense ETFs invest in many of the same companies, but their different approaches could lead to surprising outcomes as military spending and commercial aviation trends evolve.

The iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA +1.28%) and State Street SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF (XAR +4.57%) offer exposure to two important industries: military defense and commercial aviation. With defense spending facing political uncertainty and commercial aerospace benefiting from continued demand, the distinction between these funds is increasingly relevant.

While the iShares fund concentrates heavily on a few household names along with substantial commercial aviation businesses, the State Street fund spreads its investments more evenly across aerospace and defense companies. These different approaches create distinct risk profiles that could influence performance heading into 2027.

The State Street fund is slightly more affordable with a 0.35% expense ratio compared to the 0.37% charged by iShares. Investors seeking income may prefer the iShares fund, which offers a slightly higher payout to its shareholders.

iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF focuses exclusively on U.S. aviation and defense companies through its market-cap-weighted strategy. It holds 50 stocks, and its largest positions include GE Aerospace (GE +2.70%) at 21.72%, RTX (RTX -1.23%) at 16.54%, and Boeing (BA +0.52%) at 6.99%. This strategy results in a top-heavy portfolio where the success of a few aerospace titans significantly impacts the overall performance. The fund was launched in 2006 and has paid $0.57 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$214.7 share price works out to a 0.27% yield.

The State Street SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF tracks the S&P Aerospace & Defense Select Industry Index, concentrating 100% of its portfolio in Industrials. It also holds 50 stocks, but its modified equal-weighting means its largest positions include Lockheed Martin Corp (LMT -1.58%) at 3.57%, Textron, Inc. (TXT -1.98%) at 3.47%, and TransDigm Group, Inc. (TDG +0.66%) at 3.29%. This equal-weight approach limits the influence of any single company and provides more exposure to mid-cap innovators within the sector. The fund was launched in 2011 and has paid $0.42 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$231.1 share price works out to a 0.2% yield.

The iShares fund offers concentrated exposure to established industry leaders that account for nearly 38% of its portfolio. These substantial commercial aviation businesses offer opportunities beyond government defense contracts, such as aircraft production and maintenance. However, this concentration also leaves the fund particularly vulnerable to disappointing results from a handful of companies.

The State Street fund takes a more balanced approach, spreading its investments across large, mid-, and small-cap aerospace and defense companies. Its modified equal-weighting reduces dependence on individual industry giants while providing greater exposure to specialized manufacturers and emerging defense technologies. It also carries a slightly lower expense ratio of 0.35%, compared with 0.37% for the iShares fund.

For long-term investors the State Street SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF looks like the better buy. Its broader distribution of holdings offers a more balanced way to participate in military modernization and commercial aerospace growth without relying so heavily on a few industry leaders. That said, smaller contractors can introduce additional volatility, and neither fund is immune to changes in defense budgets or a slowdown in aircraft demand.

These Robotic Arms Are Powered by Algae. Watch How They Move

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

What if a robot could be squishy? Soft, flexible robots could be useful for a wide variety of applications, including artificial muscles and wearable devices—or really any application where a robot must be especially delicate.

This is what has fueled development of “soft actuators” that can flex like a bicep and have a fine-tuned sense of touch as in the human hand. Like your stereotypical robot, soft actuators convert energy—such as electricity, heat, or light—into mechanical motion, but they look nothing like the metal and wire constructs that the word “robot” most often evokes.

One variety of these soft robots, known as bioactuators, utilizes the functions of living cells to generate motion. These biological machines have attracted particular attention in recent years, and now a research team at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands has developed a soft robotic arm colonized by algae on its surface, and when the microbes photosynthesize, the arm moves.

“We didn’t start with a specific application. Our goal has always been to bring the research of living materials to new levels,” Marie-Eve Aubin-Tam, who led the research, explained in a press release. “We wanted to explore what becomes possible when living cells are integrated into functional materials.”

The tiny robot arms—about three-quarters of an inch long and about as thick as a credit card—are constructed of two layers of hydrogel that the algal cells live on. One of these layers responds to changes in pH, which are mediated by the metabolism of the algae. When light strikes the algae, they begin to photosynthesize. By absorbing carbon dioxide and fixing nitrogen in this process, the algae make the local pH more basic, making the responsive hydrogel swell. Conversely, when it becomes dark, the algae release carbon dioxide through respiration, returning the pH to the acidic side and the hydrogel shrinks.

The difference in the volumes of the two hydrogel layers controls the bending. According to the researchers, the pH-responsive hydrogel layer changed significantly with the algae’s activity. In acidic conditions, such as between pH 3 and 7, the layer was approximately 37 percent of its original weight, but it had quadruple its original weight at pH 11.

Other researchers have constructed soft robots that respond to pH, but typically the chemistry of all the surrounding liquid has to be altered. This is not the case for the algal bots. The photosynthetic activity has an effect that’s much more local, meaning it would be better suited to applications where minimal environmental impact is essential.

These aren’t, however, the speediest of robots. The chemical shift begins quickly, about 20 seconds after light is applied and about 10 seconds after it becomes dark, but it takes several hours for the hydrogel to bend significantly.

After their first proof of concept, the research team constructed several more algal devices. One was a four-fingered gripper that closes when exposed to light. It successfully grasped and lifted a hollow metal sphere, releasing it when the light was turned off. Another hook-shaped gripper could pick up and put down a rubber ring. These items were extremely light, weighing far less than a small paperclip, but the arms were able to grasp them with great dexterity.

These soft robots also require no external energy source other than light and no complex mechanical parts. “Microalgae are plantlike organisms that are largely self-sustaining,” Aubin-Tam said. This “gives the actuator potential for long-term operation with minimal external input. In theory, it wouldn’t require a lot of energy to run a system based on this technology.”

In the long term, the research team hopes this will contribute to the development of more autonomous, environment-responsive soft robots that could be used as automated agricultural equipment or for fluid control in lab-on-a-chip devices or really as part of any underwater robotics system.

Since the algae do not need to be genetically modified, there are none of the biosafety concerns often associated with microbe-based technologies. If anything, these are exceptionally environmentally friendly machines, since these organisms can absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere while deriving energy from sunlight.

The 13 Best London Museums to Visit

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

London’s numerous museums feature some of the best art, history, science, and cultural collections in the world. With many of these institutions free to enter, paying a visit to one or more can even be an inexpensive day out.

Many of the museums listed here have recently gone through major revamps, and over the coming year there will be even more destinations to visit, with the long-awaited London Museum coming to the historic Smithfield Market and the Museum of Shakespeare opening on the site of the Curtain Playhouse.

If you’re looking for extraordinary cultural objects from the dawn of civilization, head over to the venerable British Museum. There you’ll find such treasures as an impressive collection of ancient Mesopotamian items; the second-century BC Rosetta Stone, which provided the key to deciphering ancient Egyptian hieroglyphics; and the fifth-century BC ancient Greek Parthenon Marbles. Look up to see British starchitect Norman Foster’s amazing glass roof, completed in 2000. The British Museum holds more than eight million objects covering over two million years of human history, with about 80,000 objects on view at any given time. If you feel overwhelmed with the choice of what to see, book a tour and enjoy the luxury of being in the hands of a knowledgeable guide.

Right in the center of London on Trafalgar Square, the National Gallery offers artworks dating from 1260 right up to the 20th century—from Da Vinci to David, Canaletto to Constable, Michelangelo to Monet, Rousseau to Rubens, Cezanne to Seurat, Vermeer to Velazquez to Van Gogh, and that’s just a small fraction of its offerings. And it’s free to enter. For a fee, there are also larger exhibitions in the much acclaimed, recently renovated Sainsbury Wing. The Sainsbury Wing is quite the character itself, of course, ever since in 1984 King Charles III, while still Prince of Wales, described the initial proposed design for it as a “monstrous carbuncle on the face of a much-loved and elegant friend.” The plan was altered, and what opened in 1991 is what you see now: classical mannerism in a sensitively designed addition to the main gallery’s domed facade. After your visit, head to the St-Martin-in-the-Fields Crypt just over the road for a good-value hot canteen meal, and linger in this famous neoclassical church by 18th-century architect James Gibbs for one of its candlelight concerts.

The beating heart of the city, the Tower of London is steeped in so much history it’s dizzying—from the White Tower’s inception as a fortified palace by William the Conqueror on his invasion after the Battle of Hastings, in 1066, to its long-term use as a prison. King Henry VIII’s wife Anne Boleyn was imprisoned and executed there in 1536 after she was accused of adultery; Guy Fawkes, Robert Catesby, and the Gunpowder Plotters were kept in the Tower in 1605 after their failed plan to blow up the Houses of Parliament; and deputy Nazi Party leader Rudolf Hess was confined there in 1941. The Crown Jewels have been safeguarded at the Tower since 1661 and are immaculately displayed; other highlights include Anne Boleyn’s final resting place, an incredible collection of armor, twisted metal artifacts from the 1666 Great Fire of London, the Medieval Palace (in use until 1307) that recently opened to the public, and of course the Yeomen Warders, or Beefeaters, the retired military personnel who run the place. And if that isn’t enough, there’s also a fantastic immersive experience about the Gunpowder Plot in the old tunnels just outside the walls of the Tower, a superb addition that should be booked in advance.

A shrine to all things bright and beautiful and to creatures great and small, the Natural History Museum in South Kensington (around the corner from the Science Museum) was founded with proceeds from the Great Exhibition of 1851. Quite apart from what’s inside, this museum’s facade encapsulates its purpose: Stand in front of the building and see how many extinct and living species you can spot— there are saber-toothed cats, pterodactyls, ancient palaeotherium (stocky mammals with a beaklike snout), extinct and modern fish, lizards, goats, lions, and wolves. Opened in 1881, this museum was fast dubbed a “cathedral of nature,” and it still is, filled to the brim with flora and fauna, including a magnificent blue whale skeleton suspended from the ceiling. The museum holds an astonishing collection of specimens, 80 million in total, only a fraction of which can be displayed at any one time. Head to the all-time favorite Dinosaur galleries, complete with an animatronic Tyrannosaurus rex; then take your pick from volcanoes, fossils, human evolution, mammals, fish, amphibians, reptiles, minerals, and more. If the sheer amount of stuff is daunting, start with the Treasures gallery for the cream of the crop.

There is so much to see at the Victoria and Albert Museum in South Kensington that you just have to prioritize. With astonishing collections from Britain and across the world, the V&A holds examples of jewelry, design, fashion, art, and architecture, from medieval times to the modern era. One of the timeless highlights of this museum is the Cast Courts: The Victorians were mad about taking casts of classic sculptures, and we reap the rewards today. Built specifically to house the enormous reproductions of Trajan’s Column from ancient Rome, these monumental courts also hold an electrifying cast of Michelangelo’s David. Make sure you also see the Raphael Cartoons housed in their own gallery, and find out which William Morris works the museum currently has on show; his enduring Arts and Crafts textile and wallpaper designs are widely loved. And allow at least half an hour for a good browse of the V&A shop. It’s one of the best museum stores in existence.

Tate Modern has been a fixture of the art world since it was inaugurated in the year 2000 in an ex-power station, an ambitious undertaking by the Swiss starchitects Herzog and De Meuron. There’s a lot to see here; the best approach may be to concentrate on either the collections or the special exhibitions and not attempt to absorb it all in one sitting. Collection wise, don’t miss Surrealist Salvador Dalí’s 1938 Lobster Telephone; Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913, cast 1972) by Italian futurist Umberto Boccioni; Composition (1913) by British Vorticist Wyndham Lewis; the notorious Fountain by Marcel Duchamp (1917, replica 1964); the calming color fields of Mark Rothko’s Seagram murals (commissioned in 1958); and the classic Pop Art punch Whaam! (1963) by Roy Lichtenstein.

A treat for kids and adults alike, the Science Museum is always captivating. Wander into the Energy Hall for a hit of seductive steam-driven engines and then into the freshly reopened Space section to see space capsules from the 1960s to today. For those wanting a more interactive experience, there is the Wonderlab, for visitors 7 to 14 years old, and Power Up, a hands-on gaming experience for anyone over the age of 5, where guests can play on 160 consoles spanning five decades. (Be sure to book the interactive galleries ahead of time.) There’s an IMAX Cinema, too, with one of the biggest screens in Europe. The Science Museum has redone many of its galleries in recent years, including the excellent Energy Revolution, about the history of energy production, with examples of an antique electric car and offshore energy near the coast of Scotland; and the stunning Mathematics gallery, housed in a wickedly futuristic design by the late architect Zaha Hadid, showing objects such as intriguing handheld mathematical instruments, a 1929 experimental aircraft, early computers, and coding machines. There’s much more, covering such subjects as flight, clockmaking, science from 1550 to 1800, engineering, and medicine. And if you still want more, you can stay the night in the museum’s current season of Astronights, for space-obsessed children and their parents.

Founded by sugar magnate Henry Tate and opened in 1897, Tate Britain holds cornerstone works of the Victorian circle of Pre-Raphaelites, including Ophelia (1851–2) by John Everett Millais, John William Waterhouse’s 1888 portrayal of The Lady of Shalott, and lesser-known works by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, who exhumed his wife’s corpse just to recover an unpublished poetry manuscript with which she’d been buried. If you want to delve deeper into darkness, seek out the contorted forms of Francis Bacon’s 1944 work Three Studies for the Figures at the Base of a Crucifixion. Then recover by contemplating the gentle forms of British sculptor Henry Moore. Also in the collection are Torso in Metal from the Rock Drill (1913–15) by Jacob Epstein, a pivotal Vorticist work; Fall (1963) by Bridget Riley, one of her signature Op Art illusions; and the bright tones of A Bigger Splash (1967) by David Hockney.

Plunge into the decadent rococo interiors of the Wallace Collection with feel-good artworks and exquisitely crafted armor displayed in luxurious settings. Make sure you see The Swing (Les hasards heureux de l’escarpolette) (1767–68) by the Rococo hedonist Jean-Honoré Fragonard—it’s one of the sexiest paintings ever made—as well as the museum’s fabulously sumptuous collection of François Boucher paintings. The Laughing Cavalier (1624) by Dutch portraitist Frans Hals, enigmatic and engaging, also adorns the Wallace’s walls.

Entering the Courtauld’s permanent collection feels like getting together for a few beers with a bunch of old school friends—people you know, love, and will always relish the company of. With all the greats from the Renaissance masters to the Postimpressionists, the Courtauld shows you an old friend everywhere you look. There’s plenty to reacquaint yourself with, including Adam and Eve (1526) by Lucas Cranach the Elder, A Bar at the Folies-Bergère (1882) by Édouard Manet, Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear (1889) by Vincent van Gogh, and a strong array of works by Paul Cezanne, Edgar Degas, Paul Gauguin, Claude Monet, Auguste Renoir, Georges Seurat, and many more.

Within the concrete jungle of the Southbank Centre nestles the Hayward Gallery, which consistently delivers excellent contemporary works in its novel 1960s concrete environment. There is not permanent collection and all temporary shows are ticketed. Pop over to the British Film Institute afterwards for a movie, or to the Royal Festival Hall for a meal or a performance.

From 1512 until the mid-18th century, Greenwich was the headquarters for the British navy. First established under King Henry VIII, the Royal Navy became key to Britain’s success as a nation in trade and warfare alike, and its base was always Greenwich, now a UNESCO World Heritage site. Among the noteworthy attractions is the Queen’s House, built for Anne of Denmark (1574–1619) and designed by the so-called father of British architecture, Inigo Jones. (And if you approach from the river, you’ll see the remarkable symmetry in the buildings that flank the house, one of Christopher Wren’s classic feats of architectural wonder.) Once a royal residence, the Queen’s House now holds an excellent collection of art, including the 1588 Armada Portrait of Elizabeth I sitting in regal splendor before scenes showing the ruined Spanish Armada and the victorious British fleet. You can also see 19th-century master J.M.W. Turner’s largest canvas, The Battle of Trafalgar (1822), only recently put on display.

But that’s only one small part of the Greenwich complex. Don’t miss England’s answer to the Sistine Chapel at the Old Royal Naval College: the Painted Hall (1707–26), James Thornhill’s masterpiece, reopened to the public in 2019 after major conservation. For a comprehensive history of Britain’s maritime exploits, from sailing the seven seas and polar exploration to warfare and global trade, visit the National Maritime Museum, whose highlights include the Trafalgar Coat, the uniform that Admiral Horatio Nelson wore on his deathbed in 1805. Then visit the Cutty Sark, the fastest sailing ship of its time. Built in Scotland in 1869, she traded tea from China with a typical round trip taking just seven to nine months. And then, to tie the whole site together, head to the iconic onion dome of the Royal Observatory, where time was first standardized, in 1884. Step on the prime meridian line, gratis, or buy advance tickets to see the Great Equatorial Telescope, installed in 1893.

Complete with its own ride through the old underground tunnels used to transport mail, the Postal Museum tells the story of the Royal Mail, which might not sound immediately enticing, but this museum does it so well, it’s worth a visit. Its manageable size, brilliant story, and ride on the Mail Rail, with history explained via engaging projections on the tunnel walls, make this museum a gem and superb for visitors of all ages.

Real Madrid: why José Mourinho has enormous hatred for FC Barcelona

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Tonight is Clasico! FC Barcelona welcomes Real Madrid to the Camp Nou, in a match followed by the whole world, and with a lot at stake on all sides. Beyond the historic rivalry, the Merengues must win or risk finding themselves 9 points behind their sworn enemy in the La Liga standings. There are players like Lamine Yamal or Kylian Mbappé who will also want to show their best side. But if there is a man who plays big, and who is keen to hurt his evening rival, it is José Mourinho.

And for good reason, the Portuguese coach has real hatred for FC Barcelona. And it’s reciprocal, even if the tension has weakened a little since his first sulphurous stint at the head of the Merengues. It must be said that the story of hatred between the Special One and the Catalan club had the potential to be in reality… a love story. Younger generations don’t know it, but in 1996, a young José Mourinho arrived at FC Barcelona as assistant to Bobbt Robson, whom he had known in Portugal. He then remained at the club despite the Englishman’s dismissal a year later, occupying different roles in the different staffs that followed, for example being a video analyst, then again assistant.

If many supporters culés make fun of him by calling him “the translator” – he actually took care of translating Robson’s instructions at the time – he was very appreciated internally by the players, including, at the time, young Carles Puyol, Xavi or Pep Guardiola. However, the Catalan management never really wanted to offer him a more important role, he who was rather happy, to the point where some explained that his dream was to become the coach of the first team. Which could have happened well after his departure from the club in 2000, since on several occasions, his name was announced as a potential coach of the A. In 2008, the club thought very strongly of him… But Laporta finally offered to Guardiola. Multiple disappointments which caused real scars for the Portuguese.

In 2010, before arriving on the bench at the Bernabéu, he also had a very lively Champions League semi-final against FC Barcelona, ​​with barbs at a press conference, which had already put him in the sights of the Barcelonans. And inevitably, upon his arrival, this aversion was accentuated on one side or the other. Not helped by a very complicated media context, and a rather difficult sporting context with the man who was surely the best Barça in history opposite, José Mourinho adopted an almost warlike strategy upon his arrival. The Special One had thus multiplied the media outings against the Catalan club, with accusations linked to arbitration, and this famous “Por Qué? » (“why” in Spanish, Editor’s note) after the 2011 semi-final in which he questioned the decisions of many referees who would have favored Barça in previous years.

It is also said that the famous Clasico de la manita (5-0) in November 2010 had particularly affected him on a moral level, and the presence of a Pep Guardiola praised by the public in front of him also frustrated him. On the pitch, his team also played particularly aggressively, and there was also this episode of the Spanish Super Cup in the summer of 2011, during which he put his finger in the eye of Tito Vilanova (then in Guardiola’s staff) during a scuffle. For Mourinho, creating permanent tension with Barça was also a strategy, serving to mobilize everyone madridismo behind him, and to put pressure on his rival. Since his return this summer, and after several rather peaceful weeks, he very quickly put a part back into the machine, more than ten years later.

Even if he is perhaps a little less virulent and explicit in his remarks than at the beginning of the 2010s, the Special One does not miss an opportunity to launch accusations against Barça and the arbitrations which would be favorable to it. The Negreira affair has logically been mentioned several times by the Lusitanian since the start of the season. Even if it is clear that this season, Mourinho’s speech is a little less popular with madridistas, with a good number of supporters accusing him of wanting to divert attention from the team’s real problems by talking about Barça or the referees. Tonight, at the Camp Nou, José Mourinho will meet his sworn enemy, and there is no doubt that any result other than a victory will be a huge disappointment for him…

Which Generation Of Hyundai Tucson Is Safest According To The IIHS

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Car safety has come a long way since the days of yore. Ask the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS). The organization's crash testing standards have evolved significantly over 30+ years as more new vehicles keep getting better at protecting their occupants. By now, we're pretty familiar with the IIHS Top Safety Pick awards. However, just because your second-gen 2011 Hyundai Tucson has the Top Safety Pick under its belt doesn't mean it's as safe as the third-gen 2021 model, which also happens to be a Top Safety Pick winner.

Although the IIHS rates the small SUV highly, understand that it also keeps updating the award's eligibility criteria as the years go by. For example, headlights were not even part of the Top Safety Pick criteria in 2010, nor were vehicle-to-vehicle or pedestrian crash-prevention features. And Top Safety Pick+ wasn't even a thing before 2013. In fact, looking at the many generations of the small SUV, it's fair to conclude that fourth-generation Tucsons (2022-2026) appear to be the safest, according to the IIHS. Not only have certain fourth-generation Tucsons gone through the organization's slew of updated testing standards, but they have consistently been part of the IIHS Top Safety Pick+ category between 2022 and 2026, unlike the third or prior generation models.

If you're looking for the safest model year within the fourth generation, the 2026 Tucson looks like the strongest candidate. While models built after March 2024 have fared really well in the updated tests, the eligibility criteria for Top Safety Pick+ have changed between 2024 and 2026, with the most recent update pushing automakers to improve their crash avoidance systems and protection for back seat passengers. Still, despite the tougher rules, the 2026 Tucson meets all the requirements for Top Safety Pick+.

Although the 2026 Tucson won the Top Safety Pick+ award, it's worth noting that the small SUV performed poorly in the IIHS's latest whiplash prevention test. This replaces the original head restraint test that was discontinued in 2022. The new test evaluates three important aspects: "head and spine support, the interaction of the head with the head restraint, and how well the seat handles the energy transfer during impact."

If it means anything, only four out of the first 18 small SUVs tested scored really well and earned a Good rating, according to the IIHS. Although the Tucson isn't part of the group, that's not to say no Hyundai is — the Ioniq 5 was among the SUVs that scored a Good rating, as are the Subaru Forester, Toyota RAV4, and Audi Q3. Joining the Tucson with a poor whiplash prevention score are the Ford Bronco Sport and Mazda CX-50.

According to the IIHS, the Tucson's head restraint pushed the dummy's chin down toward the chest. While most of the vehicles tested were 2025 models, the IIHS says these ratings can apply to certain 2026 models as well. Something similar happened with the aforementioned second-generation Tucson as well. Although all model years except 2014 and 2015 were awarded the Top Safety Pick, they performed poorly in the driver's side small overlap test. IIHS noted that the side curtain airbags didn't deploy, the seatbelts allowed excessive movement, and the dummy's head barely contacted the front airbag. Third-gen and later Tucsons, however, performed considerably better in the test.

Notably, the fourth-gen Tucson received Good ratings in every other area except its LATCH ease of use and headlights (for trims other than Limited), where it received Acceptable ratings.
