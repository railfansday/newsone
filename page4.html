8 Modern Thriller Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There's plenty of adrenaline to be found in modern thriller books. With anxiety, crime, and social tensions seemingly at an all-time high, these novels keep readers up past midnight, tapping into some of society's biggest fears. Whether it’s fractured relationships or institutional corruption, thriller books translate these tensions into pages.

Whether you're into psychological mind games or constant plot twists, there's something for every kind of thriller fan. From an international murder conspiracy to a desperate attempt to prove someone's loyalty to both sides of a war, here are modern thriller books to read before kicking the bucket.

Shutter Island is proof that books can be better than their movie adaptations. Dennis Lehane's novel follows U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his new partner, Chuck Aule, who are sent to a remote island to investigate a patient's disappearance from a hospital for the criminally insane. The staff's strange behavior makes their investigation anything but routine, and a storm soon leaves them with no way off the island.

Unlike the movie, the novel allows Teddy's descent into madness to progress more organically. The limited point of view makes his increasingly questionable thoughts and assumptions feel convincing, and that's all thanks to Lehane's ability to hide Teddy's real experiences with eloquent prose. With the hospital staff constantly making him question his own judgment, readers are left just as uncertain as Teddy about what is real and what isn't.

Viet Thanh Nguyen's The Sympathizer explores the Vietnam War through the eyes of someone living in a constant state of in-betweens. A half-Vietnamese, half-French communist spy, the novel's unnamed narrator is forced to flee Saigon for Los Angeles. Despite having to rebuild his life in a new country, he secretly keeps tabs on South Vietnamese military officials living in exile.

It's a never-ending losing game in The Sympathizer, with the narrator unable to please either side without betraying the other. He is constantly caught between his political beliefs, personal identity, and the need to stay alive. No matter where he goes, he cannot simply leave his past behind. The Sympathizer explores this constant duality between two identities, two loyalties, and two countries, showing just how difficult it is to start over when his own allegiances keep pulling him in opposite directions.

BookTok sensations do not always deserve their stellar reputation, but Alex Michaelides' The Silent Patient is a cautionary tale of what it means to be a silenced woman, quite literally. Alicia Berenson seems to have the perfect life as a successful painter married to Gabriel, until she shoots him five times. After his death, she stops speaking and is admitted to a psychiatric facility. Six years later, psychotherapist Theo Faber takes on her case.

Michaelides complicates the story by bringing in two near-convincing perspectives. Theo's investigation and Alicia's diary entries reveal different sides of the events surrounding Gabriel's murder, though neither account can be trusted entirely. With nobody telling the whole truth, readers must decide whose version to believe: the accused murderer, or a man convinced he has her figured out.

Lucy Foley's The Guest List is essentially Agatha Christie meets Knives Out. Magazine publisher Jules Keegan and television personality Will Slater gather their handpicked guests for a glamorous wedding on a remote Irish island. But the celebrations take a grim turn when a storm cuts off the island from the mainland. Before the couple can even say their "I dos," Will is found stabbed to death, leaving everyone a potential suspect.

Unlike most murder mysteries, The Guest List doesn't exactly make readers feel sorry for the victim. The guests' motives trace back to an incident from years earlier, one that could ruin Will's reputation (before he died). There's something almost satisfyingly karmic about his tragic fate. But then again, no one wants to be on the same stranded island as an unknown killer.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

Anyone who enjoyed Stephen King's Misery should give Taylor Adams' novel a read. No Exit follows college student Darby Thorne, who gets stranded at an isolated highway rest stop during a Colorado blizzard. With four strangers for company, Darby is horrified to discover a kidnapped nine-year-old girl locked inside a van outside. The worst part is that the kidnapper is one of the four strangers sharing the rest stop with her.

Darby is just someone who happens to be in the wrong place at the wrong time, which makes her situation all the more dire. With no one she can trust, she has to rely on her limited abilities to stay alive and save the child. It's a nerve-wracking survival thriller, but also a disturbing reminder that situations like these aren't just fictional scenarios and can happen to anyone, particularly women who find themselves in vulnerable situations.

Stieg Larsson's The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows disgraced journalist Mikael Blomkvist, who accepts a job from wealthy businessman Henrik Vanger to investigate the disappearance of his niece, Harriet, who vanished during a family gathering on the Vanger family's remote island four decades earlier. Working alongside hacker Lisbeth Salander, Mikael soon realizes that there's much more to the case.

Often considered a quintessential Nordic noir, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo is dark, bleak, and shows little mercy towards its flawed characters. Mikael and Lisbeth are far from conventional heroes, and neither has had an easy life. In many ways, they're both just trying to survive. But as they uncover the truth behind Harriet's disappearance and the dangers surrounding it, they find a new purpose: fighting back, even if it means breaking the law.

Adapted into an HBO limited series, Sharp Objects follows journalist Camille Preaker, who reluctantly returns to her Missouri hometown to investigate the murder of a young girl and the disappearance of another. Forced to live with her estranged mother, controlling stepfather, and rebellious half-sister Amma, Camille must also confront memories of her late sister Marian. As the investigation takes a darker turn, Camille begins to suspect that the case is closer to home than she would like.

Gillian Flynn might be best known for her work Gone Girl, but Sharp Objects raises an important warning about the dangers of unaddressed intergenerational trauma. Through Camille's strained family relationships, the series explores how abuse doesn't just affect one person; it creates a hostile environment where trauma gets passed down, damages relationships, and breeds new forms of violence.

Few works of art are as iconic as Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa, so it's no surprise that Dan Brown uses it as the starting point for The Da Vinci Code. After Louvre curator Jacques Saunière is found murdered, Harvard professor Robert Langdon becomes involved in an investigation that takes him through hidden messages, famous paintings, and the mystery of the Holy Grail.

Brown isn't simply interested in telling a murder mystery. He also plays with historical accounts, particularly those surrounding Christianity, by exploring what might happen if widely accepted beliefs were challenged. Some readers may find this approach uncomfortable, but it makes the novel all the more interesting. Brown takes complicated subjects like art, religion, and ancient history and turns them into a puzzle that makes readers want to crack open a history book for fun.

Star Trek: Across The Unknown Is Officially Reviving A Major Voyager-Era Villain

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Star Trek remains one of the most enduring franchises in the sci-fi genre, and though things are going to be a little quieter on the television front for a while, there's still plenty going on besides. Both of the currently active shows are set to end relatively soon, with both Strange New Worlds and Starfleet Academy's final series set to air in 2027.

Though that could be seen as a bad sign, plenty of other exciting projects have been released this year in other mediums, and there's more coming down the pipeline all the time. In fact, one of the best arrivals of this year has just confirmed a major update, and it gives players the chance to explore some of the most compelling portions of Voyager's narrative.

As first reported by BleedingCool, Daedalic Entertainment and gameXcite have confirmed that the fourth and final DLC for Star Trek: Voyager – Across the Unknown is titled Year of Hell. As was the case in the show, you'll be taking on Krenim captain Annorax, and his ability to rewrite history as he sees fit.

In confirming the news, the developer shared the following post on the game's Steam page:

"The U.S.S. Voyager has survived the Borg, the Kazon, and everything else the Delta Quadrant has thrown at her. But how do you fight an enemy who can erase an entire civilization from history? Meet Annorax. His idea of correcting history is making entire species disappear. Year of Hell, the fourth Expansion Pass DLC for Star Trek: Voyager - Across the Unknown, is a self-contained mini-campaign of three new sectors, inspired by one of Voyager's most memorable two-part episodes. Annorax's timeship is on your trail, and difficult challenges lie ahead.

"Annorax is a Krenim captain obsessed with saving his family and armed with a weapon that can erase an entire species in an instant. History changes, and whole civilizations cease to exist. Voyager has the misfortune of getting in his way. If you thought the journey home was difficult before, this particular year may change your mind. The mini-campaign follows its own story and leaves your main game save untouched. Your fully built out ship? That is another matter."

The update is set to go live on December 9, and it's set to serve as the final DLC for a game that has proved very popular with existing Star Trek fans, as well as those coming into the experience cold. The developers have also done a great job of updating it so far, moving the reviews from Mostly Positive overall, to Very Positive in more recent times.

All of that has coalesced to make the game one of the best adaptations we've seen for Star Trek in recent years, and it's well worth a go if you're looking to immerse yourself as a fan.

After that, we also have the new Star Trek cinematic adaptations to look forward to from Paramount, which leaves the Kelvin Timeline behind in favor of a full reset and reboot for modern audiences.

One of Star Trek's most iconic fight scenes was also one of the most miserable to film. William Shatner recently talked about how bad it was to do the Gorn fight in the original series, and how it felt even worse to actually shoot than it looked onscreen.

"Arena" is likely one of the most memorable episodes of Star Trek decades later. Unfortunately, that's not necessarily a great thing, as it contains (in my opinion) an obscenely bad fight scene. It's one in which Shatner's Kirk fights in slow motion with a member of the Gorn species. In addition to the iconic scene aging poorly, with effects that even looked dated back then, Shatner mentioned at New York Comic Con it was also terrible to film (via Reddit):

The guy was in this rubber suit; it's 120 degrees in the desert. I have to roll in the regolith and get all dusty. And when you go to brush it off, you get blood.

The "regolith," which by the way are the minerals that settle on top of bedrock, were so abrasive that it made the fight very difficult to film. I'm sure most Star Trek fans don't need to rewatch the scene with a Paramount+ subscription to know the end result ultimately wasn't worth the pain and toil. Still, lame fight aside, "Arena" is a pretty compelling episode.

"Arena" starts when Kirk and the Gorn are transported to the planet to battle thanks to a third species named the Metrons. The Metrons wish to see a fight to the death, but are impressed when Kirk outmaneuvers the Gorn and spares its life instead. Both are then returned to their ship, with the lesson being that brains can overcome brawn with some quick thinking and skills.

It's great to hear William Shatner talk about the sillier times, as Star Trek fans were hit with some heavy news as of late. The passing of Walter Koenig was a gut punch to many, and following it with Patrick Stewart's death had many Trekkies out of the count. Shatner has even joked he's keeping an eye out for the Grim Reaper, trying to take the edge off such a heavy time.

As for Star Trek in general, there's an overarching question about what direction the franchise is headed after its 60th anniversary. There's a movie in development, but with no solid timeline and no word that there are any shows actively being made, there are a lot of questions that still need to be answered.

For the time being, there's a lot of Star Trek to watch and enjoy over on Paramount+. Those who have never seen "Arena can go and check it out now for the first time, or some of the other classic Trek shows as we wait for new entries in the franchise.

World football’s governing body, Fifa, has been targeted in a cyberattack that compromised what it describes as a "limited set of personal and business information," it confirmed on Thursday.

"Fifa is working closely with the relevant authorities, including law enforcement," the organisation stated in a message.

Fifa did not reveal when the cyberattack occurred or provide the precise nature of the breached data.

Last week, a group calling itself the Handsome Boy Hacking Club posted a screenshot on X of what it claimed were Fifa files. Three days later, the group appeared to take credit when responding to a post stating that more than 12 gigabytes of internal documents had been leaked.

"FIFA condemns in the strongest possible terms the unlawful acquisition and publication of information, particularly personal data," the governing body said in its statement. "Such actions constitute a serious and unacceptable violation of privacy and can have significant consequences for the individuals concerned."

The breach occurs at a time when Fifa and its president, Gianni Infantino, face heightened scrutiny following a foiled, secretive plan to sell stakes in the World Cup to private investors.

Infantino is also attempting to extend his 11-year presidency of Fifa to 2031. He remains the favourite when the election is held on March 18, with Kohzo Tashima of Japan and Philippe Diallo of France standing as the other candidates.

TSA reportedly told United Airlines to suspend a pilot accused of supporting a terrorist group, after the pilots union resisted action according to a federal government source cited by TV news reporter Mike Wagenheim. The new report says the intervention happened last week. The pilot hasn’t been named. Neither has the terror group he allegedly supported. Mike Wagenheim’s November 24 update on the reported TSA intervention Wagenheim’s earlier report cited two sources saying United knew of up to six additional crew members who’d expressed support for terrorist groups. United’s pilots union spokesman declined to discuss individual cases. He said the Air Line Pilots Association has a professional standards committee and legal team that assess accusations against its ethics code and United’s workplace guidelines. The Pilot Israel Barred Was Already On Leave In October, Israel prohibited flights with Sina Amjadian serving as crew from arriving in the country. Israeli officials confirmed the restriction after receiving allegations that he’d expressed support for Iran’s Revolutionary Guards and Hezbollah. But United said he wasn’t currently flying, having been on leave since 2024. He flew Boeing 737s and wasn’t qualified on long haul aircraft. Apparently there was material showing Iranian leadership imagery and mourning civilians killed in strikes. There are claims of Hezbollah support and that there was a video of him kissing the regime’s emblem. United Pilot Celebrated October 7 Rapes And Murders As ‘Bravery’ I first wrote about Ibrahim Mossallam in November 2023, after his Facebook description of the October 7 attack as “resistance by a brave people.” United removed him from service with pay but he was reported back flying. On October 6, 2026, after the flydubai pilot sought to use his plane as a missile to attack Israel, United told me: We have withheld this pilot from service pending an ongoing review. There were also reports that he returned after just a day, and that two colleagues had given up first class seats to monitor him in the cockpit. United subsequently denied both accounts. A reported claimed the union blocked his firing. TSA Can Order A Pilot Out Of The Cockpit It was striking that the reported order came from TSA. It has authority that extends well beyond passenger checkpoints. Under federal law, a TSA determination that a pilot poses, or is suspected of posing, a terrorism or passenger safety threat requires the Federal Aviation Administration to take action against the pilot’s certificate. TSA can request that the action take effect immediately. It can also issue mandatory security directives to airlines. However, it isn’t clear from the report what legal mechanism is claimed to have been used and doesn’t report an FAA certificate action. It says the pilot’s “TSA credential” was revoked and describes that as Known Crewmember access. TSA can require an airline to remove someone’s access, but losing that privilege doesn’t mean losing a pilot’s license. Flydubai Shows Why The Concern Is Real I don’t need to know a pilot’s politics. But I wouldn’t want someone who celebrates the killing of civilians at the controls of my flight. That calls their judgment into question even without evidence they’ve planned an attack themselves. The September 30 flydubai cockpit attack makes the concern concrete. The co-pilot of that flight planned a suicide attack on the Tel Aviv airport, attacking the captain with an axe to take over the aircraft. He had satisfied the licensing, good conduct and security requirements for the job, and those checks had failed to uncover his extremist intentions.
