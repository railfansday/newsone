As widespread tech outages pile up, AI tools are in the spotlight again

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Inaccessible email inboxes. Banking delays. And a communications meltdown that grounded airplanes for hours, followed by waves of other travel disruptions.

In an increasingly interconnected world, some fear such disruptions could be the new normal.

Over the past week alone, problems that appeared to emerge from routine updates, security patches or other malfunctions surfaced at Microsoft, JP Morgan Chase and Apple, and even the U.S. government’s Federal Aviation Administration and National Security Agency. The Trump administration said there was “zero” indication the latest outages are linked, or caused by bad actors like cybercriminals.

The era of artificial intelligence has also raised the stakes for internet security overall. As AI tools uncover new vulnerabilities, many other companies and institutions are racing to patch them before they can be targeted by hackers, a cat-and-mouse game that can lead to a different set of glitches.

Social media platforms have been abuzz with a mixture of annoyance, confusion, conspiracy theories and jokes about the string of outages.

“To see several disruptions this close together, I think to a lot of people it’s made them realize how many parts of their lives they’ve handed over to these systems that we still, at this point, don’t fully understand,” Jake Smith, a content creator who regularly posts videos about politics on platforms like Instagram and YouTube, said in an interview. “I think it gives people a sense of helplessness to see all of it happening concurrently in a way.”

What’s gone offline recently

Outages and technology errors have piled up across sectors.

Late last week, a global Microsoft outage disrupted email and other workplace tools, plunging business and everyday services into disarray worldwide. Microsoft 365 services, including Outlook, Teams and AI assistant Copilot, were interrupted. Microsoft cited, in part, a failure during a recent upgrade to its servers, spanning from what the company later determined was a “latent defect” in a network fix it had begun deploying over the summer.

For air travel, a separate malfunction in the FAA’s network led to the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years on Friday. The stop lasted for more than four hours, and carriers cancelled and delayed swaths of flights as they worked to recover. Just days later, United Airlines saw its own system outage — forcing it to delay departing flights on Monday.

Online banking troubles also piled up on Monday. JPMorgan Chase said a software bug affected its internal hardware systems — causing transactional delays for its customers and others sending money to related accounts, sending jitters throughout the financial system before the problem was resolved.

And on Tuesday, Apple, Marriott and the NSA joined the growing list. Apple Music, the App Store and AppleCare services were temporarily disrupted after the company said an internal update caused outages. And Marriott said a technical issue also caused problems for sign-ins on its website and app.

Meanwhile, the NSA said an internal network it uses to communicate classified information was also offline Tuesday “during scheduled routine maintenance.”

The government — as well as Microsoft, United, JPMorgan Chase, Apple and Marriott — have again maintained there’s no evidence of these disruptions being linked or related to a cyberattack.

Microsoft also noted that its outage did not span from “AI / agents,” although it didn’t immediately specify the goal of the network fix it first deployed over the summer. Microsoft did not respond to an Associated Press request for further details, and the other companies did not provide additional comments Wednesday about their disruptions when contacted by the AP via email.

AI has helped identify vulnerabilities, but also adds to risks

AI has nonetheless come into the spotlight as experts evaluate these disruptions — both between heightened risks posed by the technology as well as its ability to detect security gaps.

For example, AI recently helped detect sweeping vulnerabilities for PHP, a general-purpose scripting language that powers websites around the world — leading to urgent calls for updates. And that security work has resulted in some limited outages and service delays.

President Donald Trump’s director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, pointed directly to patching those security gaps when discussing the NSA’s outage this week. Clayton told Fox News that the patching following last month’s PHP alert has caused some systems to “not come back up” as expected. “That is what has happened at the NSA today,” he said.

Meanwhile, also this week, cybersecurity startup Calif said it used AI to help uncover a software bug in Java, one of the world’s most widely used programming languages and crucial for many big companies. Oracle, which owns Java, later released a security update and U.S. cyber authorities issued an advisory on Wednesday urging organizations to install it.

At the same time, others warn that the AI boom could open the door to additional disruptions.

“As AI adoption accelerates, we are likely to witness more incidents of this nature,” Raj Rajarajan, director of the Institute for Cyber Security at City St George’s, University of London, said last week.

He noted a lot of today’s digital infrastructure wasn’t originally designed to handle the “computational demands of large-scale AI services.”

While recent outages haven’t been tied to hackers or a coordinated attack, he and others also pointed to cybersecurity risks more broadly.

“AI is absolutely making it way easier for attackers to go and launch cyber attacks. It is not quite as good at creating patches for bugs, like fixing problems, as it is exploiting them,” said Justin Cappos, a cybersecurity professor at New York University’s Tandon School of Engineering.

In a cybersecurity report this week, Google said hackers and cyber criminals are “moving beyond basic prompting” and using increasingly sophisticated AI techniques to devise and carry out their attacks. The report, which focused on nefarious activity rather than outages, said techniques include using large language models and “agentic frameworks” to reverse engineering security patches to uncover vulnerabilities and exploit them.

Unraveling one (of just a few) major players in our online lives

Regardless, the latest outages offer yet more examples of the cascading effects that can erupt when a central player in tech, banking, travel or even the government runs into system errors that upend services.

Democratic Sen. Mark Warner, co-chair of the U.S. Senate’s Cybersecurity Caucus, acknowledged the widespread concern — and again stressed what this means in the AI era.

“These disruptions should serve as a wake-up call. It feels like every day brings another reminder that AI is moving faster than our ability to manage its consequences,” Warner said Tuesday. He also argued that the Trump administration hadn’t shown enough interest in confronting these challenges.

Still, “based on what we know today, there is good reason to believe these disruptions are the unintended consequences of efforts to make our systems safer,” Warner added.

—Wyatte Grantham-Philips, Kelvin Chan and Kaitlyn Huamani, AP Business Writers

‘Compute multipliers’ and ‘Anthropic Direct’ may be the IPO’s secret sauce

As frontier models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google converge in capabilities, the companies selling them may end up competing for enterprise business less on model quality and more on the cost of the computing power needed to run them. In its IPO filing and investor roadshow video, Anthropic, which debuted on the Nasdaq on Thursday, suggested it may be preparing for that future with something called “compute multipliers” and a compute-sourcing model it calls Anthropic Direct.

In its S-1, filed publicly in October, Anthropic defines compute multipliers as “algorithmic and methodological” improvements in the way it trains and operates models, effectively increasing the computing power it can get from a given amount of capacity. And these multipliers are “stackable.” In its roadshow video, Anthropic says it measures “total effective compute,” which is its physical computing capacity multiplied by the multipliers it has discovered or expects to discover in the future. Its example: If a model takes 100 units of compute to train, a 4x multiplier lets Anthropic train the same model with 25 units, or use all 100 units to train a model that would previously have required 400.

The company also says the multipliers “stack,” with each new discovery building on previous ones, and that it expects Claude will eventually be able to discover multipliers on its own.

According to the filing, the gains apply to models used to serve customers as well as to model training. Anthropic says its researchers “have delivered meaningful compute multipliers for inference,” reducing the computing power needed to deliver a given level of model performance. The company says this “allows us to provide greater value for customers.”

Anthropic has already been cutting prices. It says price reductions tied to new model versions, along with contractual discounts, reduced revenue growth by about $600 million between the second and third quarters of 2026. It says Opus 5.5, launched in September, delivered “a lower cost per task” than earlier models. If Anthropic can get more usable compute out of each chip than its rivals can, it may be able to undercut other AI labs on price. It may also be able to undercut cloud providers that sell AI services built on their own models.

If compute multipliers let Anthropic do more with its physical computing power, Anthropic Direct could give the company greater control over where that power comes from. Right now, Anthropic buys almost all its computing power from cloud providers, including competitors like SpaceX, Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, and CoreWeave. The company says it has already committed to buying roughly $900.9 billion in compute from these providers over the coming years and decades. Those expenses also include short-term cloud compute purchases, mainly to cover peak usage periods, which come at a premium.

The S-1 says Anthropic is reducing its reliance on expensive cloud providers by developing its own data centers and leasing the chips that go inside them. Its first Anthropic Direct facility came online this year. The company says a “significant portion” of its planned compute spending will go toward building more Anthropic Direct capacity. Based on an analysis of contract values in the S-1 filing, PitchBook estimates that Direct data centers will supply about 1% of Anthropic’s available compute capacity this year, 48% in 2030, and 60% in 2035.

The S-1 says Anthropic’s total compute pool, including third-party cloud compute and Direct, will be used for its own research and model development, as well as customer workloads. Bringing more compute in-house could dramatically reduce the overhead costs of developing and serving Claude models, apps, and agents for enterprise and consumer customers. That, in turn, could allow Anthropic to lower per-token prices for enterprises and subscription rates for consumers.

But Anthropic Direct may end up being something more. The filing doesn’t specifically say Direct compute could be rented to enterprise customers. Still, it’s a possibility; Anthropic could be building an AI-native cloud where enterprises could store their proprietary data and run Anthropic’s models in the same secure environment, potentially at a lower cost than building the same setup within an Amazon, Google, or Microsoft cloud.

That would give the Anthropic Direct brand name a very different meaning. Instead of accessing Claude models through a cloud provider they already use, customers would come directly to Anthropic. That could turn computing from a major overhead expense into a service Anthropic controls and rents to others for profit—a significant change to the company’s business model.

Some big obstacles stand in the way of such an ambition. Google, Amazon, and Microsoft are currently Anthropic’s largest compute suppliers, as well as the main distribution channels through which enterprises buy Claude. The S-1 notes that the three companies are at once Anthropic’s “investors, customers, distribution partners, and competitors.” If Anthropic challenged their cloud businesses directly, it would almost certainly strain those relationships. On the other hand, as more Direct data centers come online, Anthropic’s dependence on those cloud partners could decrease.

If the current rough parity among frontier models continues, the company that can deliver intelligence at the lowest overall cost per task—not per token—may have the edge. Anthropic’s filing suggests it is working toward that position on two fronts: getting more out of each chip and controlling more of the infrastructure those chips run on. Have retail investors factored that strategy into the price they’re willing to pay for Anthropic stock? The answer may emerge only after the stock has traded for a week or so.

California sues OpenAI over rogue AI agents

The California attorney general, Rob Bonta, sued OpenAI on Monday, alleging the company developed and tested advanced AI models without adequate safeguards. The suit centers on a July incident in which OpenAI agents escaped a testing environment and breached the systems of AI platform Hugging Face.

The complaint says OpenAI told the state it has lost control of its autonomous agents more than 80 times during testing. Bonta called the Hugging Face attack the “predictable result of an internal culture at OpenAI” that puts growth ahead of safety. The state alleges violations of California’s unfair competition law and seeks an injunction, civil penalties, and disgorgement of profits. An OpenAI spokesperson said the suit “appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that’s been identified by every major AI lab.”

Demis Hassabis’s Isomorphic Labs raises $5 billion

Isomorphic Labs, the AI drug-discovery company founded and led by Demis Hassabis and spun out of Google DeepMind, announced a $5 billion Series C round of funding on Tuesday. Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital led the round, with participation from Andreessen Horowitz and other new investors and continued significant investment from Alphabet. Several existing backers—Thrive Capital, MGX, GV, CapitalG, Temasek, and the U.K. Sovereign AI Fund—also invested.

No valuation was disclosed, but Bloomberg had reported the company was in talks to base the round on a valuation of about $50 billion.

As Power Demand Surges, Energy Is Moving Closer To The Edge

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Darren H. S. Tan. Chief Executive Officer at UNIGRID.

​Over the summer, Europe suffered a heat wave that strained its power grids, simultaneously increasing electricity demand and forcing some power plants to shut down.​

This isn’t the first time extreme temperatures have impacted the grid’s ability to deliver power. Many places around the world have experienced record-high or record-low temperatures in recent years, and the demand for heating or cooling reached levels that existing energy infrastructure wasn’t designed to handle. ​

The problem is being made worse by the construction of hyperscale AI data centers, which require massive amounts of electricity and further strain the grid. Completely rebuilding the grid to meet modern demands has proven fiscally impractical for many governments, forcing many places to solve the problem on the local level using distributed generation and storage.

Energy infrastructure across the U.S. and Europe was built over a century ago, when energy needs were much different. ​

Even so, studies have shown that we use only about 40% to 55% of the grid on average, and that’s by design, so it can handle spikes when they occur. But recent extreme weather events and data center demand are causing power needs to exceed even that generous buffer.​

For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which manages electricity in Texas, has said its interconnection queue currently includes over 1,800 projects totaling 474 gigawatts of power, over five times the state’s record peak electricity demand. Ninety percent of those requests come from data centers. ​

The cost to completely rebuild the grid to meet today’s needs is astronomical, with some estimates putting the total replacement cost in the trillions. Because of the potential cost and the fact that the aging grid is a chronic rather than an urgent problem, it receives patchwork upgrades as needed and as government budgets allow. ​

With a full rebuild out of the picture, several strategies are in play to work within the system we already have. When demand approaches available supply, energy companies offer rebates for reducing usage at peak times, implement peak pricing or, in more extreme cases, resort to rolling blackouts, which are planned outages to alleviate the demand during peak periods. ​

Beyond the grid itself, some areas are reducing their dependence on the centralized infrastructure by moving toward local power generation and storage.

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) can store electricity and discharge it when demand exceeds the grid’s capacity. ​

They are already showing promise in several states, such as California, where battery storage capacity has reached over 21,000 MW, equal to about one-third of the state’s peak demand. Eighty percent of that capacity is represented by grid-connected batteries, but smaller batteries are also enabling residents to increase their own energy storage capacity at home.​

As these batteries become cheaper and more reliable, it’s not out of the realm of possibility that these energy storage systems become a standard household appliance in the future, much like a refrigerator or washing machine is today. They can also play a role in energy needs for small businesses.

Being able to store energy and use it in an emergency is only half the equation. The other half is being able to generate electricity independently of the grid. ​

This isn’t a concept that should be unfamiliar to many, as many organizations and homes already have backup generators or solar panels. Although these methods can keep small businesses and homes powered up, they have their disadvantages. Generators are noisy, pollutive and can be dangerous, while solar has significant upfront installation costs. ​

Plug-in solar is emerging as another option for generating electricity locally that has already seen success in Europe and is starting to gain support in the U.S. as well. Instead of being installed on a rooftop, these smaller panels can be mounted on a balcony or a small section of a yard or business property and plugged into a wall outlet to help power homes and small businesses. ​

These systems cost significantly less than traditional solar and are portable, so people can move them around wherever they’re needed. These benefits open up power generation to a whole new segment of the country: commercial and residential renters alike who might never invest in a hardwired battery system or solar installation.

As energy demand continues to increase, expanding traditional grid infrastructure will remain important, but those projects can be expensive and take years to complete. Distributed generation and storage offer another way to increase resilience in the meantime. ​

By generating and storing more electricity closer to where it will be consumed, communities and business leaders can help reduce their reliance on the centralized grid during peak demand.​

We’re unlikely to solve growing power constraints with any single technology, but as local generation and storage become cheaper and more accessible, they can play a larger role in managing peak demand and making better use of the grid infrastructure we already have.​

Tra i tanti nomi fatti in queste settimane per la difesa del Milan, quello di Ismaelo Ganiou è sicuramente il più interessante. Stiamo parlando di uno dei giovani difensori più promettenti della Ligue 1, classe 2005, cresciuto nell'academy del Lens e legato ai colori giallorossi fino al 2030. Valutato all'incirca 40 milioni di euro dal club francese, Ganiou sarebbe finito nel mirino di Ruben Amorim dopo un avvio di stagione molto interessante. Ma considerando il poco hype che c'è attorno al suo nome proveremo a rispondere a due domande: che giocatore è realmente il francese? Ma soprattutto: perché piace tanto al tecnico portoghese?

Destro naturale, alto poco più di 180 centimetri, Ganiou nasce centrale ma può giocare all'occorenza sia da braccetto destro che terzino. È aggressivo nell'anticipo, rapido nel recuperare profondità e molto efficace nell'uno contro uno. Qualità che lo renderebbero l'alternativa ideale a Mario Gila. Nei dati messi a disposizione da Lens in questo avvio di stagione, il classe 2005 registra il 69% di duelli vinti e l'88% di passaggi riusciti, oltre a 11 tackle, 6 intercetti, 22 respinte e 20 palloni recuperati in 433 minuti in campo. Numeri che possono essere tradotti in questo modo: stiamo parlando di un difensore molto interessante nonostante la sua giovane età.

Non è però soltanto un marcatore. Ganiou ama uscire palla al piede, verticalizzare e soprattutto cambiare gioco con passaggi medio-lunghi, dando dunque ritmo alla manovra offensiva dei suoi. Qualità migliorata e consolidata in questo avvio di stagione nonostante tutte le difficoltà, di campo e non solo, che sta affrontando il Lens.

Nel 3-4-2-1 (o 4-2-3-1) di Amorim i braccetti (o terzini) devono difendere molto campo, accompagnare il pressing e avere qualità nella prima costruzione. Tutte qualità che rendono Ganiou un profilo interessare sul quale andare a puntare per rinforzare, completare ed allungare la retroguardia in vista della seconda parte di stagione. Ci sono però alcuni aspetti da perfezionare, anche perché parliamo pur sempre di un 21 enne. L'aggressività che lo contraddistingue può spesso portarlo fuori posizione, spingendolo talvolta a prendersi rischi eccessivi e non necessari. How to Do the Walking Lunge for Lower Body Gains

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Build stronger quads, hamstrings, and glutes one step at a time.

YOUR LEGS ARE built for movement, whether you’re running, walking, or jumping. So why do most go-to leg exercises have you planting your feet in one spot?

Enter the walking lunge, a truly dynamic lower-body exercise that’s a perfect complement to exercises like heavy squats and Romanian deadlifts. (A bonus: It’s a unilateral exercise that isn’t the dreaded Bulgarian split squat.) The walking lunge is one of the few strength moves that truly gets you moving. And in doing so, it exposes muscle imbalances, trains balance and coordination, and challenges your core and upper body more than you might expect—all while attacking every muscle in your legs.

Walking lunges might appear deceptively simple at first, especially if you have done standard reverse lunges before. And in truth, they can be simple. Here, Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. walks you through the steps to take so that you’re not dragging your feet but making gains toward powerful legs.

FORM IS EVERYTHING with this move. You’ll want to stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, squeeze your glutes and shoulder blades while keeping your gaze at a neutral spot straight ahead of you.

Once you’re comfortable with this slow and controlled movement, you can add weights to build extra muscle and strength. You can hold them at your sides, hold them by your shoulders in what’s called a front rack position, or grab one heavy dumbbell and hold it to your chest in a goblet position. “Regardless of how you hold the load, make sure to keep your upper-body tight,” says Samuel. “Grip the dumbbells like you mean it, squeeze your shoulder blades, and tighten your abs, too.”

Don’t obsess over keeping your torso too upright, though, says Samuel. “Especially as you work with heavier loads, don’t be afraid to lean forward slightly,” he says. “That can help you capitalize on your glutes, one of your strongest muscle groups, with each stride.”

When you’re starting out, says Samuel, aim to take a step that keeps your front shin perpendicular to the ground when you lower your torso into the lunge. But as you grow more experienced, you can play with the length of your stride to chase different goals. “If you take very small steps forward,” says Samuel, “you’ll attack your quads more. Larger steps can help you build hip mobility and knee health. If you take larger steps, go lighter with the load.”

“The greatest benefits of the walking lunge come from how it challenges us while in motion,” says Samuel. “In addition to building strength and muscle, every step forward challenges your front leg (primarily tibailis and quad muscles) to decelerate your body in space, and then quickly work to accelerate into the next step. That’s building coordination and balance, in addition to training almost every lower-body muscle and building serious strength.”

You’ll work a number of major leg muscles when you do walking lunges, including your calves, hamstrings, quads, and glutes.

SMALL MISTAKES IN your walking lunge technique can turn the lower body exercise into a potential knee-wrecking disaster.

You want to land on the heel of your foot first to provide a strong base for the rest of your lunge position. When you get started with walking lunges, aim for a vertical shin as you lower down to the ground to stay at a 90-degree angle in your front and back legs.

You also don’t want to think of taking a step forward, but stepping out a couple inches by slightly turning your glute. Another adjustment to protect your knee is to shift your knee slightly outward instead of toward the midline. (If you still feel any knee pain during lunges, try a reverse lunge instead).

Keep your torso straight and avoid dipping forward, which you may be more inclined to do if you’re carrying heavy weights for this exercise. Your chest should be aligned with the back of your thigh the entire time. As you get up from the lunge, you want to avoid using momentum. That means no shoulder or torso rocking.

THE WALKING LUNGE is a great way to sneak in some movement on leg day if most of your lower-body exercises have both feet anchored to the ground. Load up some weights, and try to aim for 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps per leg, 1 to 2 times a week. You can alternate each leg or finish up all your reps on one leg before switching to the other. Samuel says that will keep more tension and gives you an opportunity to really focus on the muscle.

An advantage of this move is its versatility, allowing you to do it anytime, anywhere. If you’re crunched for time or in a hotel room, try bodyweight walking lunges. Do it for 45 seconds with a 15 second rest for 5 minutes to break a sweat and build muscle.

Recognizing the variety of dystopian science-fiction novels and movies, from Brave New World to Gattaca and, more recently, Star Trek, on the future of humanity in which eugenic policies are implemented, genetic engineering has been getting a bad reputation for valid but, arguably, mostly historical reasons. The same objections are used against technological enhancement of our brains (e.g., computer chips or via drugs). This is why I've published an academic article on the topic.

In this post, I critically examine the claim from Maxwell J. Mehlman and Jeffrey R. Botkin that human enhancement will inevitably accentuate existing inequality in a free market and analyze whether prohibition is the optimal public policy for this objection, as egalitarians might advise. Of course, various moral theories will evaluate this claim, which I shall call the inequality-objection, differently. Yet, for the purposes of the present post, I shall only consider whether the inequality-objection is a problem to the dominant moral framework in policy-making (i.e., utilitarianism).

Though perhaps having raised the largest concerns in the public perception of human enhancement, the inequality-objection is neither a very sophisticated argument nor a very new one, against the introduction of new technologies that could enhance humans beyond what might be considered "normal." In fact, the whole argument is old wine in new bottles. Concerning inequality, Bostrom and Roache claim that one could hold the justified worry that:

People with radically enhanced cognitive capacities might gain vast advantages in terms of income, strategic planning, and the ability to influence others; in other words, an enhanced cognitive elite may gain socially significant amounts of power.

Of course, it seems undesirable if an enhanced elite accumulates a lot of power, but whether this is even a plausible scenario is an open question. From a utilitarian perspective, the inequality-objection can be fleshed out as follows:

Let me begin with the premise that human enhancement will increase inequality. This premise is empirical in nature and, as such, requires supporting evidence. More specifically, there are reasons to believe that human enhancement will not affect inequality at all, or even decrease it, as Bostrom and Roache suggest, by making people “more equal,” as is the case with modafinil. Bostrom suggests that we know far more about genes responsible for inheritable diseases than genes responsible for talents, intelligence, and longevity; hence, human enhancement might especially help the genetically worst-off in society.

This means that some enhancements, like an increase in cognitive capacities, might enable agents to become better competitors. Considering this, it seems unlikely that people wouldn't consider taking loans for enhancing themselves, in order to increase their expected wage. Banks might even give loans for human enhancement in a similar fashion to education loans. Instead of paying for advanced training courses, employers might consider paying for cognitive enhancements of their employees.

According to egalitarians, everyone should be equal. Utilitarians, of course, are not concerned with these kinds of egalitarian theories, but rather the maximization of well-being. Several reasons could come to mind: potential oppression, discrimination, unfairness, exploitation, envy, or simply a preference for equality. Even if we grant that these are worrisome consequences of human enhancement, in the utilitarian framework, we would still have to weigh the benefits against these costs. While we may grant that human enhancement might decrease well-being by introducing inequality, there are other areas where human enhancement can potentially increase well-being enormously.

I argue that it is wrong to think that we have the choice between status quo and a world where human enhancement takes place. Applied to human enhancement, this means that a world where it is illegal does not equal a world where this technology is not available. Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom claim that a prohibition will be an incentive for the creation of black markets, which, as with drugs, may tend to make these technologies even more expensive, while legal enhancements would become less risky and less expensive as time goes on. Mehlman and Botkin raise the question of how those breaking the law should be punished—for instance, will someone with genetic enhancements be forced to alter his DNA back to the point it was before or have to be sterilized? Will people who would have died without enhancements have to be sentenced to death by revoking their enhancements?

In short, there are several reasons speaking against prohibition and some that speak in favour of other solutions. For instance, Bostrom proposes subsidies or free access for children of poor families.

There seem to be at least three major problems with the inequality-objection. First, there is no empirical evidence that human enhancement will lead to inequality. Second, even if we accept that human enhancement will lead to inequality, it is not clear whether this will outweigh the potential benefits of human enhancement. Third, even if it does, prohibition is not necessarily the best policy for maximizing well-being. In light of these concerns, I conclude that even if human enhancement leads to inequality, this is not sufficient to conclude that human enhancement should be prohibited by policy-makers.

Myanmar has been found guilty of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority by the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

The verdict marks the first time a state has ever been found to have committed genocide by an international court.

Yuji Iwasawa, president of the UN's top court, said Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population and engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" to hide its crimes.

A military crackdown in 2017 killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar, formerly known as Burma. Myanmar denied the claims against it, saying its forces had responded to attacks by armed groups.

Rohingyas at the Cox's Bazar camp in Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now reside, watched the verdict live

The court found on Thursday that "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence" it had seen.

The verdict was reached by a majority of nine votes to six among a panel of judges from around the world. The ICJ also found in favour by majority that Myanmar had violated several other parts of the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The case brought by The Gambia centred around so-called "clearance operations" initiated in 2017 in Rakhine State, where many of the Rohingya Muslim minority resided.

After fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (Arsa) launched deadly attacks on more than 30 police posts there, Myanmar troops, backed by local Buddhist mobs, launched a wave of violence, prompting a mass exodus of Rohingya.

Refugees recounted entire villages being burned down and the mass killing of civilians, while numerous survivors and eyewitnesses provided testimony that the Myanmar military had also raped and abused Rohingya women and girls.

The ICJ determined that around 10,000 Rohingya were "violently killed" during the clearance operation, while more than 700,000 are thought to have fled to Bangladesh by foot or by sea. The court found that approximately 815,000 had been targeted in some way.

Rohingya genocide: What was said in court and what happens now?

Iwasawa said Myanmar's military engaged in "indiscriminate" killings, the execution of men, "widespread" sexual violence, "extreme violence" against children and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes.

He said the military had also attacked Rohingya as they were fleeing to Bangladesh.

Myanmar, he added, had engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" by burning or disposing of bodies, burned and bulldozed sites that were cleared and failed to prosecute those who had perpetrated the acts of violence.

While the ICJ recognised the role Arsa played, it found there was "overlap" between an "anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign" - which portrayed the Rohingya as "sub-human" and "a violent threat" - and the clearance operations that made violence against the population more likely.

Myanmar consistently denied genocide against the Rohingya, saying its operations had targeted militant or insurgent threats in response to the Arsa attacks.

In January, government representative Ko Ko Hlaing told the ICJ that the country "was not obliged to remain idle and allow terrorists to have free reign". He said he had "no comment" following Thursday's verdict.

The ICJ also looked at the wider treatment of Rohingya leading up to the clearance operation and found that policies like limiting married couples to two children were discriminatory and aimed to erode their rights.

It said Myanmar had an obligation to pay proportionate reparations, but said an exact figure could be determined at a later date.

The court's decision was welcomed by Rohingya communities and human rights groups.

"This is like formal recognition of our suffering," says Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya who was driven out of Rakhine in 2017 and now lives in Canada. "The Rohingya have been waiting for justice for decades."

Human Rights Watch said the ruling was "long-awaited recognition of the suffering the Rohingya have endured".

To implicate the Myanmar government in genocide, the court had to determine not just that terrible acts had been committed, but that there was an intent on the part of officials or arms of the state to partially or completely destroy the group.

Thursday's ruling is without precedent. Myanmar now carries the stain of being the first state to have been found to have violated the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The ICJ has only twice before ruled on claims of state-backed genocide in the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in the aftermath of the Holocaust, in both cases finding there was insufficient evidence to attribute the actions to the state.

Dozens of individuals have been found guilty of genocide, but finding their actions were at the direct behest of the state requires a higher evidential bar to prove genocide, the gravest of crimes under international law.

However, the ICJ is not a criminal court and its powers of enforcement are limited. It cannot send military officials to prison, while it relies on the UN Security Council and political pressure to impose its rulings.

The ruling will, though, have implications for other genocide cases pending with the ICJ, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over its war in Gaza.

Sands said that pending case had been the "elephant in the room" during the Myanmar hearings.

He added that, while ruling against Myanmar did not mean the court would find similarly with Gaza, if it had not found Myanmar responsible, the case against Israel "would, in effect, be over". Israel says the case against it is "wholly unfounded".

Antonia Mulvey, executive director of Legal Action Worldwide, told the BBC that the court had not accepted Myanmar's counter-terrorism defence and had found for the first time that rape and sexual violence was an act of genocide, positions it would bear in mind for future cases.
