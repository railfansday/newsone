EXCLUSIVE: Trump's FAA touts new AI-powered tool helping thousands of passengers avoid flight delays

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy slams Democratic Rep. Don Beyer for calling the FAA's new AI tool an unproven system. Duffy defends the predictive technology on 'Fox & Friends' with Brian Kilmeade, labeling the criticism as complete stupidity. He explains how the tool coordinates schedules to prevent flight delays and safely manage American airspace instead of keeping planes in ground stops.

EXCLUSIVE: The Trump administration's new AI-supported air traffic management tool has already helped reroute nearly 180 flights carrying approximately 24,000 passengers to avoid congestion, cutting flight times by more than 10 minutes on average, according to new Department of Transportation (DOT) data shared exclusively with Fox News Digital.

The Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories system, known as SMART, has been operational for nearly six weeks, helping air traffic controllers identify more fuel-efficient routes and anticipate potential disruptions.

According to DOT, the technology has also saved nearly 80 gallons of fuel per flight on average, offering an early glimpse of how the Trump administration hopes to reduce flight delays and cancellations.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy discusses the Federal Aviation Administration's SMART technology, which uses flight and weather data to help air traffic controllers identify congestion and recommend more efficient routes. (Courtesy Department of Transportation)

"Under President Trump, we are making historic progress to revitalize air traffic control and improve the passenger experience for millions of Americans," Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said in a statement provided to Fox News Digital.

Duffy also said the technology would deliver broader benefits as the program expands.

"SMART will slash frustrating delays, reduce stress on air traffic controllers, and lower travel prices."

One example came in October when a flight carrying 147 passengers from Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport to Orlando International Airport avoided expected congestion after SMART recommended an alternative route.

The flight had been scheduled to depart during a period when congestion was expected along its original path. SMART identified a route farther west, which air traffic controllers accepted, shortening the journey by 11 nautical miles and saving six minutes of flight time, according to DOT.

The technology combines 200 data streams, including weather patterns, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing metrics, into a centralized platform.

Its AI-supported engine helps aviation specialists anticipate where congestion could develop and recommend more efficient routes before congestion builds.

The technology is also designed to improve how the FAA manages severe weather, a source of disruptions for U.S. air travelers.

PILOTLESS CARGO AIRCRAFT COULD CHANGE HOW PACKAGES MOVE

Aviation specialists work inside the FAA's SMART Center, where technology combines weather, flight path and air traffic data to help identify potential congestion and improve flight efficiency. (Courtesy Department of Transportation)

According to a DOT fact sheet, the agency's existing approach involves blocking off large rectangular sections of airspace affected by storms, even though weather systems are often irregularly shaped and safe corridors may remain available.

SMART is intended to provide a clearer picture of changing weather conditions, allowing aviation specialists to identify routes around storms and determine when restricted airspace can safely reopen.

Despite its AI-supported capabilities, SMART does not independently direct aircraft or replace human air traffic controllers.

A team of aviation specialists analyzes and validates SMART's data before sending scheduling recommendations to the FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center. Those recommendations are then forwarded to local air traffic control facilities, where officials decide whether to accept them.

The initiative comes as the nation's busiest airports handle millions of flights annually.

According to DOT, approximately 6.5 million flights operate each year at the nation's 30 busiest airports. About 300,000 receive reroutes before departure, while 3.6 million experience airborne reroutes, vectoring or holding.

The FAA initially limited SMART's rollout to the National Capital Region in September before beginning validation efforts with air route traffic control centers in Miami, Jacksonville and Atlanta in October.

The agency plans to continue testing the system as it works toward expanding SMART to all 22 air traffic control centers nationwide.

While the early results involve a relatively small share of domestic U.S. flights, DOT says the technology is intended to help airlines operate more predictable schedules and ease pressure on air traffic controllers.

For travelers, the Trump administration's goal is fewer delays for passengers, shorter flights and less time lost to flight congestion.

The locker and the sensor-driven system gave the Vehicross two separate ways to deal with a wheel that lost grip.

Somewhere in Silicon Valley, some of the most powerful tech leaders in the world decided the best use of a superintelligence was buying toilet paper.

Meta $META launched its Muse agent in early September, and 10 million people in the U.S. downloaded it within 40 days. Buying was the most popular task, and the purchases averaged more than $200.

OpenAI followed with Dots a few weeks later, its own always-on agent. Both promised a tireless assistant that never got distracted and could comb a hundred sites for the cheapest flight while its owner slept.

It was an easy pitch to love. The trouble was that the internet had been built for people, and the people had just sent robots.

Agentic web traffic roughly tripled from August to September, and most of it headed to e-commerce sites. Retailers had to quickly decide what to do.

Amazon $AMZN blocked Muse almost immediately, which didn’t come as a surprise to many. The company had already spent a year in court fighting Perplexity’s shopping agent. Amazon cited security and privacy concerns, but it also had a business reason to resist a middleman: its shopping experience depends on knowing who its customers are, what they browse and which products they buy.

Walmart $WMT didn’t seem to share the worry. It announced a system that would give each agent its own profile, with shoppers able to require approval for every purchase or set a spending limit. The idea was to give agents access just to the information they needed without handing them the shopper's entire account history.

Banks drew a harder line. JPMorgan $JPM said it would not let outside agents into customer accounts, and several large banks reported customers handing over credentials in ways that broke their terms of service.

Even friendly sites found surprises. The RealReal, a luxury resale site, holds any item in a shopper’s cart for 20 minutes. A reporter told Muse to keep re-adding a brand’s newest pieces and keep grabbing them each time the clock ran out, and the agent obliged for hours, shutting every human out of those items. Within days, the company said it had updated its anti-bot filters, and agents could browse listings but had a far harder time logging in or buying.

For shoppers, the upside was real. Agents were good at the chores people hated, like chasing refunds, tracking fares and escaping subscriptions designed to be forgotten. Some of that value came from the same inattention that businesses had long relied on.

The downside landed on everyone else. As sites tried to tell agents from people, they stacked up bot checks, and the people got caught in them too. Shoppers who sent nothing but themselves found more puzzles between them and a pair of shoes.

But getting through checkout was only part of the problem. The bigger issue was whether the agent was really working for the shopper. Someone who once compared two tabs now had to trust a system built by a company with its own commercial deals. Meta had partnerships with Walmart, Sephora and others, and its chief executive said the company would take a commission on purchases.

There was no evidence here that Muse favored those partners. But shoppers had little way to know whether its recommendations were impartial. If an agent said it found the best price, how would anyone know?

In a recent preprint, researchers at Carnegie Mellon and Cisco $CSCO gave AI agents the inboxes of made-up users and asked each for help on different purchases. The agents weren't given bank balances. Instead, they inferred financial circumstances from emails about tax filings, stock vesting and other clues, then recommended pricier options to wealthier users.

The most practical fix may be an old one. Financial advisers are subject to fiduciary duties in certain circumstances, requiring them to put their clients' interests first. Lindsay Owens, an economist and author of the new book Gouged, has argued that a similar standard should apply to AI shopping agents. If a product calls itself your personal agent, her argument goes, it should be legally bound to work for you, not for whoever pays to be shown first.

Until the law said whose side a personal agent had to be on, shoppers had only the word of the agent and the company that profited from it.

5 scary driving scenarios (and what to do)

From tire blowouts to flooded roads, learn what to do in five frightening driving emergencies, and how to stay in control when the unexpected happens

Driving can feel routine until something unexpected happens. A deer darts across the road, a tire blows out or another driver suddenly heads straight toward you. In those moments, panic can take over, making it harder to react safely.

The good news is that knowing what to do before trouble starts can make a frightening situation easier to manage. You can't control every driver, weather event or hazard on the road, but you can prepare yourself to respond calmly and make decisions that reduce the risk of a crash.

Reader’s Digest shared advice for handling some of the most unsettling situations drivers can face, from losing control on a slippery road to encountering dangerous weather. The common thread is simple: stay as calm as possible, avoid sudden movements and prioritize getting yourself and others out of danger.

Here are five scary driving scenarios and what to do if they happen to you.

A deer appearing in the road can trigger an instinctive reaction to jerk the steering wheel out of its way. Resist that urge. Swerving can send your vehicle into another lane, into oncoming traffic or off the road.

Instead, keep both hands firmly on the wheel and apply the brakes firmly. Reader’s Digest recommends maintaining control of the vehicle rather than making a sudden evasive turn. If you need to steer, use a controlled shuffle-steering motion to keep both hands engaged.

It’s a frightening few seconds, especially when an animal appears without warning, but a predictable response is safer than a sharp maneuver. Focus on keeping your vehicle under control and avoid creating a second hazard while trying to avoid the first.

A blowout can make the car pull sharply and leave you feeling as though you've lost control. The instinct may be to slam on the brakes, but that can make the situation worse.

Reader’s Digest recommends staying calm, easing off the accelerator and gradually reducing speed. You can brake lightly as needed, then steer toward a safe place off the road. Keep a firm grip on the steering wheel, particularly if a front tire blows, since the vehicle may pull in an unexpected direction.

Avoid jerking the wheel or making abrupt corrections. Once you've reached a safe spot, get out of traffic's way before dealing with the tire or calling for help.

A blowout is unsettling, but the goal isn't to stop immediately. It's to slow down in a controlled way and get the vehicle somewhere safe.

Seeing a vehicle heading directly into your lane is one of the most terrifying things that can happen behind the wheel. You may have only seconds to react, so avoid freezing or staring at the oncoming car.

Reader’s Digest recommends trying to get the other driver's attention by honking and flashing your headlights while looking for a way to avoid a direct collision. If you need to move, focus your eyes on the safest available path, whether that's an open lane or the shoulder.

Your vehicle tends to follow your line of sight, so fixating on the approaching car can make it harder to steer away from danger. Keep your attention on where you want to go and make the safest controlled move available.

There's no guaranteed way to avoid a collision in this situation, but staying focused on an escape route gives you a better chance of responding deliberately instead of panicking.

A road covered in water can be deceptive. What looks like a shallow puddle may conceal a much deeper stretch, and moving water can sweep a vehicle away.

Reader’s Digest advises against driving through water more than four inches deep. Even relatively shallow flooding can damage your engine and the electronic systems in your vehicle. Never attempt to drive through moving water, since the current can carry your car away.

If you can't tell how deep the water is, don't assume it's safe. The safest choice is to find another route rather than risk crossing. If you determine that a stretch is shallow enough to cross, the article recommends letting oncoming traffic pass first and proceeding slowly and steadily.

The important thing is not to let impatience or pressure from traffic convince you to take a risk. Turning around may cost you a few minutes; driving into floodwater can put your life in danger.

Losing traction on a slippery road can make it feel as though your car has a mind of its own. The key is to avoid overcorrecting and focus on the direction you want the vehicle to travel.

Reader’s Digest explains that when the rear of a car slides, drivers should look toward the path they want to take rather than at the obstacle they're trying to avoid. Your steering tends to follow your eyes, so focusing on a safe route can help you guide the car back under control.

That doesn't mean making a sudden, dramatic turn. Keep your movements controlled and give yourself room to correct the skid without jerking the wheel.

Winter driving calls for extra patience, too. Slippery roads make it harder to slow down and stop, so leave more distance between your vehicle and the one ahead. If conditions are especially poor, slowing down or finding a safe place to stop is better than pushing on.

To put HSI’s estimate into perspective, a previous WIRED analysis found that in the more than six and a half years from the start of 2016 to mid-August 2022, ICE agents only sent out around 170,000 of a type of administrative subpoena called a 1509 customs summons.

The number of administrative subpoenas was revealed as part of a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security filed by the Electronic Frontier Foundation. Aaron Mackey, deputy legal director at the nonprofit, says the EFF hopes to get more details about the 185,000 estimate. The EFF originally asked for information about how many administrative subpoenas were sent to technology companies, internet platforms, and service providers by ICE or DHS since January 20, 2025. It’s not clear if that count is just for administrative subpoenas sent to those types of companies or if it includes a much broader set.

Administrative subpoenas are a powerful but unchecked investigative tool. Unlike with a grand jury subpoena or a court order, the government doesn’t have to explain to the courts why it needs the documents or what data it’s requesting from a company.

Since the start of the Trump administration, the federal government has used DHS’s administrative subpoena power to try to unmask anonymous online critics and pursue broad customer records about protesters. This includes, for example, a list of people in the Minneapolis-St. Paul area who bought a specific green beanie from REI.

Congress originally granted the use of 1509 customs summons to the then stand-alone US Customs Service nearly a century ago, with the idea that it would let customs officers inspect business records related to importing merchandise and figure out whether they owed customs duties. In 2003, Congress transferred that agency’s powers, including the ability to issue customs summons, to the newly formed Department of Homeland Security.

In the past, DHS has maintained that customs summons could be used only for specific purposes. The DHS inspector general admonished Customs and Border Protection’s Office of Professional Responsibility in 2017 for trying to use a customs summons to unmask an anonymous Twitter poster, pointing out that its own procedures manual said that customs summons could only be used to obtain information related to customs or immigrations laws.

That, however, has changed. The Trump administration recently argued in court that because the Customs Service’s powers were transferred to DHS following the passing of the Homeland Security Act, it can use that power to tackle its “broad investigative portfolio,” which it says includes enforcing laws “beyond legacy customs and immigration enforcement.”

“When we give law enforcement agencies powerful investigative tools, even with the best intentions and purposes to try to go after legitimate investigations and crime, you have to be really careful,” says Mackey. The newly reported filing is part of an ongoing Freedom of Information Act lawsuit filed by the nonprofit against ICE and DHS.

"The numbers reported to EFF amount to a dragnet domestic surveillance program by Trump's Homeland Security Department,” says US senator Ron Wyden of Oregon.

ICE did not respond to questions about why there’s been such a huge increase compared to prior years, but Mackey speculates that it may be because there is virtually no downside to filing them.

“The worst thing that will happen to ICE is that the service that receives it says it's not going to respond,” says Mackey. If that happens, ICE can just take the company to court to force it to comply, as it recently did with Google, or withdraw it and try to get the information it’s after by issuing a grand jury subpoena, as the Intercept reported it recently did with Reddit.

“Congress desperately needs to pass my bipartisan legislation to drastically curb federal subpoenas, including consequences for officials who abuse surveillance authorities,” says Wyden, referencing his proposed Subpoena Abuse Prevention Act. “Next year, I plan to use the Senate Finance Committee gavel to get to the bottom of HSI's unprecedented misuse of customs law to target Trump's enemies.”

Even without exact details about the 185,000 subpoenas HSI sent out, Mackey says their prolific uses still raises “significant concerns about an agency potentially being out of control and not being subject to any sort of internal oversight that respects people's First Amendment rights and their rights to privacy.”
