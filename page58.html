Children’s Place files for bankruptcy protection, seeks buyer

📅 2026-11-09  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Children's clothing retailer The Children's Place has filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy protection, it said Sunday.

The company is looking to sell substantially all of its assets and is seeking offers from potential buyers by Dec. 9, with a goal to close a deal by the end of December.

The retailer, whose brands include The Children's Place and Gymboree, says it operates two digital storefronts and 514 North American stores, and has distribution in 13 countries through franchise and wholesale partners. The company's performance has taken a hit in recent years from tariff hikes and slower consumer spending, among other factors.

Its stores and e-commerce sites remain open for now, and the company is planning to continue its ongoing inventory sales with court approval.

"For nearly 60 years, The Children's Place has supported growing families with apparel that customers have come to know and love. We recognize the significance of this development for our employees, customers, vendors, and other partners, and we are committed to conducting an efficient and transparent sale process," said Rudy Morando, co-chief restructuring officer of The Children's Place. "In the meantime, we remain focused on serving the families who have trusted The Children's Place for generations."

This move comes days after interim Children's Place CEO Muhammad Asif Seemab and Chief Strategy Officer Muhammad Umair stepped down from their roles. Seemab and Umair will both remain on the company's board. CFO John Szczepanski took on the role of acting president and principal executive officer.

Seemab is also managing director of Saudi-based Mithaq Capital, which took a majority stake in The Children's Place in 2024 when the company was facing financial trouble.

Even with support from the investors, the company has not been able to significantly improve its financial outlook. In its bankruptcy filing, the company reported about $742 million in assets, compared to about $881 million in debts.

In the second fiscal quarter of 2026, its most recently reported quarter, the company's net sales dropped nearly 19% from the same period the year prior. At the time, Seemab said in a news release that during the quarter the company was "stabilizing our customer file" and "driving traffic to our stores and websites," and saw "lower conversion, combined with a very clearance-heavy mix."

He also pointed to tough comparisons to the prior-year period, due in part to back-to-school shopping demand that "was uniquely elevated ... ahead of anticipated tariff price increases."

The Children's Place stock has plummeted in recent years and was trading around 18 cents per share on Monday.

Apple TV's Murderbot Is Officially Not Returning In 2026

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

While Murderbot season 1 might have been one of 2025’s most acclaimed new sci-fi shows, the satirical action thriller won’t return in 2026. Even though the early 2000s was home to the iconic space western Firefly, the Battlestar Galactica reboot, multiple Stargate shows, Futurama’s original run, and the underrated James Cameron series Dark Angel, the 2020s still mark a uniquely fecund era for the sci-fi genre on the small screen.

Netflix users can choose between Stranger Things, Black Mirror, the animated anthology series Love, Death & Robots, and the ambitious epic 3 Body Problem. Hulu offers sci-fi fans the Margaret Atwood adaptation The Handmaid’s Tale and its spinoff The Testaments, while audience members who log into Apple TV can choose between Invasion, Severance, the Isaac Asimov adaptation Foundation, the acclaimed dystopian series Silo, and the Monsterverse spinoff Monarch: Legacy of Monsters.

As if this lineup were not enough, Apple TV also introduced the superb satirical sci-fi series Murderbot in 2025. Starring Alexander Skarsgård as the titular sardonic Murderbot, this series focused on a private security robot who is equally fascinated and disgusted by the peculiarities of human society. Based on author Martha Wells’ eight-book series The Murderbot Diaries, Murderbot season 1 adapts the first of the writer’s novels, All Systems Red.

Murderbot was a huge critical hit, with a staggering Rotten Tomatoes critical score of 95% and an almost equally impressive audience score of 82%. Season 1 of the series followed Murderbot on a mission to protect scientists during a dangerous trip off Earth, with the acid-tongued robot offering constant deadpan commentary on the group’s foibles. However, viewers who hoped to see season 2 of the series arrive this year will be disappointed.

Instead, season 2 guest star Steve Buscemi told ScreenRant that Murderbot’s sophomore outing wouldn’t arrive until summer 2027. Buscemi, who is also set to appear in Hulu’s upcoming reboot of The X-Files, let the season 2 release window's timeline slip while walking to ScreenRant's Tatiana Hullender about his role in director Martin McDonagh’s blackly comic crime thriller Wild Horse Nine.

Murderbot fans can expect the show’s sophomore outing to drop sometime in the summer of 2027, which puts the show right in the middle of the standard, typical breaks between seasons of streaming series. At the far end of the scale, some of the biggest streaming shows, such as Netflix’s Stranger Things, have been known to take whopping three-year-plus breaks between seasons.

Although Netflix’s Jenna Ortega vehicle Wednesday took similarly lengthy breaks between its seasons, Murderbot’s two-year gap is a lot more common among streaming shows across the board. Disney’s ambitious fantasy adaptation Percy Jackson & the Olympians had a two-year gap between its first two seasons, although seasons 2 and 3 trimmed this down to a one-year gap by shooting back to back.

This strategy might also be part of the reason that Prime Video’s upcoming historical drama Bloodaxe was renewed for a second season before its first season premiered. Murderbot’s season 1 success could not necessarily have been guaranteed before the sci-fi comedy show debuted in 2025, but the two-year wait for season 2 may be due in part to busy cast schedules.

In contrast, Prime Video’s romance novel series adaptation Off Campus is set to release its second season less than a year after its first since the show shot both outings back to back. After the success of Murderbot season 1, Apple TV may follow this release strategy for seasons 2 and 3, but, in the meantime, the wait for season 2 will sadly extend for another six months or so.

Donnie Dunagan Dies: Voice Of Young Bambi In 1942 Disney Classic Was 92

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Donnie Dunagan, who voiced the young Bambi in Disney’s 1942 animated classic during a brief Hollywood career as a child actor, has died. He was 92.

Movie historian Tom Weaver said Dunagan died November 4 but did not provide details.

Born on August 16, 1934, in Texas, Dunagan moved with his family to Memphis, where he tap-danced his way to winning a talent contest in Memphis at age 3. He caught the eye of a talent agent, who had the boy and family come to Hollywood by train.

Dunagan’s screen debut came in the 1939 monster flick Mother Carey’s Chickens, leading to more than a half-dozen screen roles including Son of Frankenstein, The Forgotten Woman and Tower of London. As the United States was entering World War II, the youngster would land his most memorable role, playing the orphaned deer in the 1942 toon classic Bambi.

In a 2011 interview, Dunagan recalled meeting Walt Disney several times on the lot. “He was a leader; all over the place, helping, coaching, laughing, and even a kid like me then could sense that he was keenly respected by the air and production people. That was not always the case in other studios when the ‘boss’ came around. Mr. Disney was a class by himself.’

Dunagan was turning 8 as the film was released, but it would be his final screen credit. He played college football before join the U.S. Marines during the Korean War, serving in the Corps for the next quarter-century. He became the youngest drill instructor in Marines history and was promoted 13 times in 21 years, risign to the rank of major. He had multiple tours of duty in Vietnam, where he earned the Bronze Star, three Purple Hearts and other accolades. He retired from the service in 1977, never having told his fellow Marines about his Bambi role.

Apple has made it clear for a while that Siri AI is bound by daily usage limits and that it will offer expanded access via iCloud+ plans. The company hasn't shed more light on exactly how that will work, but it has revealed how Creator Studio users can continue wielding the AI features of those apps after they reach the regular limits.

It has started selling $50 intelligence usage packs for Creator Studio, and Logic Pro is the first app to support these. Apple updated Logic Pro last month to include an assistant that's powered by its own models as well as those from third parties. The company said the chatbot can help "creators and producers navigate production workflows, start a new song, set up a vocal track from a single prompt or refine an active project."

As noted by 9to5Mac, folks can view their AI usage in the app by going to Logic Pro on the menu bar, then Intelligence Features > Show Usage. The window that pops up includes a Buy More Usage button — clicking this takes you to the option to buy an intelligence usage pack.

A support page explains that users with active Apple Creator Studio subscriptions in the US, UK, Canada, Brazil, France, Germany, India and Mexico can buy intelligence usage packs to keep using AI features after they reach their monthly usage limit. A pack is tied to the Apple Account used to purchase it — there's no Family Sharing support.

"Unused additional usage purchased with intelligence usage packs roll over from month to month and expire 12 months after purchase," Apple added. "Additional usage gets consumed for relevant features only after you've reached your monthly limit in any given month."

It seems likely that Apple will expand intelligence usage packs to other Creator Studio apps, such as Final Cut Pro and Pixelmator Pro. It's interesting that Apple is opting for this approach over a regular subscription, but perhaps the company has other offerings planned for the future. Cisco will add Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to AI Defense, the company announced at its Cisco Live event in Melbourne. AI Defense attacks companies’ own AI systems to find weak spots before hackers do. Mythos will come first to select enterprise customers, with no date set.

Mythos will plan tests and come up with attack ideas against AI apps and agents, a practice known as red teaming. In July, Cisco researchers used it to push 15 chatbots past their guardrails.

Customers cannot type prompts to Mythos directly. It works only on a fixed job, against systems the customer owns and has registered for testing.

Every test is tied to a named person, recorded for audit and can be stopped at any time. Mythos cannot scan the wider internet, Cisco said in a blog post.

“Enterprises are increasingly building AI agents into their core operations and want to test those agents thoroughly before putting them into production,” said Michael Moore, Anthropic’s cybersecurity lead.

That suits Anthropic, which puts Mythos inside partners’ products rather than handing out the model. It released Mythos 5.2 on Monday, still only for vetted organisations. Tight controls matter: in April, an unauthorised group reached Mythos through a contractor’s access.

Cisco also updated its Splunk tools that track what AI agents cost and grade the quality of their work. A cost tool called Tokenomics enters private preview in December.

Geothermal company Fervo couldn’t have picked a better time to deliver its first power plants.

In its third quarter earnings report, released Monday, the company said it now has $11.9 billion worth of contracted revenue on the books, up from $7.2 billion last quarter.

Fervo has been hitting its deadlines recently, which undoubtedly has helped to drive orders.

As hyperscalers scour the earth for electricity to power their AI data centers, Fervo is on track to complete a 396 megawatt power plant for Google by the end of 2028. The geothermal company connected its first 100 megawatt power plant to the grid earlier this year.

Both projects are built at Cape Station in Utah, a resource that could generate 8.4 gigawatts of electricity. Previously, experts told Fervo the site could only produce about 4 gigawatts.

The Google project, now called Cape Google 400, will be grid connected, co-founder and CEO Tim Latimer said in an earnings call Monday. The arrangement means that Google can draw power from the deal and sell it to other customers if it’s not ready to use it.

“We build Cape Station on our schedule. Google can develop a potential data center on their schedule, and neither party is waiting on the other,” he said.

Latimer’s statement also underlines an advantage that geothermal has over competing generating sources like large nuclear or natural gas power plants — the phased nature of its construction.

Fervo newer wells are deeper and hotter, each capable of generating 15 megawatts gross power, and several are linked together into what the company calls a GeoBlock. The first completed GeoBlock generated 33 megawatts, while recent ones hit 50 megawatts. They’re sized to match the demand of a typical data center hall, so hyperscalers can bring them online as they’re completed.

As a business, Fervo is still in the early stages. It ended the quarter with an operating loss of $43.7 million, though it holds cash and cash equivalents of $1.9 billion and long-term debt of $301.6 million, which should give it plenty of runway.

Much will depend on the company bringing down the cost of its power plants. While hyperscalers are hungry for power today, they may not always be as price indiscriminate.

Yet if Fervo can deliver on its promises, it could end up winning on price. The first phase of its Cape Station project cost $7,000 per kilowatt of capacity, the company said. The cost for its Google project is $5,500 per kilowatt, a significant drop that suggests Fervo is on the right path. With subsidies, that’s about equivalent to a new natural gas power plant running on fuel that costs $6 per million BTUs. Still about twice what natural gas costs today, but trending closer to where it needs to be.

But if Fervo can hit its long-term target of $3,000 per kilowatt, it could significantly undercut natural gas power, even at today’s prices. That’s a pretty ambitious target, but the company has made significant strides in just a few years. No wonder Google is getting in early.

Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries Face Democratic Leadership Revolt After Midterms

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The latest conflict brewing in the Democratic Party’s cold war between the blue dogs and the insurgent progressive wing is the fight over its next congressional leaders. But with no alternate candidates stepping up to run, it’s hard to determine how real the threats to replace Senator Chuck Schumer and Representative Hakeem Jeffries actually are.

At least six incoming Democratic senators—including Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed, South Carolina’s Annie Andrews, Minnesota’s Peggy Flanagan, Illinois’s Juliana Stratton, Kansas’s Adam Hamilton, and Maine’s Troy Jackson—have said they won’t support Schumer for majority leader. Michigan senator Elissa Slotkin has called for a “a new generation of leadership.” Even Cory Booker, who praised Schumer for helping engineer last week’s blue wave, stopped short of saying whether he would support him. On Monday, Richard Blumenthal did the same. And Jon Stewart said that the senator “does not understand the urgency of the moment and what the future demands.” Regardless, Schumer is confident. His aides say he has enough votes to become the next majority leader.

In the House, several incoming Democrats—including Claire Valdez, Angela Gonzalez-Torres, Darializa Avila Chevalier, Melat Kiros, Adam Hamawy, Chris Rabb, and Donavan McKinney—have been noncommittal with their support for Jeffries, while Ohio democrat Don Leonard has threatened to seek alternative leadership unless Jeffries changes his position on Israel.

There’s an online effort to prevent Jeffries from becoming Speaker of the House. A change.org petition demanding that House Democrats reject Jeffries as their next Speaker has attracted more than 1 million signatures as of Sunday, with actor Mark Ruffalo among those encouraging people to sign. But scroll to the bottom of the petition, and you’ll find a disclosure: Its creator, Nicole Whitted, is a paid “Change Ambassador” who receives compensation from change.org in connection with the signatures. “Thousands of organizations and brands worldwide run similar programs and in the social-cause space specifically, it’s a way to encourage people with reach and influence to use their voice for causes they care about,” Whitted told Vanity Fair in a statement.

Whitted also said she was traveling to Washington, DC next week to urge congresspeople to vote against Jeffries, and to meet with Representative Ayanna Pressley, whom Whitted told VF she’ll be supporting for Speaker. Pressley’s team did not respond to Vanity Fair’s request for comment sent on Sunday. Neither did her closest congressional allies, Rashida Tlaib, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, or Ilhan Omar.

Reports indicate that some congresspeople have been flooded with calls from constituents urging them to find new party leadership. But one Democratic aide told Vanity Fair that they’ve noticed what they claim is a coordinated effort to undermine Jeffries on social media, including AI-generated videos attacking Jeffries that have gotten tens of thousands of engagements.

The Brooklyn congressman does have good relationships with members of both the moderate “blue dogs” and the party’s progressive flank. Ocasio-Cortez has already backed him, and last month, Representative Ro Khanna declared that “Hakeem Jeffries is going to be Speaker.” “Don’t believe what you’re seeing on the internet,” the aide said. “It’s not reality.”

Schumer’s allies, meanwhile, have been reminding skeptics of the work he did to deliver the Democratic majority. The New York senator helped recruit candidates including Roy Cooper and Sherrod Brown, while the Democratic political operation supporting Senate candidates spent lavishly on competitive races. According to final figures provided to Vanity Fair, Senate Majority PAC and its affiliates spent nearly $407 million across races in eight states, including $150 million in Ohio, $67 million apiece in Iowa and Michigan, $65 million in Maine, and $40 million in Texas, all of which flipped. Another $14 million went toward Kansas, with smaller investments in Mississippi and South Carolina—$2.2 million and $750,000 respectively. Some of that money helped elect candidates who are now skeptical about whether Schumer should stay in charge.

In the House, each party will choose its leadership this month, but in order to become Speaker, a candidate still needs to get a majority of the entire chamber, usually 218 members, to vote for them when the new Congress convenes in January. It’s a little easier in the Senate, where the Democrat will only need to win a simple majority of their own caucus in an anonymous early December vote to become majority leader.

El-Sayed has suggested Maryland senator Chris Van Hollen, although a source close to Van Hollen told Vanity Fair that he isn’t planning to run, instead setting his sights on a potential presidential campaign in 2028. “Prior to selecting a majority leader, Senator Van Hollen believes the caucus needs to have an honest and open conversation about how we move an agenda forward that reflects the will of the American people,” says a Democratic aide familiar with the senator’s thinking. “He’s made clear that Democrats need to use their power to immediately end the illegal war with Iran, advance a robust pro-worker kitchen table agenda, and fight political corruption.”

Another name circulating among Democratic aides is Hawaii senator Brian Schatz, a relatively young progressive who has developed relationships across the party’s ideological factions and is already pursuing the number-two leadership position being vacated by retiring senator Dick Durbin.

“He’s the heir apparent,” one Democratic aide told Vanity Fair, describing Schatz as someone who could appeal to both progressives and moderates. Asked about speculation surrounding his leadership ambitions, Schatz’s spokesperson offered a noncommittal response: “I don’t have anything new to share on this but will keep you posted if that changes.”

Some Democrats are also hesitant to replace their experienced leaders after they’ve been given this narrow opportunity to get stuff done with control of both chambers of Congress. “I don’t think you’d want somebody new who’s, like, learning the ropes while we have the opportunity to really get meaningful legislation passed for the American people,” another Democratic aide said.

Still, the fighting online and on cable news exposes a tension within the Democratic Party. Moderates see the blue wave as vindication of the party’s disciplined, affordability-focused strategy. Progressives see it as a mandate for a more ambitious agenda—and a rejection of the establishment’s familiar faces.

Both factions are claiming the election as their own victory. But before they can fight over what to do with their new power, they’ll have to decide who’s going to lead them.

Crystals reveal what pushed seven very different volcanoes to erupt

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Crystals from seven Andean volcanoes share one chemical fingerprint, tying each volcano eruption trigger to a pulse of hot magma from below.

A volcano that erupts once and goes quiet forever seems like a different animal from one that has blown up again and again.

A study of seven Andean volcanoes suggests that, despite their different histories, their eruptions shared a trigger.

The last crystals to grow before each eruption carry the same chemical fingerprint, and it points to a pulse of hotter magma pushing up from below.

Daniela Parra-Encalada, a doctoral student in the School of the Environment at the University of Queensland, read that fingerprint out of plagioclase.

That’s the common white mineral that makes up most of the crystals in volcanic rock.

Parra-Encalada worked with senior author Teresa Ubide and colleagues from Australia, the Universidad de Chile, Spain and Argentina.

Together they collected lava and ash from seven volcanoes along 143 miles (230 kilometers) of the Central Andes.

The work rests on 28 rock samples. Whether the same signal could ever help forecast an eruption is a question the team has only begun to test.

“Plagioclase grows in magma only a few kilometers below the surface, reacting to changes in temperature, water content and magma composition,” Parra-Encalada said.

One of those reactions is a swap of calcium for sodium, so each crystal keeps a record of the melt it grew from.

Two kinds of crystal turned up in every rock the team cut open. Large ones, up to about a twelfth of an inch (2 millimeters) long, grew slowly while the magma was stored underground.

Tiny ones, shorter than about a hundredth of an inch (300 micrometers), grew during the final rise to the surface.

The tiny crystals are the only ones that formed entirely from the magma that erupted.

Across all seven volcanoes, they held nearly the same calcium-to-sodium balance, and the outer rims of the large crystals matched them.

The team named that narrow band the plagioclase eruptibility field. The volcanoes couldn’t have been much more different.

Lascar, the most active volcano in the region, has neighbors that erupted only once, some as long as 3 million years ago.

Some lie on the main volcanic arc and others far behind it, where the crust is half as thick.

In an interview with Earth.com, Parra-Encalada was asked what surprised her most about the match.

“What surprised us most was discovering that this similarity was related to the textures and shapes of plagioclase crystals,” she said.

The cores of the large crystals told a different story. About half of all the plagioclase in these rocks didn’t grow from the erupted magma at all.

Its chemistry points to a silica-rich melt that cooled and crystallized for a long time a few miles down. Along the way it soaked up material from the surrounding crust.

Volcanologists call that kind of sluggish, crystal-packed body a mush.

In the Andean rocks, pieces of it arrived as clusters of interlocking crystals and as chunks of fully solidified rock, pulled loose and carried up when the eruption came.

The trapped crystals share their chemistry with crystals in the region’s ancient super-eruption deposits, which Parra-Encalada told Earth.com was one of the two findings that excited her most.

The same stalled magma systems may also matter for how copper and gold collect underground, she said.

The team is now comparing Andean crystals with those from large ore deposits.

To work out what the fingerprint means, the researchers ran computer simulations of magma cooling and crystallizing at different depths.

The trapped crystals formed at about 1,380°F to 1,560°F (750°C to 850°C), within 7 miles (11 kilometers) of the surface.

The eruptibility-field crystals formed hotter, at roughly 1,650°F to 1,790°F (900°C to 975°C). Their magma had just been reheated by a fresh injection from deeper down.

The fresh injection also carried more dissolved water. As the mixture rose, the water began bubbling out and building pressure in the shallow magma chamber.

Strontium isotopes told the same story: crystal cores carried the signature of melt that had digested old crust, while the rims matched the fresh carrier magma.

The team’s reading is that a volcano on this arc needs a shove from below to erupt, and the shove is what the newest crystals record.

Parra-Encalada then pulled plagioclase data from a public geochemistry database covering 26 volcanic arcs.

The average crystal composition worldwide fell inside the Andean eruptibility field. So did the averages for the Cascades and for Kamchatka.

“And this was not just in the Andes!” she said.

In the Aleutians and in Indonesia, the crust changes from continental to oceanic. There too, the crystal rims fell in the field while the cores scattered.

The result also speaks to an old puzzle. Lavas with intermediate silica levels commonly erupt along these arcs, yet the silica-rich and silica-poor magmas that mix to produce them rarely reach the surface on their own.

The crystals suggest the crust works as a filter: the thick, silica-rich mush and the dense, iron-rich mush both stay trapped, and only the reheated blend in between gets out.

Two of the samples came from an ancient deposit beneath Lascar. It was left by one of the caldera-forming explosive eruptions that buried the region about 4 million years ago.

Those crystals showed almost none of the reheating signature. In a chemical map of an entire rock slice, 97% of the crystals had grown steadily without disturbance.

So the shove that sets off an ordinary Andean eruption may not be what sets off the giant ones.

The authors suggest those huge silica-rich reservoirs can destabilize and empty on their own once enough magma has piled in.

Using crystal chemistry to identify volcanoes more likely to erupt remains an untested idea.

The idea, Parra-Encalada said, is that a volcano whose large crystals also fall near the eruptibility field may hold magma already close to erupting conditions.

Some crystal interiors at Lascar do. Lascar last erupted in the year 2022, two weeks after the team finished fieldwork there.

“If we observe a similar pattern in other polygenetic volcanoes, where most plagioclase crystals have compositions within the eruptibility field, it could indicate a volcanic system that deserves closer attention,” Parra-Encalada noted.

Polygenetic means a volcano that erupts more than once.

Nothing in this study links the fingerprint to how often a volcano erupts or how soon, and the team’s next paper takes up that question.

The samples also come from one slice of one continental arc. The global comparison relies on other researchers’ data, most of it without the crystal-by-crystal sorting this study depended on.

The team is expanding its work to Tonga to test whether the pattern also holds in an oceanic setting.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.

After more than thirty years, Audi presses pause on its biggest sedan.

Ever since Skipp Sudduth asked for “Something very Fast. Audi S8. Something that can shove a little bit,” in 1998's Ronin, Audi's big body sedan has been the stuff of legend. Mercedes-Benz S-Class? Too obvious. BMW 7-Series? You don't want to look like a wannabe James Bond. But an Audi A8? That's what you drive when you have a nickname like The Transporter.

However, as appealing as the A8 is on-screen, it's not really been much of a mover in Audi showrooms of late. Almost nothing depreciates faster than a full-size German luxury sedan—you might as well invest in an ice sculpture—and both BMW and Mercedes have been outselling Audi in the segment. Thus, Audi has just pressed pause on production of the A8, with no immediate plans in place to reboot it.

The Neckarsulm plant where the A8 was being built has already pivoted to production of the Audi A5 in both sedan and Avant forms, in standard and S5 or RS5 trims. Audi has been headquartered in Ingolstadt since the 1980s, long enough to have that city be shorthand for the company the way Stuttgart is for Porsche, but the Neckarsulm plant has roots stretching back to the nineteenth century via NSU, one of the companies that ended up forming Audi's four rings

Launched in 1994, the A8 arrived with aluminum-intensive construction to keep weight down and an available 4.2-liter V8 that still rates as one of the best-sounding engines ever made. It was businesslike, composed with all-wheel-drive for all conditions, managing to be both discreet and muscular, like a bodyguard in a well-tailored suit.

Subsequent generations remained a little more understated than rivals from Mercedes and BMW, but the fifth-gen car that's just exited production was only a year away from celebrating its tenth birthday. Even with a mid-cycle facelift, that's a bit long in the tooth for a flagship.

Taking over flagship duties is Audi's Q9 SUV, a proper three-row family hauler that's aimed at the BMW X7 and Mercedes GLS. The Q9 is certainly large and imposing, and it can carry more passengers and cargo than any A8 model. Does it have that same charisma? Put it this way: Jason Bateman is a very talented actor and producer. He is not Jason Statham.

Meanwhile, Audi may return with some kind of electrified successor to the A8, perhaps as an extended, embiggened version of whatever the new battery-electric A4 ends up looking like. Until then, any Audi-loving underworld soldiers of fortune will have to make do with a brown Mercedes 450 SEL 6.9 and a rocket launcher.

Casino Mogul Steve Wynn Sells His $70 Million Central Park Condo as Manhattan Luxury Market Heats Up

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Casino billionaire Steve Wynn has finally unloaded his Central Park duplex penthouse, making it the top luxury sale of a record-breaking week in Manhattan.

The mogul's prewar condo at 50 Central Park South—first listed in 2022 for $90 million, the most expensive in the country at the time—eventually saw its price drop to $70 million.

Even at this steep discount, it still took two years for the condo to find a taker. The duplex went into contract last week with Serena Boardman of Sotheby's International Realty as the listing agent (co-exclusive with Deborah Grubman of Corcoran).

The sale means the casino magnate failed to turn a profit on the 10,875-square-foot condo. But given that, according to Forbes, Wynn is worth $4.3 billion, he likely isn't sweating it too much.

The Billionaires Row duplex takes up the 30th and 31st floors within the 36-story Ritz-Carlton Residences, formerly the Hotel St. Moritz, which opened in 1930. The top 12 floors are the private homes.

The penthouse, formerly owned by real estate developer Christopher M. Jeffries, comes with an esteemed pedigree.

It was Jeffries' Millennium Partners that purchased the Emory Roth–designed building in 1999 and reimagined it along with the Ritz-Carlton Hotel Co. Jeffries was once married to Princess Yasmin Aga Khan, the only child of Prince Aly Khan and movie legend Rita Hayworth.

Yet the real selling point of the duplex is its sweeping Central Park views. With floors that aren't too high, from the vantage of the 45-foot long terrace, it feels as if one is sitting in the famed park's trees—a "bird's-eye perspective," as the listing puts it. Another terrace along the western wall stretches 28 feet.

With three bedrooms, four full baths, and two half-baths, the prewar unit features 10 grandly arched windows, a marble staircase, 15-foot coffered ceilings, and a living room that used to be the St. Moritz ballroom.

Modern touches include a chef's kitchen; a breakfast room with its own terrace; a primary bedroom with Central Park views, en suite bathroom, gym, and dressing room; a staff room with laundry; and a library.

As the condo sits atop the flagship Ritz-Carlton hotel, it comes with access to the amenities of the global luxury name, including the La Prairie spa and health club, a business center, the Contour Bar and Lounge, in-room dining services, and housekeeping services.

The unit has $31,362 in monthly dues on top of $15,500 in taxes.

As for why a property offering such grandeur may have taken so long to move, luxury broker Michael Reisor with Compass-affiliated Reisor.Team, who is familiar with the building, says, "Properties like this are wonderful but specific. There's a more limited buyer pool."

Despite the park views and architectural significance, it's still a century-old building, and buyers at this price range have their pick.

However, "when buyers at this level want prewar charm and detail, there is only one way to get it authentically, and that is to buy of that era," he says. "Are there people in droves wanting to pay at this level for prewar? No, but there are people who do. It's usually a more sophisticated buyer.

"They want that, 'This is classic New York.'"

The final price isn't yet known, but Reisor says, "We can assume it was right around [the ask price], if not right on."

The buyer hasn't been revealed, and listing agent Boardman did not return a call for comment.

One unique aspect of the 50 Central Park prewar is that it is a condo, not a co-op. Prewar buildings—built between the late 1880s and 1939—are typically co-ops.

In general, condos outsell co-ops each week—and after two weeks of the gap between them being slim, for the week ending Nov. 8, condos were far ahead, 34 contracts versus 7. Co-ops tend to be cheaper, with an average ask of $8.6 million last week versus $10.5 million for condos, according to Olshan Realty.

Another unusual feature of the sprawling duplex is that it has only three bedrooms. Often condos at this scale have expanded to four or more. Last week's top sale, a $31 million penthouse condo at 111 Murray St. in Tribeca, had five bedrooms.

"It's more of a psychological sticking point than an actual need," Reisor says of a high-end buyer's desire for plentiful bedrooms. "They think, 'At a certain price, I should be overflowing with bedrooms.' Even if they never need them."

Whoever snapped up the trophy unit likely wanted to be in that particular building, which is low-density, has only 11 private residences inside of a global luxury brand, and comes with a glamorous history and illustrious architect.

"It could be someone high-profile," Reisor speculates of the mystery buyer. "Someone high-profile would want something like this."

The Wynn sale was one of a slew of high-end contracts last week, with 45 signed, a big leap of 11 over the week before, and the biggest luxury sales week for the entire year. Of that, an astonishing 14 sales were priced over $10 million.

The New York City pied-à-terre tax doesn't seem to be squeezing the top end of the market. Reisor says last week's surge of luxury (over $4 million) buys can be attributed to the midterm elections.

"We always see a freeze-up before elections," he says. "The enemy to any financial market, including the housing market, is uncertainty. The biggest thing in play is that the election happened; it's over."

Additionally, he thinks that the "blue wave" was a positive for the high-end market, speculating that President Donald Trump's somewhat chastened stance on immigration is good news for monied buyers.

"This is especially relevant to New York City because of international buyers," he says, theorizing that affluent foreign buyers are wondering, "Will this be more friendly to me as an international purchaser?"

The second-highest contract of the week was a triplex penthouse with an asking price of $25.36 million. The condo, EA at 95 Madison Ave., is also in a 1912 prewar building. Although smaller at 8,355 square feet, the condo came with six bedrooms.

In a key coastal area of Peru, violence was low despite the practice of human sacrifice.

Despite engaging in human sacrifice, the people who lived in the Lambayeque Valley of northern Peru had low levels of violence, a new study of their remains finds.

Human sacrifice occurred in the valley over the millennia. It's thought that at least some of these mass sacrifices were attempts to appease the gods in the wake of El Niño, a periodic weather phenomenon that can lead to intense rains and flooding in Peru and droughts in other parts of South and Central America.

"We have one example at the site of Sicán where around [A.D.] 1050/1100, around 200 individuals were killed and ritualistically buried during an intense El Niño event," study co-author Haagen Klaus, an anthropology professor at George Mason University in Virginia, told Live Science in an email.

Other human sacrifice findings include more than 200 children found near the modern-day town of Huanchaco who were sacrificed sometime between 600 and 800 years ago, possibly during an El Niño.

However, despite the occurrence of human sacrifice, levels of violence unrelated to sacrifice were actually quite low in the Lambayeque Valley. In the study, scientists analyzed the skeletal remains of 1,519 people from the Lambayeque Valley who lived between roughly 1500 B.C. and A.D. 1800, and whose remains were preserved enough to detect signs of violent trauma, including interpersonal violence.

A skull showing blunt force trauma that dates to the Chimu period, roughly 1375 to 1490.

Of these skeletons, "only 25 (1.6%) exhibited traumatic lesions consistent with interpersonal conflict, and 77 exhibited post-cranial traumatic lesions consistent with accidental trauma (5.1%)," Klaus and co-author Thomas Snyder, a bioarchaeologist at New York University, wrote in the paper, which was published Nov. 8 in the journal Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology.

The team didn't find any link between a person's sex and violence, they noted. The findings mean that despite the presence of human sacrifice, the amount of violence unrelated to sacrifice was low. This leaves a question: Why?

"Ritual violence may have alleviated non-ritual violence by creating spaces where social tensions could get worked out in a few different ways," Klaus said.

It's important to remember that other areas of Peru also practiced human sacrifice but didn't have the same low levels of interpersonal violence. Klaus noted that areas in the Peruvian highlands sometimes had higher rates of interpersonal violence. In their paper, Klaus and Snyder noted that societies that originated in the highlands, such as the Tiwanaku, Wari and Inca had to grapple with poorer soils and cold temperatures, and these challenges may have been a factor in increasing the levels of interpersonal violence.

The "evidence of sub-lethal and lethal violence forceful enough to break bone is significantly higher in some areas of Peru's highlands," Klaus said. He and Snyder noted in their paper that the inhabitants of the Lambayeque Valley may have had better access to marine and water resources, such as seafood and rivers that could be used for agricultural irrigation, than those who lived in highland areas. Access to these resources may have helped alleviate interpersonal violence in the valley by providing more food resources, they wrote.

Aside from practicing human sacrifice, people in the valley may have engaged in other practices that helped lower levels of interpersonal violence, researchers who were not involved in the new study told Live Science.

Environmental conditions may have helped reduce conflict or there could be "really unusual cultural practices in this region of avoiding or mediating conflict — both between and within groups," Elizabeth Arkush, an anthropology professor at the University of Pittsburgh, said in an email.

Constanza Ceruti, an anthropologist and explorer with the National Geographic Society, said in an email that she's glad that researchers are studying human sacrifices "after decades of being ignored as a taboo topic in academic discussion." She praised the study’s large sample size and noted that human sacrifice is a "phenomenon primarily associated [with] the manipulation and concentration of power by elites."

Jordi Rivera Prince, an anthropology professor at Tulane University, said that the findings dovetail with her excavations in the region, where she has "found little evidence of violent trauma during the Salinar (400-200 BCE), a period of great social change in the Andes."

Weston McCool, an archaeology professor at Cal Poly, said in an email that the newly published paper "is an interesting study that provides additional evidence for a sharp break in patterns of violence between the Andean coast and highlands." He noted that it "supports the hypothesis that coastal populations were buffered from terrestrial environmental downturns in ways that highland populations were not. The nature of that resilience, however, remains an important question for future research."
