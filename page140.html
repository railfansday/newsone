Dal 1992 a oggi, cinque formazioni si erano spinte fino a quota sette: il Marsiglia nel 2010, lo Shakhtar Donetsk nel 2014, il Liverpool nel 2017, il Bayern Monaco nel 2019 e il PSG nel 2025. Soltanto il Bayern era riuscito a fare meglio, arrivando a otto gol nella storica trasferta di Barcellona del 2020. Un primato che sembrava destinato a resistere, almeno fino alla notte del Bollaert-Delelis, dove la banda di Fabregas ha alzato ulteriormente l'asticella, spingendosi dove nessuno era mai arrivato prima.

Con il 9-1 rifilato al Lens, il Como entra anche in un club esclusivissimo: quello delle squadre capaci di chiudere una partita di Champions League con otto reti di scarto. Un'impresa riuscita, prima dei lariani, soltanto a Liverpool e Real Madrid. I Reds ci erano riusciti nel 2007, travolgendo il Besiktas con un 8-0 ad Anfield, mentre i Blancos avevano replicato nel 2015, rifilando lo stesso risultato al Malmo al Bernabeu. Da questa sera, però, anche la squadra di Fabregas può vantare un posto in questa ristretta élite.

Con i dieci gol segnati allo Bollaert-Delelis, Lens-Como diventa anche la terza partita con più reti nella storia della Champions League assieme ai dieci gol sommati che ci furono in Barcellona-Bayern Monaco del 14 agosto 2020 (2-8) e alle spalle degli undici gol in Monaco-Deportivo La Coruña, terminata 8-3 il 5 novembre 2003 e dei dodici che si videro a Signal Iduna Park il 22 novembre 2016 tra il Borussia Dortmund e il Legia Varsavia, che finì 8-4.

Scott Parker saw his West Ham side recover from three goals down to draw 3-3 at Cardiff in a thrilling first game in charge.

Parker was confirmed as Hammers boss on Wednesday morning – just 24 hours after Nuno Espirito Santo’s departure – but it was a tough watch for the former England midfielder initially as Moylan had Cardiff 3-0 ahead inside 39 minutes.

Moylan scored a hat-trick on his Republic of Ireland debut against Grenada in May, but this was his first club treble since scoring three for Shelbourne in May 2023.

The draw extends Cardiff’s unbeaten home league run to 10 games over the last two seasons, while West Ham occupy the final play-off place in eighth.

Nuno’s 13-month tenure had ended after the Hammers had won just twice in six matches and lost successive home games to Southampton and West Brom.

Parker sent on Mohamadou Kante and Joel Piroe for Soungoutou Magassa and Castellanos at half-time and there was instantly more conviction from the Hammers.

Bowen floated a delicious pass through after 58 minutes, and Mukasa lifted the ball over Trott and in off a post.

The deficit was reduced further seven minutes later when Piroe flicked on Bowen’s pass and Engels slid home his first Hammers goal.

Kante nearly toe poked the ball past Trott before Kilman touched home Bowen’s brilliant cross, and West Ham almost won it through substitutes Ezra Mayers and Tomas Soucek.

Durbin Is Retiring, Marshall Just Lost—Why The Bill Threatening Your Credit Card Rewards Isn’t Dead

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Dick Durbin is retiring and Roger Marshall just lost his Senate seat. The two most prominent champions of the Credit Card Competition Act, the law intended to regulate credit card interchange which will mean far fewer rewards for consumers, will be gone in January. The bill threatening the economics of card rewards, and the airlines that depend on them, has support from President Trump and Vice President JD Vance was an original co-sponsor so it may come back with his presidential campaign. But the thing about this legislation is that it’s more useful as a way to raise campaign money from both retailers and banks than as something Congress actually passes. Departures of Durbin and Marshall haven’t made the Durbin-Marshall bill go away. Durbin identified Vermont Senator Peter Welch as his successor on the issue. The Airline Lobby Is Right To Worry In this week’s Airlines Confidential, Airlines for America CEO Chris Sununu warns that state initiatives interfering with card fees would eliminate points-funded travel. Banks buy miles because rewards help attract customers and their spending. By reducing margins on that spending, it’s less valuable to spend to encourage consumers to use the product. In markets where government has gotten involved in interchange rates we’ve seen: smaller initial bonuses higher annual fees annual caps on awards award chart devaluations (if each mile is worth less, an airline can still award one mile per dollar on spend despite receiving less money for it from the bank) It also means less revenue for the airline. In the third quarter, American Express represented all of Delta’s profit. American Airlines is barely breaking even even with several billion net from Citibank. We’d have much shakier airlines and fewer flights, and aspirational leisure destinations like Hawaii in particular would see less air service. What The Credit Card Competition Act Actually Does The current Senate bill, S. 3623, would require covered issuers with more than $100 billion in assets to enable at least two unaffiliated payment networks. At least one would have to be outside of Visa and Mastercard. Merchants get to choose the routing. That’s meant to put downward pressure on merchant payment costs by changing who controls which network handles the transaction. Cards issued in a three-party system, such as American Express issuing its own cards, are exempt from the routing requirement. Merchants benefit from accepting cards. They get more sales, and higher average sales. It’s often less expensive to accept credit cards than to take cash (due to employee theft, incorrect change, higher insurance costs). Retailers want the service cards offer, but want the government to force Visa and Mastercard to provide it for less. Trump’s promise of $1,200 in annual family savings was completely made up and inconsistent with the bill. Currently a majority of interchange gets rebated to the customer. Regulation of interchange has never lowered prices to consumers. And card payments, funded in large part by interchange, provide services to consumers beyond just payments (and borrowings). They often fund consumer protections – not just stronger dispute resolution than debit cards, but also extended warranty, return protection, and travel protections. The Bill’s Strongest Value Is As A Threat Back in July 2023, I argued that this was an especially useful issue for opening contribution wallets on both sides. Retailers have a large, concentrated interest in lowering their acceptance costs. Banks, payment networks and airlines have a large, concentrated interest in preserving the existing economics. An unresolved legislative threat gives both sides a reason to pour dollars into campaigns and political committees. I’d still bet on another round of proposals, threatened amendments and lobbying. Durbin and Marshall are leaving but the retailers and banks with billions at stake aren’t. Politicians still want the money. Support the urgent need for brain research funding

MINNEAPOLIS, Nov. 4, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- The need for brain research funding has never been more urgent. That is why the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) today announced its year-end giving campaign for the AAN Research Program, inviting people to support the researchers working to find new treatments and cures for brain diseases and disorders.

One hundred percent of every tax-deductible donation will go directly to brain research.

"Scientific breakthroughs begin with researchers who have the ideas and expertise to pursue important questions," said American Academy of Neurology President Natalia S. Rost, MD, MPH, FAAN, FAHA. "The AAN Research Program gives all of us the opportunity to help advance high-quality brain research, bringing hope to people affected by brain disease."

Brain research is what makes it possible to prevent, diagnose, treat, and eventually even cure conditions of the brain and nervous system. It also advances our understanding of memory, learning, sleep, mental health, and healthy aging, helping people live healthier lives.

The AAN Research Program has funded brain research for more than 30 years, owing to its success the exceptional pool of talented researchers in the AAN community, the world's largest association of neurologists and neuroscience professionals.

Demand for funding, however, continues to outpace available resources. Hundreds of researchers apply for AAN grants. The AAN invested nearly $1.8 million in 2026, but millions in viable neuroscience research went unfunded.

"We know that excellent research proposals go unfunded," said American Academy of Neurology Chief Executive Officer Mary E. Post, MBA, CAE. "We want to make sure that we have the funding to support the research that may eventually lead to a cure for MS or a way to prevent Alzheimer's. Every contribution can help researchers pursue discoveries as they work toward improving brain health for all."

With neurologists leading the AAN Board of Directors, Science Committee, and Research Program Subcommittee, the AAN is guided by trusted experts who understand both the science and the real-world application of research.

The AAN's mission is to enhance member career fulfillment and promote brain health for all. Is It Over for DAPT After Left Atrial Appendage Closure?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

— Antithrombotic monotherapy worked at least as well at 6 months

Antithrombotic monotherapy was similar to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6-month outcomes after left atrial appendage (LAA) closure, the SIMPLAAFY trial showed.

The incidence rate of combined all-cause death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding at 6 months was 5.1% with reduced-dose non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs), 3.3% with aspirin alone, and 6.9% with DAPT, reported Saibal Kar, MD, of Los Robles Hospital and Medical Center in Thousand Oaks, California, at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation.

Statistically, the strategy of reduced-dose NOACs was at least as good as DAPT (risk ratio [RR] 0.75, 95% CI 0.48-1.18), as was aspirin alone (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.29-0.82).

"These findings support the simplification of antithrombotic management after uncomplicated LAA [closure] and may be individualized according to competing bleeding and device thrombosis risks and patient preferences," Kar said.

The study was simultaneously published in JAMA.

Among the secondary analyses in the trial, there was no excess stroke at 6 months with the aspirin or NOAC monotherapy regimens. However, bleeds alone gave both an edge over DAPT, while low-dose NOAC therapy stood out for a relatively low incidence of device-related thrombus (DRT) at 45 days (0.9% vs 3.4% with DAPT and 2.7% with aspirin).

As it stands, standard practice varies widely for antithrombotic therapy after LAA closure on account of a "lack of legitimate evidence" for guidance, said session discussant Mohamad Alkhouli, MD, of the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota.

"Aspirin seems to emerge a winner," Alkhouli suggested, based on SIMPLAAFY, while he noted that "the jury is still out" for NOACs.

Complicating the picture is the ANDES trial, which found that after LAA closure, short-term NOACs were no better than DAPT at preventing DRT at 60 days, and were associated with even more DRTs at 1 year (5.2% vs 2.3%, P=0.154).

Kar said these data gave him pause on making a blanket recommendation for NOACs after LAA closure.

Meanwhile, 12-month results from SIMPLAAFY are still pending.

"The fact is that we really didn't know what to do with these patients," said Kar. "What we will do beyond 1 year, I don't know."

SIMPLAAFY included patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who had the Watchman FLX Pro LAA occluder successfully implanted. The investigators randomized 1,857 patients 1:1:1 to aspirin 81 to 100 mg alone, initial DAPT for 6 months (followed by aspirin thereafter), or initial reduced-dose NOACs -- 2.5-mg apixaban (Eliquis) twice daily or 10-mg rivaroxaban (Xarelto) daily -- for 3 months (followed by aspirin thereafter).

Across the three study groups, mean age was 76 years, 43% were women, mean CHA2DS2-VASc score was 4.2, and mean HAS-BLED bleeding risk score was 2.6.

Acute events through 7 days were rare in general. Rates of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding were 1.0% with aspirin alone, 1.6% with DAPT, and 1.5% with low-dose NOACs; major bleeding rates were 0.2%, 0.5%, and 0.2%, respectively.

The proportion of participants adhering to their assigned medications (for 80% or more days) was 87.9% with aspirin alone, 76.8% with DAPT, and 85.3% with reduced-dose NOACs. "Both monotherapy treatments were better tolerated than DAPT," Kar noted.

A subgroup analysis indicated that people ages 75 and older had disproportionately better outcomes on aspirin (RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18-0.64) or reduced-dose NOACs (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31-0.93).

LAA imaging at 45 days suggested no differences in the Watchman device seal across groups.

Kar stressed that only uncomplicated LAA occluder implants were included in the trial, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Some high-risk populations were also excluded (e.g., those with severe kidney disease, recent stroke, myocardial infarction, or major bleeding).

Of note, there was no prespecified comparison of the two monotherapy arms.
