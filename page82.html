YouTuber Finds Ocarina Of Time Remake Dialogue Tied To A Majora's Mask Amiibo

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It has been four days since the Ocarina of Time remake was released, and dedicated fans have already made some fascinating discoveries. Not just easter eggs and subtle differences between the remake and the original, but hidden dialogue that many think suggests a Majora’s Mask remake is already in the works.

YouTuber Austin John Plays has posted a video in which he shares the codes for the upcoming Ocarina remake amiibo. The Young Link and Young Zelda amiibo, which will be released in 2027, seemingly to coincide with the launch of The Legend of Zelda movie, unlock in-game masks of the characters’ original N64 designs when scanned.

Those amiibo have the codes Mask 101 and Mask 102, but upon discovering this, John also found a third, unannounced amiibo with the code Mask 103. There is naturally no image of the mask tied to its codename; however, as is the case with the two announced masks, there are lines of dialogue attached to it.

NPCs will react to Link in unique ways if you approach them wearing the N64 masks when you can unlock them next year. It’s the lines of dialogue attached to that third mask that make it seem like we’re getting at least one more Ocarina remake amiibo, and the conclusion John and his viewers have reached is that somehow, some way, we’re getting a Majora’s Mask remake.

The dialogue attached to Mask 103 all boils down to those who see it being afraid of it, referring to it as weird, strange, and creepy. Noted in a Reddit thread covering John’s findings, one of the lines of dialogue attached to the mask comments on it scaring away monsters from whoever’s wearing it. That’s how Majora’s Mask functions in Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom.

My takeaway from that is that we will perhaps get a Skull Kid amiibo down the line that unlocks Majora’s Mask in the Ocarina of Time remake, and that wearing the mask will effectively unlock an easy mode where monsters are too afraid to attack you. However, most people think there’s more to it, especially since Majora’s Mask was the original Ocarina’s sequel.

If you’re not a little skeptical about all of this, like I am, then you seem to fall into one of two camps. Either you believe this discovery proves a Majora’s Mask remake is coming, or that this suggests it will be DLC rather than a full-blown remake sequel.

Despite my skepticism, I do think it would be a little silly of Nintendo if its next Zelda project isn't a Majora’s Mask remake. I’d also understand why it might want to give it a miss, or relegate it to Ocarina DLC. Majora’s Mask didn’t have nearly as big a cultural impact as Ocarina, so despite the overall success of Zelda in 2026, Nintendo might still be a little worried that a standalone Majora’s Mask remake might not have the same cache that Ocarina of Time carries.

Cristian Chivu said that while the last nine games could have gone better for Inter, he was impressed by their "wonderful" performance in the win over Como.

Francesco Pio Esposito and Marcus Thuram scored quickfire goals to put Inter in control after 19 minutes, with Como unable to mount a comeback.

It means that Inter have won each of their last 12 Serie A matches against Como, extending their longest-ever winning run against a single opponent in the competition.

"[Against Como], we raised the pace and intensity, and our preparation served us well in terms of quality and pressure. We played as a team.

"We did well in the first half from all points of view, and then sometimes you need to know how to manage other moments of the game. I congratulate the boys, who were wonderful.

"There's always room for improvement. You can't always have everything and be perfect every time. You have to accept that the season is long and there are lots and lots of games."

I Only Realized These 7 Movies Are Masterpieces After Watching Them Twice

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are some movies that are great the first time around. But then there are the rare few where the ending changes everything. Because now that you’ve seen how it all ends, it reframes everything you just watched and gives it a whole new meaning. And the second time around, it gets infinitely better.

Some have third-act plot twists where you suddenly see every clue hiding in plain sight. Others are just so dense that one viewing couldn’t possibly catch it all. Here are seven movies that are good on the first watch and flat-out masterpieces on the second.

This one starts out as a regular whodunit. A group of rich friends get together for a hurricane party; they play a murder game, and then people start dying for real. One by one, the group begins to turn on each other as they try to find the killer before it’s too late. It’s a pretty standard setup, and it plays like one right up until the end.

The big reveal is that there was no killer at all. The first death was just an accident, and every death after that was either another accident or someone killing a friend out of pure paranoia. It’s so well done that you never realize it in the moment. The movie moves so fast that you don’t have time to stop and think, and then you watch it again and see how perfectly the premise was set up. They’re all dying on their own, and you were too busy keeping up to notice.

Across the Spider-Verse is an absolute sensory overload on the first watch. It’s a treat even the first time around, no doubt, but there’s so much going on that you won’t pick up on most of it until you’ve watched it over and over again. Take the opening, for example. The film starts with a colorful sequence of Gwen’s voiceover as she plays drums, and you think she’s recapping the last film. But only after you’ve finished the movie do you realize she wasn’t recapping Into the Spider-Verse. She was recapping this movie. The film basically started at the end, and that opening even shows off major plot points from later, like Miguel explaining the Spider-Verse to Miles and a shot of the fully powered-up Spot.

The Spider-Society sequence took four years to animate, and it is bursting with so many references and little jokes that you possibly can’t catch everything on first viewing. For example, every Spidey variant wears the tech on their wrist, but Spider-Cat wears it around its tail. There’s even a background Spidey variant with a permanently misaligned suit texture, which pays homage to printing mistakes in old comics. Did you catch that one? Because most people don’t. And you even see Hobie stealing equipment from the Spider Society, which he then uses to build the dimensional travel device he later leaves for Gwen.

Four of the largest communities on the planet, and four completely different reasons to belong to one. Here is yours, what it would give you, and the argument you would be having within a week.

You belong to the fandom that is really about comfort. Hogwarts works on people because it is a place rather than a plot, and the pull is not the wizard war so much as the corridors, the food, the weather and the sense that somebody is expecting you.

You belong to the deepest fandom on this list, the one where somebody has always read further than you and is quietly delighted to say so. Middle-earth rewards patience more than any other world in popular culture.

You belong to the loudest and largest tent of the four, the one where three generations are all watching the same thing and none of them agree about any of it. That disagreement is not a flaw in this fandom, it is the main activity.

You belong to the thinking fandom, the one that turns up for the argument rather than the explosion. Trek has always been a series of ethical problems in space, and the people who love it love being asked what they would have done.

Tenet is undeniably Christopher Nolan’s most confusing movie to date. Even Robert Pattinson admitted that he didn’t understand it while he was acting in it! The film follows a nameless agent played by John David Washington who gets pulled into a mission involving objects and people moving backward through time, and it is a film that basically requires a rewatch just to make sense of it.

Once you know the endgame and all the secret character motivations, it gets much easier to digest. Granted, the sound mix doesn’t get any better. But the action is top-tier and so much more thrilling when you’re not constantly trying to figure out how this world works and why what’s happening on screen is happening.

Right up until the end, The Sixth Sense is a fairly decent horror movie about a kid who can see ghosts. You see the movie from the perspective of a child psychologist who encourages the kid to listen to the ghosts and help them resolve whatever unfinished business keeps them tied to the earth. That is, until you find out the psychologist had been dead all along.

That’s why the film gets so much better on a rewatch. You can see all the subtle ways it misdirects you into never realizing he was dead. He goes to an anniversary dinner at a very public restaurant, so it never even crosses your mind that he doesn’t exist. But his wife completely ignores his attempts at conversation, and the waiter never acknowledges or serves him. And whenever he gets close to his wife, her breath fogs in the cold air, and she pulls her clothes tighter, a classic sign of a ghost’s presence. All the signs were right in front of you the entire time.

Shutter Island follows U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels (Leonardo DiCaprio) and his partner Chuck Aule (Mark Ruffalo) as they travel to a fortress-like mental institution for the criminally insane to investigate the mysterious escape of a patient. The whole film plays like a top-tier crime thriller where something always feels off. And then the ending reveals that Teddy was actually a patient at the hospital all along. His entire investigation was a staged role-play therapy devised by his doctors to break his delusion and save him from a lobotomy.

That’s why a rewatch is so much more rewarding. When Dr. Sheehan (who’s actually Chuck) is mentioned by name early on, a nearby patient looks directly at Ruffalo’s character. Chuck also struggles to unholster his gun like he’s doing it for the first time, because he isn’t actually a trained U.S. Marshal. In another blink-and-you-miss-it moment, a woman drinks from an imaginary glass and sets it on a table. Then, in the following scenes, a real glass keeps appearing and disappearing, which highlights Teddy’s broken sense of reality.

Midsommar follows Dani (Florence Pugh), a young woman reeling from a devastating family tragedy. She tags along with her boyfriend Christian (Jack Reynor) and his friends to a once-in-ninety-years midsummer festival in a remote Swedish village. The film starts as a bright, flowery, folk getaway but gradually turns into a nightmare once the villagers’ rituals begin.

And of course, it’s creepy the first time around, but it gets so much more horrific on a rewatch. Before the film even begins, we see a creepy folksy tapestry covered with intricate designs. But once you’ve seen the film, you realize it reveals all the important events to come. And that keeps repeating for the rest of the movie. There are murals and tapestries all over the backgrounds, and on a rewatch they take on a whole new meaning because you realize they depict the horrific things that are about to happen. These characters’ fates were right in front of them (and you) the entire time.

The Hateful Eight follows eight stranded strangers trapped in a lodge during a severe blizzard in post-Civil War Wyoming. The Hangman (Kurt Russell) is transporting a chained outlaw, Daisy Domergue (Jennifer Jason Leigh), to have her hanged. And he’s convinced that at least one of the people in that lodge is her accomplice and intends to break her free. It’s a slow burn that feels like a stage play, and it’s full of paranoia and bloody Tarantino chaos.

But once you’ve seen the movie and you know the secret motivations and what happened before they all got there, it gets so much funnier. General Sanford Smithers’ (Bruce Dern) face when you first meet him, sitting grumpy in that chair, is so much funnier on the second watch. Every bit with the fake letter is downright unhinged. And then watching everyone who was secretly Domergue’s accomplice put on their performances is hilarious. It’s a completely different viewing experience.

If a Recession Hits in 2027, This Is the Dividend Stock I Want to Own

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's not the name you'd usually expect from this particular business either.

As of the beginning of this year, the economy looked fine. Lingering inflation was annoying, but manageable. AI technology stocks were performing because they held so much promise, boding well for the rest of the market and the overall economy.

Now the risk of a recession is all too real. Inflation hasn't abated, and the Federal Reserve is seemingly uncertain if an interest rate increase will help or do more harm. The prospect of prolonged economic weakness can't be ruled out.

I've got one dividend-paying consumer-goods name in mind to defend my portfolio against this possibility, though. That's beverage and snack outfit PepsiCo (PEP -0.96%).

It's not the go-to beverage stock most investors usually select when seeking safety. That honor still broadly belongs to bigger rival Coca-Cola (KO -0.06%).

However, there's a not-so-minor nuance with these two tickers right now that matters. That's their yields. Thanks to Coca-Cola's 50% share-price run-up over the past two years, Coke's forward-looking dividend yield has been dialed back to only 2.2%, or nearly half of the 4.2% yield PepsiCo currently offers.

Investors keeping their finger on the pulse of these two stocks know the reason why. That is, PepsiCo has struggled with inflation far more than Coca-Cola has, pulling PepsiCo shares down 36% from their 2023 high to last month's multiyear low.

As the old adage goes, nothing lasts forever. We're seeing glimmers of hope that the company's turned the corner, in fact. Its third-quarter organic revenue improved 3.1% year over year, a trend PepsiCo expects to more or less repeat in the quarter currently underway. That's not impressive by technology stock standards, but it's solid for a consumer goods name. It's also better than we've seen from the company for the past couple of years.

PepsiCo's stock has a pretty strong record of performance during recessions, too.

While it struggled in the wake of 2008's subprime mortgage meltdown and during the short-lived recession linked to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, those were unusual circumstances. During the more ordinary cyclical weakness seen beginning with the implosion of dot-com names in the early 2000s as well as back during the 1990s recession, PepsiCo shares actually logged gains, reflecting the resilient nature of the beverage business even when consumer spending on other fronts dries up.

Obviously there's no guarantee this ticker will perform as well again during the next recession whenever it might take shape. There's a reason it was able to do so in the past, however.

Perhaps more than anything, though, PepsiCo's dividend is almost sure to continue being paid even if we do run into an economic headwind. Even with all the inflationary pressure of the past couple of years, the company has earned more than enough profits to continue fully funding its dividend payments. This isn't likely to change going forward either. The company's track record of 54 years of uninterrupted annual dividend growth confirms this resilience.

Besides, when little else about the stock market is working in your favor, a little cash flow is a pretty big deal.

Trump claims little knowledge of World Liberty Financial, with UAE investors securing a major stake in the crypto venture.

President Donald Trump said he knows “nothing” about World Liberty Financial, the crypto company he co-owns, when asked about foreign investors and potential conflicts of interest during a 60 Minutes interview aired Sunday.

“Because I know nothing about it,” Trump told CBS News correspondent Ross Douthat when asked why foreign investment in his crypto business would not raise the same corruption concerns he might have raised if former President Joe Biden had similar interests.

Douthat also asked why Trump could not sell his stake in World Liberty Financial while allowing his sons to continue running the company. “How do you divest from cash?” Trump replied. He said most of his wealth was held with major Wall Street firms and invested in index funds.

The President had earlier defended retaining his stake by saying his sons ran the business. World Liberty launched in September 2024, naming him its “Chief Crypto Advocate,” while Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump were designated “Web3 Ambassadors” and Barron Trump was listed as “Chief DeFi Visionary.”

“I have a company. My sons run the company. It’s hard to say to them, ‘Don’t do anything anymore for four years because I’m president,’” he said.

The remarks come amid scrutiny of World Liberty's foreign investment and ownership structure. In August, the Wall Street Journal reported that UAE national security adviser Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed al Nahyan and co-investors held a 49% stake in WLFI-linked WLTC Holdings. The company recently received the OCC's preliminary approval for a national trust bank charter.

Separately, an earlier WSJ report said a Tahnoon-backed investment vehicle secretly acquired a 49% stake in World Liberty Financial for $500 million shortly before Trump's inauguration.

World Liberty is also facing a legal dispute with investor Justin Sun, who says he invested $45 million in WLFI tokens. Sun has sought sanctions against the company, alleging it failed to disclose that entities in its ownership structure had UAE citizenship until months into their legal dispute. The Tron founder sued in April over the alleged freezing of his tokens, while World Liberty has called his case a “meritless PR stunt.”

Meanwhile, Public Citizen estimated in August that investors across Trump-linked crypto products were at least $4.7 billion underwater, largely in unrealized losses. It attributed at least $1 billion of those paper losses to WLFI.

A River Runs Through It. An AI Data Center Tried to Move In.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anglers enjoy the iconic Blackfoot River in Montana, known for its blue ribbon trout fishing (Photo: Matt Roberts)

Brenna Love grew up fishing on the Blackfoot, a snow-fed Montana mountain river that spans 130 miles from its headwaters on the Continental Divide to the confluence of the Clark Fork River, seven miles east of Missoula. Its cold, fast-flowing water makes for premier blue ribbon trout fishing, scenic canyons, and whitewater recreation.

The Blackfoot was immortalized in Norman Maclean’s 1976 book A River Runs Through It and Other Stories, which was turned into a Hollywood film starring Brad Pitt in 1992.

Love, 29, is a resident of Bonner, a former mill town with just 2,700 residents located along the banks of the Blackfoot.

“My earliest memories are on my family’s raft,” Love told Outside. “Every time you’re on the water, you’re surrounded by other people loving the same backyard that we love so deeply. You look around and see people on boats of all shapes, sizes, and colors, recreating and enjoying. It’s the reason we live here in Montana.”

In March of 2026, an Idaho-based technology company called Krambu announced plans to build an artificial intelligence data center in Bonner, just steps from the banks of the Blackfoot. The project would convert an old mill building into a warehouse for computer servers, and the complex would draw water from the nearby Blackfoot River to provide continuous cooling for the computer racks.

Love and her community fought back—and won.

“It was hard,” she told Outside. “It was really hard. It took organizing and talking with each other about what this means. It took our evenings, our lunch breaks, reviewing the new application materials and doing our own research.”

It’s the latest in a growing wave of community pushback against big tech’s data center expansion—and a reminder that local voices, when organized, can have a lasting impact.

Data centers are rapidly popping up across the United States, fueled by the computing needs of artificial intelligence and cloud-based software. These centers house massive servers, data storage systems, and networking equipment, and they require sizable amounts of electricity and water to run and maintain the hardware.

According to Public Lands Intelligence, as of this writing, 1,302 data centers are proposed or under construction, in addition to 904 already operating across 49 states and Washington, D.C.

But as construction crews have erected these centers, they have become political lightning rods in towns and cities. The centers are often loud and large, and they eat up valuable electricity. As data centers have faced bipartisan backlash in cities across the country, tech firms have looked to rural communities, farming towns, and even unused public land to build.

At least 35 of current data center applications are on public lands, the Center for Western Priorities reports, and some are directly adjacent to national park units.

Secretary of the Department of the Interior Doug Bergum has tens of millions invested in AI data centers, according to a report published by ProPublica. In September, The Washington Sun reported that the Trump administration was considering at least 12 data center-related infrastructure projects on federal public lands.

Private equity firm Blackstone is building the Tallgrass Energy Project, a massive AI data center located south of Cheyenne, Wyoming. Blackstone also backed a historic $105 million donation to Yellowstone National Park.

The list goes on. Roughly 115 miles from Yellowstone’s north entrance, Quantica’s Big Sky Campus is proposing a 5,100-acre, or nearly eight-square-mile, data center. In Kentucky, Cave Point Commerce Center is a proposed 381-acre hyperscale data center near Mammoth Cave National Park. The City of Page, Arizona, is considering a $10 billion data center two miles from Horseshoe Bend near Glen Canyon National Recreation Area. In July, the federal government fast-tracked a data center ten miles from Lake Mead.

In Bonner, the proposed data center would be in the Bonner Mill, a historic 169-acre former lumber mill along the riverbank. Originally built in 1886, the sawmill closed in 2008 and was transformed in 2011 into a multi-tenant industrial park.

Ben Catton, a legislative assistant at the Montana Environmental Information Center (MEIC), told Outside that sites like the Bonner Mill are ideal for companies that build data centers.

“Tech companies target old mill sites,” Catton said. “That’s part of their business model: to look for this existing infrastructure. Because those were really energy-consumptive sites, they have a lot of that energy infrastructure in place already.”

According to the Krambu proposal, the Bonner Mill data center would draw 16 million gallons of water from the river in July and August to cool its servers. Under Catton’s calculations, that means the 240,000-square-foot center would require roughly one megawatt of power—enough to power 800 homes for a year.

Montana’s energy and water needs during its driest months didn’t sit well with him.

“They’re going to be very consumptive in the two months when the landscape is the thirstiest for that water, when our rivers are at their lowest, and when our fish are most in need of cold water,” Catton said.

The Bonner Mill sits 100 yards from Love’s front porch, and as a concerned community member, she began reaching out to her neighbors one by one to discuss the plan.

“Start by having a conversation with your neighbor, and then your other neighbor, and then your other neighbor,” Love said. “Those conversations really build up to a community-wide conversation.”

On March 20, local nonprofit Friends of Two Rivers—so-named for the confluence of the Clark Fork and Blackfoot Rivers—hosted the first public meeting at Saint Ann Catholic Church, a rural, single-story neighborhood church originally built in 1905. Against a wood-paneled backdrop, representatives from Krambu and Missoula County, along with Bonner Mill property manager Mike Heisey, spoke on a panel to a standing-room-only crowd.

Organizers were surprised by how many people showed up, Love recalled. Most people were there to voice their concerns about the project.

“There were just so many different people engaging in so many different ways through different avenues, and that’s what eventually changed the tide,” Love said. “It was organized in all of these different pockets, and I think any one of those groups couldn’t have done it alone.”

It was the first of many meetings held at libraries, schools, in living rooms, and at coffee shops. Love said she participated in at least eight, primarily working with her HOA members in closed meetings, as well as informal working groups of neighbors and the managers of the Bonner Milltown Historical Center.

“Rather than a scattershot signs and flyers approach, we were trying to find avenues where we thought we could make an impact,” Love said. “We organized as our HOA and then submitted our concerns and questions directly to the property manager.”

Love is one of hundreds who rallied through social media and in person to oppose the data center. Local regulations at the time weren’t rigid enough to deny Krambu approval, so Love and her team took their concerns directly to the property manager.

“The more direct action and impact you can make, the better,” Love said. “If you can figure out who has the authority, whether it is your county or your city, via the regulations in place there, the owner of the site, or who has investment in the community, find them.”

Between March and June of 2026, Krambu submitted five iterations of its application to build the data center, each time drawing backlash from the community. Love and other local advocates launched a petition that garnered more than 50,000 signatories to reject the AI data, and flooded every town meeting with shoulder-to-shoulder opposition.

Citing overwhelming public feedback and neighborhood opposition, Heisey officially announced on July 6, 2026, that the property would not move forward with the data center and withdrew his support for the application.

A Krambu spokesperson told Outside in a statement said that the company is under new leadership and that the Bonner data center was spearheaded by the company’s former management. The company has no plans to revisit the Bonner project, the spokesperson said.

In direct response to the community concerns exposed during the Krambu review, Missoula County Commissioners approved a temporary one-year moratorium on July 9 on new zoning permits for large-scale data centers to establish more comprehensive regulatory frameworks.

Love credits the victory to her community’s ability to organize across multiple fronts: viral online opposition, petitions, public comments and meeting attendance, coordinated feedback from small groups and the HOA, and direct outreach to decision-makers, from county commissioners and city council members to the landowner.

It’s a big win for a small mountain town, and one that could pave the way for other rural areas facing data center proposals.

Montana is perhaps uniquely positioned for these types of fights. The Montana Constitution enshrines the right to a “clean and healthful environment” as a fundamental right, requiring the state and individuals to maintain and improve the environment for present and future generations. Outdoor recreation is also a major economic input for Montana, contributing $3.8 billion to the Montana economy.

Adam Schellhammer, Mid-Atlantic regional director for the nonprofit advocacy group American Rivers, warns that data centers pose new threats to states without such incentives or protections

“On the national level, trends show that the speed and scale of development is just ramping up so quickly that we’re just having trouble keeping up,” Schellhammer said.

According to Schellhammer, data center regulation is fragmented across federal, state, and local municipal agencies. Water usage details are frequently concealed under non-disclosure agreements or classified as proprietary corporate information. Because many areas do not legally mandate public disclosure, city planners cannot always establish accurate water budgets to understand a center’s full impact. Schellhammer says communities should start by updating industrial zoning and land-use ordinances to make a difference, rather than reacting after sites are chosen.

“The lesson learned is that every single voice matters,” Love said. “If there’s something you care about, say so. Find a place to speak your truth, speak your concerns, be an advocate for the things that you are passionate about, and someone will hear you.”

Spy x Family Officially Announces New Season and Movie Coming Soon

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the best anime series in recent years is coming with a new season and a feature film. Spy x Family, the comedy-action manga created by Tatsuya Endo and published by Shueisha, is getting a fourth anime season and a new movie, both coming in 2027.

The announcement, shared on Oct. 8 at the Spy x Family Anime Extra Mission II special event and on the official Spy x Family website, came as a pleasant surprise for fans. Season 3 of the anime aired from October to December 2025, exactly two years after season 2 ended. If the timeframe remains consistent, fans can expect season 4 to air at the end of 2027. The franchise's second movie will come out in the same year, following the success of Spy x Family's first outing in cinemas, Spy x Family Code: White, released in 2023.

Spy x Family is a deceptively lighthearted story set in a fictional country resembling Cold War-era East Germany. The two countries of Ostania and Westalis maintain a fragile peace after years of conflict. Westalis' intelligence agency, WISE, sends its top agent, codename Twilight, to Ostania's capital, Berlint, to spy on Donovan Desmond, the country's top politician and former prime minister during the war years. To do so, Twilight has to build a fake family and use his "daughter" to approach Desmond's son in the prestigious Eden Academy. However, Twilight, now posing as esteemed doctor Loid Forger, doesn't know that the child he adopts from an orphanage, Anya, is secretly a telepath, and the woman he convinces to take the role of his wife is Ostania's best assassin, the "Thorn Princess" Yor Briar.

The series' comedy stems from three people living together while hiding their true identities. Airheaded Anya is one of the most adorable characters in anime, while Loid and Yor's marriage of convenience is slowly (too slowly!) developing into a real romance. Seeing this dynamic built on lies grow into a real found family is truly heartwarming, but Spy x Family hides a much more serious side. The themes of war and violence are never treated dismissively, and the series has delivered some of the best reflections on the topic seen in the medium.

One of the most important shonen series ever could return soon

An anime series coproduced by Wit Studio and Cloverworks debuted in 2022 to critical acclaim. The two animation companies also produced the movie Spy x Family Code: White, an original story not adapted from the manga, which sees the Forger family going on an unexpected adventure in a northern town. The movie grossed $95.7 million worldwide, which isn't surprising, considering the series' popularity. The Spy x Family manga has sold over 42 million copies.

Spy x Family is the rare series that can provoke a whole range of emotions in its fans. Wit and Cloverworks have done a great job with the animated adaptations, and it's great to see that they will be coming back for season 4 and, most likely, for the new movie, too. Yasuhiro Akamatsu will replace Yukiko Imai as the season's director, while Aya Takaha will handle the series' composition, and Kazuaki Shimada and will return for character designs.
