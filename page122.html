54 Years Ago, Peanuts Went From Meh To Masterpiece With Just One Comic

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Volumes have been written about what makes Peanuts great. And rightly so. But what about the moment when Peanuts became great? That's a different story. What was the comic where things really clicked for Charles M. Schulz? Where Peanuts leveled up and officially became what it was destined to be? It all goes back to a small but significant milestone for the strip's unexpected breakout hit character: Snoopy.

The Peanuts Gang never grew up. They might have celebrated birthdays now and then, but they never got older. They never matured. They did, however, evolve, especially early in the strip's 50-year run. And no character evolved faster, or more significantly, than Snoopy. The black-and-white beagle started out as a canine companion, a scrappy sidekick for Charlie Brown. By 2000, when Peanuts ended, Snoopy was the star of the show, and arguably had the most developed personality out of the whole Gang.

Over time, Snoopy's internal monologue became a Peanuts staple. It became familiar to readers to watch him react in his head, via thought balloon, to something Charlie Brown, or Lucy, or Linus just said. Or to carry on entire, one-sided conversations with himself. Or to lapse into fantasy entirely, imagining himself as a World War I pilot or a famous writer. Snoopy's interior life became more fleshed out than many of Peanuts' more ancillary characters.

To get to that point, there had to be a first time Snoopy had a thought. Or otherwise, the first time the audience was privy to what Snoopy was thinking. It might have just been a fun wrinkle for Charles Schulz to throw into the mix at the time, but it wound up having a profound effect on Peanuts, helping seal its place in pop culture in perpetuity.

It is May 1952. Peanuts has been running for about a year and a half. It is still trying to get its syndication numbers up to 50 newspapers across the United States. The strip has steady momentum. Back in January, it started running extra-sized Sunday strips, a signal that publisher United Features Syndicate and the editors at the papers that bought Peanuts believed in it.

It is a milestone year creatively too. Lucy van Pelt debuts in March. Linus, her brother, first appears later that fall. The catchphrase "good grief" pops up for the first time. The infamous "football gag" starts before the end of the year. And Snoopy, most of all, becomes a main cast member. That starts in May, when his interiority is depicted for the first time.

Originally, Snoopy looked quite different from the droopy-eared, peanut-headed icon he's known as today, starring in a beautifully minimalistic strip.

Before considering the moment everything changed for Snoopy, it is worth putting his look under the microscope. In '52, the beagle is still barely more than a doodle of a dog. He hasn't taken on his more recognizable form yet. That's still a few years off. So, to get all philosophical for a moment, like Peanuts was occasionally prone to, existence preceded essence for Snoopy, but once he gained the essential component of an interior monologue, he was finally able to evolve into the beloved pop culture icon that has existed ever since.

May 27, 1952. It is a Tuesday. Readers in 40-45 newspapers, from Allentown, Pennsylvania to Seattle, Washington open up the funny pages to find this strip. In the first frame, Snoopy sits serenely on the lawn, minding his own business. In the second panel, along comes Charlie Brown, humming some tune to himself. In panel three, Charlie Brown playfully tugs on Snoopy's ear and says, "kind of warm out today for ear muffs, isn't it?"

It is a dumb joke. A childish joke. Which makes sense. It is made by a child. But it is playful, and innocent enough. It is a joke, but it isn't this Peanuts strip's punchline. Instead, it is the set-up for the punchline, which comes in the final frame. For readers at the time, in '52, it would have been a delightful surprise. Snoopy can speak like a human! Or at least, think like one.

"Why do I have to suffer such indignities?" Snoopy thinks, walking away in a huff. The dog's thought bubble is a squiggly line, perhaps to convey that Snoopy is rattled by the interaction, or maybe just to visually differentiate it from the human Peanuts characters' dialogue. Either way, that stylistic touch wouldn't last, but what would is the portrayal of Snoopy as not just thinking complex human thoughts, but having complex human emotions.

Sure, Snoopy would become a bit more Zen as time went on, but he also demonstrated a temper at times. In this gamechanging Peanuts comic, he's indignant at having his idyllic moment interrupted. The foundations of Snoopy's long-term character had already been laid by the time Charles M. Schulz decided to give him an internal voice; that decision allowed Schulz to build Snoopy up from there in ways that never would have been possible otherwise.

Within a few years of Snoopy's first thought, Peanuts exploded in popularity. The strip went from appearing in dozens of newspapers to hundreds. It got media attention, as a result of its exponential growth and developing fandom. Snoopy's voice, and Snoopy's personality, were arguably the missing pieces of the puzzle that pushed Peanuts from good to great, from "meh" to masterpiece.

Another big moment for Snoopy in '52 was the first time he danced, something that would become a key part of his character when the Peanuts Gang made the jump to TV in the 1960s. It was another step toward humanizing him as a character. In turn, it was another level of depth added to Peanuts, opening up a whole new world of jokes for the series to make. So, this was a pivotal moment for more than just furthering the strip's appeal to readers. It was also critical to the longevity of Peanuts.

The Peanuts have many classic comics that are amazing, but here are seven comic strips related to Snoopy that are genuine 10/10 masterpieces.

It might not have been immediately evident as a "level up" moment for Peanuts, but in retrospect, it is the moment that Charles M. Schulz figured up the missing ingredient to his series and compensated. It was when Schulz solidified his place, and his characters' place, in the pop culture canon forever.

Hackers inject credit card, crypto-stealing malware into 178 major websites

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers say attackers compromised scripts delivered through Optimizely, exposing visitors to malware designed to replace crypto wallet addresses and steal payment card details.

Major global brands including Ledger, Salesforce and BBC served cryptocurrency and credit card-stealing malware to website visitors on Monday, in a major attack exploiting website optimization platform Optimizely, according to security researchers.

Dubbed “Optimjack” by researcher Amirul Iman, the campaign delivered malicious code through scripts hosted by Optimizely. The malware can replace legitimate cryptocurrency wallet addresses with attacker-controlled alternatives when visitors copy addresses. It also captured credit card details entered into website forms, according to a separate analysis by cybersecurity firm StepSecurity.

The campaign affected website scripts belonging to 178 organizations, including hardware wallet Ledger, OANDA / Coinpass and trading platforms IG, Interactive Investor and TMS Brokers, according to Report URI’s investigation. Other affected brands included IKEA, Salesforce, Zoom, the BBC and CNN, websites that collectively attract millions of visitors a day.

BBC was among the first websites observed to serve the Optimizely injection at around 4:21 am UTC on Monday. By 9:52 pm UTC, most of the malicious code had been removed, with Ledger’s snippet cleaned at about 8:41 pm UTC.

Cointelegraph reached out to Ledger, Coinpass, and a number of other major businesses reported to be impacted by the malicious injection.

Student loan scams use pressure tactics and make false promises. Here’s what to watch out for.

Student loan scams can be costly, but they usually leave clues. Scammers will often try to hook victims with tempting claims of “act now or miss out” and “we promise to get you student aid."

The communication might seem realistic, but unexpected fees, unknown contacts and big promises are usually red flags. The age-old adage is true: if an offer sounds too good to be true, it probably is.

Here’s a breakdown of common student loan scams and what to do about them.

» MORE: How to spot student loan repayment scams

The scam: A fraudulent private company offers to manage your student loans and help you get them relieved or even fully forgiven. These services can cost more than $1,000, and there’s no guarantee that you’ll actually get the help you’re looking for.

How to avoid it: You can manage your federal student loans for free. According to the U.S. Department of Education, you never have to pay for help with your federal student loans[1]U.S. Department of Education. Avoiding Student Aid Scams. View all sources . You can work with your loan servicer to adjust your payment plan, consolidate loans and apply for loan forgiveness at no cost to you.

If you’re being promised immediate student loan forgiveness, are asked for personal identification information like your FAFSA login credentials or asked to authorize a power of attorney, proceed with caution — it’s likely a scam.

» MORE: How to get help with student loans

The scam: A company charges you an upfront fee to combine multiple student loans into one new loan to reduce your monthly payment. The company may be illegitimate and is likely to pocket the fee without actually consolidating your loans.

How to avoid it: You can consolidate federal student loans for free. There is no fee to consolidate your loans through the Department of Education at studentaid.gov. If you’re looking to reduce your monthly payment through consolidation, start by contacting your loan servicer. And if you get unsolicited communication from a company promising federal student loan consolidation, it's probably a scam.

The scam: Scammers use transitions in federal loan programs to take advantage of borrowers. For example, the Education Department was set to resume wage garnishments after a long pause, but in January 2026 announced a temporary delay implement repayment reforms.

When federal decisions go back and forth like this, it creates a gray area for scammers to make wage garnishment threats. For borrowers who may not know if wage garnishment restarted, it can be hard to know what to do. This goes for other federal changes, too; any uncertainty can be an opportunity for bad actors to take advantage of the confusion.

How to avoid it: If your wages are truly being garnished, the communication will come from the federal government and not a private company. Likewise, if a company offers to stop your wage garnishment for a fee, you’re probably getting scammed.

The scam: Criminals impersonate a real person and apply to colleges in their name. Then, they can collect financial aid, take out loans and use their college admission to net discounts on goods and services. This is known as a “ghost student” scam.

How to avoid it: Follow the steps on identitytheft.gov to report fraud and get a recovery plan. There are a few safeguards you can put in place to prevent this form of identity theft, such as setting up a credit freeze and monitoring your FSA ID activity.

How it works: You might receive a call from someone claiming to be from your school’s financial aid office saying you have unpaid tuition. The caller may threaten that you cannot start your classes until your tuition bill is paid in full. Scammers can be creative and convincing, even going so far as to replicate a school’s phone number to display on your caller ID.

How to avoid it: The Federal Trade Commission warns that schools will not call and pressure you to pay tuition[2]Federal Trade Commission. Got a call about unpaid college tuition? It could be a scam. Accessed Nov 16, 2026. View all sources . Rather than trust the caller, use your school's secure student portal to check your account balance. Know for certain what you owe and to whom before sending money. You can also call your financial aid office to confirm the legitimacy of a phone number that has called you requesting tuition funds.

Realizing you’ve been scammed is stressful, and it’s natural to feel overwhelmed or embarrassed. But acting quickly can make a real difference. The sooner you report what happened, the better your chances of limiting the financial damage and getting your accounts back on track. Here’s what to do:

If you’ve already paid a scammer, report the problem to the bank that issued the payment. If you paid with a credit card, for instance, contact the company and tell them what happened. They may be able to freeze your account or reverse the transaction.

Reach out to your federal student loan servicer as well and report what happened. They can check to make sure no changes have been made to your account and, in the case that changes did occur, they can help you get your loan back on track.

File complaints with the Education Department, the Federal Trade Commission and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Filing complaints with these agencies helps hold scammers accountable, can result in enforcement action or business shutdowns, and in some cases may help you recover lost funds.

8 War Movie Masterpieces Praised By Real-Life Veterans

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

War movies movies have been a consistent genre throughout the history of cinema. Hollywood has repeatedly tapped into the stories of real-life conflicts, showing dramatized versions of what really happened. These efforts came with great rewards, too, as countless war movies have been hugely successful at the worldwide box office.

Audiences typically look for a lot of things when choosing their next war movie to watch — action, drama between characters, and potentially world-defining stakes. Yet, there’s another element that typically comes into play, too, and that is what real-life veterans think of a given film. After all, getting their stamp of approval is mightily important to any war movie, as it shows that the story they told is (for the most part) accurate to what really happened.

Some war movies have been praised for their portrayal of the conflict at hand. Andrew Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge, which tells the story of a soldier who, due to his strong religious beliefs, opts not to carry a weapon of any kind during battle, is one war film that has been praised repeatedly. These accolades have come not only from historians who have spent their entire careers studying the conflict, but veterans who were actually there, too.

War films praised by veterans aren’t just limited to World War II, either. Movies that have centered on more recent conflicts, such as the wars in the Middle East, have also been lauded by those who served for their accuracy of their portrayal of history. Regardless, war movies remain an incredibly popular subgenre in Hollywood today, and if they’re praised by real-life veterans, then chances go up that it’s an excellent film as well.

Andrew Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge is one war movie that has consistently been praised by real-life veterans. As stated, the film tells the story of Desmond Doss, a Seventh-Day Adventist who objected to conflict of any kind. However, when drafted into the United States military, he was forced to either forgo his religion or risk criminal punishment for not fighting.

Miraculously, Doss found a solution to this problem — not carrying a weapon at all. During combat, such as at the Battle of Okinawa (he later received a Medal of Honor for his actions during the battle), Doss had no weapons on him. He had no rifle, no pistol, and nothing that could cause harm to another human being, as Doss was adamant that he wouldn’t hurt anyone. Instead, he served as a medic, thus saving the lives of those on the battlefield, and earning a reputation as one of the bravest soldiers in history in the process.

When Hacksaw Ridge released in theaters in 2016, the question naturally arose about how much of the film’s story was accurate to real events. After all, while some war movies are based in realism, others also take plenty of story liberties. Garfield’s Hacksaw Ridge wasn’t one of these films, however, and has instead been praised by several veterans.

For example, the film’s director, Mel Gibson, showed the finished product at the Disabled American Veterans national convention in Atlanta, Georgia. The film received great applause upon its conclusion, with many veterans praising its accuracy. Dan Clare, an Iraqi war veteran, noted that Hacksaw Ridge was a surprise hit with the crowd.

“These were people who avoid war movies because they fear they will either be unrealistic or that they will trigger post-traumatic stress. To their surprise, most people felt that it was a realistic depiction of combat — one of the most accurate ever — and, by and large, they appreciated the way it portrayed PTSD. Bottom line? Unlike most Hollywood movies, this one felt like it represented us.”

Ridley Scott’s 2001 film Black Hawk Down tells the story of two Black Hawk helicopters that were shot down during the Battle of Mogadishu. Once again, this film was praised for its depiction of the battle that its based on, with many veterans who fought during this conflict noting the film’s accuracy. Not only that, but one veteran stated that “90–95% of the film” is accurate, a high number that shows just how much attention to detail went into this movie.

Another more recent title, The Outpost is based on a non-fiction book by newscaster Jake Tapper. The film tells the story of the Battle of Kamdesh and the United States soldiers stationed at the Combat Outpost (COP) Keating in Afghanistan. A fatal design flaw of the base meant that the soldiers were vulnerable to ambush from the surrounding mountains, and the film then shows their desperate fight to survive.

One of the things that makes The Outpost such a unique film is that it not only received praise from veterans, but was actually worked on by veterans, too. Director Rod Lurie is a graduate of West Point and served in the U.S. Army, and not only that, but the film also recruited several survivors from the actual events to serve as advisers on the story. This helps to make The Outpost feel raw, lived in, and true to real-life events.

Another recent film with plenty of veteran involvement is Warfare, which tells the story of an encounter that a group of Marines had shortly after the Battle of Ramadi in 2006. Interestingly, the film is told in real time, with each second passing at the same rate for both the audience and the characters. Not only that, but the script was also based solely on personal accounts that survivors gave after the attack occurred.

These methods were praised by veterans, including survivors, for adding to the film’s overall authenticity. In particular, veterans praised the “smaller moments” for depicting the quiet moments of battle, which many war films skip over. The performances of the cast were also praised, as actors like Joseph Quinn and Will Poulter did extensive research to make their portrayals as accurate as possible.

One of the earliest films on this list is The Best Years of Our Lives, which released just one year after the conclusion of World War II, in 1946. The film tells the story of three United States soldiers who return to their small towns after serving, but find the transition back to civilian life especially difficult. For example, Al Stephenson (played by Frederic March) struggles with alcoholism upon his return and, at the same time, also has a very difficult job at a bank, in which he is given the task of approving or denying loans for fellow veterans.

The Best Years of Our Lives is timeless in this regard. The film may focus on World War II veterans, but it can just as easily translate to virtually every conflict in modern human history, with soldiers struggling to find their footing after returning home. The realistic-ness of this portrayal was praised by veterans upon its release, many of whom felt seen by the film. Amazingly, many of the film’s actors were veterans themselves, including Harold Russell, who, just like his character, lost both his hands during the war.

The head of Veterans Affairs at the time, General Omar Bradley, praised this film upon its release. In fact, he was such a fan of the movie that he organized a showing for VA headquarters staff, and later went on to write to the film’s producer to express his appreciation.

Steven Spielberg has directed so many iconic films in his career, and Saving Private Ryan is right up there among the very best. The film, which released in 1998, tells the story of a battalion of U.S. soldiers who set out on a mission to rescue Private James Francis Ryan (after his brothers had already tragically been lost in battle). Tom Hanks plays Captain John Miller, the leader of the rescue mission, and Matt Damon takes on the role of Private Ryan.

Many World War II veterans, some of whom actually fought in the specific battles that Saving Private Ryan depicts, praised Spielberg’s film. D-Day veterans noted that the Omaha Beach sequence captured the true violence of the war, with paratrooper Jack Evans saying: “A lot of people will see this and think it's gruesome, but that's the way it was. There were a lot of times it was a lot worse."

One of the most recent films on this list, Atonement tells the true story of a deadly battle during the Iraq War and its longstanding aftermath. Starring Kenneth Branagh and Boyd Holbrook, Atonement begins with an extended sequence showing American soldiers patrolling the streets of a populated Iraqi village. They hear gunfire, however, and this quickly sends them into action.

On the civilian side of things, an Iraqi family similarly hears the gunfire and decides to make a run for it and escape to their hometown. What happens, though, ends up being a tragedy in every sense of the word, as the family is caught in the crossfire between Iraqi militants and the U.S. Army. Their attempts to inform the latter that they aren’t a threat prove to be fatal, however, as several of the family members are killed by U.S. soldiers, who mistakenly think of them as a threat.

Atonement is based directly not just on real events, but on real people, too. The Khachaturian family were the ones impacted by this tragedy, and Boyd Holbrook’s Lou D'Alessandro was (one of) the real soldiers in question. Meanwhile, Kenneth Branagh’s Michael Reid was the journalist who covered the events (and who facilitated a meeting between both parties years later).

When the film premiered at the Toronto International Film Festival in September, director Reed Van Dyk stated that he wanted to honor those in this story by depicting the events as accurately as possible. During the Q&A after the film’s premiere, Van Dyk noted that he and his team had received the stamp of approval from veterans who served during the War in Iraq, with particular praise going to the intensity of the combat sequence that then sets up the rest of the movie.

The 1993 film Gettysburg tells the story of the Battle of Gettysburg during the American Civil War in the mid-1800s. The film, which is written and directed by Ronald F. Maxwell, was originally filmed as a miniseries but was released for a limited run in theaters after positive reception. Because of this, it remains the longest film released in theaters in U.S. history.

Because of how long ago this war was, no direct veterans were around to see this movie. However, veterans of future conflicts noted that Gettysburg has a surprising commitment to depicting all aspects of war. Not only that, but historians praised Maxwell for including real battlefield formations, re-enactors, and for filming near the real locations where key battles took place.
