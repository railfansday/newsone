North Korean hackers create memecoins to launder funds from Binance hack: Elliptic

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The blockchain firm traced stolen funds through six new tokens on NEAR, where hackers used liquidity pools to obscure transfers between wallets.

North Korean hackers created memecoins to launder cryptocurrency stolen in the $6.4 billion Binance hack, routing roughly $430,000 through six newly launched tokens on the NEAR blockchain, according to a report from blockchain analytics firm Elliptic.

Between Nov. 5 and 7, the hackers created at least six tokens on NEAR and used liquidity pools on decentralized exchanges to move stolen funds between wallets, Elliptic said. The tokens attracted almost no outside trading and were abandoned shortly after launch.

The hackers used one set of wallets to swap stolen NEAR tokens for the new memecoins, then used another set to sell those memecoins back into the pools for NEAR. The swaps let them move funds without direct wallet-to-wallet transfers, though the transactions remained traceable onchain.

How stolen NEAR moved between wallets through liquidity pools. Source: Elliptic

Elliptic also found that five of the six token creators were funded with proceeds from earlier token sales just two to 13 minutes before the next launch, linking the tokens in a chain of transactions.

“North Korea’s launderers rarely rely on a single technique,” Elliptic co-founder Tom Robinson wrote in the report. “Instead, they layer many cheap techniques, each adding a little more delay.”

Elliptic said the same laundering technique was also used following the $1.5 billion Bybit hack in 2025, when North Korean-linked actors moved at least $24 million through a newly created token on the TRON blockchain.

At the time of Elliptic’s analysis, about 11.8 million NEAR stolen in the Binance hack remained unmoved. Elliptic valued those funds at $43.7 million.

Amazon has refreshed its Kindle lineup for 2026, introducing new versions of the popular Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition. Both e-readers have notable design updates, but if you already own a previous model, you might wonder whether it's worth upgrading.

The short answer? It depends on which Kindle you're using. Compared to the 2024 models, the latest Paperwhites offer a sleeker design, updated display technology, and some welcome improvements to everyday usability. The Signature Edition, in particular, gets a few extras that make it feel more like a premium device. Here's what's changed and what's stayed the same.

Amazon Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition

The most noticeable upgrade is the design and appearance. Amazon has made the standard Paperwhite thinner and slightly lighter, reducing its thickness from 7.8 millimeters to 6.8 millimeters. It also weighs approximately 204 grams, compared to the previous model's 211 grams. That might not sound like much, but a slimmer e-reader is always welcome when you're holding it for hours or slipping it into a bag.

The redesigned display construction also creates a more seamless, edge-to-edge look. Amazon uses a new display stack with an oxide thin-film transistor backplane, designed to deliver excellent contrast, deeper blacks, and crisp text. The screen is still 7 inches with a 300-ppi resolution, so don't expect a dramatic jump in sharpness over the 2024 model.

Another small but genuinely useful change is the power button's placement. Instead of sitting along the bottom edge, where it was easy to press accidentally, it's now positioned on the upper-right side.

And if battery life is your biggest concern, there's good news: Amazon still promises up to 12 weeks on a single charge, just like the previous generation. The new model also maintains its IPX8 waterproof rating and adjustable warm lighting.

The Signature Edition receives the same thinner design and updated screen construction, but Amazon has made a few additional changes to distinguish it from the standard Paperwhite.

For starters, it now has an aluminum back instead of the previous model's plastic construction, giving it a more premium look and feel. That upgrade comes with a trade-off, though: At approximately 229 grams, the new Signature Edition is actually a little heavier than its 214-gram predecessor.

The new model also introduces pogo-pin connectors for charging and accessories. These let the Kindle connect to Amazon's new charging stand and a magnetic Page-Turn Cover with physical buttons, which could appeal to readers who miss the page-turn buttons on older e-readers.

One important difference is that the 2024 Signature Edition supported conventional Qi wireless charging, while the 2026 model switches to the new contact-based pogo-pin system.

Amazon also highlights a double-tap feature that lets you advance pages by tapping the side or back of the device, making one-handed reading a little easier.

Otherwise, you'll still get 32GB of storage, an auto-adjusting front light, and up to 12 weeks of battery life. Both generations are waterproof, too.

If you're shopping for a new Kindle rather than replacing an older one, the decision mostly comes down to whether the Signature Edition's premium features are worth an additional $50.

The standard Paperwhite starts at $199.99 and includes 16GB of storage, adjustable warm lighting, and a 7-inch glare-free display.

For $249.99, the Signature Edition doubles the storage to 32GB, adds an aluminum back and automatic brightness adjustment, and supports the new pogo-pin charging accessories. It's also available in Seaglass Green, whereas the regular Paperwhite comes in Graphite.

Both deliver essentially the same core reading experience, so casual readers probably won't miss much by sticking with the less expensive version.

If you already own a 2024 Kindle Paperwhite or Signature Edition and it's working perfectly fine, you probably don't need to replace it. You're still getting the same screen size and resolution, excellent battery life, waterproofing, and access to Amazon's massive Kindle library.

However, if you're upgrading from an older 2021 Paperwhite—or you're shopping for your first e-reader—the 2026 models are worth considering. The thinner design, improved screen construction, and updated controls make the latest lineup more appealing.

For most readers, the regular Paperwhite will be more than enough. But if you read every day, want the premium aluminum design, or like the idea of a case with physical page-turn buttons, the Signature Edition is a worthwhile step up.

A weakened immune system can let the contagious window run well past the typical range for healthy adults.

The term "weakened immune system" covers a broad group. It includes people whose immune defenses are reduced by an illness. It also includes people whose defenses are lowered by medical treatment. Severely immunocompromised patients sit at the far end of that spectrum. They are the group in which shedding can last the longest.

That range does not set a fixed end date for any one patient. Two people with weakened immunity can follow very different timelines. Each person's contagious period depends on their own condition and care. A clinician who knows the case can advise on how long to keep apart from others.

The longer window carries the most weight for the people around the patient. Household members and caregivers can face exposure for far longer than they would with a healthy adult who has the flu. The same applies to anyone who shares a care facility with the patient.

The CDC advises people with the flu to stay home until they have been fever-free for at least 24 hours without the help of fever-reducing medicine. That threshold is the benchmark for deciding when to go back to work after the flu.

The guidance applies to schools as well as workplaces. The same 24-hour benchmark covers a child heading back to class and an adult heading back to an office. Families, teachers and employers can all work from one standard.

The medication condition carries as much weight as the time limit. Acetaminophen and ibuprofen lower body temperature. They can make a sick person feel better and produce a normal thermometer reading while the infection is still active. A normal reading taken a few hours after a dose reflects the medicine, not recovery.

In practice, the 24-hour count starts only when a fever stays down on its own. A normal afternoon reading after a morning dose of ibuprofen does not count. The clock begins once the medicine has worn off and the temperature remains normal without another dose. If the fever comes back, the person has not yet gone a full day without one, and the count starts again.

The phrase "at least" matters as well. The 24-hour mark is a minimum, not a deadline. Nothing in the guidance requires a person to return the moment the threshold passes. Someone who still feels too weak to work can stay home longer.

Fever works as a marker because it is concrete. It is a sign that the immune system is still responding to an infection. Anyone can check it at home with an ordinary thermometer. That makes it a more consistent test than judging whether a person looks or sounds well enough to be around others.

For workplaces and classrooms, the rule turns a judgment call into one measurable condition. That condition is a full day without fever and without the medicine that can hide it.

Flu spreads mainly through droplets that an infected person releases when they cough, sneeze or talk. Contaminated surfaces can also pass the virus along, but they play a smaller role than droplets moving directly from one person to another.

These droplets come from the respiratory tract, where influenza viruses multiply. A cough or sneeze expels them with force. Ordinary conversation releases them too. A sick person does not need to be coughing hard to put virus into the air around them.

Infection begins when those droplets land in the mouth or nose of someone nearby. Some can also be breathed in. Influenza infects the nose, throat and lungs. The face is the entry point the virus needs to reach.

Close and prolonged contact raises the chance of transmission. Shared offices, classrooms, public transit and family homes keep people within range of one another's coughs and conversations for hours at a time. In a poorly ventilated room, respiratory particles linger longer than they would outdoors or in a space with fresh air moving through it.

The surface route depends on hands. An infected person coughs into a palm or sneezes near a desk. Virus then ends up on a doorknob, phone, keyboard, light switch or shared utensil. Someone else touches that object and then touches their mouth, nose or eyes. The virus reaches the same entry points a droplet would, by a slower and less direct path.

How long flu virus remains infectious on an object depends on the material and the surrounding conditions, including temperature and humidity. Hard, nonporous surfaces such as steel and plastic generally hold infectious virus longer than fabric or paper.

Influenza is an enveloped virus. A fatty outer layer surrounds it. Soap and common household disinfectants break that layer down, which inactivates the virus.

Droplets carry most of the risk. The people most likely to catch the flu are those who share air and space with a sick person. Surfaces add a secondary path, one that runs through hands rather than through the air.

Some people infected with influenza never develop symptoms, yet they can still pass the virus to others. An infection like this carries no warning sign. Neither the infected person nor the people nearby know to take precautions.

This differs from the short window of spread just before symptoms appear. A person in that window will soon feel sick. An asymptomatic infection never reaches the sick stage at all. The person can carry and spread the virus without ever learning they had the flu.

Ordinary speech is one way this happens. Flu travels mainly in droplets. Talking releases them just as coughing and sneezing do. A person with no cough and no sneeze can still pass the virus on during a conversation, a meeting or a shared meal.

Asymptomatic spread also slips past the usual signals people use to decide whether to stay home. The CDC's guidance for returning to work or school centers on fever. Someone who never runs a fever has no trigger to stay home under that rule. They can carry on with their normal routine at the office, on public transit and at home with family.

Without symptoms, these infections also have no visible start or finish. Nothing tells the person when they began carrying the virus or when they stopped. That leaves no practical way for them to time their own precautions.

The protective habits that matter here are the ones that do not depend on how anyone feels. An annual flu vaccine lowers the chance of being infected at all. Regular handwashing and good ventilation in shared indoor spaces also work without anyone needing to notice a symptom first.

Masking when ill offers less help in this case. A person with no symptoms has no reason to put on a mask.

In a household where one person already has the flu, those everyday steps also protect relatives who feel fine but may be carrying the virus without knowing it.

Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, sold under the brand name Tamiflu, can shorten a bout of flu. They work best when a patient starts them within 48 hours of the first symptoms.

These drugs act against the influenza virus itself. That sets them apart from over-the-counter remedies that only ease aches, fever or congestion.

The 48-hour window is counted from the onset of symptoms, not from a diagnosis or a doctor's appointment. Someone who wakes up with a fever and body aches has started the clock that morning. Waiting several days to see whether the illness passes on its own uses up the period in which the medicine works best. Once symptoms have run for two full days, that period is over.

Anyone who suspects the flu and wants to consider an antiviral should contact a doctor, nurse or other clinician early in the illness. A call in the first day of symptoms leaves time to decide on treatment inside the window. The clinician can then judge whether an antiviral fits that patient's case.

Shortening an illness is not the same as switching off contagiousness. Oseltamivir can make a flu episode shorter. It does not end a person's ability to spread the virus at a fixed point.

Starting a course of Tamiflu does not mean a patient stops being contagious after a set number of doses or days. That holds whether the drug was started on the first day of symptoms or the second. No antiviral comes with a built-in date when the risk to others ends.

That distinction matters for anyone deciding when to rejoin coworkers, classmates or family. A patient who feels better sooner on an antiviral may still be carrying the virus. The prescription's start date is not a reliable guide to when it is safe to be around other people.

A cough and tiredness can hang on for a week or more after a flu fever has broken. Those leftover symptoms do not necessarily mean a person is still as contagious as they were in the first days of illness.

Recovery from influenza tends to unfold in two stages. The fever, chills and body aches usually fade first. The cough and the low energy often take longer to clear.

That gap can confuse coworkers and family members. Someone still coughing at their desk may look sick to colleagues, even after the period of heaviest viral spread has passed. The cough on its own is a sign of healing airways rather than a reliable gauge of how much virus remains.

The lingering cough reflects the damage the infection does to the airways. Influenza attacks the cells lining the nose, throat and lungs. After the immune system brings the virus under control, that lining still needs time to repair. Irritated airways can stay sensitive in the meantime. A dry, nagging cough is a common result.

Fatigue follows a similar pattern. The immune response that clears influenza also leaves many people drained. Sleep broken up by fever and coughing fits adds to the deficit. Energy often comes back in stages rather than all at once.

Some changes during the recovery phase call for medical attention. A fever or cough that improves and then returns or worsens can signal a complication. Bacterial pneumonia, sinus infections and ear infections are among the complications that can follow the flu.

Influenza can also make chronic conditions such as asthma and heart disease worse. For someone with asthma, a cough that grows harder to control during recovery is a reason to call a clinician.

Difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, and ongoing dizziness or confusion warrant prompt care. Those signs can point to a new problem rather than an ordinary slow recovery.

Without those warning signs, a leftover cough and fatigue generally fade with time as the airways heal and strength returns.

Five habits lower the chance of passing influenza to others during recovery. They are an annual flu vaccine for the people around you, regular handwashing, covering coughs and sneezes, wearing a mask while sick and keeping indoor air moving.

Each habit targets a different link in the chain of transmission. Together they cover the hands, the air and the people a sick person is most likely to be near.

Vaccination is the one step that works before anyone else gets sick. A new flu vaccine is produced each year because the strains in circulation change from season to season. Last season's shot may not match the viruses going around now. Household members and coworkers who are vaccinated are less likely to catch the virus and carry it further. For a family caring for someone with the flu, an up-to-date shot for everyone else adds a practical layer of protection.

Handwashing deals with the virus that ends up on hands after coughing, sneezing or blowing the nose. Soap and water remove it from the skin. An alcohol-based hand sanitizer is a workable substitute when a sink is not nearby. The moments that matter most are after using a tissue, before preparing food and before touching shared items such as remote controls, door handles or phones.

Covering coughs and sneezes keeps droplets out of the air and away from the people nearby. A tissue that goes straight into the trash does the job. The inside of the elbow is the fallback when no tissue is at hand. Coughing into bare hands moves the virus onto everything those hands touch next.

A well-fitting mask adds another barrier. Wearing one while sick reduces the droplets released when talking, coughing or sneezing. It is most useful in shared spaces — a kitchen, a car or the waiting room at a clinic or pharmacy.

Ventilation matters because droplets and smaller particles linger in still indoor air. Opening a window, running an exhaust fan or using an air purifier with a HEPA filter keeps air moving. Moving air dilutes what a sick person breathes out. Small, closed rooms hold the heaviest concentration.

Beyond those five habits, distance inside the home also helps. Where space allows, a sick person can sleep in a separate room and use a separate bathroom. Cups, utensils and towels are best kept to one person until recovery. These steps carry the most weight around people at higher risk of serious flu complications. That group includes infants, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a chronic health condition.

Which flu shot should you get? It depends on age and health

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Types of flu shots differ by age, health and manufacturing, from standard-dose and cell-based vaccines to high-dose shots for seniors and the FluMist nasal spray

Picking a flu shot is less about brand than about birthday. Age drives most of the decision, followed by health status and whatever the local pharmacy or clinic has in stock. The names on the consent form — Fluzone, Flucelvax, Flublok, Fluad, FluMist — mostly describe different ways of building the same kind of protection.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends a flu vaccine every year for everyone six months and older, with a few exceptions. For most healthy people under 65, any licensed vaccine approved for their age is acceptable. CDC does not rank one of those options above another. The guidance shifts at 65. Since 2022, the agency's vaccine advisers have preferentially recommended certain products for that age group. It shifts again for young children, pregnant people and anyone weighing the nasal spray.

The shots themselves changed recently. Before the 2024-25 season, U.S. flu vaccines were quadrivalent, built to protect against four flu strains. Starting that season, they became trivalent, covering three strains, after the B/Yamagata component was removed. The specific strains are not fixed from year to year. Each season's composition is set anew.

The differences among the types of flu shots come down to three questions. How is the vaccine made? Who is it built for? And how does it enter the body? The traditional shot uses killed virus grown in chicken eggs. Flucelvax grows its vaccine virus in mammalian cell cultures instead. Flublok skips the virus entirely, relying on a lab-produced version of a flu protein called hemagglutinin. Fluzone High-Dose and Fluad are designed for adults 65 and older, whose immune systems respond less strongly to vaccines. FluMist is a live, weakened vaccine sprayed into the nose rather than injected.

The seven entries below are a selection of vaccine types and situations, not a ranking. The first six explain who each vaccine is licensed for, how it works and where its limits sit. The last entry covers three common questions. It explains what people with egg allergy can get, which shots suit pregnancy and why some young children need two doses in one season.

The standard-dose inactivated flu shot is the traditional injected vaccine, made from killed influenza virus grown in chicken eggs. Brands include Fluarix, FluLaval and Fluzone, and some of these products are licensed for children as young as six months.

This is the version most people picture when they think of a flu vaccine. Pharmacies, clinics and doctors' offices commonly stock it for a wide range of ages, from young children to working-age adults.

"Inactivated" means the virus in the vaccine has been killed. The shot contains no live virus. It cannot cause the flu in the person who receives it. Instead, it shows the immune system the surface proteins of the season's selected strains. The body learns to recognize those proteins and can respond faster if the real virus arrives.

Manufacturing starts with fertilized chicken eggs. Vaccine makers inject the selected flu viruses into the eggs and incubate them for several days while the viruses multiply. The virus-laden fluid is then harvested. The virus is killed and the antigen is purified before it goes into vials and syringes. This egg-based process has been the main way to make flu vaccine for decades. Methods using cell cultures or lab-made proteins arrived later.

The dose is measured by hemagglutinin, the protein on the virus surface that the immune system targets. A standard-dose shot contains 15 micrograms of hemagglutinin for each strain it covers. Shots built for older adults change that formula, either with more antigen or with an added ingredient.

In the U.S., the FDA selects the strains for each season's flu vaccines, drawing on recommendations from the World Health Organization. Individual brands do not choose their own strains.

The shot is given as an injection into a muscle, usually the upper arm for adults. Infants and young toddlers often receive it in the thigh. Common side effects include soreness, redness or swelling where the needle went in. Some people also have a low fever, headache or muscle aches that pass within a few days.

Age eligibility differs from brand to brand. Some standard-dose products are licensed from six months, while others start at older ages. The pharmacist or the product label confirms which brand fits a given person.

For most healthy people under 65, a standard-dose shot is an acceptable option. Adults 65 and older have separate preferred options from CDC's vaccine advisers. A standard-dose shot remains a fallback for that age group when none of those is available.

Flucelvax is a flu vaccine whose virus is grown in mammalian cell cultures instead of chicken eggs. It is approved in the U.S. for people six months and older. That matches the full age range of CDC's yearly flu vaccine recommendation.

The difference lies in one production step. Every flu vaccine starts with virus strains chosen for the coming season. Makers of most flu shots then grow large amounts of that virus. For traditional shots, that growth happens in eggs. For Flucelvax, it happens in mammalian cells kept in culture.

That choice addresses a known issue with egg-based production. A flu virus grown in eggs can pick up small changes as it adapts. Those changes can leave the vaccine virus slightly different from the virus selected for the vaccine. Avoiding that step is one reason cell-based manufacturing exists.

That rationale is about how the vaccine is made. It is not a finding that Flucelvax protects people better overall. CDC does not rank Flucelvax above other licensed flu vaccines for healthy people under 65.

The cells used for Flucelvax come from a line called Madin-Darby canine kidney, or MDCK, cells. That line was originally derived from a dog's kidney. Cell-based production also does not depend on a large supply of chicken eggs.

The FDA approved Flucelvax in 2012. It was the first U.S. seasonal flu vaccine made using cell culture technology. Its first approval covered adults only. Later expansions brought the age range down to six months. CSL Seqirus makes the vaccine.

The broad age approval means a parent and a baby can receive the same product. Whether a particular pharmacy or clinic stocks it depends on location.

Flucelvax is not among the shots singled out for older adults. In 2022, CDC's vaccine advisers preferentially recommended high-dose, adjuvanted or recombinant vaccines for adults 65 and older. An older adult can still receive Flucelvax when none of those preferred shots is available.

Flublok is a recombinant flu vaccine, which means it is made without the flu virus itself and without chicken eggs. It contains a laboratory-produced version of hemagglutinin, a protein found on the surface of the influenza virus.

Hemagglutinin is the part of the virus the immune system learns to recognize after vaccination. It sits on the outside of the flu virus and helps the virus attach to the cells it infects. Antibodies that recognize this protein can help block that step. A shot that delivers the protein alone gives the body that target without exposing it to any virus.

The other U.S. flu vaccines all begin by growing influenza virus. Flublok skips that stage. Its hemagglutinin is made in insect cells that carry the genetic instructions for the protein. The protein is then purified for the vaccine rather than harvested from a grown virus. The finished shot contains no live or killed virus.

Flublok was first licensed for adults only. In 2025, the FDA extended its approval to people aged nine and older. That expansion is recent. Children younger than nine need a different licensed vaccine that covers their age. Several injected options, including Flucelvax, are approved from six months.

For adults 65 and older, Flublok is one of three vaccine types that CDC's advisory committee, ACIP, preferentially recommended in 2022. The other two are the high-dose and adjuvanted shots. That makes Flublok the only one of the three preferred types that is also licensed for people under 65.

Flublok is also an option during pregnancy. CDC advises pregnant people to get an injected inactivated or recombinant shot rather than the live nasal spray. Flublok, as a non-live recombinant vaccine, fits that guidance.

For healthy people under 65 who fall within its approved age range, CDC does not rank Flublok above other licensed, age-appropriate flu vaccines.

Fluzone High-Dose is a flu shot licensed only for people 65 and older. Each dose carries four times the antigen of a standard-dose shot: 60 micrograms of hemagglutinin per strain, compared with 15.

Hemagglutinin is the protein on the surface of the flu virus that the immune system learns to recognize. A flu vaccine presents this protein to the body, which then builds antibodies before a real infection arrives. The amount in each dose helps set how strong a signal the vaccine sends.

The higher dose exists because immune systems change with age. Older adults respond less strongly to vaccines than younger people do. A standard dose can prompt a weaker antibody response in a 75-year-old than in a 30-year-old. Fluzone High-Dose aims to close part of that gap by giving the immune system more of the target protein to react to.

Across the three strains in the current trivalent formula, a high-dose shot holds 180 micrograms of hemagglutinin in total. A standard-dose trivalent shot holds 45 micrograms.

The age limit is firm. Adults under 65 choose from the shots licensed for their own age group instead. The extra antigen targets the weaker vaccine response seen later in life, not a general need for a bigger dose.

The product shares the Fluzone brand name with a standard-dose shot. At a pharmacy or clinic, the "High-Dose" label is the part that identifies the version built for older adults.

Fluzone High-Dose is one of three vaccine types that CDC's advisory committee, ACIP, preferentially recommended in 2022 for adults 65 and older. The other two are the adjuvanted shot Fluad and the recombinant shot Flublok. The recommendation does not favor one of the three over another.

The strain composition of the high-dose shot, like that of every other flu vaccine, is set anew for each season. A dose from a previous year does not substitute for the current season's shot.

Fluad is a flu vaccine for adults 65 and older that adds an ingredient called MF59 to strengthen the body's immune response. MF59 is an adjuvant, an oil-in-water emulsion mixed into the shot alongside the flu antigen.

An adjuvant works differently from simply putting more antigen in a dose. The antigen is the part of the vaccine the immune system learns to recognize. In flu vaccines, that is mainly the hemagglutinin protein on the virus surface. An adjuvant does not add more of that target. It helps provoke a stronger reaction to the antigen that is already there.

That distinction separates Fluad from Fluzone High-Dose, another shot built for older adults. The high-dose vaccine raises the amount of antigen per strain. Fluad keeps a standard amount of antigen and relies on the adjuvant to do more of the work. Both designs address the same problem: the body's response to vaccines weakens with age.

Adjuvants are not unique to flu shots. Aluminum salts have long served as adjuvants in other routine vaccines. MF59 is built around squalene, an oil the human body also produces.

The FDA first licensed Fluad in the U.S. in 2015. It was the first seasonal flu vaccine containing an adjuvant to win approval in the country.

Fluad is an inactivated vaccine. It contains no live virus and cannot cause the flu. It is given as an injection into the muscle, usually in the upper arm.

In 2022, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, which advises CDC, preferentially recommended three kinds of flu vaccine for adults 65 and older. Those were high-dose, adjuvanted and recombinant vaccines. Fluad is the adjuvanted option in that group. The committee did not rank the three against one another. An older adult can receive whichever of them is on hand.

The preference does not mean older adults should go unvaccinated when a preferred shot is out of stock. If none of the three is available, CDC guidance says any age-appropriate flu vaccine should be used instead.

Younger adults and children are not eligible for Fluad. They choose from other licensed flu vaccines suited to their age.

FluMist is a needle-free flu vaccine sprayed into the nose, and it is approved for people aged two through 49. It contains live attenuated influenza viruses. These viruses are weakened so they do not cause flu illness in healthy recipients.

That design sets the spray apart from every injected option. Flu shots contain inactivated virus or a single lab-made flu protein. The nasal spray instead uses viruses adapted to grow at the cooler temperatures inside the nose. They can replicate there, but they grow poorly in the warmer lungs.

AstraZeneca $AZN makes the spray. Each dose is split between the two nostrils, with half sprayed into each side.

The age window is firm. FluMist is not licensed for babies and toddlers under two. It is not licensed for adults 50 and older either. Both groups need an injected vaccine instead.

Some people inside that age range should also skip the spray. It is not recommended for pregnant people. It is also not advised for people with weakened immune systems. The vaccine contains live virus, which is the reason for that caution.

CDC lists other conditions that rule it out. Children and teens taking aspirin or other salicylate medicines should not get it. Children aged two to four who have asthma, or who have wheezed in the past 12 months, should get a shot instead. People who recently took flu antiviral drugs should check with a clinician first. Those medicines can stop the vaccine virus from replicating.

The spray has a history of shifting guidance. CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices advised against using it in the 2016-17 and 2017-18 seasons. That decision followed weak performance against one influenza A strain. The committee restored it as an option for the 2018-19 season.

The newest change concerns who gives the dose. In 2024, the FDA approved FluMist for self-administration or for administration by a caregiver. That approval has made home delivery possible. Eligible people can get a flu vaccine without visiting a pharmacy or clinic.

For healthy people in the eligible age range, CDC treats the nasal spray as one acceptable option alongside age-appropriate shots. The agency does not rank the spray above or below them.

People with an egg allergy can receive any licensed flu vaccine that suits their age. CDC says they need no special precautions beyond those that apply to any vaccination.

That guidance covers egg-based shots as well. Standard-dose brands such as Fluarix, FluLaval and Fluzone are produced with chicken eggs. Under CDC guidance, an egg allergy does not rule them out.

Some people with an egg allergy still prefer a vaccine made without eggs. Two licensed options meet that preference. Flucelvax is approved for people six months and older. Flublok was first licensed for adults, and the FDA extended it to people aged nine and older in 2025. Choosing either one is a personal preference, not a requirement under CDC guidance.

Pregnant people should get an injected flu vaccine. CDC guidance points to either an inactivated shot or a recombinant shot during pregnancy. In practice, that includes standard-dose brands, the cell-based Flucelvax and the recombinant Flublok.

The FluMist nasal spray is not recommended during pregnancy. It contains live virus, unlike the injected options. Fluzone High-Dose and Fluad do not apply here either. Both are licensed only for adults 65 and older.

Young children can need more than one flu vaccine dose in a season. Children aged six months through eight years who have not previously received enough flu vaccine doses need two doses that season. CDC says those two doses should be given at least four weeks apart.

Children in that age group who already have enough prior doses need only one dose for the season. From age nine, one dose per season is the rule regardless of history. Parents unsure of a child's vaccine history can check it with the child's health care provider.

10 flu warning signs that mean it's time to see a doctor

These flu warning signs, drawn from CDC guidance for adults and children, show when flu symptoms call for a doctor or emergency care rather than rest at home

For most healthy people, the flu is an illness to ride out at home. The harder part is spotting the moment when rest and fluids are no longer enough.

Influenza usually arrives fast. The typical pattern is a sudden fever, cough, sore throat, body aches and fatigue. Recovery usually takes about two weeks. In some patients, the illness takes a more serious course and leads to complications such as pneumonia.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publishes a list of emergency warning signs for exactly that moment. The CDC states that these symptoms can signal complications and need urgent medical care. Some signs apply to adults, some to children and several to both. A few look different depending on age. Breathing trouble in a child, for example, can show up as fast breathing or ribs that pull in with each breath. Other signs, such as bluish lips or face and any fever in a baby younger than 12 weeks, appear only on the child list. The guidance also covers changes in alertness, hydration and fever patterns.

Timing also matters for treatment. Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, sold under the brand name Tamiflu, work best when started within 48 hours of the first symptoms. A doctor decides whether a prescription is appropriate.

Some people face higher odds of serious illness from the start. The CDC's higher-risk groups include adults 65 and older and children under five, especially those under two. Pregnant people and those up to two weeks postpartum are also on the list. So are people with chronic conditions such as asthma, heart disease or diabetes, and people with weakened immune systems.

The 10 flu warning signs below are a selection from the CDC's guidance for adults and children. Each slide explains what the sign looks like, who it applies to and what to do next. None of these signs is a diagnosis on its own. Each one signals that a patient needs professional medical attention.

If any of these signs appear, call a doctor right away. When symptoms are severe, call emergency services.

Shortness of breath or difficulty breathing while sick with the flu is a reason to get emergency medical care, not to keep resting at home. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it among its emergency warning signs of flu in adults.

This sign is separate from the ordinary symptoms of influenza. A cough, a sore throat and a stuffy nose are common with flu and usually ease as the illness runs its course. Shortness of breath is different. It describes a struggle to get enough air, even while sitting or lying still.

In children, the CDC describes the warning sign in slightly different terms. It lists fast breathing or trouble breathing as an emergency sign. It also lists a pattern in which the ribs pull in with each breath.

That rib movement is often called retractions. The skin between or just below the ribs sinks inward as the child inhales. It happens when the chest muscles work harder than normal to pull air into the lungs.

The CDC's wording for children centers on what an adult can observe: the speed of the breathing and the movement of the chest. A young child may not be able to describe feeling short of breath.

Breathing trouble during flu can point to pneumonia or other lung involvement. Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs in one or both lungs. Those sacs can fill with fluid or pus. That makes it harder for oxygen to reach the bloodstream.

No single breathing change confirms a specific diagnosis. A doctor can assess the cause, often by listening to the lungs. Where needed, the doctor may order tests such as a chest X $TWTR-ray or a pulse oximeter reading of blood oxygen.

The CDC applies this warning to adults and children alike, with the wording adjusted for age. In both groups, the recommended response is the same. Get emergency medical care right away rather than waiting to see whether breathing improves on its own.

A child with flu whose lips or face turn bluish needs emergency care right away. Call emergency services or go to an emergency room rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

The CDC lists bluish lips or face as an emergency warning sign of flu in children. The agency's list of emergency warning signs for adults does not name it as a separate item. For parents and caregivers, that makes it a child-specific sign to watch for during a flu illness.

The bluish tint reflects low oxygen in the blood. When the body is not getting enough oxygen, the lips and skin can take on a blue or grayish cast. The medical term for this discoloration is cyanosis.

Flu is a respiratory illness. It infects the nose and throat, and it can also reach the lungs. When breathing is affected, less oxygen may reach the bloodstream. A color change in the face or lips is one outward sign of that problem.

Where the color appears matters. Central cyanosis shows up in the lips, the tongue and the inside of the mouth. Peripheral cyanosis affects the hands and feet. Blue fingertips or toes can come from cold or from reduced circulation to the limbs. A blue tint in the lips or tongue is more closely tied to low oxygen in the blood.

The change can show up around the mouth, on the lips or across the face. On darker skin, a bluish tint can be harder to see. Looking at the lips, gums and tongue can make the change easier to notice.

A bluish color is a reason to act, not a diagnosis. It does not on its own say what is causing the low oxygen. Finding the cause is the job of the medical team that examines the child.

Parents do not need to wait for other symptoms to appear before acting. The color change on its own is reason enough to get emergency help.

Pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that persists while someone has the flu calls for emergency care right away. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it as an emergency warning sign of flu in adults.

The key word in the CDC's description is persistent. Body aches are a common flu symptom and part of a typical illness. This warning sign is different. It refers to pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that stays rather than easing.

The adult sign covers two parts of the body. Pain or pressure in either the chest or the abdomen fits the description. Symptoms do not need to appear in both places.

Some people with flu also have vomiting and diarrhea. The CDC notes that these stomach symptoms are more common in children than in adults. A brief upset stomach of that kind is not the same as abdominal pain or pressure that will not go away.

The CDC keeps a separate list of emergency signs for children. Chest pain appears on that list. The abdomen is named only in the adult wording.

Flu can affect organs beyond the airways, including the heart. The CDC names myocarditis, an inflammation of the heart muscle, among the serious complications flu can cause.

No single episode of chest pain points to one cause on its own. Chest discomfort can arise from the lungs, the heart, the chest wall or other structures. A clinician has to examine the patient to tell them apart.

The CDC places this sign in its emergency category, not among symptoms to manage at home. The appropriate response is to call emergency services or go to an emergency department. Waiting to see whether the pain fades is not part of the guidance.

The CDC notes that its warning-sign lists are not all-inclusive. The agency advises contacting a medical provider about any other symptom that is severe or concerning.

Sudden dizziness, confusion or being unable to wake up are emergency warning signs of flu in adults, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Anyone showing these signs needs medical care right away. That means calling emergency services or going to an emergency room.

These signs concern how alert a person is and how clearly they think. That sets them apart from the cough, fever and body aches that most people with flu manage at home.

Fatigue is a normal part of flu, and many people sleep more than usual while sick. The CDC's warning sign describes something different. It refers to a person who cannot be woken, not someone who is simply tired or drowsy.

The CDC's adult sign pairs dizziness with confusion. Its wording describes dizziness that comes on suddenly during the illness.

A person who is confused may not recognize that anything is wrong. A family member, roommate or caregiver may be the one who notices the change and calls for help.

For children, the CDC describes this warning sign through behavior. Its list includes a child who does not wake up or who does not interact when awake. A child who is awake but does not respond to parents or others nearby fits that description.

The CDC also lists irritability as a warning sign in children once it crosses a specific line. That line is a child so irritable that they do not want to be held.

Young children often cannot explain how they feel. Changes in how a child responds to a parent's touch or voice are one way a caregiver can see that the illness has become more serious.

The CDC does not tie these signs to a single cause. No one of them confirms a specific diagnosis. Its guidance treats sudden changes in alertness or behavior during flu, in adults or children, as reasons to get urgent medical attention.

A seizure during a bout of flu is an emergency warning sign that calls for emergency medical care right away. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists seizures among its emergency warning signs of flu in both adults and children.

The CDC keeps separate warning-sign lists for adults and for children. Several items on those lists take a different form depending on age. Seizures are one of the signs that appear on both lists.

The guidance attaches no duration or count to this sign. It does not say a seizure must last a set length of time before it counts. It also does not say a seizure must happen more than once. A single seizure during a flu illness fits the CDC's description.

The CDC groups seizures with its emergency signs. It does not place them among symptoms that can be managed at home. Anyone who sees a person with flu have a seizure should call emergency services.

The sign is defined by its timing. The CDC lists a seizure as a flu warning sign when it happens while the person is sick with flu.

An adult with the flu who stops urinating needs medical care right away, under emergency warning-sign guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). These signs can point to dehydration. The body is not taking in enough fluid to replace what it loses. Dehydration can become a complication of flu. It tends to develop when fever, poor eating and drinking, or vomiting continue without a break.

Fever plays a direct role. A raised body temperature increases the fluid lost through sweating and breathing. A sore throat, nausea or exhaustion can also lead a sick person to drink far less than usual. Vomiting adds to the problem by expelling fluid before the body can absorb it.

Children face added exposure. The CDC notes that vomiting and diarrhea can occur with flu and are more common in children than in adults. Both drain fluid quickly. Young children can also become dehydrated faster than adults.

Urine output is one of the simplest measures to track at home. For babies and toddlers in diapers, caregivers can count wet diapers through the day. A diaper that stays dry for eight hours meets the CDC's warning threshold for children. For older children and adults, a long stretch without needing the bathroom is the signal to watch.

Tears and the mouth offer further checks in children. A child who cries without producing tears may be short on fluid. A dry mouth is another clue. The CDC groups both with missing urine under the same dehydration warning sign.

Other common dehydration clues include unusual thirst, very dark urine and lightheadedness on standing. These do not appear on the CDC's emergency list by themselves. They can still tell a caregiver that fluid intake is falling behind what the body needs.

A doctor can assess how much fluid a patient has lost. Treatment can range from drinking an oral rehydration solution to receiving fluids through a vein.

Severe muscle pain, or severe weakness or unsteadiness, is an emergency warning sign of flu in adults, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Anyone with these symptoms during a flu illness should contact a doctor or seek emergency care.

The line between an expected symptom and a warning sign here is one of degree. Body aches are a standard part of influenza. The sign on the CDC list is narrower. It covers muscle pain the agency describes as severe, not the general soreness that comes with a typical case.

The second sign uses a fixed threshold: a fever above 104°F. On a Celsius thermometer, that line sits at 40°C. The CDC's wording gives no age range for this sign. An older child whose temperature climbs past that mark meets the same sign as a younger one.

Neither threshold appears on the CDC's list of emergency warning signs for adults. Both belong to the agency's guidance for children only.

10 ways to tell cold, flu, COVID and RSV symptoms apart

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Cold vs flu vs COVID vs RSV symptoms often overlap; these 10 slides explain how each illness starts, how testing and treatment differ and when to seek care

A cough, a sore throat and a stuffy nose can come from at least four different viruses, and those viruses often circulate in the same months. A common cold, seasonal flu, COVID-19 and respiratory syncytial virus, or RSV, can look nearly identical early on. Each one still carries its own risks, and some come with treatments that work best when started early.

Risk varies most by age and health. In U.S. babies under one year old, RSV is the leading cause of bronchiolitis and pneumonia, according to the CDC. Older adults and people with heart or lung conditions also face a higher risk of serious RSV illness. COVID-19 can range from no symptoms at all to severe illness.

Timing is a second reason to pin down the cause. Antiviral drugs exist for flu and COVID-19, and each has a limited window after symptoms begin. Antibiotics do not help with any of the four illnesses, because all of them are caused by viruses.

Symptom patterns offer useful clues. A cold usually builds gradually and starts in the nose. The CDC describes flu as arriving abruptly, often with fever, chills and body aches. Loss of taste or smell points toward COVID-19 rather than a cold, though its absence does not rule COVID-19 out. The overlap between flu and COVID-19 is wide enough that symptoms alone cannot reliably separate them. A test is the way to confirm which virus is responsible. Rapid antigen tests for COVID-19 are sold for home use, and some clinics and labs check for COVID-19, flu and RSV from a single swab.

The 10 slides that follow walk through the differences in cold, flu, COVID and RSV symptoms one illness at a time, with separate attention to RSV in babies. They then cover vaccines, testing, antiviral treatment and the emergency warning signs that call for urgent medical care. Along the way, they address two practical questions: whether a test is needed, and when it is time to see a doctor.

A common cold usually builds slowly. It starts with a runny or stuffy nose, sneezing and a sore throat. Fever is uncommon in adults with a cold, and body aches are usually mild.

Rhinoviruses are the most frequent cause of colds. Their name comes from the Greek word for nose, the part of the body a cold reaches first. Rhinoviruses are not the only culprit. Some seasonal coronaviruses also cause colds.

Those seasonal coronaviruses are different viruses from SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19. They belong to the same broad virus family. The illness they usually produce is the ordinary head cold rather than COVID-19.

Symptoms tend to arrive in stages rather than all at once. A scratchy throat or a round of sneezing may come first. Congestion or a dripping nose follows.

A cough often develops after the nasal symptoms are underway. That order — nose and throat first, cough later — is the typical shape of a cold. A sudden start, with several symptoms hitting at once, fits the flu pattern better.

The fever point applies to adults. Young children with a cold are more likely than adults to run a fever. A raised temperature in a child therefore does not rule out a cold on its own. In an adult, a fever makes a typical cold less likely.

Heavy, whole-body aching is not the standard picture of a cold in an adult. Chills are uncommon with a cold. Fatigue tends to be milder than it is with the flu.

Colds spread from person to person through droplets from coughs and sneezes. They also spread when hands touch a contaminated surface and then the eyes, nose or mouth. Washing hands with soap and water lowers the chance of catching or passing on a cold. There is no vaccine for the common cold.

These patterns are clues rather than a diagnosis. RSV and other respiratory viruses can also cause a cold-like illness. A gradual start, nasal symptoms first and no fever in an adult all point toward a cold. None of them identifies the virus on its own.

Flu usually starts fast. The CDC describes seasonal influenza as arriving abruptly, often with fever or a feverish feeling, chills, muscle or body aches, headache, fatigue and a cough.

The speed of onset is one of the more useful clues available at home. A person can feel well in the morning and feverish and achy by evening. That sharp shift across the whole body contrasts with the slow, nose-first build of a typical cold.

Two types of virus, influenza A and influenza B, cause the seasonal flu that circulates each year. Influenza A viruses are further divided into subtypes, such as H1N1 and H3N2. How a person feels does not reveal which type or subtype is responsible.

The body-wide symptoms are what set flu apart from a typical cold. Muscle aches, chills and heavy fatigue point to more than a stuffy nose. A headache often comes with them. Together with a cough, this cluster is the classic flu picture.

Nasal and throat symptoms do not rule flu out. The CDC also lists a sore throat and a runny or stuffy nose among flu symptoms. A person with congestion and sudden aches may still have influenza rather than a cold.

Fever is a common flu symptom, but it is not a reliable dividing line. The CDC notes that not everyone with flu runs a fever. Someone with chills, aches and exhaustion but a normal temperature may still have influenza. A missing fever does not rule it out.

Children can show signs that adults usually do not. Vomiting and diarrhea occur more often in children with flu than in adults. Parents may see stomach symptoms alongside the fever, cough and aches that adults typically report.

Most people recover from flu in a few days to less than two weeks, according to the CDC. Some develop complications, such as pneumonia.

The pattern of symptoms gives a clue, not a diagnosis. Flu shares much of its symptom list with COVID-19. A sudden start and body aches narrow the possibilities without confirming which virus is to blame.

COVID-19 shares most of its symptoms with the flu: fever, chills, cough, fatigue, body aches, headache, sore throat and congestion. Because the lists match so closely, how someone feels is a weak guide to which of the two is causing the illness.

The virus behind COVID-19 is SARS-CoV-2, a coronavirus first identified in late 2019. It belongs to the same broad family as the seasonal coronaviruses that cause some common colds. It is still a separate virus. Seasonal flu comes from influenza A and B viruses, which belong to an entirely different virus family.

The CDC's list of COVID-19 symptoms goes beyond that core set. It also includes shortness of breath or difficulty breathing, nausea or vomiting, diarrhea and a runny nose. Each of those can appear with flu as well. None of them points clearly to one virus over the other. The agency adds that its list does not cover every possible symptom.

Severity varies widely. Some people infected with SARS-CoV-2 never develop symptoms. Others have an illness that feels like a mild cold, and some become seriously ill and need hospital care. People without symptoms can still pass the virus to others.

That range adds to the confusion. A mild case of COVID-19 can resemble a cold. A harder case can look like a typical bout of flu. How sick someone feels says little about which virus is responsible.

The CDC also notes that a person can be infected with flu and COVID-19 at the same time. With symptoms this similar, a double infection is hard to spot from how someone feels.

The distinction has practical weight. Flu and COVID-19 are treated with different antiviral drugs, so a doctor needs to know which virus is present before choosing one. Knowing the cause also helps a household decide how to protect older relatives, infants and anyone with a weakened immune system.

For that reason, doctors rely on testing rather than symptoms to confirm whether a patient has COVID-19, flu or both.

Respiratory syncytial virus, known as RSV, usually causes a mild, cold-like illness in healthy adults and older children. Most people recover within a week or two.

The usual symptoms are a runny nose, sneezing, a cough and a reduced appetite. Some people also develop a fever. Others start wheezing, a high-pitched whistling sound made as air moves through narrowed airways.

Much of that list matches a common cold. A runny nose, sneezing and coughing are typical of both infections. A healthy adult with RSV may assume it is a routine cold. Without a test, there is no way to know for sure.

Wheezing is less typical of a cold. It points to narrowed airways in the chest rather than congestion in the nose and throat. An adult or older child who does not normally wheeze, and starts to during a cold-like illness, has a symptom worth mentioning to a clinician.

RSV is not only a disease of infancy. Most people catch it first in early childhood. A first infection does not give lasting protection. People can catch the virus again throughout their lives. Repeat infections in older children and adults tend to be milder.

The virus spreads through droplets from coughs and sneezes. It also spreads when people touch a contaminated surface and then touch their eyes, nose or mouth. In the U.S., RSV circulates mainly in the fall and winter, the same part of the year as flu season.

Mild cases still matter for households. A healthy adult with a mild case can pass RSV to an infant or an older relative, who may become much sicker. Covering coughs, washing hands and staying home while sick limit that spread.

The mild pattern does not hold for everyone. In older adults and people with heart or lung conditions, RSV can move into the lower airways and cause pneumonia.

In U.S. children under one year old, RSV is the leading cause of bronchiolitis and pneumonia, according to the CDC. In infants, the signs that most need a doctor involve breathing, feeding and changes in behavior.

Bronchiolitis is inflammation of the bronchioles, the smallest airways in the lungs. As those passages swell and fill with mucus, air moves in and out less easily. Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs of the lungs. Both conditions can make breathing harder for a baby, whose airways are much narrower than an adult's.

Irritability and decreased activity are often early warning signs in infants with RSV. One baby may be unusually fussy and hard to comfort. Another may seem sleepy and far less responsive than usual. Either change is a reason to watch the baby closely.

Poor feeding is a separate red flag. Young infants breathe mainly through the nose. Congestion can therefore make sucking and swallowing difficult. A baby who tires quickly at the breast or bottle, or refuses feeds, needs to be checked by a doctor.

Breathing changes are the most urgent group of signs. These include short, shallow or fast breaths and labored breathing. Labored breathing can look like flaring nostrils or grunting. It can also show up as the skin between and below the ribs pulling in with each breath.

Some infants have pauses in breathing, a symptom known as apnea. A pause in breathing, even a brief one, calls for immediate medical attention.

The youngest babies have a lower threshold for a fever call. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises calling a doctor right away if an infant under three months has a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher. A fever is not required for a baby to need care. RSV does not always cause fever, and breathing trouble alone is reason enough to call.

Some babies face higher odds of severe RSV. The CDC lists premature infants and infants six months and younger among those at greater risk. It also names children younger than two with chronic lung disease or congenital heart disease, and children with weakened immune systems. For these children, any of the warning signs above warrants a quick call to a pediatrician.

Flu, COVID-19 and RSV can all be partly prevented with vaccines. The common cold is the outlier among the four, with no approved vaccine.

The flu shot is the most established of the three. The CDC recommends an annual flu vaccine for everyone aged six months and older, with limited exceptions. The yearly schedule reflects how influenza viruses keep changing. Manufacturers update the formula each season to match the strains expected to circulate.

COVID-19 vaccines follow a similar pattern of revision. They are updated periodically to target more recent versions of SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes the disease. A shot from an earlier season is not the same as the current formula.

RSV protection is the newest addition. In May 2023, the FDA approved the first RSV vaccines. Those two products, Arexvy and Abrysvo, were approved for adults aged 60 and older.

Protection for babies arrived later that year through two separate routes. In July 2023, the FDA approved nirsevimab, sold as Beyfortus, for infants. Nirsevimab is a monoclonal antibody rather than a vaccine. It gives the baby ready-made antibodies instead of training the infant's immune system to produce its own.

The second route works through pregnancy. In August 2023, the FDA approved Abrysvo for pregnant people at 32 to 36 weeks of gestation. The aim is to protect the newborn. Antibodies the mother makes in response to the vaccine pass to the baby before birth.

Anyone considering an RSV shot for themselves, an older parent or a newborn should check current CDC guidance or ask a clinician. FDA approval and CDC recommendations are separate steps, so the ages on an approval may not match who the CDC currently advises to get a shot.

None of these products treats an illness that has already started. Each is given ahead of time to lower the risk of infection or serious illness. Treatment for someone who is already sick is a separate question, handled with antivirals for some of these viruses.

A sudden loss of taste or smell is a recognized symptom of COVID-19 and is not a typical feature of the common cold. It has been reported less often with more recent variants. A normal sense of smell therefore does not rule COVID-19 out.

The CDC includes new loss of taste or smell on its list of COVID-19 symptoms. Clinicians use two medical terms for these changes. Anosmia is the loss of smell. Ageusia is the loss of taste.

The two senses are closely linked. Much of what people call flavor comes from smell, as aromas travel from the mouth to the back of the nose. A person who loses smell may say food tastes bland, even when the tongue still detects sweet, salty, sour and bitter.

That link explains why a cold can dull taste as well. A stuffy nose stops air from reaching the smell receptors high in the nasal cavity. Food can seem flat until the congestion clears. In that case, smell usually comes back as breathing through the nose improves.

The change tied to COVID-19 can follow a different pattern. Early in the pandemic, patients often described losing smell abruptly, sometimes without a blocked nose. That pattern drew attention because it set COVID-19 apart from ordinary congestion.

The clue has clear limits. Many people with COVID-19 notice no change in smell or taste. Someone with a fever, cough or sore throat can have COVID-19 with both senses intact.

Smell loss also has many causes beyond COVID-19. Sinus infections, nasal polyps and head injuries can all affect the sense of smell. The sense also tends to weaken with age. A change in smell on its own cannot identify which illness is responsible. Only a test confirms COVID-19.

For many people, smell and taste return within weeks. Some have longer-lasting changes. Others develop parosmia, a condition in which familiar smells seem distorted or unpleasant. A doctor can assess a smell or taste change that does not resolve.

A test, not a list of symptoms, is what identifies the virus behind a respiratory illness. COVID-19 is the easiest of the four to check at home, while flu and RSV testing mostly happens in a clinic or lab.

Most home kits are rapid antigen tests. They detect proteins from the virus and return a result in minutes. A COVID-only kit can show whether SARS-CoV-2 is present, but it says nothing about flu or RSV. The FDA has also authorized some home tests that check for both COVID-19 and flu from one sample.

Antigen tests are less sensitive than lab-based molecular tests, such as PCR, which detect the virus's genetic material. That makes a negative home result less reliable than a positive one, especially early in an illness. The FDA advises people with symptoms who get a negative result to test again 48 hours later.

Rapid flu tests are mainly a clinic tool. A clinician swabs the nose or throat and runs the test during the visit. Many rapid flu tests miss some infections. A negative result does not rule out flu.

Multiplex tests go a step further. A single swab is checked for COVID-19, flu and RSV in one run. The patient gives one sample instead of three. The clinician gets an answer on three viruses whose symptoms can look alike. Many multiplex panels are molecular tests run on lab or clinic equipment.

RSV testing also happens mostly in clinical settings. It is often reserved for infants, older adults and hospitalized patients. A healthy adult with a mild, cold-like illness may never be tested for RSV.

Whether a test is needed often depends on what a result would change. A positive flu or COVID-19 result can lead a doctor to consider a prescription antiviral. A negative result can point the search toward another cause. For people at higher risk of severe illness, that difference can shape what a doctor recommends.

Flu and COVID-19 both have prescription antiviral drugs, and both treatments depend on timing. Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) works best when started within 48 hours of flu symptom onset, according to the CDC. Paxlovid must be started within five days of COVID-19 symptoms appearing.

Those short windows are a practical reason to find out which illness is involved. A person who waits several days to see whether a fever breaks may miss the period when an antiviral helps most. Contacting a clinician early keeps that option open.

The 48-hour mark is not an absolute cutoff for flu. The CDC still recommends antiviral treatment for people hospitalized with flu and for those at higher risk of complications, even when treatment starts later.

Oseltamivir targets influenza viruses and does not treat the virus that causes COVID-19. It is also not the only flu antiviral. The FDA has approved baloxavir (Xofluza), zanamivir (Relenza) and peramivir (Rapivab) as well. Because flu and COVID-19 drugs differ, a doctor may want a test result before writing a prescription.

Paxlovid combines two drugs, nirmatrelvir and ritonavir. Nirmatrelvir is the part that works against the virus. Ritonavir slows the body's breakdown of nirmatrelvir and keeps it in the bloodstream longer. Ritonavir also interacts with a long list of other medicines, including some common heart and cholesterol drugs. A clinician reviews a patient's current prescriptions before prescribing it.

Paxlovid is meant for COVID-19 patients at higher risk of severe illness, not for everyone who tests positive. A clinician decides whether a patient falls into that higher-risk group.

Colds and RSV in otherwise healthy people have no routine antiviral treatment. Care centers on rest, fluids and over-the-counter relief for fever and aches.

Antibiotics do not treat colds, flu, COVID-19 or RSV. All four illnesses are caused by viruses, and antibiotics act on bacteria. A doctor may still prescribe one if a separate bacterial infection develops alongside the virus.

Children with flu should not be given aspirin. It carries a risk of Reye syndrome, a rare but serious condition that can damage the liver and brain. Parents can ask a pharmacist or pediatrician which fever reducer to use instead.

Trouble breathing, chest pain or pressure that will not go away, new confusion, bluish lips or face and signs of dehydration all need urgent medical care. These signs matter whether the illness turns out to be a cold, flu, COVID-19 or RSV.

In an emergency, the name of the virus matters less than how the body is coping. A test can wait. Struggling to breathe, or breathing that grows harder over a few hours, is a reason to seek help right away instead of riding the illness out at home.

Chest pain or pressure counts as a warning sign when it persists. A brief ache during a coughing fit is not the same as pressure that stays put. If the feeling does not ease, it needs a clinician's attention.

Confusion is another red flag. A person who is unusually hard to wake, disoriented or not making sense should be seen promptly. A relative or caregiver may notice this change before the sick person does.

A bluish tint to the lips or face can signal that the body is not getting enough oxygen. Treat it as an emergency. In the U.S., that means calling 911.

Dehydration can build up quietly during a respiratory illness. The risk rises when fever, vomiting or diarrhea combine with eating and drinking less. Passing very little urine, a dry mouth and dizziness when standing are common signs. Someone who cannot keep fluids down needs care.

Babies show distress differently from adults, and they cannot describe what they feel. An infant who refuses to feed, produces fewer wet diapers than usual or is working hard to breathe needs prompt medical attention. Parents who are unsure should call the child's doctor rather than wait to see whether things improve.

Some people should not wait for emergency signs before getting help. Those who are pregnant, aged 65 and older, immunocompromised or living with chronic conditions should contact a clinician early in any of these illnesses. An early call gives a clinician the chance to assess risk before symptoms escalate.

When the right next step is unclear, a call to a doctor's office, nurse line or urgent care clinic can help. Staff there can help decide whether a symptom needs an emergency room visit or can be watched at home.

In my new book, The Future Begins With Z—I challenge business leaders who report that young employees are the toughest age group to manage. I suggest we reframe the issue: Instead of complaining about their attitudes, unprofessional conduct, and disrespect, we should look for any kernels of truth and foresight into the future. It’s easy for older generations to assume the world is getting worse.

No doubt, there’s a tension at play—but I wonder if we’re solving the wrong problem.

Is Generation Z genuinely difficult to manage, or are they simply less willing to quietly accept workplace norms that previous generations were taught never to question?

It is easy to describe a generation as demanding or entitled when it asks for more feedback, greater flexibility, meaningful work, attention to mental health, and for leaders to listen. But a more revealing question is why we interpret these expectations as unreasonable rather than as signals that the traditional relationship between employee and employer is shifting.

Caleb would agree. After he graduated college, he went to work for a Fortune 500 company in New York, serving on the business development team. Caleb was a talented 22-year-old who quickly saw ways the company could improve their use of AI, as well as its HR and operational systems. His supervisor, however, wasn’t interested in his ideas and told Caleb to “pay his dues” before he offered any advice. In less than a year, Caleb quit his job and has since launched three businesses of his own. He’s now leading one that leverages AI to get work done more efficiently. I believe his first boss missed out on an incredible talent.

Nicki would agree. She was 21 years old when her supervisor exposed how little he knew about his young team member. He accused her of having a poor work ethic, since she never worked a minute past 5:00 pm. She had to explain that she left at 5:00 to rush over to a side hustle, since her salary wasn’t enough to pay the bills. (She worked another side hustle on weekends as well.) Like Caleb, Nicki left the company. This turnover will continue to happen until older managers stop stereotyping and get acquainted with their young employees.

Ashton would agree. He started working as a sixth grader but saw jobs differently than his much older boss. This 11-year-old left behind a structured lawn company to start his own business after noticing his neighbors struggling with dirty trash bins. He wondered: Would someone pay to have them cleaned? He discovered they would. Within months, Ashton’s new company was earning him a high income to put in savings, and his work went viral on TikTok, showing how a young leader solving a local problem can inspire everyone.

Reframing this issue has helped so many organizations I have served. Rather than treating Gen Z as a workplace problem to solve, I ask leaders to consider what this generation might be revealing about the future of work.

Perhaps it’s time we seek growth over comfort and familiarity. If we do, however, it will require intentionality. I’m in Orlando today, spending time with my new grandson, Max. He’s 9 weeks old. When he’s awake, he constantly fidgets and groans, making sweet little noises and movements. Doctors say babies often do this in search of comfort. Each move is a search for comfort for their bodies or food if they’re hungry. This is a natural urge humans have all our lives—to find comfort and satisfaction. Call it "the comfort zone."

Leaders, however, must resist this natural quest for comfort and seek growth and progress. In our day, I believe this means learning from our young instead of just lecturing them. Let’s reframe how we perceive our youngest teammates. Maybe, just maybe, they’ll lead us into a better future.
