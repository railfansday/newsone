Currently, Dinniman has released the Prologue, Chapters 1 through 5, and an Interlude chapter, and they give fans a proper look at what's next for Carl, Donut, and the rest of the party, though these chapters are clearly just the tip of the iceberg.

While there have been a few details revealed in interviews over the past few months, such as Book 9's working title, The Beautiful Place, as well as the number of crawlers left in the game, the teaser chapters give us a real sense of what's ahead, including the return of a fan-favorite character.

One of the most exciting developments in Dungeon Crawler Carl Book 9 is the return of Ferdinand, the lovable yet insufferable orange tabby who has become an unlikely fan favorite. Also known as Gravy Boat, Ferdinand has a... complicated history with Princess Donut, and their bizarre relationship has provided some of the series' funniest moments.

Ferdinand played a substantial supporting role in Book 7, This Inevitable Ruin, particularly during Faction Wars, when he became co-warlord of Team Retribution alongside Juice Box. His newfound authority, inflated ego, and ongoing obsession with Donut made him an hilarious addition to the larger conflict, but then he was more or less sidelined in Book 8, A Parade of Horribles.

Ferdinand was largely absent from Book 8's central storyline, although one memorable sequence offered a bit of insight into his troubled past. Fortunately, the opening chapters of Book 9 suggest Ferdinand will be returning to a much more prominent role, beginning with an unexpected reunion with Donut.

In the teaser chapters, Ferdinand finds himself in a dangerous situation involving a mysterious new crawler, prompting Donut to intervene on his behalf. The encounter offers a touching reminder of how much their relationship has evolved, while potentially setting Ferdinand up for a larger role in the remaining story. With Dungeon Crawler Carl approaching its conclusion, Ferdinand's return is a welcome development for longtime readers.

Fortunately, Dungeon Crawler Carl fans might not have to wait too much longer for Book 9, at least if author Matt Dinniman's publication history is any indication. Since launching the series in 2020, Dinniman has released eight mainline novels, maintaining an impressively consistent schedule compared to many other major fantasy authors.

While the gaps between installments have grown as the series has progressed, This Inevitable Ruin arrived in November 2024, followed by A Parade of Horribles in May 2026, just 18 months later. Following that pattern, a late 2027 release for Book 9 certainly isn't out of the question, although nothing has been officially confirmed. Dinniman has also offered some encouraging comments about his progress, suggesting that the next installment could follow a similar timeline, estimating "about a year, give or take a few months."

Still, with several teaser chapters already available to Patreon supporters, there's plenty of reason to be optimistic about Dungeon Crawler Carl returning sometime in 2027.

One More Test

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As I get ever closer to finishing my hike, I must take on the northern Pennsylvania section notorious for its endless rocks.

A problem for future me. That is what I thought to myself as I finished my hike up and over, and then up and over again, Mount Moosilauke. I had finished the Whites, that meant the rest of the Appalachian Trail would be a cake walk. There was just one problem, northern Pennsylvania. Consistently rated the worst part of the AT not because of steep climbs, but because of the rocks. Throughout my time on the AT, I’d heard tales of this section, and some rather tall ones at that. I pushed the thought out of my mind. It would take me weeks to get there. Including New Hampshire, it was six states away. Well, now I am officially starting northern Pennsylvania. The problem for future me has become the problem for current me. To make matters worse, there was rain in the forecast overnight into tomorrow. It wasn’t just any old rocks I’d be contending with, it would be wet rocks. Lucky me.

I originally planned to drive across the Delaware River to the Dunnfield Creek Trailhead where I’d turned around yesterday. Since it was a lighter mileage day I decided to keep my van where it was and walk north to the trailhead, turn around and continue past the van southbound. This would add some miles to today, but trim them from tomorrow. It would also save a bit of toll expense since hikers aren’t charged for crossing the bridge. I made my way onto the bridge, briefly crossing back into New Jersey and along River Road. I was back at the van before 9:00AM and ready to tackle the climb up Mount Minsi.

On the bridge through the Delaware Water Gap.

Briefly back into New Jersey before leaving for the last time.

The trail passed the small Lenape Lake before ascending the south side of the Delaware Water Gap. Eureka Creek flowed across the Appalachian Trail which continued climbing up Mount Minsi until it reached a few rocky outcrops presenting hikers with a view high above the Delaware Water Gap. I had a perfectly clear view of Mount Tammany, with its rock slides corkscrewing upward. The trail turned along the ridge, relieving me of any more vertical steps. A dirt road made for easy hiking while passing by great views of the valley to the southeast. Soon the trail turned off the dirt road and I had my first encounter with the rocks of northern Pennsylvania. While small, they stretched on for far longer than any other rocky patch on the AT. I couldn’t hike at my usual pace, I would have twisted my ankle in no time. I paid extra close attention to navigating through the rocky maze, moving left and right to find the best spots to put my feet. The constant back and forth made it a chore to keep my momentum up. Still, it was more of an annoyance than a dreadful experience, at first.

The trail follows a gravel road briefly after Lenape Lake.

Looking across the Delaware Water Gap at Mount Tammany.

Following the ridge in northern Pennsylvania.

Crossing the road at Wolf Gap took a bit of courage as there is no line of sight down either side of the crest. I waited for the passing cars to clear and then listened for any new traffic approaching before jogging across the roadway. I was now within striking distance of Wold Rocks. As is customary, I stayed on the AT over Wolf Rocks, even though there is a bypass in case the weather is rainy and windy. That might be the case tomorrow, but all I had today were blue skies. This was the first scramble of the day, which only lasted a minute or so. What followed was a sloped rocky ridge with views to the northwest. Honestly, if it weren’t for the reputation of this section of the trail, I wouldn’t have thought the rocks were that bad, and it made sense for the trail to come up here for the view. I am sure more challenging terrain lay ahead.

Sure enough, this was about where things got more trying. The trail became something of a dense field of jagged rocks sticking up from the earth. The trail went on like this for mile after mile with only brief reprieves to be found. I was managing, but I cringed when thinking of what it would be like tomorrow in wet conditions. I fought my way to Wind Gap, a gap in the ridge that was thankfully not all that windy today. I checked in on a few more viewpoints before continuing the trek about 2 miles past the Leroy A. Smith shelter. Knowing there was finite daylight remaining I turned around to investigate the shelter.

Following the ridge in northern Pennsylvania.

I was struggling to decide where to spend the night. I was going to reach the Leroy A. Smith shelter with some daylight left. If the shelter was empty it might be a good idea to stay in it. I’d avoid a wet tent, but I’d have a longer hike back to the Delaware water gap. I also have had a lingering feeling ever since I passed on staying at the secret shelter that aid be able to get a much earlier start heading out from a shelter than my tent. Packing up camp requires light and warmth. It’s possible to pack up with just my headlamp, but there are a lot of things that could be accidentally left behind like tent stakes. It would be much easier with everything in plain sight in a shelter. If the goal was to finish hiking as early as possible, it might make more sense to stay in the shelter rather than hike a few miles up the trail and camp.

In the end, the decision was made for me. Someone had set up in the shelter already. Two dog bowls suggested that they either had a dog or unconventional eating habits. I dropped my backpack at the shelter and headed down to the spring. I encountered a man and a young dog at the spring. Both seemed friendly enough. The dog reminded me a lot of the dogs I used to foster when I lived in Maryland. It looked tough on the outside, but after a few good ear scratches it melted into a pup as gentle as they come.

I drank a liter of water at the spring and collected two more to get me through the morning. The pipe spring gushed water like a champ. It was quite a hike up from the spring. I noticed that water is rarely on the trail in this section because the mountain is so narrow. I’ll need to expect fewer streams right in front of shelters and more steep treks for water over the next week or so.

View from the trail near the Leroy A. Smith Shelter.

Back on the trail I had limited time to find a camping spot before light ran out. It seemed cruel that now that I was finally back in a state that allows camping along the trail with few restrictions, the areas near the trail rarely seemed fit for tenting. The trail was rocky, and so was all the flat ground near the trail. I was only sure of one spot about 2.5 miles back, I wasn’t going to make it there unless I wanted to retrieve my headlamp from my pack, which I was starting to consider. Before I could, I spotted what logged to be a suitable spot. I turned off the trail and proceeded to walk over the area, checking for sticks and rocks. It felt decent, so I rolled out my tent and felt the ground with my hands to see if there would be any lumps where my tent floor would be. I removed a few more rocks and sticks but was soon satisfied that the spot would work. I finished pitching the tent while my water filtered in the background. It wasn’t easy to get the stakes into the ground. A couple were more stuck between rocks than buried in the ground, but they seemed like they would hold. I went to bed hoping that the precipitation superintendent would cancel the rain tomorrow.

Still rocky, going to be hard to find a spot before it gets dark…

Disregarding legally mandated approval processes and protocols, his administration has knocked down buildings and torn up trees without permission.

Though legal challenges have, at times, slowed projects like the White House ballroom, arch monument, a D.C. golf course, and John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts renovations, they haven’t been effective.

The landscape is shifting, however. Once the the next Democrat-led Congress is in place, changes to the built environment will face oversight that Republicans didn’t previously provide. This is how it might shake out.

3 new roadblocks to Trump’s projects

First, Democrats will get two ex officio seats on the 12-person National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC)—the federal government’s D.C.-area planning agency—that are reserved for U.S. Senate and House of Representatives members. The president appoints some members of the commission, and there are others who are there because of their job titles.

Democrats will replace Senator Rand Paul and Representative James Comer, both Kentucky Republicans, with their own people. Those two new members of the commission could join with the District of Columbia’s four members, who have consistently voted against Trump’s building priorities, to block his projects from passing with a tied 6-6 vote.

Second, Democrats will have standing to sue in federal court on behalf of the Senate and House, using Senate and House counsel. Leveraging congressional subpoenas and investigations, lawmakers could learn more about private donations Trump used to fund the projects.

Third, and most important, Democrats will hold the purse strings in the appropriations process, which means they can restrict or defund executive branch activities. In addition to accepting private donations, Trump has also funded his construction with public, taxpayer funds. Under Democrats, that practice could soon come to an end.

What Democrats are saying

These potential roadblocks could be why Trump seems to be rushing to get construction work done before the next Congress is sworn in. The day after the midterm elections, Trump signed and raised a beam at the ballroom site. Meanwhile, the NCPC approved plans for his arch the following day, though a judge issued a temporary restraining order shortly after.

The restraining order lasts just 14 days, and Democratic lawmakers are gearing up to fight it long term.

Speaking about Trump’s arch, Representative Don Beyer, the Democrat whose northern Virginia district includes Arlington National Cemetery, said at a press conference Friday that he’s “never seen a project with such profound, widespread opposition from the very people who it will affect most.”

To fight the project, Beyer authored the Arlington National Cemetery Viewshed Protection Act, which would block the arch’s construction outright. Introduced in May, the bill hasn’t been brought to a vote under Republican House leadership. Under Democrats, that and similar legislation to defund or explicitly ban Trump projects could more easily make it to a vote.

Representative Jared Huffman, a California Democrat and ranking member on the House Natural Resources Committee, which oversees the Department of the Interior, said “the ground between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington belongs to the American people and the families who have buried loved ones there.” He warned officials who keep the project moving forward or firms that sign on to build the arch to “understand the legal risk they’re taking on.”

“If Trump wants a tribute to himself, he can build one at one of his glitzy resorts or private golf clubs,” Huffman said Friday in a statement. “Come January, a new Congress will demand answers from the people enabling this abuse of our public lands and our laws.”

For two years, Trump hasn’t faced pushback from Congress over his construction projects. That’s about to change.

James Webb’s Mysteriously Bright Galaxies May Finally Have an Explanation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Calculations suggest powerful ultraviolet light from young stars could sweep gas out of early galaxies while leaving dense star-forming regions intact, offering a clue to their mysterious brightness.

The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed something unexpected about the early universe: bright galaxies were far more common just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang than astronomers thought possible. A new analysis suggests these galaxies may not necessarily be producing more light than expected. Instead, powerful ultraviolet radiation from young stars could be clearing away gas and dust, allowing more of their light to reach us.

Researchers at the Simons Foundation’s Flatiron Institute in New York City and the University of Texas at Dallas investigated this possibility by examining how ultraviolet light called Lyman-alpha radiation interacts with material surrounding young stars. Their findings, published November 4 in The Astrophysical Journal, suggest that this radiation can drive gas and dust out of less dense regions of galaxies, potentially explaining why some of the earliest galaxies appear unusually bright.

“Our results show Lyman-alpha radiation can push low-density gas and dust away really, really fast,” says Shyam Menon, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute’s Center for Computational Astrophysics and the study’s lead author. “It can sort of clear the ‘fog,’ allowing us to see stars in the early universe much more clearly than we would otherwise.”

Lyman-alpha radiation forms when an electron collides with a hydrogen atom or joins a hydrogen ion, and it can be especially abundant in regions where young stars are forming. When this light encounters gas or dust, it transfers momentum to the material, creating pressure that can push it outward.

What makes Lyman-alpha radiation particularly powerful is how hydrogen responds to it. While most absorbed light is released at a different wavelength, hydrogen atoms almost immediately re-emit Lyman-alpha radiation at the same wavelength, allowing nearby atoms to absorb and release it repeatedly. These repeated interactions increase the force exerted by the light, potentially pushing surrounding material away from regions where stars are forming.

Astronomers have long suspected that this pressure could reshape stellar nurseries by removing gas needed for star formation, possibly even stopping the process under extreme conditions. However, the repeated interactions between radiation and hydrogen are difficult to reproduce in computer simulations, making it challenging to determine how much influence the radiation actually has.

“We’ve known for a very long time that Lyman-alpha radiation could be important because it carries a lot of force,” Menon says. “But we’ve never actually been able to estimate how important it is in a realistic environment because of how challenging it is to model.”

To address this problem, Menon and collaborator Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas analyzed snapshots from an existing simulation of a star-forming region in the early universe. Rather than tracking every interaction between light and matter, they calculated how much pressure Lyman-alpha radiation would exert on the surrounding gas and dust.

Their calculations revealed that the radiation behaves differently depending on the density of the surrounding material. In dense star-forming regions, gravity remained strong enough to hold gas and dust in place despite the outward pressure, suggesting that Lyman-alpha radiation had little effect on star formation there. In less crowded regions between stars, however, the radiation could overcome gravity and drive material outward at high speeds.

Multiple views of a 2.5-million-year simulation visualizing a gas cloud as it is cleared away and the formation of stars. From left to right, the views show the gas density, ionization, and temperature. Credit: Shyam Menon (Flatiron Institute) and Aaron Smith (UT Dallas)

“When the density is low enough, the Lyman-alpha radiation can push the gas to really high speeds, launching explosive winds that can shoot material clear out of the galaxy,” Menon says.

These winds could help explain the unexpectedly bright galaxies detected by the James Webb Space Telescope. By removing loosely distributed gas and dust from between stars, the radiation may create clearer paths for starlight to escape, making distant galaxies appear brighter without requiring them to produce unusually large amounts of light.

Important questions remain, particularly about the properties of dust in the early universe and how radiation interacts with moving gas as stars form. Researchers will need more comprehensive simulations that account for these processes together to better understand their influence on early galaxies.

Recent work by Olof Nebrin of Stockholm University and study co-author Smith has demonstrated that these interactions can be modeled in two dimensions, although more realistic three-dimensional simulations remain a challenge.

“But the hope is that in the coming years, with improvements to computing power and the development of more effective algorithms, we can eventually do these simulations in three dimensions.”

Reference: “Lyα Radiation Pressure in Dense Star Clusters: Implications for Star Formation and Winds at Cosmic Dawn” by Shyam H. Menon and Aaron Smith, 4 November 2026, The Astrophysical Journal. DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/aeaed6

NFL Panic Meter: While other NFC contenders loaded up at deadline, Rams and Lions were quiet

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The theme of the trade deadline seemed to be contenders being more aggressive than ever before in their pursuit of a Super Bowl, particularly in the NFC.

That's what made the inactivity of the Detroit Lions and Los Angeles Rams a bit startling.

The Seattle Seahawks, Chicago Bears and San Francisco 49ers all made trades to help themselves now. The Green Bay Packers did not, but they're getting Micah Parsons back soon and they're relying on that being a big midseason addition. The 49ers did the least of that group, but they're also having the best season in the conference and starting to get a little healthier. And we don't need to count anyone in the NFC East or NFC South as a contender.

The only move the Rams or Lions made was Detroit trading a sixth-round pick to the Jets for safety Andre Cisco, who was a part-time starter for a bad New York team.

Let's start with the Rams, because they had the biggest need and their inactivity goes against their normal mode. The Rams have lost Puka Nacua and Davante Adams to injuries, and don't know if they'll be back before it's too late. They're dreadfully thin at receiver for the next few weeks, at least. The Rams have gone from preseason Super Bowl favorite to probably a slight underdog to even make the playoffs. Yet, they did not make a trade before the deadline. The Rams could argue, like the Packers, that adding Myles Garrett back is their in-season addition. But that doesn't help at receiver, and it's hard to believe the Rams have championship upside building a passing game around three or four tight ends and nothing else. It seems like the Rams put three-quarters of their chips in the middle, then folded.

The Lions probably could have used a cornerback or linebacker nearly as much as the Rams needed a receiver, but they did nothing other than a low-cost move to add Cisco. Detroit's defense probably isn't good enough to win a championship, and their fans will be frustrated after seeing other teams, particularly in the NFC North, make moves. They were clamoring for a cornerback for weeks and Jack Campbell's injury left them with a weakness at linebacker. Those areas of need still exist.

The Rams have a "forget them picks" attitude but it still has a limit. They gave up a lot to get Garrett and Trent McDuffie in the offseason. They might have also privately seen a team that is slipping further from being a Super Bowl contender and didn't want to chase their losses. The Lions didn't want to pay the escalating prices to make a huge move on the back end of their defense, and while that might be prudent for the long haul, Detroit fans don't want to be patient anymore. Maybe the Lions or Rams still make a deep playoff run, but they'll have to do it against teams that got much better over the past couple weeks.

Panic meter: The Rams and Lions are still good, but everyone around them got better.

Sean McVay's Los Angeles Rams did not make a trade before the deadline on Tuesday. (Photo by Patrick Smith/Getty Images)

The biggest secondary domino from the trade deadline moves happened with the Las Vegas Raiders. Trading Kirk Cousins to the Minnesota Vikings kicked off the Mendoza era. The No. 1 overall pick will be the team's starter going forward.

The timing isn't perfect. The next two games for the Raiders are against the Seahawks and Broncos, two of the toughest defenses in the NFL. The Broncos game is at Denver, which is a tough place to play.

It could be rough for Mendoza early on. The Raiders had to make the move when the Vikings offered a third-round pick for a 38-year-old quarterback who was probably starting only two more games for them anyway. Two games won't ruin the rookie but he's in for a welcome to the NFL moment or two.

Panic meter: Mendoza will be fine but expect some early struggles.

Rhamondre Stevenson has an injured tendon in his knee, TreVeyon Henderson has been out with a murky injury and that led people to believe the Patriots could trade for a running back. That didn't happen.

No running backs moved at the trade deadline. The Colts were reportedly asking way too much in return for Jonathan Taylor (more on Indianapolis in a moment) and perhaps the prices of others like Tony Pollard were egregious too. But now the Patriots move forward for a while with Corey Kiner as the lead back. That will put more pressure on Drake Maye, who is coming off a poor game against the Packers. The dream of contending in the AFC East is slipping away. Will the Patriots be a playoff team?

Panic meter: The Patriots should still sneak in the playoffs, but a running back trade would have been nice.

The Colts traded an eight-time Pro Bowl guard, a three-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle. a six-time Pro Bowl receiver and a 25-year-old receiver who has shown promise, and somehow they didn't get a first- or second-round pick back for any of them.

The Colts got a lot of mid-round picks back for Quenton Nelson, DeForest Buckner, Keenan Allen and Josh Downs, but they didn't seem to maximize the value of any of them other than perhaps Allen. Buckner's return of a fourth-round pick seemed quite light given what some other defensive tackles went for. And the Jonathan Taylor situation was just odd.

The Colts reportedly received offers of a second-round pick and more for Taylor and passed. Taylor is a great player but he's a running back on a team with a long rebuild ahead of it. It made little sense to hold onto him. But the Colts, who don't have next year's first-round pick due to the Sauce Gardner trade, don't have much of a plan.

Panic meter: The Colts have a long rebuild ahead and this isn't a good sign it'll go well.

The Bengals can argue that trading the 10th overall pick of the draft for defensive tackle Dexter Lawrence II in the offseason was their big move. But some more might have been nice.

The Bengals are getting an MVP level season from quarterback Joe Burrow, their offensive line is better than it has been in years and the defense has been competent. That defense could be better though. Couldn't they have outbid the Broncos for Chargers linebacker Daiyan Henley, who went for fourth- and fifth-round picks?

The Bengals' ownership has always been ripped for being cheap and maybe that was part of the team making no moves before the deadline. Either way, it's a team that should have Super Bowl hopes and might regret not doing anything to improve itself with another trade.

Panic meter: The Bengals are good but might ultimately find they're a piece or two away.

Amanda Seyfried Lights Up Tiffany & Co.’s Iconic Bird on a Rock to Usher in the Holiday Season

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On Tuesday evening, all that glitters was indeed gold—and adorned in diamonds. For the third year in a row, Tiffany & Co.’s festive fête at its Fifth Avenue flagship served as the unofficial ribbon-cutting moment to kick off the holiday season, hosted this time around by Amanda Seyfried.

The actress, who is the House’s newest ambassador, was also tapped to illuminate the Bird on a Rock installation. A glitzed-up and glammed-out guest list made its way to Midtown for the occasion, passing the Fifth Avenue snowflake—now lit in Tiffany Blue for the holiday season—and stopping to marvel at the Landmark’s twinkling facade.

A brief elevator ride upstairs opened up to reveal a winter wonderland. Sparkling displays showcased the House’s pieces in front of the floor-to-ceiling windows, trays were laden with Tiffany Blue martinis, and the belle of the ball—the Bird on a Rock—gleamed outside. As guests counted down the moments until its lighting, they nibbled on petite T&C stamped-paninis and micro tuna crispy rice bites.

Seyfried arrived, dazzling in a Tiffany HardWare white gold and pavé diamond link necklace and the hallmark Bird on a Rock Wing brooch—with over 13 carats of diamonds. The award-winning star was just as excited for the party as everyone else. “As a young American girl, you have these fantasies that you might get to be a part of something really special. It’s pretty iconic that I’m here and that I get to represent Tiffany for the holiday season. There’s something very classic, traditional, and symbolic about Tiffany’s—it’s the best legacy jewelry brand there is,” Seyfried told Vogue. “My sister-in-law is here tonight with her baby, my godson. I wish my kids were here, but I didn’t even think to invite them, and they’re actually in the city. I don’t know what I was thinking!”

The holidays evoke all types of fuzzy feelings, and Mikey Madison reminisced about one of her favorite family traditions. “My dad and I always cook, so we’ll cook a big Hanukkah dinner and we’ll do all the classics that my grandma used to make. He does the brisket, I’ll do the latkes, the matzah ball soup, the kugel,” Madison shared. “It’s always meaningful to go to an event with Tiffany. It feels like a big family for me, and there’s so much intention behind their artistry, what they do, and how they bring people together,” the Oscar-winning ambassador added. “It’s the loveliest job that I have to get to work with them and get to wear all this incredibly beautiful jewelry as well.”

When it was finally time to light the Bird on a Rock, everyone gathered near the tree—one constructed from countless coveted Blue Boxes. “Love is a gift,” Matthieu Garnier, president and CEO of Tiffany & Co. North America, told the crowd during his toast. “The most meaningful gifts carry stories, create memories, and connect generations.”

Garnier wasn’t just referring to the tree: The entire 2026 holiday campaign—starring Seyfried—centers on gifts becoming future heirlooms, which in turn bridge a connection between generations. Just like in 1965, when Jean Schlumberger’s Bird on a Rock design became one of those treasured keepsakes intended to be passed down.

Right on cue, Seyfried stepped up, placing a Tiffany ornament on the tree, and immediately, the Bird on a Rock shimmered to life, illuminating the bustling streets below. Let the holidays begin!
