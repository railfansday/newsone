DNC Vice Chair: “We Had to Hit the Bottom”

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A leader of the Democratic blue-nami on viral videos, the billionaire’s tax and how everything had to go wrong before it could go right.

The Democrats and their many supporters in Hollywood had a historic election Tuesday night, and it’s leaving some of the people who orchestrated it both celebratory and trying to learn from the results.Jane Kleeb is one such person. A vice chair of the Democratic National Committee under Ken Martin who also heads up the Association of State Democratic Committees (which basically represents the Democratic party in each state and territory), she helped lead the movement that resulted in Tuesday’s many wins.

A native Nebraskan and longtime activist, Kleeb was in a giddy mood Wednesday, and also a pragmatic one. We got on the phone with her to break everything down.First I have to ask, how much of a surprise were some of these races? A few felt on the bubble coming in but others were just shockers. Does it feel that way to you?I definitely thought we were going to win the House and the Senate back because I knew the quality of candidates and the infrastructure we were building, and I thought we would win some governors’ races and break some supermajorities at the state legislative level as well. But I didn’t know it would be to this extent — that the margins would be so wide. In some of these races it wasn’t one or two percentage points; it was five or six or more. So it was a resounding victory, not just voting against Trump.So you don’t see a lot of these as “anti” votes.When I was watching some pundits [on cable news] last night people were pontificating saying this was just a rebuke to Trump. And no question it was. But it was also because Democratic organizers everywhere and candidates who looked and sounded like their communities and were also fighters. So it was all of those factors.Of course, you’ve tried to do that before. What changed?I think it’s because our candidates this cycle thought they had nothing to lose. That Donald Trump was such a threat to democracy that they were putting everything on the field and because of that it happened all across the country not just in targeted battleground states.You say nothing to lose. In your mind did you need to hit rock bottom — did 2024’s calamity enable this?There’s no question we had a major reality-check as a party in 2024. And even in 2016. We felt like we recovered some footing in 2020 but not enough to carry us. We saw that with the divisions over keeping Biden as the nominee or switching candidates 100 days out in [2024]. And that’s a picture of where we were as a party, that we couldn’t find our voice, we couldn’t find our footing. We couldn’t figure out how to communicate to the American people all of the good things we were doing. So we hit the bottom. We had to hit the bottom. When Trump won again we saw with so many of the communities that we frankly took for granted — Black voters and young voters and Latino voters — they saw Trump as a change agent and someone who would break up the status quo. And obviously he didn’t deliver on any of that, so we picked back up and said “actually we’re the party fighting for change to level the playing field.” And voters received that message.That certainly happened across the Midwest and the South. But a lot of our readers are in California, where that didn’t happen — it looks like the billionaire’s tax is going to fail pretty badly. Why couldn’t you make that case to Californians?If the tax fails it could be a lot of reasons. Maybe it’s that people don’t like taxes, it could be that.Honestly the way it was laid out on the ballot was also confusing — you basically had a billionaire tax and a ban on a billionaire tax right next to each other, both just looking like a property tax.I think it could also have been that. I definitely still believe people are deeply frustrated by the billionaire class and want to see accountability from billionaires. So maybe it’s not a tax but maybe it’s stopping giveaways at the federal level. There are other ways to get that accountability.One thing that struck me about the election is that unlike in past party sweeps — say, the Democrats in 2008 with Obama — there was no central charismatic figure around which all these House and Senate and local races coalesced. That you did it bottom-up instead of top-down.

The difference this time was definitely the story of a team instead of a lead actor. And I think that’s where the party shines. When rely on one person to carry us through we’re going to lose that battle. That happened with President Obama during his terms. We lost 1,500 seats at the local level over his eight years because we weren’t investing in state and local infrastructure; we got so wrapped up in “Obama.” So I think we learned the hard lesson that it’s this infrastructure model that works. And we saw that this time.What would you say was the biggest surprise last night?Symone Sanders, who is from [here in] Nebraska had been talking about Mississippi. So I went down and visited. I thought ’this was a state where it could happen, but not this cycle.’ But that was a mistake. We had Brandon Presley, [a moderate Democrat] who almost won [for governor in 2023]. But we didn’t give the resources to get over the finish line. And to see what Scott [Colom] has done without those resources — if this happens [Colom is currently leading his opponent Cindy Hyde-Smith by a few dozen votes with 44,000 ballots outstanding] it’s from the sheer grit of Scott and the Democratic Party there and the candidates down-ballot.And the viral videos were insane — he did that whole pick “another protein/pick another Senator” bit to troll Hyde-Smith’s line from a debate. I don’t think I can remember a political ad as subtly effective and smile-inducing as that.He’s been great. And it does make me feel like, damn, we should have known.You do have limited resources, though, and I wonder how you spread that out now that so many states seem to be in play, places like Kansas and Nebraska and Texas and South Carolina and maybe even Alaska. It seemed a lot easier when certain red states were just automatically out of bounds. Does this indeed make where to spend your money more complicated?What is very clear from the amount of money raised this cycle is that we don’t have limited resources. There’s plenty of money in the political system. What you will hear from me and Chair Martin and many who live and work in our 57 states and territories is we can and should have permanent infrastructure everywhere, no exceptions, year-round.You don’t think there are simply some states too far gone to justify Democrats spending money in — that if, say, Florida couldn’t elect a Democratic Senator or governor in this climate, maybe it’s beyond hope for the party?

I think this is absolutely the wrong way to look at it. Colorado was red not that long ago, and then we began organizing and getting young people to vote. Georgia was red not that long ago and now look at it. [SZ: Both those states saw large influxes of Democrats from other states that Florida has not seen.] I grew up in Florida and it was a swing state. We’re starting to organize there. You have places like Colorado and Mississippi where there are cities that are blue and then you spread out to the suburbs and beyond making inroads there. Florida has that too. I think you’ll begin to see things shift significantly in places like Florida and Nebraska [where the party came close to defeating a Republican this cycle with an independent Senate candidate] as we head into 2028.Speaking of looking ahead, now the Democrats have to govern. And they have to govern with moderates and progressives and far-left activists in the came caucus. We see here in Hollywood the rifts that can happen even among Democrats — we see it, and I’m kidding but not kidding, all too well unfortunately. How do you ensure those rifts don’t happen in the next two years?I think having progressives and moderates gives us an advantage because we’ll have durable long-lasting policies that all kinds of Americans will embrace. Because the American people have all those different views too. People look at these disparities as a risk, but I see them as an advantage.

10 Classic Rock Albums You Must Listen to Before You Die

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Classic rock is maybe definable as the kind of rock you're likely to hear on classic rock radio stations (unsurprisingly), but a better definition could be narrowing it down to rock released during a certain period. That period was when rock was at its most popular and culturally dominant, and arguably, during that time, rock music was also at its best, so it’s classic rock, and then it also happens to be rock that is classic (in terms of quality and historical importance).

There’s an attempt to single out some particularly great classic rock albums below, all of them worth listening to before you die. Or, less morbidly, listen to them all while you're still breathing; before the bucket’s kicked. That’s still pretty morbid. Sorry. But these albums are amazing, and enough so that you’ve probably heard most of them before, but if you haven’t, they're essential to give a spin at least once before… you know…

It feels uncool to call them Creedence Clearwater Revival instead of just “Creedence,” but either way, here's Cosmo’s Factory, which is probably the band’s best album. Creedence (yeah, let’s be cool for a bit; for fun) is entirely classic rock in sound, really being demonstrative of this particular era of rock, and so sure, Cosmo’s Factory is blunt, certainly straightforward, and pretty easy to listen to quite passively.

Sometimes it’s just what you need, though? Something loud, catchy, and very of its time, but all of those things in good ways. Cosmo’s Factory is strong enough that you might not love Creedence, or the particular kind of rock they made, but you're still likely going to, at the very least, tolerate this particular album. And if you are fond of classic rock and/or Creedence, then it’s safe to say you're going to go well beyond tolerating this, since you'll probably love it.

If there’s one flaw with Damn the Torpedoes, it’s that it starts in a way strong enough that you could argue the momentum isn't entirely made, yet that’s a nitpick, since the album after the first three tracks is still strong. It’s more just that the run from “Refugee” to “Here Comes My Girl” to “Even the Losers” is especially great, with three high-quality songs on their own all feeling perfectly sequenced, and thereby made even better.

But Damn the Torpedoes is not a long album, so once you're three tracks down, there are only six to go, with “Don't Do Me Like That” and “Louisiana Rain” among those remaining songs. Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers had a knack for pumping out punchy rock music early on, so the two albums before Damn the Torpedoes and the one following it are also great, yet it’s Damn the Torpedoes that’s the most consistent and memorable of the bunch.

Make no mistake: The Rolling Stones had a great run throughout a good chunk of the 1960s, but there’s an argument to be made that it was their 1970s run that demonstrated the band at their strongest. Sticky Fingers is especially strong, and the one being singled out here, but it wouldn’t be wise to overlook the 1960s, and then ‘80s-wise, the band was still going fairly strong there, even if their music in that decade wasn’t quite as “classic rock” in sound.

Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century later.

Anyway, Sticky Fingers was the album that helped demonstrate The Rolling Stones could well be the new kings of rock, making a great start for the whole decade, right around a time when another legendary British band had broken up, leaving the future of rock temporarily uncertain, to some extent. More on that other band later, by the way. But yes, Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century on from its initial release.

Easily one of the best double albums of all time, Electric Ladyland is probably The Jimi Hendrix Experience’s greatest album, which is a big take, even without too many albums by the band. It comes down to all those albums being phenomenal and influential, and it’s just the case that Electric Ladyland, with its size and also all the wild things it’s going for – and successfully trying to cram into – a single album.

Electric Ladyland is still such a wonderful and strange album to dive into, even today, with it coming up on nearly six decades since it first came out (safe to assume it would've been even more mind-blowing to hear back in 1968). There’s an undeniably interesting atmosphere to the album overall, and it’s surprisingly consistent, all the while saving two of the best songs until the very end: a cover of Bob Dylan’s “All Along the Watchtower” and then “Voodoo Child (Slight Return).”

This spot could’ve easily gone to Who’s Next, which has the singles and perhaps more historical/cultural importance, so consider that an honorable mention for that possibly easier pick (the one that’s slightly more straightforward, not to mention potentially simpler to praise). But Quadrophenia is even better, and maybe ever so slightly underrated, being like Electric Ladyland in the way it sustains itself for twice as long as a normal album, but then it’s also more of a concept album.

Broadly, Quadrophenia is about struggling to fit in while growing up and dealing with intense isolation, and lots of what it tackles lyrically still packs a punch. The story might not be as comprehensible as was the case with The Who’s Tommy, but it doesn’t really matter when there’s so much to feel throughout Quadrophenia, since it’s very emotional, cathartic at times, and then also a bit intense at times thanks to how full-on the introspection gets at other points.

Bruce Springsteen had a remarkable run throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since it’s that period of time that saw the release of the majority of his all-time great albums. There are albums after that era that are still pretty great, but then again, it’s also hard for anything to be close to Born to Run in terms of quality. And even compared to the other Springsteen albums of those two decades, Born to Run is special.

It does a lot to condense everything great about Springsteen into just eight largely phenomenal tracks, and while they'd all be worth mentioning, it’s especially worth mentioning the title track (an all-timer), “Thunder Road,” and “Jungleland,” the former kicking off Born to Run expertly, and the latter being one essentially perfect closing track. Everything here works, and it’s 40-ish perfect minutes of classic rock, in the end.

It says something about how great Led Zeppelin were, as a band, that you could probably put an album of theirs without “Stairway to Heaven” in a ranking like this and it would still feel right. The first half-dozen albums are all very hard to fault, but among greatness, it’s probably Led Zeppelin IV that well and truly is the greatest, and sure, “Stairway to Heaven” helps, but the other songs here are also fantastic.

Technically, this album’s untitled, but it’s easier to call it Led Zeppelin IV after the band put out three self-titled albums in the couple of years before Led Zeppelin IV. There’s also a great variety of tracks here, with some not being particularly rock-heavy, but then other parts do indeed get very energetic and loud. The slower songs make for a nice contrast, and Led Zeppelin IV transitions from song to song incredibly well, with the pacing and overall flow being impeccable.

There are arguments to be made for several other albums by The Beatles that aren’t Abbey Road to be worthy of being called the best, but it’s Abbey Road that feels like a culmination of the band’s entire run. No shade to those other albums, though, since the band was remarkably consistent and boundary-pushing, especially through the last half of the 1960s (see also Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, the self-titled or "White" Album, Revolver, Rubber Soul, and Magical Mystery Tour, if you want to count that one).

Abbey Road has some all-time great tracks on it, and the whole thing flows incredibly well, too, especially all those short songs in the second half that make up a medley. It’s got almost everything great about The Beatles packed into one album, and is also significant for being the final one the band recorded, even if Let It Be, largely recorded earlier, was the last album of the band’s to be released (and the only one that didn’t come out in the 1960s, since it dropped in 1970).

Pink Floyd is known for making albums that you can really get lost in, and The Dark Side of the Moon is especially dense and immersive in a mostly good way. “Mostly” because this album is pretty heavy-going at times, so maybe it’s not for every mood, yet it’s for a lot of them, and it is overall about life quite broadly, helping it be a little easier to get into than some of the band’s other great (and also ambitious) rock albums.

So, it’s great within Pink Floyd’s discography, and then when judged on its own, The Dark Side of the Moon is still phenomenal and everything. It’s just such a quintessential album – rock or otherwise – that it’s hard to know how to even praise it or convince someone who’s yet to listen to it that they really need to get on it. Also, it’s probably the kind of thing almost everyone’s heard at this point, so maybe it’s an odd recommendation for present purposes. Still, uh, maybe re-listen to it before you die? Yeah, there we go. That works.

If you want to find great David Bowie songs, you can go as far back as the 1960s, since “Space Oddity” came out then and is an obvious classic, but if you want essential albums of his, it’s best to start in the 1970s. He kept going beyond that decade, of course, with his final album (Blackstar) even being a masterpiece, but the most masterful of his masterpieces is probably The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars.

And since no one has really ever done it better than Bowie, why not also call it the best classic rock album of all time? The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars is just too good, with everything being perfect to the extent that it’s hard to know what else to say. Why bother saying anything when you can just listen? Thankfully, The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars never really gets old, either.

Harry Bosch's New Replacement In The Lincoln Lawyer Season 5 Clarified By Michael Connelly

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Lincoln Lawyer's final season is leaving Harry Bosch out of the case.

Netflix's The Lincoln Lawyer and the multiple series that feature Titus Welliver's Harry Bosch on Prime Video all come from the same author. Bestselling writer Michael Connelly has penned several novels starring Bosch, who Welliver has played in Bosch, Bosch: Legacy, and Ballard; and Mickey Haller, who is the star of The Lincoln Laweyer, portrayed across four seasons thus far by Manuel Garcia-Rulfo. The two characters are not only connected by the fact that they were created by the same author, but also due to how Bosch and Haller are half-brothers in Connelly's novels. However, Bosch is being replaced for season 5.

On the Northwest Passages conversation series shared by The Spokesman-Review, Connelly addressed Bosch's replacement in The Lincoln Lawyer season 5.

According to the writer, very early in the final season of the Netflix legal thriller series, Garcia-Rulfo's Mickey Haller will find out that he has a half-sister, Emi Finch, played by Cobie Smulders, continuing the cliffhanger from season 4's finale, where the mysterious character claimed to be Haller's sister. Connelly claims that she is a detective, replacing Bosch's dynamic with Haller in this adaptation of the 7th book in the franchise, Resurrection Walk. The author also claims that the change works well. Check out Connelly's full quote below:

Michael Connelly: "So, in this season – it's not too much of a spoiler alert, it happens right away – Mickey finds out he has a half-sister who's a detective. Like, where'd they get that idea? So a relationship is exploited in the fifth season. At the same time, it's based on a book called Resurrection Walk, which Bosch was in, and now it's Mickey's half-sister instead. And it worked out pretty well."

The decision to replace Bosch with a new original character in The Lincoln Lawyer really seems to have worked as well as Connelly suggests it did, as Netflix already has plans for how to tap further into it. According to a report from Deadline on August 12, Netflix is exploring a spin-off to The Lincoln Lawyer starring Smulders as Emi Finch. The Lincoln Lawyer executive producers Ted Humphrey, Dailyn Rodriguez, and Matthew J. Lieberman were revealed to be writing the script for the potential spin-off. Deadline claimed that Netflix loved the team's pitch and fast-tracked a script.

Is It Over for DAPT After Left Atrial Appendage Closure?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

— Antithrombotic monotherapy worked at least as well at 6 months

Antithrombotic monotherapy was similar to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6-month outcomes after left atrial appendage (LAA) closure, the SIMPLAAFY trial showed.

The incidence rate of combined all-cause death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding at 6 months was 5.1% with reduced-dose non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs), 3.3% with aspirin alone, and 6.9% with DAPT, reported Saibal Kar, MD, of Los Robles Hospital and Medical Center in Thousand Oaks, California, at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation.

Statistically, the strategy of reduced-dose NOACs was at least as good as DAPT (risk ratio [RR] 0.75, 95% CI 0.48-1.18), as was aspirin alone (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.29-0.82).

"These findings support the simplification of antithrombotic management after uncomplicated LAA [closure] and may be individualized according to competing bleeding and device thrombosis risks and patient preferences," Kar said.

The study was simultaneously published in JAMA.

Among the secondary analyses in the trial, there was no excess stroke at 6 months with the aspirin or NOAC monotherapy regimens. However, bleeds alone gave both an edge over DAPT, while low-dose NOAC therapy stood out for a relatively low incidence of device-related thrombus (DRT) at 45 days (0.9% vs 3.4% with DAPT and 2.7% with aspirin).

As it stands, standard practice varies widely for antithrombotic therapy after LAA closure on account of a "lack of legitimate evidence" for guidance, said session discussant Mohamad Alkhouli, MD, of the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota.

"Aspirin seems to emerge a winner," Alkhouli suggested, based on SIMPLAAFY, while he noted that "the jury is still out" for NOACs.

Complicating the picture is the ANDES trial, which found that after LAA closure, short-term NOACs were no better than DAPT at preventing DRT at 60 days, and were associated with even more DRTs at 1 year (5.2% vs 2.3%, P=0.154).

Kar said these data gave him pause on making a blanket recommendation for NOACs after LAA closure.

Meanwhile, 12-month results from SIMPLAAFY are still pending.

"The fact is that we really didn't know what to do with these patients," said Kar. "What we will do beyond 1 year, I don't know."

SIMPLAAFY included patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who had the Watchman FLX Pro LAA occluder successfully implanted. The investigators randomized 1,857 patients 1:1:1 to aspirin 81 to 100 mg alone, initial DAPT for 6 months (followed by aspirin thereafter), or initial reduced-dose NOACs -- 2.5-mg apixaban (Eliquis) twice daily or 10-mg rivaroxaban (Xarelto) daily -- for 3 months (followed by aspirin thereafter).

Across the three study groups, mean age was 76 years, 43% were women, mean CHA2DS2-VASc score was 4.2, and mean HAS-BLED bleeding risk score was 2.6.

Acute events through 7 days were rare in general. Rates of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding were 1.0% with aspirin alone, 1.6% with DAPT, and 1.5% with low-dose NOACs; major bleeding rates were 0.2%, 0.5%, and 0.2%, respectively.

The proportion of participants adhering to their assigned medications (for 80% or more days) was 87.9% with aspirin alone, 76.8% with DAPT, and 85.3% with reduced-dose NOACs. "Both monotherapy treatments were better tolerated than DAPT," Kar noted.

A subgroup analysis indicated that people ages 75 and older had disproportionately better outcomes on aspirin (RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18-0.64) or reduced-dose NOACs (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31-0.93).

LAA imaging at 45 days suggested no differences in the Watchman device seal across groups.

Kar stressed that only uncomplicated LAA occluder implants were included in the trial, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Some high-risk populations were also excluded (e.g., those with severe kidney disease, recent stroke, myocardial infarction, or major bleeding).

Of note, there was no prespecified comparison of the two monotherapy arms.

Hong Kong artists create a poetic moment in Venice

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kingsley Ng’s installation Laundry Nocturne (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

For Fermata: Hong Kong in Venice, the Hong Kong-born artists Kingsley Ng and Angel Hui are training visitors’ attention on the quiet moments and subtle details connecting both cities. The exhibition, marking the 25th anniversary of Hong Kong’s first participation in the Venice Biennale, is curated by the Hong Kong Museum of Art (HKMoA) and continues until 22 November. It is housed in a historic Venetian residential building in the floating city’s Campo della Tana neighbourhood, a setting that underlines the meditative, lived-in quality to the works.

“What we hope visitors take away from the exhibition is a moment of reflection,” says Dr Maria Mok, the director of the HKMoA. “Through Kingsley and Angel’s attention to everyday details, we hope visitors feel invited to slow down and become more attentive to their surroundings, even within the hustle and intensity of urban life.”

One of Kingsley Ng’s installations, for instance, Laundry Nocturne (2026), consists of a projection and soundscape that evoke the play of light across laundry hung between buildings. It was inspired by the artist’s experience visiting Venice, where billowing laundry on clotheslines is still common, though for Ng it also evoked memories of Hong Kong.

Kingsley Ng in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“The laundry becomes a point of entry into a wider soundscape of nighttime labour: recordings from a wastewater treatment plant, a newspaper-printing facility and a postal sorting centre evoke the work that continues while much of the city sleeps,” Ng says. “These modest, often overlooked processes that keep a city alive do not only connect Venice and Hong Kong. They are like a low hum in almost every city, to be picked up by an ear that doesn’t only listen to loud grandeur.”

For her installations, Angel Hui shines a spotlight on humble features of Hong Kong’s everyday life, including goldfish in plastic bags and a neighbour’s ornate but faded window frame. In her magical rendering, glowing plastic bags embroidered with goldfish motifs are suspended overhead, while a handwrought iron window made by Hui and traditional metalsmiths rotates slowly in the gallery.

“In Hong Kong, a goldfish bag is an everyday object; in Venice, it seemed almost precious—delicate and exposed,” Hui says. “The experience reminded me that a work does not need to be grand to hold meaning. Sometimes, its poetry lies in inviting us to look more attentively, and to meet the familiar with tenderness.”

Angel Hui’s installation I Would Like to Open a Window for You (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

Both artists, through their site-specific installations, encourage visitors to mark a pause while heightening their attention to details of light, sound, colour and texture. This invitation to contemplation is echoed in the exhibition’s title, Fermata, which is a type of musical symbol instructing a performer to hold a note or a pause for an extra moment, and the term for the places where Venetian vaporettos stop to let off and pick up passengers. For Hui, this title and its dual meanings resonate across the exhibition’s disparate locales.

“The difference in pace is pronounced: Hong Kong often presses forward, while Venice seems to make room for pause,” she says. “This is why Fermata—a moment of stopping, of stillness—felt so resonant. In both cities, the ordinary endures, and in that endurance, everyday objects acquire a quiet radiance.”

The exhibition’s title and installations also complement the theme of this year’s Venice Biennale, In Minor Keys, set by the late curator Koyo Kouoh.

Angel Hui in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“I come to see the works as experiences to return to and live alongside,” Ng says. “For me, that is one meaning of In Minor Keys: making time for the ordinary and immediate—the air, light and weather around us—and becoming more attentive to our presence within them.”

The artists’ ability to turn familiar objects and moments into tableaux where time pauses and senses are heightened is key to the exhibition’s power. It allows their installations to resonate across localities, from the Venetian Lagoon to Hong Kong’s cityscape.

“The artworks merge into the Venetian neighbourhood, from laundry hanging across nearby alleys and above our courtyard exhibition space to the view of the neighbourhood outside Kingsley’s Laundry Nocturne gallery, to wrought-iron windows that echo Angel’s work,” Dr Mok says. “All these details are blurring the boundary between the exhibition and its surroundings. They also show how small moments of daily life can quietly synchronise across different cities and audiences.”

For Billy Joel, New York is more than just a place. It’s an inextricable part of what makes the singer who he is. “My speech patterns are New York. The music that I like, it was all New York–based. The Bronx native grew up in Hicksville, a middle-class suburb of Nassau County on Long Island. His family struggled to make ends meet. “There was poor, and then there was Billy Joel poor. According to Joel’s sister Judy, no matter how hard times were, their mother Rosalind insisted he take piano lessons. His talent “had to be nurtured,” Judy said in the HBO doc. “She knew that he was going to be something someday.”

Before he indeed became something, Joel worked on an oyster boat. “I used to look up at this mansion on the hill and wonder what it would be like to live in a house like that,” he said in the documentary, referencing the waterfront property on Centre Island he nicknamed MiddleSea. “I used to think, ‘They’re rich bastards. They never had to work a day in their life.’ Well, I own that house now. It’s not finished yet. And neither am I.”

Read on for a glimpse into the Grammy winner’s life, from his early days in New York to his brief stint in Los Angeles and return home.

Joel’s music career took the Long Island native to Los Angeles in the early 1970s, and he signed with Columbia Records in 1973. After three years in LA, he and his first wife, Elizabeth Weber, returned to his home state, where he completed his fourth studio album, Turnstiles. “I wanted to get back to New York and make more of a rock and roll album,” Joel explained in And So It Goes. “I was on a bus on my way back to Highland Falls, New York. Elizabeth found us a house up on the Hudson River, and it was fall. It was just gorgeous. The colors were ablaze, the trees were, like, gold and red, and I remember thinking, ‘This is the New York State that I loved when I was a kid. This is magic. The singer-songwriter poses with some of his records in January 1978.

Joel’s fifth studio album, 1977’s The Stranger, was a runaway success—but the singer, pictured alongside his accolades in New York in 1978, told Playboy a few years later that his newfound fortune didn’t faze him. “I read these stories about Peter Frampton’s multimillion-dollar mansion, 15 Rolls-Royces and all that,” he said. “Who cares? Joel, his then-wife Elizabeth, and their dog in 1978.

Joel and Elizabeth at their Long Island residence.

The musician mixing tapes in the music studio of his New York home.

Boating represented “freedom,” Joel said in his documentary. “I am the captain of my ship.”

That was the plan, the one I told you about. But the truth is, by the time I hit Maine, I’d stopped believing it.

I had my reasons, and they were good ones.

Heading south would have meant finishing in December and a flip within my flop to get through the Smokies before snow turned the trail into a post-holing nightmare.

Then there were my spring classes to prep (I still teach), my wife’s birthday, an election to vote in, and one more Boundary Waters trip to take. Plus, I looked forward to resuming my barista job. I was excited about improving my latte art and counter skills.

Better to come back next year, I told myself. Better weather, more time, and a straight shot to Georgia. I would finish the first week of November. Perfect.

Unlike Killington in 2024, getting off the AT this time wasn’t a surrender. It was a conscious choice. I would be back next summer, but I would return as a section hiker. And to my surprise, that mattered. A lot.

Many of us deal with depression after getting off trail. I am a member of this sad clan. On trail I made new friends, shared parts of myself that hadn’t seen the light of day in years, shed pounds, and became crazy-fit while eating like a joyful pig.

Being on trail expanded my life and gave it purpose by shrinking it. The daily routine of miles and chores, the resupply stops of splurge and cleansing, and the slow accretion of accomplishment.

But now, back home and staying, that bigger life has abruptly ended. The better version of myself, the one who crushed miles despite the bad joints, is slowly fading.

I’ve been following Guru on Instagram, the guy in the kilt I hiked the 100-Mile Wilderness and summited Katahdin with. Seeing his photos of autumn sunsets, fall-stripped trees and leaf-strewn trails, I can smell the damp leaves, feel the cold air. I think, “That could have been me.”

There are no guarantees I’ll come back and finish. Not for lack of desire, but because of my waning ability. My arthritis, my stamina, and who knows what other shoe might drop between now and next July.

I now believe I should have kept going. It’s not just that I can’t trust my body; it’s like I broke a promise to myself.

When I set out to hike the Triple Crown in 2022, the subtext read “thru-hike.” My dad’s passing, and my AT attempt in 2024 that followed, changed the rules. It seemed that just finishing, no matter the method, was all that mattered.
