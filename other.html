Forget Butter And Oil: Cook Steak In This Rich Ingredient For A Better Crust & Beefier Flavor

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When preparing steak, cooks often focus on selecting the best cut, then preparing it with a specific method to an ideal doneness. Less emphasis is placed on the fat employed for the task — although what you pour into the pan also impacts both the flavor and texture of your meal. So skip standard pantry staples like butter and oil, and try a version with beef tallow instead.

So — what is beef tallow? It's essentially rendered beef fat, an ingredient that lends numerous advantages to steak night. Foremost is the ultra-savory profile: Tallow is made by slowly cooking down beef trimmings, resulting in a rich, meaty taste. This staple works as a flavor enhancer in addition to a cooking medium, complementing the deep, savory character of steak. And if you buy a higher-quality grass-fed variety, you may impart even more complexity, introducing earthy and grassy notes to your dining experience.

In parallel, beef tallow helps create a mouthwatering crust. The ingredient boasts a higher smoke point than butter and some other fats. So whether you're searing on high heat or finishing the steak in the oven, the fat can withstand high temperatures, enabling browning and Maillard reactions that create a terrific crust. That adds up to an elevated steak experience, all from swapping out the fat that goes into the pan.

Preparing a steak with beef tallow doesn't require any special accommodations. The fat is solid at room temperature and can be shelf-stable when properly stored, making it an easy pantry addition. You'll only need about a tablespoon per portion: Simply spoon the tallow into the skillet, where it'll melt and cover the cooking surface. It's a cooking medium that's especially effective on cast iron — whether a griddle or a pan — as it can also be used to season the cookware. You can also use tallow when grilling by coating both the cut itself and the grill grates with a thin layer of beef fat.

Any steak cut can be prepared in tallow; just note that it lends a rich flavor. So if you're making a mild-tasting filet mignon, for example, the addition could be overpowering. It's better to pair tallow with robust cuts like a ribeye, porterhouse, or New York strip, thereby producing an especially beef-forward meal. Otherwise, there's no need to worry about the details. Simply add a spoonful to your pan and sear away; this staple makes for a dependably delicious steak experience.

Swatara Treasure Hunt

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I battle rocks and falling temperatures while searching for a lost item on trail.

My plans are rarely flexible, until they are. I checked in with Pebbles last night to see what she, Rebar, and Greasetrap had planned (Soaked completed his hike the previous day, huge congrats to him!). They planned to slack-packing 15 miles starting from Swatara Gap and heading south. I would be doing the same – but I had the section heading north scheduled first. Not a big deal, I could just swap them. However, my section south of Swatara Gap called for 24 miles of hiking. I would need to start early and hike flat out all day to make it before sunset. I knew that isn’t really their style, which is valid. I had made up my mind and was sticking to my plan. I was up early and decided to head towards town to buy a coffee. While enjoying my hot beverage in my car I decided to take one last look at both itineraries. I might not be able to hike with them, but simply flipping my current section might allow me to pass them early in the day. Eventually I have to earn my trail name, right? I set a course for Swatara Gap and was on trail close to 8:00AM.

I texted my change in plans to Pebbles as I made my way down the trail. No sooner had I switched my phone into airplane mode and put it back in my pack than 3 hikers appeared ahead. Ca-caw! The group chimed. Ca-caw! I called back. Soon we were catching up and discussing our future hiking plans and Rebars new kicks. His previous Altras had taken a beating on the trail, I recall him putting some kind of indestructible adhesive on them during a previous encounter. He now had a snazzy new pair of Altras and was clearly enjoying the upgrade. After chatting for a few minutes we agreed to keep in close contact since I’ll soon be skipping ahead of them to close out my hike. The trail crosses the Swatara Creek on the 19th century Waterville Bridge, an interesting piece of industrial era engineering.

As I approached the bridge, I felt my phone vibrate. I had left it on since I had parked my car in a lot that requires calling the state park if you intend to leave your vehicle there overnight. I thought I might get a call back since I left a message. Instead I saw that it was Pebbles calling me. “So, you know how you like treasure hunts? Well, Rebar left a dry bag at the last campsite…”. For the third time, I was going to help the crew out by retrieving an object they’d left behind. I think I could make a living doing this professionally given the dozens of items reported missing on FarOut. Drive to the nearest trailhead, hike out and retrieve the item, drive it up the trail or ship it back to its owner, profit. Of course, for friends I’ll stick with the rate of a few guava candies. A few miles later I was staring at a bag resting neatly by a fire pit, it was definitely Rebar’s, my easiest find yet!

The trail had leveled off onto another long ridge, but the rocks made sure things weren’t too easy for me. The rocks and cold became the theme of the day. I had endured many cold days but not one single time had I ended a day with my sleeping layers still on. Hiking always warmed me up a ton and often after 30-minutes of hiking I’d shed them and hike in my shorts and sun hoodie. The climb out of Swatara Gap warmed me up and had me considering stopping to make the adjustment. But I wanted to see how I felt once the climbing stopped and I was just moving along the mostly level ridge. My temperature returned to a comfortable level quickly, so I was content to keep moving and keep the layers on. There were a handful of views along the ridge, but otherwise minimal points of interest aside from some boulder fields.

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

121pilot, our resident commercial airline pilot on Live And Let’s Fly, examines what we know about the crash of Learjet 60 N722BD and pushes back against the rush to blame its young first officer, Nathalia Filgueiras.

The crash of N722BD, a Learjet 60 operating on a charter flight, has drawn enormous attention. Much of that is understandable. We have extremely dramatic video of the aircraft descending almost vertically at a very high rate of speed, along with equally dramatic ATC audio recordings.

As is typical of the aviation YouTube community, many of whom I respect, people have been rushing to get videos out about this accident. What I really don’t care for, however, are the clickbait titles. Titles like “Could Any Pilot Have Saved This Learjet 60?” or “Lear 60 Florida Crash: It Happened Before” or “Expert Pilot Explains What Likely Caused SHOCKING Plane Crash in Florida” by Megyn Kelly.

These kinds of titles make me angry because, regardless of the actual content of the videos, they are misleading. The simple fact is that we know very little about this accident at this point, and certainly not enough to be speculating about a probable cause.

The aircraft, a Learjet Model 60 with nine people aboard, reported moderate turbulence and icing at Flight Level 350 and requested a climb to Flight Level 370. Moments later, the aircraft departed controlled flight, eventually entering a descent at an extraordinarily high rate of speed.

We know that the NTSB has recovered components of the horizontal stabilizer that separated from the aircraft, and widely circulated video appears to show the aircraft without this crucial component.

What we DON’T know is whether the aircraft flew into a thunderstorm. The weather imagery I’ve seen to date comes from sources that have time lags built into them and don’t necessarily represent the real-time weather picture. We don’t know what caused the horizontal stabilizer to separate, or even when that happened. Did it separate during the dive, or was its failure what initiated the dive? That’s an important distinction, and right now it’s an unknown.

We know the Learjet 60’s wing is sensitive to ice accumulation and that the aircraft is susceptible to a phenomenon called Mach tuck. We don’t know whether either played any role in this accident, or even whether the aircraft’s critical surfaces had accumulated ice.

We know the aircraft had been operating at higher altitudes, with a reported descent from Flight Level 410 to 350 before the final sequence of events. But without knowing the aircraft’s speed, configuration, weight, and other performance parameters, we cannot draw meaningful conclusions about its operating margins.

We know the captain was 55 years old and very experienced. We know the first officer was 20 years old and relatively inexperienced, but fully qualified to act as a second in command.

We know the CVR, or cockpit voice recorder, has been recovered, but we don’t yet know whether its contents can be successfully recovered or what information it might provide. We also know the aircraft was not equipped with a flight data recorder, or FDR. That means information about the aircraft’s speed, heading, pitch, roll, system status, and other parameters may remain unknown or will have to be reconstructed using external data sources and whatever information can be gleaned from the CVR.

But perhaps the most disturbing part of this entire event, aside from the tragic loss of life, has been the reaction of so many people who couldn’t explain the difference between a slat and a flap, yet have felt perfectly qualified to judge the performance of the first officer based on her age and gender.

In the Megyn Kelly video, she questions whether someone who isn’t even old enough to drink should have been acting as a pilot.

I think the better question is why we allow 18-year-olds to join the military and go into combat, but apparently don’t think they’re old enough to have a beer. Why can a 20-year-old warrant officer fly a Black Hawk full of troops into the maelstrom of a hot landing zone, but we think he or she can’t be trusted with alcohol?

Even more disgusting have been some of the reactions to the ATC recordings.

First, let me suggest that if you haven’t already listened to them, don’t. Second, and most importantly, as a 56-year-old male airline captain, I hear absolutely nothing in that recording, either in her voice or in her actions, that merits criticism.

I don’t think people who haven’t experienced it can appreciate just how incredibly violent and terrifying that initial descent must have been. In one recording, we go from a seemingly calm situation, with the crew requesting a climb, to a loss of control and a reported descent of approximately 8,000 feet per minute in about 10 seconds. Just 16 seconds later, the aircraft is in an overspeed condition, and the first officer reports a descent rate of 20,000 feet per minute. That’s an absolutely eye-watering number. It defies belief and would be utterly terrifying to any pilot. About 10 seconds later, she reports that they are going to crash into the ground. Not long after that, someone can be heard in the background reciting the Lord’s Prayer.

I don’t know this for certain, of course, but my suspicion is that somewhere during this sequence the crew became aware that the horizontal stabilizer had separated and that they had lost the ability to recover the aircraft.

If there is anything to be said about her performance as a pilot, it’s that she was clearly doing her job right up to the end.

We teach Aviate, Navigate, Communicate. Aviate means flying the airplane. Navigate means determining where you need to go and how to get there. Communicate means keeping the appropriate people informed.

In this situation, the captain was presumably concentrating on flying the aircraft while the first officer was communicating with ATC. That is exactly what we expect from a two-person crew in an emergency, assuming those were their respective roles at the time.

What’s more, in the absence of an FDR, the information she provided to ATC may prove absolutely crucial to understanding what happened. We may eventually discover that her actions and communications were instrumental in allowing the NTSB to piece together this accident. That information could well save lives in the future.

I also find it interesting, and troubling, that contrary to the media’s usual fixation on the captain, as though he’s flying the airplane solo, the exact opposite has happened here. The first officer has become the focus of an extraordinary amount of attention, even though we have not established who was flying the aircraft.

It’s been my absolute privilege to fly with a number of female pilots throughout my career, and not one of them has ever done anything less than a sterling job. Frankly, the airplane doesn’t care about your gender or the color of your skin. And when it comes to aviation, that’s exactly how it should be. We should care about qualifications and competence. That’s it.

If I step onto my jet tomorrow and find a young female first officer waiting for me, I’ll be just as happy to have her there as I would a young male. At this point in my career, flying with a young and relatively inexperienced first officer gives me the opportunity to act as a mentor and pass along some hard-won flying wisdom. That’s something I genuinely enjoy. Nathalia had earned her place in that cockpit. Not one iota of the solid evidence we have right now leads me to believe that this accident can or should be blamed on her.

It may well turn out that crew errors figure prominently in the probable cause. We simply don’t know yet. But if that turns out to be the case, it’s important to remember that the captain was the pilot in command. He was responsible for the operation of that aircraft, and the first officer was serving under his command. If poor decision-making put the aircraft into a situation from which there was no escape, the captain would bear the primary responsibility for those decisions.

But that’s a hypothetical. And right now, we don’t know that any such decisions were made.

We owe the nine people who lost their lives, their families, and the people who will eventually investigate this accident something better than speculation, sensationalism, and a rush to judgment.
