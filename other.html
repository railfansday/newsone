These Rare Pokémon Cards Are No Longer Eligible for PSA Grading

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

English-language Mega Hyper Rare cards have a manufacturing issue

Mega Evolution returned to the Pokémon Trading Card Game with the launch of the Mega Evolution series in 2025. In this expansion, collectors can find extremely rare Mega Evolution Pokémon EX cards known as Mega Hyper Rare cards in English and Mega Ultra Rare cards in Japanese. These holographic secret cards show alternative artwork of Pokémon's Mega Evolutions on a beautiful gold-etched display.

Given how rare these cards are, most collectors lucky enough to find them quickly sent them to PSA for grading. Unfortunately, in many cases, this will no longer be possible. On Nov. 25, 2026, the PSA announced that it has stopped grading English-language Mega Hyper Rare Pokémon cards effective immediately.

In an official blog post, PSA explains that some English-language Mega Hyper Rare Pokémon cards are defective, and therefore, none can be accurately graded. Card grading experts "identified a manufacturing issue that can cause the gold foil on the front of these cards to deteriorate over time, leaving visible patches or fading." While the severity of the defect varies from card to card, it's impossible to determine whether a card will eventually be affected by it and deteriorate. The issue can arise even if the card is properly stored and encapsulated.

"A card can begin deteriorating after it’s graded, even with no visible signs at first, so we can’t assign a grade that will reliably hold. Continuing to grade these cards wouldn’t meet the standard of accuracy and consistency that collectors rely on us for."

Grades already assigned to English-language Mega Hyper Rare cards by PSA will not be revoked and can be submitted for Review and Reholder service, allowing collectors to reevaluate their condition. Cards in transit or submitted for grading will be returned ungraded, and PSA will waive the grading service fee as compensation.

On the bright side, PSA states that Japanese-language Mega Ultra Rare Pokémon cards do not appear to be defective, as they were printed at a facility that uses different techniques, and can be submitted for grading for the time being.

It is currently unknown how this will affect card prices. It could go either way. The price of defective cards might rise before the manufacturing error is fixed, or it could fall sharply, as collectors begin their search for a pristine Japanese MUR card or look for an already graded English-language card instead.

There are dozens of things to discover throughout the opening chapter of Grand Theft Auto 6, from unique weapons and clothing items to optional missions involving some of Jason's closest friends. However, unlike many of the game's side activities, some of these encounters and rewards are permanently missable if you progress too far in the main story.

While you can eventually return to the Leonida Keys, many of the activities available during Chapter 1 will no longer be accessible. There are also several mission-specific rewards and collectibles that you'll only have one opportunity to acquire. If you're looking to experience everything GTA 6 has to offer, you'll want to take your time before moving on to Chapter 2.

The official point of no return in Chapter 1 is the "Tourist Trap" mission, which becomes available after completing "Recalibration." You'll eventually see an objective to "Take Up Mattia's Offer," which will kick off a sequence of three consecutive story missions that lead directly into Chapter 2.

However, there are several earlier points in the story where specific activities and rewards become unavailable. It's worth keeping multiple manual saves throughout Chapter 1, especially since GTA 6 doesn't currently feature a traditional mission replay system. You don't need to complete every activity in Chapter 1 to progress through the game, but anyone looking to acquire every unique item or experience every optional encounter should be careful about advancing the main story.

The US Army is moving to resume helicopter training flights in airspace near Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport (DCA) as soon as the week of December 6, according to a recent report by Politico. That timeline comes less than two years after the January 29, 2025 midair collision between an Army Sikorsky UH-60 Black Hawk and a PSA Airlines regional jet operating as American Airlines Flight 5342 over the Potomac River, a disaster that killed all 67 people aboard both aircraft.

Training operations in the corridor were halted in May 2025 after an Army Black Hawk came too close to Reagan National and forced approaching commercial airliners to abort landings. Resuming training requires Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) approval, and the service had not yet filed a formal request with regulators even as military officials briefed congressional staff on their plans.

Existing Federal Aviation Administration regulations explicitly exclude routine training from essential flight categories in the restricted zone around Reagan National. Under interim final rule 2026-01226, which took effect on January 23, 2026, and permanently closed Helicopter Route 4 between Hains Point and the Wilson Bridge, only rare essential flights may enter designated airspace up to 1,500 feet (457 meters) mean sea level. Those narrow exceptions cover lifesaving medical missions, active law enforcement responses to ongoing crimes, national defense or counterintelligence operations, continuity of government, and transport of the President or Vice President.

The rule specifies that proficiency evaluations, routine training, and non-essential personnel transport do not qualify as essential operations. Because the regulation bars routine training inside the designated Potomac zone, military leaders would need either a formal regulatory modification or an approved authorization pathway before standard pilot currency missions resume. Federal Aviation Administration officials opened public comment on the interim rule through February 23, 2026, indicated a final rule would follow after reviewing submissions, eliminated visual separation within five nautical miles (9.3 km) of the airport, increased staffing and oversight, and required air traffic controllers to manage commercial runway use when essential vertical-lift aircraft enter restricted airspace.

Investigations by the National Transportation Safety Board and the Army traced the January 2025 collision to overlapping operational, technical, and airspace vulnerabilities rather than a single error. National Transportation Safety Board findings and reporting by Task & Purpose highlighted tight vertical margins near low-altitude river routes, radio frequency congestion, altimeter instrumentation discrepancies, and bright urban lighting that complicated visual detection at night.

Investigators also noted that radio transmissions that might have helped crews confirm visual separation were missed or complicated by overlapping traffic. The National Transportation Safety Board issued 50 safety recommendations, including eight directed at the Army, and concluded that an Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast system could have alerted the airliner crew 59 seconds before impact. In response, the Army conducted fleet inspections, tested altimeters across Black Hawk variants, and reviewed crew separation procedures. These technical and procedural reviews reflect a broader effort to pair airframe checks with stricter altitude discipline across congested metropolitan corridors.

Meanwhile, lawmakers remain divided over final statutory mandates governing shared airspace and traffic-alerting equipment. According to The Washington Sun and Senate Commerce Committee reporting, the Rotor Operations Transparency and Oversight Reform Act passed the Senate unanimously in December 2025 but stalled in the House, though leaders announced an agreement in principle in mid-November 2026 on a framework to reconcile competing bills before the end of the year. Negotiations involving the House Armed Services Committee remain central to settling how military helicopters and fixed-wing planes share crowded corridors, while commuters and airlines watch whether flight delays or runway adjustments could return.
