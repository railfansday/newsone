Reds' Sal Stewart 'just getting started' with NL Rookie of Year award

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

LAS VEGAS — Sal Stewart had no sooner accepted the National League Rookie of the Year award than he embarked on the task of getting the Cincinnati Reds back to the playoffs next year.

Well, he planned to embark on that once the party in Miami with dozens of friends and family concludes at some point this week.

"Just a kid from Miami living his dream!" he said at the end of a video conference with Cincinnati media after becoming the ninth player in franchise history to win the award.

"You might wake up at 8. a.m., and I'm still gonna be out partying. I'll be enjoying it."

No matter how long that party might last, no matter how long a labor lockout might last once MLB's labor agreement expires Dec. 1, Stewart has already fixed his sights firmly on 2027 and helping lead the Reds from last place back to the postseason he experienced a month into his big-league career in 2025.

"My career is just getting started. I promise you that," he said. "I promise you this is just a steppingstone, and there's more to come.

"This season didn't go the way we wanted," he added, "but I feel like if I want to be the player that I want to be for this organization, (this award) is a good steppingstone to show everybody (coming up), 'Hey, I'm willing to do it. Everyone that comes up here better be willing to do it, to put your life on the line for your team.'

"I feel that will go a long way, and I feel our season will turn out better next year."

Stewart, 22, hit 32 home runs and drove in 112 runs in 2026, earning an All-Star selection and the Reds' first Rookie of the Year award since Jonathan India in 2021 – winning 20 of 30 first-place votes and beating second-place Carson Benge (seven first-place votes) of the Mets 170 points to 107. JJ Wetherholt (three) of the Cardinals was third with 99 points.

The award was a goal Stewart set for himself long before getting to spring training, even with a loaded field of impact rookies who figured to be in play across the league.

"Because so many people doubted me. So many people didn't think I could," he said. "I love that. My whole life I've been doubted. Even when I got drafted, I had people on MLB Network saying I was a bad decision. 'He didn't have a hit ability.' 'His power needed to come.' 'His defense was bad.'

"I don't forget those things," he said. "I keep all the receipts for all the people that talk. I just wanted to prove those people wrong."

The ultra competitive Stewart played all season with a level of confidence and apparent chip on his shoulder that belied his age and experience level. It especially showed up in high-leverage moments, with men in scoring position and in close-and-late game situations.

It's what led to RBI totals that led the league much of the season before Stewart eventually finished four behind the MLB leader.

Beyond the significance of being one of just seven players to play all 162 games this season, he preaches the value of an RBI stat that has fallen out of favor in this era of advanced stats and analytics – an appreciation instilled by his baseball mentors growing up and that boils down to situational hitting to try to win games.

"Whether it's a ground ball to shortstop with the infield back or whether it's a sac fly," he said. "You can't win a game if you don't drive in runs at the end of the day."

The Reds didn't have enough runs or enough wins regardless in this case. Which already seems to be Stewart's focus with the prestigious award in his back pocket.

He says he wants to get with team officials to find out what their plan is for him positionally next year – back to his original third base or staying primarily at first – then working this winter on getting better there.

And performing again, and helping lead others, to get back to the playoffs.

"I really want to win for this city," he said. "This award's great, but at the end of the day I don't like sitting down and watching the playoffs. I want to be playing in them. And that's something I've got to figure out a way to help my team do that.

"I know what we're capable of," he added. "We have the right guy at the helm (in manager Terry Francona). Tito knows what the expectation is. And he's the first one to make sure I'm living up to it, and if I'm not he's the first one calling me into his office making sure I'm living up to it. We know what it takes. We know what the expectation is. Losing's not fun, man. We do this to win."

Hundreds protest US Senate bill that would gut key section of National Historic Preservation Act

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Protesters, including members of tribal nations, at the US Capitol on 9 November to protest the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

On Monday (9 November), hundreds of protesters gathered on the West Lawn of the US Capitol in Washington, DC, to protest the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA, or senate bill S.5653), which would gut a key section of the National Historic Preservation Act. Representatives from more than 100 tribal nations, members of congress, leaders from the Association of African American Museums, the DC Preservation League, the American Institute of Architects and the Archaeological Institute of America gathered to protest a bill that would remove the requirement for federal agencies to notify and consult with the public and tribal nations about potential harms to historic sites, as stipulated in Section 106, Title III of the National Historic Preservation Act.

The BAAJA, described by New Mexico congresswoman Melanie Stansbury at the protest as a “dirty deal”, was created without tribal consultation or public input. According to California representative Jared Huffman, the 500 new pages of legislative text, which he described as “the most sweeping legislation we’ve seen on energy and environmental policy in decades”, was released just 48 hours before the Senate was set to vote on it Monday evening.

“They scheduled this vote for tonight to try to prevent something like this,” Huffman told protesters gathered on the Capitol lawn; organisers estimated around 800 people attended. “To try to prevent the American people from having a say, from looking under the hood of this sweeping legislation and understanding what’s actually it, but you came anyway.” Holding signs with illustrations of President Donald Trump’s planned Triumphal Arch in flames and “Nix 106”, protesters gathered for over two hours to listen to more than 15 speakers who argued that BAAJA would seriously threaten US history.

The 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

Greg Werkheiser, a leader of the Section 106 Coalition and co-founder of Cultural Heritage Partners, kicked off the protest. “We gather at the seat of American democracy, where the laws of this nation are written, to affirm one simple principle: the people deserve a voice in decisions that affect the places that we love,” he said. “That is the promise we have come here today to defend.”

Along with removing the requirement for federal agencies to engage with the public, the BAAJA would also make consultation with tribal nations optional, narrow what types of activity trigger a federal historic preservation review, restrict the definition of what constitutes a historic site, and remove any consideration of sensory, indirect and cumulative effects of changes to historic and sacred sites, essentially allowing federal agencies to take over tribal and state historic preservation offices that do not abide by limits and deadlines that the new bill would set. The BAAJA would even restrict what legal action people and organisations can take against the destruction of historic and sacred sites.

“For tribal nations,” Arizona congresswoman Adelita Grijalva said, “the fight is more than historic preservation. It’s about sovereignty. It’s about self-determination. It’s about the fundamental right to protect our ancestral homelands, culturally significant sites, and traditions that have existed since time immemorial.”

Protesters at the 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

In its current form, Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires federal agencies to consider the impact of federally funded building projects and requires that people receiving federal funds or permits consult with tribal nations. If passed, S. 5653 would make that consultation—the requirement that tribal nations participate in decision-making—optional.

“Our sacred places are living places of prayer, our responsibilities to them do not end at the reservation boundary,” Harry Antonio Jr, the governor of the Pueblo of Laguna in New Mexico, said at the rally. “You do not have to share our prayers to understand why those places deserve respect. Section 106 gives our governments a way to bring those concerns into federal decisions.”

Senators in favour of the bill have argued that its passage would lead to more economic opportunities. Articulating a position held by most rally attendees, Grijalva said: “Economic development does not require sacrificing our history, our culture or our sovereignty. We can create jobs without destroying sacred places. We can create jobs without sacrificing Mother Earth, our land, our water.”

Protesters at the 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

Rashida Tlaib, the congresswoman from Michigan, said the bill would be used to fast-track projects with severe environmental consequences, including data centres and gas pipelines. “This bill strips away what little tools we already have to defend ourselves from corporate polluters,” she said.

The senate’s vote on the BAAJA coincides with Trump’s push to build a 250ft-tall arch between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. On 5 November, protesters gathered at its proposed site and spoke against it during a National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC) meeting. Although the NCPC voted to approve the arch, a Washington, DC judge then immediately blocked construction for 14 days.

“The court orders stopping construction of those projects would not be possible if this BAAJA legislation becomes law,” Huffman said. “If this becomes law, the administration will use that in those pending court cases to make it much, much harder to stop Trump from advancing those and other vanity projects.”

Speakers urged protesters to call their senators, and implored senators to vote down the bill or to table a vote until the newly elected members of congress are sworn in in January.

Josh Allen has done it again. He has rewritten another NFL record.

The Buffalo Bills quarterback has tied Peyton Manning for the most combined touchdowns by a quarterback in his first 10 NFL seasons.

Yes, you read that right. His first 10 seasons. Allen is only in Year 9.

With his first touchdown pass against the Minnesota Vikings, Allen brought his career total to 322, moving even to the 322 touchdowns Manning recorded during his first decade with the Indianapolis Colts.

Of course, Allen has had a little help from his legs along the way. Manning's touchdowns came almost exclusively through the air, while Allen has been putting up numbers as both a passer and a runner since Buffalo drafted him in 2018.

But here's where the numbers get interesting.

Manning played 160 regular-season games during his first 10 seasons. Allen entered Week 9 of the 2026 season with just 135 games under his belt and tied Manning in his 136th game. That's 24 fewer games, and Allen still has the rest of this season and all of 2027 to add to his total.

Allen also moves ahead of some pretty familiar names on the all-time list, including Russell Wilson, Dan Marino, Brett Favre, and Cam Newton.

Patrick Mahomes is the only other active quarterback who can still add to his first-10-season total. The Kansas City Chiefs quarterback is in his 10th season and finished Week 9 with 301 touchdowns. He would need 21 more just to get past Manning's mark, and with Allen still adding to his own total, that target is only going to get harder to reach.

Marvel's Biggest Spider-Man Lore Reset Is Already Doomed

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Marvel wants fans to think it might be on the verge of undoing the most controversial Spider-Man storyline of the 21st century, so far, but what are the odds it actually goes that far? Let's break it down.

Essentially, the last 20 years of Spider-Man history have been dealing with the consequences of 2007's One More Day storyline. That includes the plots of the blockbuster films Spider-Man: No Way Home and Brand New Day, which took its title directly from the '08 relaunch that followed OMD, as it is disaffectionately abbreviated by generations of dissatisfied readers. Now, after almost two full decades, Marvel Comics is doing a direct sequel to the storyline, which has some people buzzing with hope, yet again, that One More Day can be totally undone.

Others are skeptical, and with good reason. Marvel has repeatedly stated over the years that One More Day will remain canon in perpetuity. Still, by making it a major plot point for not just Spider-Man lore, but also Marvel's latest Avengers storyline, the company is clearly ready to reckon with the infamous story's role in its history, and maybe try to redeem it after all these years.

Warning! Spoilers ahead for Marvel's new Avengers relaunch!

A quick recap of what has been going on in the Marvel Universe: Red Hulk caused a nuclear explosion that seemingly killed Bucky Barnes, Iron Man and the Fantastic Four are feuding, and Spider-Man has found himself the leader of a revamped Avengers roster. Oh, and Doctor Doom claimed Mephisto's throne in hell, voiding all his extant contracts in the process. Including one that was very important to the demon, for reasons we'll return to later: Mephisto's infernal contract with Peter Parker.

And another one that involves brand-new Marvel protagonist Paige Howley. In the new Avengers #1, she learns that her entire existence is the result of a deal her parents made with Mephisto. Readers have a lot more to learn about Paige, her parents, and the deal, but what is clear from the jump is that "the universe is trying to kill [her]," sending literal angels after her on a cosmic assassination mission. As Paige runs for her life, she's joined by a crucial ally: a talking book that seemingly contains all the Marvel Universe's most closely guarded secrets.

The book, Grimsby, leads her to Spider-Man and his Avengers squad, who protect her from a squad of the killer angels, after which she reveals to Spidey that he once made a pact with Mephisto "to save someone." He asks her who, while in the same panel his internal monologue is a step ahead. "I know before she says anything," the hero narrates. Under his torn mask, shocked after hearing he "made a deal with the devil" that he can't even remember, Peter Parker still knows there's only one person he'd do that for: Aunt May.

What Peter doesn't know, but which he's presumably going to find out as this story unfolds in Marvel's Avengers and Spider-Man titles, is what his side of the bargain was. Marvel is betting on that being juicy enough, dramatically, to change how fans feel about One More Day a full two decades after it changed the course of Spider-Man history forever.

One More Day was widely rejected by fans at the time of its release for more than just what it did to Spider-Man canon. It was disliked for why. The point of the story was to write Spider-Man and Mary Jane's marriage out of existence. The two had gotten married in a landmark 1987 issue, but over the next twenty years, there was always a faction of editors and writers behind the scenes at Marvel who didn't like the marriage. Who thought it was wrong for Spider-Man.

In 2007, they finally won that internal battle. One More Day mystically annulled the marriage, with Peter trading his relationship with Mary Jane to save Aunt May's life. In 2027, Peter and MJ will have officially been not married as long as they were married, and that remains a bitter pill to swallow for legions of Peter/MJ shippers. And for anyone who hates drastic retcons.

In a moment that Spider-Man fans thought would never come, Marvel just reversed the controversial 'One More Day' retcon of Peter's marriage.

What Marvel is doing now, though, is saying that there's another chapter to the story. One More Day is maligned as the end of one era in Spider-Man lore, and the start of another. Will readers feel differently about it as the beginning of a story?

Again, that's the creative gamble Marvel seems to be taking right now. And naturally, the answer to that question is going to depend largely on how this new sequel story to One More Day ends up itself, and what the era of Spider-Man beyond it looks like. And there are a couple possibilities worth exploring up front as the story gets underway.

We mentioned before that Mephisto has a specific beef with Spider-Man. That's because he believes the Webslinger's offspring is destined to destroy him. For all One More Day's faults, this does give the Parker family a grandiose role in the mythos of the Marvel Universe. And it means that Mephisto, and his successor in hell Doctor Doom, will have an important part to play in the sequel.

The dream scenario for One More Day haters is a full timeline reset. That's what One More Day did, after all, so it is certainly on the table. Which is something Marvel is counting on to keep people invested in the story. Even if that means disappointment if, and most likely when, Marvel doesn't go all the way and restore Spider-Man canon to its 2007 status quo, Parker marriage and all. That's right, readers would do well to calibrate their expectations for One More Day to be totally undone.

Marvel is teasing the possibility, but that's likely all it is. A tease. The odds are much greater that the new era of Spider-Man will be about Peter Parker sharing the burden of knowing what was lost, what he gave away, with readers. Because remember, part of the deal in One More Day involved erasing everyone's memories of the deal, Peter included. For twenty years of real time, and at least a couple years on Marvel's sliding timescale, Peter has been blissfully ignorant of the life-altering decision he made.

And so has Mary Jane, and so has Aunt May. That's probably going to change, and all of those characters are going to be changed by the truth. That's dramatically rich soil for Avengers and Spider-Man to grow the next generation of storylines out of. In that regard, it's the better outcome than a full reset, by far. But that means further encoding One More Day in canon, in ways that can't be quickly or easily undone. Ironically, that's a kind of Faustian bargain Marvel is offering Spider-Man fans, and it might be one they can't refuse.

In a Reddit thread, three Googlebook apps built by Googlers through Antigravity are shown off, and they’re pretty cool, regardless of how you might feel about the platform itself.

I had no idea the status bar on Googlebook could even be used like this, and it really makes that space a lot more useful. Of course, you have to draw a comparison here to macOS, which also uses this space for app shortcuts and tools, though BentoBox offers a different type of customization.

BentoBox is available for download via Google Play for free.

The other Mac-like app here is “Booklight,” a launcher that’s basically Apple’s Spotlight, just for Googlebook. It has a transparent design and lets you search “apps, settings, and the web.”

Supreme Court allows Trump administration to continue border wall construction

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Supreme Court on Monday cleared the way for the Trump administration to resume construction of a border wall in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas. In a four-page, unsigned opinion, the justices granted the government’s request to pause an order by a federal district judge that had temporarily halted the project. Only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson indicated, without explanation, that she would have denied the government’s request.

The dispute began earlier this year, when then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and her successor, Markwayne Mullin, issued three waivers of various environmental and conservation laws to authorize construction of the wall in the Big Bend region, which includes parts of Big Bend National Park. Noem and Mullin relied on a provision of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 that gives the DHS secretary the power to “waive all legal requirements” necessary “to ensure expeditious construction” of border walls and roads. When they announced the waivers, Noem and Mullin indicated that the Big Bend area “is an area of high illegal entry” requiring additional walls and barriers.

A group of plaintiffs opposed to the construction – an environmental group, a local landowner who works as a river guide, and a group formed to preserve a church – went to federal court to challenge the waivers’ legality. On Oct. 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone temporarily barred the Department of Homeland Security from continuing construction on the border wall in the Big Bend region “until and unless” the government complies with environmental and other applicable laws.

In a written opinion that she released on Oct. 14, Cardone reasoned that the challengers were likely to prevail on their claim that the DHS secretary did not have the authority to issue the waivers. She relied on the major questions doctrine – the idea that if Congress intends to give a federal agency the power to make decisions of vast economic or political significance, it must say so very clearly. In particular, she emphasized, the Big Bend region is not an area with a high number of illegal entries. To the contrary, she stated, although it “comprises over 25% of the U.S.-Mexico border,” it makes up only a tiny percentage (“between 0.3% and 2.2%”) of the migrants apprehended while crossing the border. Therefore, she wrote, “if the Big Bend Sector is an area of high illegal entry, so is every other sector, and the waiver authority would apply to the entire U.S.-Mexico border. If that were the case, Congress could have simply authorized construction ‘to deter illegal crossings.’” The major questions doctrine instructs courts to avoid “interpretations that would render limiting language meaningless,” Cardone concluded.

After Cardone declined to pause her ruling to give the government time to seek Supreme Court review, the Trump administration came to the Supreme Court, where it asked the justices not only to temporarily freeze Cardone’s order but also to grant review and summarily (that is, without additional briefing or oral argument) reverse it. U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer argued that Cardone lacked the power to consider the challengers’ claim based on the major questions doctrine because their complaint specifically alleged that the DHS secretary “lacked ‘clear congressional authorization’ … to issue the waivers” under the IIRIRA – which, he said, was a statutory claim that “Congress denied district courts” the authority “to entertain.” But in any event, Sauer continued, even if the DHS secretary’s power to issue waivers under IIRIRA “implicates a question of ‘vast economic and political significance,’ Congress did clearly authorize the Secretary both to build border barriers and to waive legal requirements in order to facilitate their expeditious construction.”

The challengers urged the justices to leave Cardone’s order in place. Eric Glitzenstein, a lawyer representing the plaintiffs, told the justices in a 40-page filing that if her order were paused or lifted, it would permit the Trump administration to “immediately resume blasting, bulldozing, road construction, and other border barrier-related construction across one of the most remote and ecologically and culturally significant landscapes in the United States.”

The challengers argued that the major questions doctrine applied to their claim. They emphasized that the DHS secretary’s “decision to waive myriad federal, state, local, and tribal laws across vast geography for the purpose of building border infrastructure, even through national and state parks where border crossings are miniscule, is a truly ‘extraordinary’ assertion of power.” Because Congress “did not clearly authorize the sweeping exercise of power asserted here,” they wrote, the government’s “actions therefore amount to a constitutional violation of the major questions doctrine.”

In Monday’s opinion, the justices denied the government’s request to summarily reverse Cardone’s ruling but granted its request to put it on hold. “The Government,” the court explained, “is exceedingly likely to prevail on its argument” that Cardone did not have the power to consider the challengers’ “so-called major-questions-doctrine claim.” That claim, the court emphasized, contends only “that the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority,” which is not a constitutional claim. This is true, the court continued, even if the challengers rely on the major-questions doctrine, because that doctrine is a rule used to interpret laws. “There is no such thing as a ‘constitutional’ major-questions claim.”

The other factors that courts normally consider in deciding whether to pause a lower court’s decision also point in the government’s favor, the justices said. For example, Cardone’s order “delays the Government’s construction of border barriers and roads in the Big Bend Sector” and, as a result, “impedes the Government from advancing its ‘compelling interests’ in border security and integrity.” Moreover, the government is suffering permanent harm from the costs – “nearly half a million dollars … per day” – that it is incurring while the construction is on hold, but which cannot be recovered. Finally, the court wrote, “[t]he Government’s interest … outweighs any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm alleged by” the challengers.

Call of Duty: Warzone players have been utilizing one of the oddest forms of cheating I’ve ever heard of, peeking at the skill level of everyone in the lobby through the app Warzone Agent to decide whether or not to just leave the game. Activision has announced they’re clamping down hard on the practice, looking to eliminate it going forward.

Call of Duty players have been complaining about the franchise’s skill-based matchmaking (SBMM) for years now, a system where players are matched against foes equivalent to their skill level. This is, of course, how most matchmaking systems work, but COD players have been asking for its elimination since the original Modern Warfare remake.

In order to avoid these close matches, they utilize apps like Warzone Agent to figure out the average rank of the lobby before deciding to play, a practice Warzone’s anti-cheat division, Ricochet, has officially stood against. In their release surrounding the practice of lobby shopping, “moving forward, players who leave Warzone Agent loaded will not be able to access Multiplayer modes.”

Threatening to drop the ban hammer on violators is a great way to shake the practice off, and Activision isn’t stopping there, clarifying what it considers cheating. That includes external account boosting, led by swarms of bots they’ve managed to start stomping out.

Bots plague any free-to-play game people want to put time into, and focusing more on curtailing them is a great step toward slowing the flow, even if it is just a bandage over the wound. For now, Activision has drawn their line in the sand. The only question is whether or not they’ll flinch in the face of community complaints, but they’ve stuck by their guns this long.

The community complaint around skill-based matchmaking has always struck me as incredibly odd, with these players mostly desiring a true random selection process. These random matches are, on paper, more varied, but in practice lead to matches that are total stomps for one side or the other, leaving plenty of players with a bad taste in their mouths.

Getting to be the one stomping can be great fun, but that’s also possible against players of your skill level. SBMM’s main purpose is to minimize the games that feel like complete wipes from the loser’s perspective, keeping them playing against those who are actually equivalent to them on a broad scale. It’s far from perfect, but it's better than literally just tossing a random selection of players in a room.

Without it, not only would many casual players have to gamble on getting crushed every other game, but Activision could lose those players after enough rough matches, the true reason for the practice’s continued support. Despite the complaints, Activision has clearly long since decided that skill-based matchmaking is the best middle ground for skilled and casual players alike, giving everyone designated lobbies where they’re at least promised matches that are competitive.

The latter point led to a final eight where two Legends accounted for seven decks, drawing comparisons to the domination of certain archetypes in Magic: The Gathering in recent years.

Update: As this story went live, game director Dave Guskin confirmed that Riftbound bans are due to be announced this Friday, with an effective date TBC.

The first flashpoint from the Riftbound ERC revolved around a player called Templar, who was accused on day one of deliberately slow-playing to run out the clock on opponents, so they could score points late in the game and leave opponents in a position where they couldn't respond.

Worse still, Templar reached a record 8-1, with time concerns in every match, before he was eventually disqualified from the event. There are some unsubstantiated rumors floating around on Reddit that Templar wasn't actually disqualified for his slow play, and it was instead for marking cards, another huge TCG no-no, but most players commenting on the situation believe it was for the slow play, and they're also demanding a follow-up punishment.

After Koko Lopez's ban last month, players are now calling for an example to be made of Templar, with a swift, long ban. If it's possible to prove that Templar was indeed intentionally slow-playing, and their deck was built for this exact purpose, we can see a reality where a ban is handed out, although it would likely be less than what was given to Koko Lopez, which was a much more flagrant breaking of long-standing rules and dishonesty.

Criticism is also being directed at the judges, who were asked to address pace-of-play issues in previous rounds, with players documenting how poorly some judges handled the situation and repeatedly warning Templar to speed up without taking formal action.

We can forgive Riot's mistakes just this once.

For Riftbound right now, Riven is a problem, and while card bans are expected soon to help address it before it develops further, the numbers from Stuttgart are telling. On day one, 126 decks were Riven decks; that's 43.3% of all decks at the event.

The number of Riven decks on day two fell to 47, but that was still 37% of the remaining field. Finally, in the top eight, four of the players were running Riven decks, meaning the percentage was back up to an even 50, and it just showcases the game in a negative light, because from the outside looking in, it appears as though there is little creativity in the strategy of the game, and players end up playing with cards they feel like they have to play with, rather than ones they want to play with. Ultimately, a Kennen player, Evanshrim, won the event; Kennen was the third most popular Legend at the event, with 44 total decks on day one. Outside of the top three Legends (Kai'sa being the remaining top three Legend) there were very few Legends with a meaningful following, and given how young the game is, that's quite disappointing. Riot will surely know this and will be working to balance the meta as soon as possible, especially with the first full year of tournaments now in the books, but the sooner these changes are implemented, the better.
