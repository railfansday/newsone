U15 : de la joie contre les Caladois

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Les U15 de Benjamin Petit pouvaient être heureux à la fin de leur match contre les Caladois du FC Villefranche Beaujolais. A l'issue d'une rencontre très disputée, comme souvent dans cet exigeant championnat, ils n'ont rien lâché face à un adversaire de valeur et ont su trouver les ressources pour l'emporter. Cela récompense leur volonté d'aller de l'avant, leur ténacité et leur concentration dans l'aspect défensif. Sans quelques joueurs retenus avec les U16 de Kevin De Jesus (Ilan Clapeyron, Riad Moutae, Kaïs Boukerma et Hugo Durin) tous ont su se mettre au diapason, et cette victoire est celle d'un collectif très solidaire.

L'entame du match a été stéphanoise, les Verts s'installant dans le camp caladois. Dès la 9ème minute, sur un centre venu de la gauche, Nolan Lebreton remettait pour Charly Davignon qui était devancé de peu par un défenseur. Deux minutes plus tard, un coup-franc de ce même Nolan Lebreton était capté par le gardien. Les Verts monopolisaient le ballon mais le dernier geste, souvent dans les petits espaces, manquait de justesse pour mettre de conclure. Peu à peu, Villefranche déserrait l'étreinte stéphanoise et se montrait dangereux sur coup de pied arrêté, mais Manoa Frison faisait bonne garde, notamment, à la 26ème, où il réalisait une double parade avant que Victor Hamouma ne s'interpose pour stopper la tentative adverse. Quelques minutes plus tard, Charly Davignon, qui repiquait au centre, était victime d'une faute laquelle donnait lieu à un coup-franc. Nolhan Fernandes le frappait et mettait le gardien à contribution. Peu après sur un long centre de Nolhan Fernandes, Hugo Bastide obtenait un corner. Ce corner, joué à deux, permettait à Nolan Lebreton d'adresser un long centre au deuxième poteau que le portier caladois dégageait. Les contres de Villefranche butaient sur une solide défense stéphanoise, concentrée et rigoureuse qui gérait bien les espaces et s'attachait à ressortir proprement les ballons. Le score était donc nul et vierge au repos.

La deuxième mi-temps repartait sur les mêmes bases, avec une maitrîse verte retrouvée et des situations intéressantes, sans parvenir toutefois à faire la différence.  Mais les Verts, suite à un carton blanc à la 54ème minute, se retrouvaient réduits à 10 pendant une dizaine de minutes, et ce fait de jeu freinait un peu leur dynamique. Ils allaient trouver la faille suite à un cafouillage dans la surface, avec une main adverse un accrochage sur Mathis La Rosa. Toujours est-il que l'arbitre sanctionnait cette action d'un penalty que Charly Davignon transformait, donnnt l'avantage aux Verts (68ème). La fin du match allait être plutôt hâchée avec plusieurs coups-de-pied arrêtés que Villefranche jouait directement. Les Verts se créaient cependant quelques opportunités mais un manque de justesse ne leur permettait pas de les concrétiser. Le match se terminait donc sur cette victoire stéphanoise somme toute logique, tant elle avait été bien construite.

La composition : Frison - Bergougnoux (Uneau, 50ème), L. La Rosa, Hamouma, Bastide - Wojcik (Davignon, 50ème), M.La Rosa, Lebreton - Fernandes (Madjidi, 50ème), Maganga Verrier, Davignon (Linganzi, 41ème).

La réaction de Benjamin Petit : "Le match a été serré, très disputé face à une bonne équipe de Villefranche, comme souvent dans ce championnat. Il n'y a pas de match facile et c'est un bon apprentissage pour les jeunes. Nous avons fait vingt premières minutes plutôt intéressantes, avec de la maîtrise, une volonté de défendre en avançant, un point que travaillons. Nous avons baissé en fin de première mi-temps, avec plus de difficulté à maintenir notre pression, ce qui nous a mené à défendre plus bas. En conclusion, je dirais que globalement, nous avons eu la maîtrise, mais mon regret est le manque de justesse, de percussion et d'efficacité pour créer du danger sur le but adverse et se mettre à l'abri. Je retiens notre solidité défensive, notre rigueur dans les prises en charge, notre détermination à ne rien encaisser, notamment sur les coups-de-pied arrêtés, un domaine dans lequel nous avons progressé. Il faudra néanmoins éviter certaines fautes, évitables, qui offrent des situations dangereuses à l'adversaire. C'était un match intéressant dans l'apprentissage, la gestion des temps forts et des temps faibles. Il faut toutefois  essayer d'être plus efficace dans nos temps forts tout en restant solides dans les temps faibles."

Fortnite Officially Reveals Pirate Shop Access Token Odds

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

How much are you willing to grind for each treasure?

Since Fortnite Treasure Hunters began on Nov. 1, Epic Games has been flooded with disgruntled fans wanting to know the odds of treasures that can be found in chests this season, namely the Pirate Shop Access token. Epic has finally relented, revealing the odds for each item and how much Treasure Hours influence them.

This season, the Pirate Shop Access token was introduced in Fortnite. The token is an item that, upon activation, makes every item sold in the Item Shop with in-game currency free for two weeks. It can be used twice: once in 2026 and again in 2027. This item has created an unpredictable frenzy among the community. Even though it's against Epic Games' terms of service, players have been selling accounts and offering token-hunting services at hourly rates.

The Pirate Shop Access token led fans to focus on opening chests rather than enjoying the highly-anticipated One Piece-themed season. After opening thousands of chests with no luck, fans wanted to know their chances of finding it. On Saturday, before Treasure Hours began, they spammed the hashtag ReleaseTheOdds, demanding Epic Games share the odds of each treasure in Fortnite. It finally complied.

According to Epic Games, the Pirate Shop Access token has a drop chance of 1 per 137,500 chests, which is the equivalent of 0.000007273%. Odds have not been tweaked throughout the season except for Treasure Hours timed-event — they were boosted 1.5x for the first Treasure Hour on Saturday, and 2x the second time around. The golden skin, which will rotate each week, is the rarest treasure players can find by far (1 per 1,562,500 chests), but it's safe to say that the token is still more desirable because it's worth the equivalent of thousands of dollars.

It's good that Epic Games was finally transparent with fans, but one question remains. The Solid Gold A$AP Rocky outfit has now rotated out of the weekly treasures available, replaced by Solid Gold Lil Uzi Vert. These skins are free to find during Treasure Hunters, but it is unknown whether either will be available for purchase in the future. If not, they would become the rarest cosmetics in Fortnite history.

Nov. 14 marks the last set of Treasure Hours before the launch of Grand Theft Auto 6, so if you're still grinding for the token, this is the best time to try to find it. The season ends on Dec. 5, so gamers can still search for it on Nov. 21 and 28 if they're willing to put GTA 6 aside for a couple of hours.

These Robotic Arms Are Powered by Algae. Watch How They Move

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

What if a robot could be squishy? Soft, flexible robots could be useful for a wide variety of applications, including artificial muscles and wearable devices—or really any application where a robot must be especially delicate.

This is what has fueled development of “soft actuators” that can flex like a bicep and have a fine-tuned sense of touch as in the human hand. Like your stereotypical robot, soft actuators convert energy—such as electricity, heat, or light—into mechanical motion, but they look nothing like the metal and wire constructs that the word “robot” most often evokes.

One variety of these soft robots, known as bioactuators, utilizes the functions of living cells to generate motion. These biological machines have attracted particular attention in recent years, and now a research team at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands has developed a soft robotic arm colonized by algae on its surface, and when the microbes photosynthesize, the arm moves.

“We didn’t start with a specific application. Our goal has always been to bring the research of living materials to new levels,” Marie-Eve Aubin-Tam, who led the research, explained in a press release. “We wanted to explore what becomes possible when living cells are integrated into functional materials.”

The tiny robot arms—about three-quarters of an inch long and about as thick as a credit card—are constructed of two layers of hydrogel that the algal cells live on. One of these layers responds to changes in pH, which are mediated by the metabolism of the algae. When light strikes the algae, they begin to photosynthesize. By absorbing carbon dioxide and fixing nitrogen in this process, the algae make the local pH more basic, making the responsive hydrogel swell. Conversely, when it becomes dark, the algae release carbon dioxide through respiration, returning the pH to the acidic side and the hydrogel shrinks.

The difference in the volumes of the two hydrogel layers controls the bending. According to the researchers, the pH-responsive hydrogel layer changed significantly with the algae’s activity. In acidic conditions, such as between pH 3 and 7, the layer was approximately 37 percent of its original weight, but it had quadruple its original weight at pH 11.

Other researchers have constructed soft robots that respond to pH, but typically the chemistry of all the surrounding liquid has to be altered. This is not the case for the algal bots. The photosynthetic activity has an effect that’s much more local, meaning it would be better suited to applications where minimal environmental impact is essential.

These aren’t, however, the speediest of robots. The chemical shift begins quickly, about 20 seconds after light is applied and about 10 seconds after it becomes dark, but it takes several hours for the hydrogel to bend significantly.

After their first proof of concept, the research team constructed several more algal devices. One was a four-fingered gripper that closes when exposed to light. It successfully grasped and lifted a hollow metal sphere, releasing it when the light was turned off. Another hook-shaped gripper could pick up and put down a rubber ring. These items were extremely light, weighing far less than a small paperclip, but the arms were able to grasp them with great dexterity.

These soft robots also require no external energy source other than light and no complex mechanical parts. “Microalgae are plantlike organisms that are largely self-sustaining,” Aubin-Tam said. This “gives the actuator potential for long-term operation with minimal external input. In theory, it wouldn’t require a lot of energy to run a system based on this technology.”

In the long term, the research team hopes this will contribute to the development of more autonomous, environment-responsive soft robots that could be used as automated agricultural equipment or for fluid control in lab-on-a-chip devices or really as part of any underwater robotics system.

Since the algae do not need to be genetically modified, there are none of the biosafety concerns often associated with microbe-based technologies. If anything, these are exceptionally environmentally friendly machines, since these organisms can absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere while deriving energy from sunlight.

If you're looking for the safest model year within the fourth generation, the 2026 Tucson looks like the strongest candidate. Although the 2026 Tucson won the Top Safety Pick+ award, it's worth noting that the small SUV performed poorly in the IIHS's latest whiplash prevention test. Third-gen and later Tucsons, however, performed considerably better in the test.

Keno RTP by Number of Picks: What Changes and What Doesn’t

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Home » Blitz news » Keno RTP by Number of Picks: What Changes and What Doesn’t

This article explores how keno pick counts affect RTP, hit frequency, and volatility. Using probability calculations and simulated sessions, it shows why more numbers do not necessarily mean better returns, and how paytable structure determines your long-term expected payout.

Ask keno players which pick count “pays best” and you get strong opinions: one or two numbers for steady hits, ten for the big multipliers. Both camps are usually arguing about the wrong thing.

Return to player, or RTP, is the share of every dollar wagered that a game pays back over the long run. In keno, that figure is set by the paytable, and each pick count has its own paytable. So the honest answer to “which pick count has the best RTP” depends entirely on how those tables were built. On a modern crypto board, the answer is often “none of them,” and the more useful question becomes what the pick count does to everything else.

Below, the math runs on a 40-number board that draws 10, using openly published paytables plus a few hypothetical edits. Probabilities are exact; session figures come from 20,000 simulated runs.

Traditional casino keno uses an 80-number board with 20 numbers drawn. Each spot count gets its own paytable, and operators calibrate each one separately. Published estimates put the house edge in land-based keno somewhere between 20% and 40%, and it can shift by several points from one spot count to the next. That is why old guides compare 4-spot and 7-spot tickets so closely.

Online and crypto versions changed the design brief. Many operators now publish a single RTP for the whole game and then build every pick count’s paytable to land on that number. When that is the case, the pick count stops being a lever on expected return. A well-run bitcoin casino keno table that advertises 98% should return close to 98% whether you pick one number or ten. Rounding multipliers to one decimal means no table hits its target exactly.

Before any paytable comes into it, the board itself fixes the odds. With 40 numbers and 10 drawn, each number you pick has a 1 in 4 chance of landing. Your average number of hits is simply your pick count divided by four: 0.25 hits on one pick, 1.25 on five, 2.5 on ten.

The chance of hitting every number you picked collapses even faster. One pick is 1 in 4. Five picks is 1 in 2,611. Ten picks is 1 in 847,660,528. None of this says anything about RTP yet. RTP is the sum of probability times payout across every paytable row.

Here is what that sum gives for three paytables published on a live bitcoin keno page that states a 98% RTP:

The spread between them is 0.04 percentage points. Over $10,000 of total wagers, that is a difference of about four dollars in expected return. Pick count and risk setting do not move RTP here; the gap to 98% is rounding.

The same arithmetic tells you what a fair one-pick table would look like. One pick hits 25% of the time, so a 98% table has to pay 3.92x on a hit. If a one-pick ticket somewhere pays 3x instead, it returns 75%.

If RTP stays flat, pick count changes two things: how often you get paid and how hard the balance swings.

That last line is the surprise. Five picks on this table is almost nine times as swingy as ten picks on Low, even though both return the same 98 cents per dollar. More picks does not mean more volatility; the paytable’s shape decides it.

Breaking each table’s return down row by row shows where the money comes from.

On ten picks at Low risk, the 2-, 3- and 4-hit rows deliver about 90% of the entire return. The 1,000x prize for ten hits contributes roughly 0.0001 cents per dollar, close enough to zero that removing it would not change the RTP at the second decimal. Medium risk on ten picks moves the weight up a little: the 3- and 4-hit rows carry 74%, and the 5-hit row adds another 17%.

Five picks is different. The 2-hit row carries 42.5% of the return and the 3-hit row 34%, but the 300x prize for five hits carries 11.7% on its own, the same share as the 12x row below it. On a five-pick bitcoin keno game, the top prize is a real part of what you are being paid back. On ten picks, it is decoration.

Knowing which rows carry the return shows where a stingy operator could cut quietly. Two hypothetical edits:

On ten picks at Low risk, cutting the 2-hit payout from 1.1x to 1.0x would lower RTP by 3.1 percentage points, from about 98% to about 95%. Halving the 1,000x top prize to 500x would lower it by less than one ten-thousandth of a point. The jackpot is the row players look at, and the one that barely matters.

On five picks, the picture flips. Halving the 300x prize to 150x would cost 5.7 points of RTP, which is more than the 2.8 points lost by trimming the 2-hit row from 1.5x to 1.4x. So for high counts, check the small rows; for low and middle counts, check the top prize too.

Beyond megawatts: Nuclear’s other capacity expansion challenge

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nuclear growth plans are accelerating as data centers, large-load customers, uprates, restarts, extended operations and new generation projects reshape the industry’s outlook. But the challenge of capacity expansion extends far beyond megawatts. Every utility must also expand the capacity of its workforce.

The workforce challenge is not simply about hiring more people. Recruiting is critical, but so are retention strategies, qualification timelines, knowledge transfer processes and the availability of experienced employees and contractors. Utilities must continue operating existing plants safely and reliably while extending asset life, modernizing infrastructure, preparing for growth and developing the next generation of workers. In many cases, the same people are asked to do all of it.

An experienced engineer supporting an uprate may also be needed for aging management reviews. The supervisor helping qualify new employees may also be responsible for outage preparation. The skilled craft resources needed for capital projects are often the same resources supporting ongoing plant reliability and maintenance activities. To execute an ambitious growth plan, your workforce needs support and clear expectations as it navigates the demands of the present and the future.

That reality makes employee engagement and retention critical operational necessities.

Across the industry, organizations are managing a growing list of demands. Employees are operating plants, supporting outages, resolving equipment issues, onboarding new hires, transferring knowledge, implementing modernization efforts and preparing for future growth. Subsequent license renewal and long-term operations programs add further demands through inspections, evaluations, documentation and equipment replacement activities.

At the same time, many organizations continue to face shortages in experienced operators, engineers, technicians and other critical roles. Long qualification timelines and retirements only increase the pressure.

Growth introduces another challenge that is less visible but equally important: cognitive load. Experienced employees must continually sort through competing priorities, interpret changing guidance and determine where to focus limited time and attention. When this becomes a constant state, organizations risk change fatigue, burnout and disengagement.

For nuclear leaders, the consequences extend beyond employee experience. The loss of experienced personnel can slow qualification programs, reduce knowledge transfer, limit an organization’s ability to respond to emergent issues and increase execution risk across both life-extension and capacity-expansion efforts.

As a result, workforce planning must address not only how organizations attract talent but also how they retain, support and fully leverage the people already in place.

Three leadership responsibilities can help organizations strengthen workforce capacity while navigating growth and modernization: simplify priorities, enable leaders and engage employees.

A common challenge in growing organizations is that new priorities are added without older priorities disappearing. Individually, each initiative may be justified, but collectively, they can create confusion about what matters most.

Organizations should begin with an integrated view of operational commitments, major projects and workforce capacity. Leaders must identify where critical resources are supporting multiple high-priority activities and make deliberate decisions about sequencing, staffing, or scope rather than assuming employees can absorb additional work indefinitely.

One nuclear organization recently addressed this issue by consolidating multiple layers of strategic imperatives into a small number of employee-facing priorities. These priorities allowed employees to make a clearer connection between their daily work and fleet strategy, while leaders retained the layers behind the scenes. Simplifying the message at the right level reduced complexity without reducing rigor.

Even clear priorities require interpretation. Supervisors and managers play a critical role in helping employees understand what work matters most, where trade-offs exist and how decisions connect to organizational goals.

To do this effectively, leaders need more than communication materials. They need context, practical guidance, opportunities for discussion and the authority to resolve or escalate conflicts when competing priorities emerge.

Workforce plans should also acknowledge the time experienced employees spend mentoring, coaching and qualifying newer workers. Organizations cannot develop future capability if their most knowledgeable employees are fully consumed by current demands. Creating capacity for knowledge transfer is an investment in future operational performance.

Employees closest to the work usually identify constraints long before they appear on a project dashboard or management report.

Whether discussing outage activities, modernization efforts, operational improvements, or growth initiatives, leaders should regularly seek employee input on assumptions, risks, bottlenecks and execution challenges. Just as importantly, organizations must demonstrate how that feedback influences decisions.

When employees see concerns acknowledged and acted upon, trust grows. When feedback disappears into a process without visible outcomes, engagement often declines.

One utility has invested in dedicated engagement coordinators to help leaders stay more connected to employee feedback, concerns and ideas. By creating additional channels for communication, sense-making and listening, the organization is strengthening its ability to identify issues early and improve execution across the fleet.

Delivering the next generation of nuclear capacity requires leaders to address workforce capacity constraints that go beyond the ability to hire and train new employees. Nuclear organizations must also create space for the existing workforce to sustain today’s operations while helping build tomorrow’s future.

By simplifying competing priorities, equipping leaders to translate strategy into daily work and engaging employees as active contributors to change, nuclear organizations can strengthen retention, protect institutional knowledge and create the workforce capacity needed for growth.

The people being recruited to support tomorrow’s nuclear expansion will depend on the knowledge, experience and commitment of the people doing the work today. The most sustainable growth strategies recognize that retaining and engaging those employees is not separate from capacity expansion. It is a prerequisite for it.

Mike Flanagan’s 10-Episode Netflix Thriller Masterpiece Is Everything ‘Carrie’ Should Have Been

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Carrie is missing something this show has in spades

Mike Flanagan knows how to make horror. He also knows how to make serialized TV. His most recent show is a mix of most of the things we’ve come to love about him: his bond with Stephen King, his skills as an adapter, his great eye for casting, and his ability to deliver pathos. But one key thing is missing: fear.

Carrie came out in October of this year, sparking much conversation, with many praising how it brought King’s ‘70s novel to 2026. One of its major criticisms, though, is that it’s not scary, especially for Flanagan, a guy who’s known for making some truly spooky stuff. Case in point: The Haunting of Hill House.

Released on Netflix in 2018, The Haunting of Hill House was Flanagan’s first TV show. Based on Shirley Jackson’s landmark novel of the same name, Flanagan takes her characters and reimagines them, at times scrapping their identities completely. While Jackson collected a group of strangers in a haunted house to explore their deepest traumas, Flanagan turned them into siblings, transforming Hill House into a story about their childhood home.

It’s an adaptational choice that many hardcore Jackson fans don’t forgive. Still, if Flanagan does one thing, it’s commit fully to the haunted house concept, taking the psychological fear that permeates the novel and literalizing it. Hill House is a true house of horrors.

Rather than leaning on jump scares, Hill House runs on dread, the type that has your eyes scanning through every inch of the screen so you’re not caught off guard by something that looks out of place. “If you go back and watch the series again, in every episode there’s probably somewhere between eight and 10 ghosts that are just hidden in plain sight that we called no attention to but they’re there,” Flanagan told Bloody Disgusting.

Flanagan also revealed that the ghosts had personal origins. The tall man with the bowler hat was added because one of the writers is afraid of tall people. “People on stilts freak her out,” he said. “It’s one of the most specific and weird phobias I’ve heard. We all thought it was so funny that we decided to make the tall man.”

And while Hill House delivers plenty of scary ghosts for texture and disquiet, some pull double duty, driving the story too. Flanagan splits Jackson’s Eleanor in two characters, giving her instability to the Crain siblings’ mother, Olivia (Carla Gugino), and her frailty and imagination to Nell (Victoria Pedretti). He turns the latter into the perfect example of a house cannibalizing its weakest. The series’ standout episode, “The Bent-Neck Lady,” relies on the twist that (spoiler alert) the ghost that has haunted Nell her entire life is herself, driven mad and killed by the house. Hill House’s biggest twist makes the horror intimate. Carrie’s twist does the opposite, removing the horror from her completely.

Warning: Spoilers follow for Carrie’s final episodes.

While Flanagan’s Carrie sticks closer to the book, keeping its mythology, sequence of events, and its characters' identities, it veers off in the one place it shouldn’t: the prom. We spend an entire season building up to one of the most famous moments in horror history, only for the series to unnecessarily complicate it. Instead of making the prom the place where Carrie breaks and enacts her revenge, it’s Emaline (Thalia Dudek), Carrie’s friend, who bears the majority of the blame, taking over Carrie’s body for most of the massacre.

In an interview with Polygon, Flanagan discussed the prom scene at length, clarifying what had happened. He shared that he and the writers wanted Carrie to be "100% solely responsible for" the deaths of key characters, innocent and guilty alike, including Tommy Ross (Joel Oulette), Billy Nolan (Arthur Conti) and Chris Hargensen (Alison Thornton).

They drew the line at the prom massacre. "None of us believed the Carrie White we created would consciously choose to kill Principal Grayle (Matthew Lillard), would choose to kill Ms. D (Amber Midthunder), and would choose to ignite innocent people around her,” he said.

Carrie has always been more than a story about a girl setting her prom on fire. It’s about the cruelty of high schoolers who use their weaker peers to feel better about themselves. That comes through in Flanagan’s show; as with Hill House, he has the ability to make us care deeply about his characters. But the prom is the moment where all that cruelty infects Carrie, where she transcends the page and becomes a horror icon. It’s there the show flinches. There’s no Bent-Neck Lady moment, just a massacre with no one clearly to blame. Is the show called Emaline?
