📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Blackburn Rovers fell to a second consecutive Championship defeat on Tuesday night as they lost 2-1 to Stoke City. The hosts were angered to see a late Andri Gudjohnsen equaliser ruled out for a push on a Potters defender, but they might not have been chasing the game had Tony Mowbray’s warning been heeded.

Speaking to the Lancashire Telegraph post-game, Mowbray revealed he warned his players about Jack McGlynn’s ‘wand of a left foot’. Yet, the American midfielder starred for the visitors and caught goalkeeper Balazs Toth out with a speculative near-post free-kick.

Mowbray said: “I’ve been watching all week, I’d said before the game McGlynn’s got a want of a left foot, be careful. From where I was sitting, I could see he was going to only shoot but the goalie thought he was crossing. Crazy goal. A great bit of play from the individual, of course, but I don’t think they warranted it.”

Mowbray was right to warn his Blackburn Rovers players of McGlynn’s abilities. The midfielder has been in excellent form for Stoke City, and his midweek goal takes him to four goals and two assists in 10 Championship games.

It was only Saturday that McGlynn scored one and set up the other in a 2-0 triumph over Rovers’ Lancashire rivals Preston North End. He was the scourge of Mowbray’s side too in another influential display.

McGlynn’s goal was the standout moment, but he also created two chances and completed 40 of his passes. He was heavily involved in the Potters’ play in possession with the second-most touches of their players (65).

He chipped in with dirty work out of possession too though, making six defensive contributions including three tackles and one interception. McGlynn also won five of his 10 duels, coming out on top in the midfield battle with Kristi Montgomery and Sam Morsy (all stats via Fotmob).

McGlynn had impressed in the United States with Philadelphia Union and Houston Dynamo before joining Stoke City for a little over £4m. He’s quickly emerged as an influential player for Stoke City and Mowbray’s comments show he’s someone opposition managers are increasingly wary of.

His form to date more than justifies the fee paid for him, and may have some Championship rivals wishing they had pounced to bring him in themselves.

Blackburn Rovers have influential attacking midfielders, but Todd Cantwell has struggled for consistency in the past and Ryoya Morishita is out injured. Someone of McGlynn’s goalscoring threat and technical ability would not go amiss in the middle at Ewood Park, despite Montgomery’s growth and Morsy’s emergence as a prominent player since joining.

Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Escalating to a heavier setting didn't pay here. Routing hard tasks to Flash on default thinking was cheaper than running default thinking everywhere. It still cost more than running low thinking on everything, which already solved all 50. A heavier escalation target only makes sense when the low setting starts failing.

Limits: one run per setup, 50 prompts with hand-assigned difficulty and simple automatic grading.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.

The Mystery of Hair Growth Patterns May Finally Have a Simple Explanation

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A new study reveals how subtle interactions between cells and chemical signals may shape the intricate patterns of mammalian hair.

The precise arrangement of hair follicles across an animal’s skin may arise from a surprisingly simple process. Researchers at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) have developed a mathematical model showing how cells responding to chemical signals can generate distinct hair patterns in two mammalian species, without requiring a complex system to determine where each follicle forms.

Published in PNAS, the research challenges a long-standing explanation for hair follicle development and suggests that variations in the same underlying cellular process could produce different patterns across species.

Hair follicles begin forming before birth in specialized embryonic structures called placodes. These structures also give rise to feathers and most types of scales. Their arrangement varies between species, but scientists have long debated how developing tissues establish such organized patterns.

For laboratory mice (Mus musculus), the prevailing explanation has been the expansion-induction model. According to this theory, each newly formed placode releases an inhibitory molecule that temporarily prevents others from developing nearby. As the skin expands, new areas move beyond the molecule’s influence, allowing additional placodes to form in the spaces between existing ones.

Although this model can account for the developmental patterns observed in laboratory mice, researchers have not experimentally established that it explains hair follicle organization more broadly across mammals.

A research team co-led by Athanasia Tzika, Senior Lecturer, and Michel Milinkovitch, Professor in UNIGE’s Department of Genetics and Evolution, investigated a different mechanism called chemotaxis. This process allows cells to move in response to chemical gradients. It also helps white blood cells navigate toward areas of inflammation.

The researchers created a mathematical model in which mobile cells in the dermis respond to an attractive chemical signal produced by the epidermis, the skin’s outer layer. As the simulated embryonic skin grows, these interactions spontaneously produce placodes in patterns that closely match the developmental sequence observed in laboratory mice.

“Our findings show that the observed patterns do not require a complex system telling each placode where to form,” explain Muhamet Ibrahimi and Ebrahim Jahanbakhsh, researchers in the Department of Genetics and Evolution. “Instead, placodes emerge spontaneously from local interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

The researchers next examined the spiny mouse (Acomys dimidiatus), a rodent whose hair follicles form a highly regular, directionally organized pattern. Unlike the arrangement found in laboratory mice, this distinctive configuration cannot be explained by the traditional expansion-induction model.

Using experimental data obtained through three-dimensional imaging of embryonic skin, the team adapted its mathematical model to reflect the spiny mouse’s particular biochemical characteristics and skin growth patterns. The same chemotactic mechanism successfully reproduced the species’ characteristic follicle arrangement.

These simulations suggest that different mammalian hair patterns could emerge from a shared process governed by cell movement and chemical signaling, with species-specific differences in how those interactions unfold. The results provide a potential alternative to the conventional model, although mathematical agreement with observed patterns does not establish that chemotaxis is the mechanism operating in living skin.

“Our work suggests that simple cellular interactions can generate the remarkable diversity of tissue architectures observed throughout evolution,” concludes Athanasia Tzika. “The differences observed between species therefore result from the same self-organization process, but with variations in the interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

Where The Rings Of Power Fits Into The Middle-earth Timeline

📅 Aug 21, 2024  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power explores a different point in Middle-earth history than past screen adaptations of Tolkien's work. While Frodo's story in The Lord of the Rings is at the foundation of the overall franchise, it is set at the tail end of a timeline over 60,000 years long. The Rings of Power is also set in this timeline, but several thousand years before the One Ring ever fell into the hands of Hobbits. Understanding precisely how all this fits together gets a little more complicated since Prime Video has made some changes to Tolkien's Middle-earth timeline.

The events of The Rings of Power are based on the various works that Tolkien constructed to outline the lore of The Lord of the Rings. This includes the appendices of The Return of the King, as well as posthumously published books like The Silmarillion and Unfinished Tales of Numenor and Middle-earth. The stories within begin at the very beginning of time and the creation of the universe and span all the way through tens of thousands of years, through the First, Second, and Third Ages of Middle-earth. Prime Video's The Rings of Power is set somewhere in the middle of all this.

The Rings of Power is set during the Second Age of Middle-earth, a period in Tolkien's timeline that spanned 3,441 years. It followed the First Age, an approximately 6,000-year period, and preceded the Third Age, a 3,021-year period. Since Prime Video's series is predominantly about Sauron's Rings of Power, and these were created in the year SA 1500, this is approximately when The Rings of Power season 1 and season 2 are set. This means the events of the series take place about 7,500 years after the start of the First Age and 4,959 years before Frodo leaves the Shire in The Lord of the Rings.

The beginning of Rings of Power season 1 featured moments from the First Age, when the Dark Lord Morgoth was the central antagonist of Middle-earth.

While Sauron made his Rings of Power in SA 1500, the timeline for Rings of Power is a little more complicated than just this. For example, Isildur, the future king of Gondor, isn't born until SA 3209, over a thousand years after Sauron's deception at Eregion. Prime Video condensed the timeline, making Isildur a young man when Sauron forges the Rings of Power in season 2. This was necessary because it would have been difficult to cover the stories of the mortals and immortals of Middle-earth at the same time.

Rings of Power has also done things out of order. Sauron's story is the foundation of the series, and Rings of Power season 1 covered his actions between about SA 50 and SA 1500. However, Pharazôn taking power and the downfall of Numenor didn't happen until around SA 3261 and SA 3319, both events that have been teased to be on the way in Rings of Power.

Second Age Events Relevant To Rings Of Power Seasons 1 & 2

Future seasons of Rings of Power are sure to dive into the Last Alliance of Elves and Men, which forms in SA 3430, as well as the Battle of Dagorlad, which is when Sauron is destroyed and the One Ring claimed by Isildur. This will likely happen sometime near the end of the Prime Video series, but it's certainly possible that Rings of Power will take things forward into the Third Age of Middle-earth.

The Third Age kicks off right after Sauron's downfall, and this period in Middle-earth's timeline sees a lot of important development for the characters of The Rings of Power. Isildur becomes king of Gondor, Elrond marries Celebrian (Galadriel's daughter), and Gandalf the Gray makes his official arrival in Middle-earth (though Rings of Power has moved that up with the Stranger). It's certainly possible that Rings of Power will carry on into this period, exploring Isildur's decline to the One Ring and the dwindling of Sauron's remaining Rings of Power in Middle-earth. Only time will tell.
