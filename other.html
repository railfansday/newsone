James Webb’s Mysteriously Bright Galaxies May Finally Have an Explanation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Calculations suggest powerful ultraviolet light from young stars could sweep gas out of early galaxies while leaving dense star-forming regions intact, offering a clue to their mysterious brightness.

The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed something unexpected about the early universe: bright galaxies were far more common just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang than astronomers thought possible. A new analysis suggests these galaxies may not necessarily be producing more light than expected. Instead, powerful ultraviolet radiation from young stars could be clearing away gas and dust, allowing more of their light to reach us.

Researchers at the Simons Foundation’s Flatiron Institute in New York City and the University of Texas at Dallas investigated this possibility by examining how ultraviolet light called Lyman-alpha radiation interacts with material surrounding young stars. Their findings, published November 4 in The Astrophysical Journal, suggest that this radiation can drive gas and dust out of less dense regions of galaxies, potentially explaining why some of the earliest galaxies appear unusually bright.

“Our results show Lyman-alpha radiation can push low-density gas and dust away really, really fast,” says Shyam Menon, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute’s Center for Computational Astrophysics and the study’s lead author. “It can sort of clear the ‘fog,’ allowing us to see stars in the early universe much more clearly than we would otherwise.”

Lyman-alpha radiation forms when an electron collides with a hydrogen atom or joins a hydrogen ion, and it can be especially abundant in regions where young stars are forming. When this light encounters gas or dust, it transfers momentum to the material, creating pressure that can push it outward.

What makes Lyman-alpha radiation particularly powerful is how hydrogen responds to it. While most absorbed light is released at a different wavelength, hydrogen atoms almost immediately re-emit Lyman-alpha radiation at the same wavelength, allowing nearby atoms to absorb and release it repeatedly. These repeated interactions increase the force exerted by the light, potentially pushing surrounding material away from regions where stars are forming.

Astronomers have long suspected that this pressure could reshape stellar nurseries by removing gas needed for star formation, possibly even stopping the process under extreme conditions. However, the repeated interactions between radiation and hydrogen are difficult to reproduce in computer simulations, making it challenging to determine how much influence the radiation actually has.

“We’ve known for a very long time that Lyman-alpha radiation could be important because it carries a lot of force,” Menon says. “But we’ve never actually been able to estimate how important it is in a realistic environment because of how challenging it is to model.”

To address this problem, Menon and collaborator Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas analyzed snapshots from an existing simulation of a star-forming region in the early universe. Rather than tracking every interaction between light and matter, they calculated how much pressure Lyman-alpha radiation would exert on the surrounding gas and dust.

Their calculations revealed that the radiation behaves differently depending on the density of the surrounding material. In dense star-forming regions, gravity remained strong enough to hold gas and dust in place despite the outward pressure, suggesting that Lyman-alpha radiation had little effect on star formation there. In less crowded regions between stars, however, the radiation could overcome gravity and drive material outward at high speeds.

Multiple views of a 2.5-million-year simulation visualizing a gas cloud as it is cleared away and the formation of stars. From left to right, the views show the gas density, ionization, and temperature. Credit: Shyam Menon (Flatiron Institute) and Aaron Smith (UT Dallas)

“When the density is low enough, the Lyman-alpha radiation can push the gas to really high speeds, launching explosive winds that can shoot material clear out of the galaxy,” Menon says.

These winds could help explain the unexpectedly bright galaxies detected by the James Webb Space Telescope. By removing loosely distributed gas and dust from between stars, the radiation may create clearer paths for starlight to escape, making distant galaxies appear brighter without requiring them to produce unusually large amounts of light.

Important questions remain, particularly about the properties of dust in the early universe and how radiation interacts with moving gas as stars form. Researchers will need more comprehensive simulations that account for these processes together to better understand their influence on early galaxies.

Recent work by Olof Nebrin of Stockholm University and study co-author Smith has demonstrated that these interactions can be modeled in two dimensions, although more realistic three-dimensional simulations remain a challenge.

“But the hope is that in the coming years, with improvements to computing power and the development of more effective algorithms, we can eventually do these simulations in three dimensions.”

Reference: “Lyα Radiation Pressure in Dense Star Clusters: Implications for Star Formation and Winds at Cosmic Dawn” by Shyam H. Menon and Aaron Smith, 4 November 2026, The Astrophysical Journal. DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/aeaed6

Alaska, Hawaiian Pilot MECs Approve Tentative Joint Contract

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Pilots at Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines are one step closer to a deal that will bring both work groups together under a single labor contract.

ALPA announced Wednesday that its Alaska and Hawaiian master executive councils unanimously approved a tentative agreement on a joint collective bargaining compact. The two councils had been negotiating since February 2025.

The proposed agreement will now go before the combined membership for a ratification vote.

Details of the agreement were not immediately available to the public.

Union officials said they worked to address the priorities of both work groups and have established a “contractual foundation for their future as a combined workforce.”

“This TA is the result of more than 600 days of challenging negotiations, countless hours of work by our volunteers, and an unwavering commitment to the pilots we represent,” Drew Coyle, the Alaska MEC chair, said in a statement. “Our Joint Negotiating Committee remained focused on the priorities of both pilot groups while working through some of the most complex issues facing our combined membership. Nothing about this process was easy. Now it’s our pilots’ turn to review the agreement, ask the tough questions, and decide whether it meets their expectations.”

Ratification voting starts Dec. 1 and closes Dec. 22.

Alaska and Hawaiian merged in 2024 and received a single operating certificate from the FAA in 2025. This effectively transformed Hawaiian from an independent airline to a brand under Alaska Air Group. Alaska has pledged to maintain Hawaiian’s brand identity, and Hawaiian retains a separate corporate leadership based in Honolulu.

The two brands have continued to integrate however, with Alaska now making use of long-haul aircraft originally owned by Hawaiian. Earlier this year, Alaska implemented a single passenger service system.

A Planet 11 Times Bigger Than Jupiter Is Challenging What We Thought About Planet Formation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

New observations of the giant exoplanet Beta Pictoris b are challenging assumptions about how and where massive planets form.

New observations of Beta Pictoris b, a massive planet orbiting a young star, are challenging a method astronomers hoped could reveal where giant planets form. More precise measurements of the planet’s atmosphere have overturned an earlier result, raising doubts about whether a particular chemical signature can reliably trace a planet’s birthplace.

Located in a 23-million-year-old planetary system, Beta Pictoris b is approximately 11 times Jupiter’s mass. It orbits about 10 astronomical units (approximately 930 million miles) from its star, completing one revolution every 23 years. Earlier atmospheric measurements suggested the planet might have formed much farther away before migrating inward, but the latest evidence points to a different possibility.

Astronomers have proposed using the relative abundance of two forms of carbon monoxide (CO) to determine where giant planets formed within their original disks of gas and dust. These molecules contain different carbon isotopes, 12C and 13C, which have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Their slightly different masses can influence how they behave during chemical processes.

The method relies on the carbon monoxide snowline, the distance from a young star beyond which temperatures become cold enough for CO gas to freeze onto dust grains. Planets forming beyond this boundary may accumulate carbon monoxide ice, potentially leaving a different isotopic signature than planets that develop in warmer regions where CO remains gaseous.

Earlier observations of Beta Pictoris b using the original GRAVITY instrument produced a relatively low ratio of 12CO to 13CO. That result appeared consistent with formation beyond the snowline, followed by inward migration to the planet’s present orbit. However, the researchers cautioned that instrumental limitations could have affected the measurement.

A team led by Antonia von Stauffenberg, a doctoral student at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy (MPIA) in Heidelberg, Germany, has now revisited the planet using GRAVITY+. The upgraded instrument operates at the Very Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI), managed by the European Southern Observatory (ESO) in Chile.

The new observations reveal a substantially higher 12CO/13CO ratio than previously reported. While 12CO produces a readily detectable signal, its less abundant counterpart, 13CO, is considerably harder to measure accurately. GRAVITY+ provides improved adaptive optics and greater observational precision, allowing researchers to better distinguish the two molecules.

“The GRAVITY+ interferometric instrument is highly stable […], making it uniquely capable of high-fidelity characterization of directly imaged exoplanets,” says co-author and MPIA scientist Jonas Sauter.

The revised ratio agrees with independent measurements reported by González Picos et al. (2026) using a different instrument. This agreement supports the new result and indicates that systematic uncertainties affected the earlier GRAVITY measurements.

The researchers also detected tentative variations in the planet’s observed brightness. These fluctuations appear to follow a period of approximately 8.7 hours, close to the planet’s rotation period.

If confirmed, the variations could arise from atmospheric clouds or chemical differences moving in and out of view as the planet rotates. However, the evidence remains statistically weak, and more sensitive observations will be necessary to establish whether the changes are real.

“The ability to accurately constrain both isotopologues and potential rotational variability using ground-based observations of a bona fide planet such as Beta Pictoris b demonstrates the exceptional data quality achieved with the updated GRAVITY+ instrument,” says von Stauffenberg.

Under the proposed formation model, the revised carbon monoxide ratio would favor an origin inside the snowline, consistent with Beta Pictoris b’s current orbital distance. Yet the measurement also closely resembles ratios observed throughout the Solar System and the interstellar medium, the gas and dust occupying the space between stars.

Similar values have been measured in most of the roughly dozen young giant planets studied using this technique. This widespread consistency suggests that differences in carbon isotope abundances across planet-forming environments may be too small to distinguish reliably, limiting the method’s usefulness for reconstructing individual planetary histories.

“It is still difficult to utilize 13CO as a formation tracer of giant planets, due to the uncertainties that still persist in the models and measurements,” explains von Stauffenberg.

Current models may be missing important details of carbon monoxide ice chemistry in planet-forming disks. Until those processes are better understood, atmospheric carbon isotope ratios alone cannot establish whether a giant planet formed in a warm, gas-rich region or farther out among frozen materials. More precise observations and additional chemical indicators will be needed to reconstruct these planets’ origins, with GRAVITY+ offering a powerful tool for investigating both their formation and atmospheric behavior.

My Grandma Got Me Through the Water Temple. She Would’ve Loved the Ocarina of Time Remake

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On Saturday nights, I’d take my Nintendo 64 cartridge upstairs to Mémère’s apartment. She always knew where I needed to go next.

I still remember the buzz my grandmother’s woodgrain TV made when she turned it on. I remember fiddling with the knobs and getting in trouble for it, too. When my older brother started grade school in the early ’90s, I spent a lot of time at her house. We called her Mémère (pronounced “mem-may” by French Canadians like us), and she’d fire up the Nintendo to keep me entertained.

For a while it was the NES. I’d shoot ducks until I got bored, and I hated Mario because it was too hard. What I really remember fondly was watching Mémère play The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past after she upgraded to the SNES. She’d tell me who she was fighting and why, and following along felt like having one of my favorite people read me a book. When it was my turn to play, I’d just charge up Link’s sword and mow every blade of grass I could find. Then I’d go looking for chickens. Then I’d ask what she had for snacks. She always had good snacks for me.

By the Nintendo 64 era, Mémère had moved into an apartment in a house my uncle owned. He lived on the first floor, she lived on the second, and my cousin lived on the third. Most Saturday nights, the whole family would get together. While the adults played cards (always Pitch, if you're curious), my brother and I would drift between floors. Grand Theft Auto or Dynasty Warriors on the first? Dino Crisis, horror movies, or kung fu flicks on the third? Nope. For me, it was usually Ocarina of Time with Mémère on the second floor.

I can still picture her lying sideways on her bed with that plain gray controller in her hands. The TV was only a few feet away, and the N64 sat right on the corner of the dresser beside it. By then, I had my own N64, and I was always excited to tell her where I had been and which Spiritual Stones or Medallions I still needed. I usually brough my own cartridge over, and she’d help me through a part I was stuck on. Yes, including the Water Temple. I remember being in awe when she showed me how to get the Fire Arrows at Lake Hylia. How was I supposed to know you could shoot the sun? And when the skeletons rose from the ground at night in Hyrule Field, I'd hand over the controller and cower behind her as she challenged them head-on.

Mémère owned a beat-up copy of Prima’s official strategy guide. With it, she’d played through the whole game and found every heart piece. Then she did it two more times. When she finished the third file, she went back to the first slot and started over. I have no idea how many times my grandmother beat Ocarina of Time, but it was probably more than you have. All three of her save files always had the same name: Adeline.

I thought it was kind of weird. I didn’t know anybody named Adeline. I asked her about it a few times, and she always told me to ask my mother. When I was finally old enough to hear the answer, I cried. Adeline was Mémère’s first daughter, the only sibling older than my mom. She died from complications related to spina bifida when she was only a few years old. My mom’s three younger siblings were either too young to remember her or hadn’t been born by the time she died. My mother and grandmother were the only ones who could tell me much about “Addy,” and they rarely talked about her.

I’d spent all those Saturday nights watching Mémère play, asking her questions about practically everything on the screen. I could ask her how to get through the Water Temple, and she’d walk me through it. When I asked her about Adeline, she didn’t answer. And every time she started a new game, she typed in her daughter’s name again.

I bought her Majora’s Mask once. She told me she’d played it, though I’m still not sure how far she got. All she had to say about it was that it was “just not the same.” That alone made me pretty sad about the whole thing, and it made me rethink Mémère's whole Zelda ritual. Even though she never really talked about it, she was thinking about her lost daughter every single day as she ventured into Hyrule to fight the growing darkness and defeat Ganondorf. She was content with that same adventure, replayed endlessly. A lot of people might argue that Ocarina of Time is just that good. Mémère would certainly say there's nothing else like it.

That's what makes the remake's release on the Nintendo Switch 2 feel so bittersweet. Mémère died back in 2017 at the age of 70, but not before my brother had his first daughter, a child he named Adalyne. During one of the last times I visited Mémère, she still had that same dusty old N64 next to her TV. My mother told me that Mémère “talked” to Addy every day. I wondered if Mémère meant she was playing Ocarina of Time. Maybe all of these complicated memories help explain why I’ve never been a huge fan of the Zelda franchise at large. Yes, I’ve played over a hundred hours between Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom. They’re excellent in many ways. But I can’t stand the fragile weapons and the clunky feel of combat. I also don’t like how they tend to, even unconsciously, make me a little bit sad.

If Mémère were still alive today, what would she think of the remake? Would she shed a tear seeing her beloved video game lovingly remade in such a beautiful way? She was always shockingly savvy for her generation when it came to technology. Though I’d probably have to hook the Switch 2 up to the TV for her. (Her eyesight was always fairly bad). Honestly, it makes me almost happy that I don't have a Switch 2 myself to experience the new Ocarina of Time, because even just thinking about it makes me emotional. If she were still alive, I’d probably have tried harder to go out of my way to get the console and game just so we could game together.

I do know one thing: If I ever do get around to it, I know exactly what I'll name Link.
