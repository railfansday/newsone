iFixit teardown shows how Apple's glowing PROLED MacBook logo works

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The new laptop goes the extra mile to recreate the iconic feature.

iFixit has shared its teardown of Apple's first touchscreen MacBook Pro and besides bemoaning the company's half-hearted commitment to user repairability, it also showed off the physical components that make the laptop's glowing logo work. The new lid design is one of the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display's many charms, alongside its tandem OLED display and lighter weight.

Older LCD MacBooks featured a physical cutout that let the display's backlight illuminate the logo, but since OLED screens don't require backlights, Apple had to come up with a different solution to bring the design element back. iFixit found that the company uses a light strip with four LEDs layered behind the display, whose light is reflected through a diffuser to illuminate the logo. The Apple logo itself is also coated "like a two-way mirror," according to iFixit, so that it can appear metallic and reflective when the logo is off, but still let light through when the LEDs are on.

Apple ditched the glowing logo in 2016, so it's a bit ironic that the company waited until it stopped using backlit displays to bring it back. It used to be a cool bit of branding before, but now the company may see the glowing logo design as an important way to distinguish its most expensive laptop from the kind we peons are forced to use.

The rest of the internal changes to the laptop are more expected. Apple was able to achieve the new MacBook Pro's lighter weight by using less aluminum in the base and lid of the laptop and including a slightly smaller trackpad. The laptop's hinge was also reworked to make the display wobble less when you tap the screen. Those changes don't make it any easier to fix, though. The laptop is still complex to disassemble: For example, iFixit says that you have to remove the laptop's logic board to switch out its USB-C ports. Plenty of other components, like the MacBook's memory and the HDMI port, are also soldered in place. Anyone dropping up to $15,000 on a laptop probably isn't worried about doing their own repairs, but Apple's thin and light (and glowing) design comes with some tradeoffs, as always.

At the Supreme Court, the justices seemed divided on Tuesday in a case that could undermine the ability of many federal regulatory agencies to fine businesses that violate federal laws.

For decades, farmers have relied on a federal immigration program that allows them to temporarily employ migrant workers to pick crops.

In addition to wages, farm owners must provide basic necessities, such as housing, access to meals and water. The whole system is essentially policed by the Department of Labor to ensure that foreign workers don't undercut domestic laborers.

In that role, the department fined Sun Valley Orchards, based in New Jersey, a half-million dollars for unsanitary conditions, including providing unsafe drinking water, housing people in 20-bunk rooms, broken sinks and other violations, including failure to pay promised wages.

Sun Valley is disputing the charges and contends that the Department of Labor imposing monetary fines based on its administrative proceedings is unconstitutional. Sun Valley also says the process denies them the right to a jury trial when and if they appeal to a federal court.

At the high court Tuesday, Chief Justice John Roberts strongly suggested that the Trump administration, and many administrations before it, have exceeded the power of the executive branch. In this case, potentially wading into the judicial branch's territory.

Addressing Deputy Solicitor General Sarah Harris, who represented the Labor Department, Roberts said, "It seems to me you're just trying to leverage … the broad authority [of the executive branch] beyond the justification for its application."

Justice Sonia Sotomayor asked how disruptive it would be for the federal courts to enforce the law instead of the designated agency (in this case the Department of Labor).

Harris responded succinctly, "If the court were to say civil penalties always have to be in Article III courts, that's kind of the nuclear option."

Harris added that such a ruling would disrupt programs as diverse as Medicare, Medicaid, the federal student loan program and more.

That prompted this question from Justice Elena Kagan: "Why not just go to court always?" Harris quickly responded, "I think it would be catastrophic." She went on to explain that a system like that would inevitably result in massive under-enforcement of the law, given limited resources.

Lawyer Robert Johnson represented Sun Valley Orchards. "To be sure," he said, "the statute contemplates penalties, but it says nothing about where those penalties should be adjudicated."

Johnson further contended that penalties should be imposed by the federal courts, not the administrative agencies that do most of that work under current law.

To that Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson replied, "It seems like you're just sort of fighting the reality of … what Congress was trying to do … in ensuring that we don't have farm workers coming in and undercutting domestic workers."

A more skeptical observation came from Justice Brett Kavanaugh: "What you're proposing is really at war with clarity," he said, adding, "I think that's not great systematically going forward."

Justice Samuel Alito pointedly added, "What you're outlining is sort of a hodge-podge opinion, and maybe sometimes courts write that sort of thing," to which Justice Kagan quickly chimed in with a chuckling crowd in the court, "Not ever us!"

In rebuttal, Deputy Solicitor General Harris was unusually candid. "It would be a big deal for the government to lose, but an even bigger deal would be to have a mess," she said. She later added, "A lot of other statutory regimes also would be on the cutting-room floor."

And if that's the case, she said, the administration will have to go to Congress. Her voice rising, she added, "Please tell us now ... if we need to get Legislative Affairs on it right now, because this will snowball."

a giant circle frames NIKO’s timber guesthouse and tiny curved sauna in japan

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

NIKO design studio's 'marumi house' in japan features a giant circular facade opening, exposed cedar logs, and a tiny sauna.

NIKO Design Studio has completed Umino Marumi, a two-story wooden guesthouse on a hillside overlooking the Pacific coast of Chiba, Japan. With its enormous circular facade opening and exposed timber structure, the house makes an unusual addition to the surrounding residential neighborhood. In the garden, a much smaller companion building takes shape as a sauna hut, its curved roof designed to accommodate the trees growing around it.

Completed in 2025, Marumi was conceived as a second home where the owners could gather with family and friends, while also creating a welcoming presence within the local community. The architects approached this ambition through an engawa, the traditional Japanese veranda, transforming it into a series of staggered outdoor platforms that climb through the house and connect to the upper floor.

The Marumi House’s most recognizable feature is the enormous circular opening cut into its dark facade, revealing a network of timber columns and elevated walkways beneath the pitched roof. Around one-third of the covered building is dedicated to the semi-outdoor engawa, where visitors can sit, gather, or move between levels without entering the enclosed rooms.

With this layout, the architects at NIKO Design Studio give the house an unusually open relationship with its surroundings. From outside, the veranda offers glimpses of people moving through the timber structure, while its various levels accommodate both intimate gatherings and larger groups. The circular opening brings these activities into view, turning the facade into an inhabited space that faces the neighborhood.

NIKO Design Studio worked with substantial cedar logs measuring approximately 400 millimeters in diameter, assembling the structural frame using traditional kanuki joinery. Horizontal timber members pass through openings in the round columns, creating a robust structure that retains the natural geometry of the wood. The holes were cut using chainsaws, following an approach suggested by the carpenters and inspired by forestry techniques.

The same hands-on approach extends into the landscape. To address the sloping site, a stone retaining wall was assembled during a two-day workshop using nozumi, a traditional stone-stacking technique. The rough stonework sits alongside the large timber structure, which brings a sense of craftsmanship to a building designed for communal use.

Beyond the main house, the architects faced a different challenge when designing a freestanding sauna for the garden. The owners initially wanted a cabin for three or four people, but commercially available models appeared surprisingly bulky when placed within a model of the site. Looking more closely at the surrounding landscape, the team noticed a neighboring palm tree extending over the property boundary. Its continued growth became the starting point for the sauna’s unusual curved roof.

Working with carpenter Murakami of Murakami Carpentry Workshop, the architects sourced naturally bent timber from trees that had grown along a hillside. These curved members formed the roof structure, allowing its profile to rise and dip around the surrounding vegetation. When the timber arrived thicker and longer than anticipated, the team decided to retain the excess length, leaving the beams projecting beyond the roofline. The unexpected detail gives the small building much of its character.

Despite its expressive roof, the sauna occupies remarkably little ground. Its foundation measures just two by two meters, with a total floor area of five square meters including the stepped portion. Inside, timber-lined surfaces and a tall ceiling make the small space feel surprisingly generous. Although originally intended for three or four bathers, the completed sauna can accommodate five or six people.

The hut combines electric and wood-fired heating, while a skylight brings daylight into the compact interior. It connects with the surrounding terrace and the house’s bathroom, which can serve as a cold bath during sauna sessions. Marumi and its miniature companion together explore two scales of wooden architecture, with one opening itself toward the neighborhood and the other adjusting its form to the trees already growing in the garden.

The House has voted 211 to 204 to refer the articles of impeachment filed against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to committee, effectively killing the impeachment effort spearheaded by Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky.

Tuesday's vote short-circuits any potential floor vote on the articles of impeachment, and virtually guarantees the measure will not be taken up before the current session of Congress expires.

The vote provided a surprise reprieve for President Trump's embattled Defense Secretary, who was facing an impeachment vote by the full House as early as Wednesday.

Massie reintroduced the articles of impeachment on Monday, accusing Hegseth of high crimes and misdemeanors in connection to the war with Iran and U.S. strikes against Iran and on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean Sea and Eastern Pacific Ocean.

Massie's impeachment resolution sought to oust Hegseth over the capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro without congressional authorization, and the effort to punish Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Ariz., for a video telling service members they can refuse illegal orders.

Hegseth defended himself against the impeachment effort during a rare press conference at the Pentagon on Tuesday, criticizing Massie as a "disgruntled and defeated congressman."

On Monday, Trump expressed support for Hegseth during a wide-ranging, impromptu press briefing at the White House.

Unlocking Fire Arrows is a great way to expand Link's arsenal in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake. By hitting targets with these special arrows, Link can set them on fire, which is fairly useful in different scenarios, from fighting certain enemies to lighting totems to open doors. Before you can get your hands on them, you will need to progress through the story and solve a riddle.

If you need help figuring out all the steps, don't fret. Here's how to unlock Fire Arrows in Zelda Ocarina of Time.

Before you start shooting Fire Arrows around Hyrule, you need to meet two prerequisites: get the Fairy Bow from the Forest Temple and complete the Water Temple in Lake Hylia.

If you have already finished the Water Temple, head to the largest island in the middle of the lake (the one with the large tree) and read the tablet located on the its southeast side. The message says, "When the lake is full and dawn breaks, release an arrow toward the halo of light," which basically means that you need to shoot an arrow at the sun.

Wait until daytime and aim your bow at the rising sun. If you're visiting the lake during the night, you can use the Sun's Song to speed the process up. As you aim your bow at the sun, an orange-reddish circle will appear around it, indicating that you should fire the arrow. A short cutscene will play, showing the arrow landing on the island in front of you. Head there and pick up the Fire Arrow.

When it comes to using Fire Arrows, it's important to keep in mind that they aren't a separate type of arrow. What you unlock is the ability to magically imbue your arrows with fire. While this means you won't have to carry a stock of each type, it also means that you have only one set of arrows to use, whether you're shooting regular or Fire Arrows. Additionally, Fire Arrows cost one magic power per shot, considerably limiting the number of times you can use them.
