Houston Preview

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kansas played West Virginia in Las Vegas Wednesday night, and now the Jayhawks get Houston on Thursday. Two Big 12 opponents in two days, with neither game counting toward the Big 12 standings. If we win this one, do we get to hang a banner for winning the November Big 12 championship? Asking for little brother.

Anyway, Kansas survived West Virginia 86-82 Wednesday night, overcoming an 11-point second-half deficit to remain undefeated. Houston, meanwhile, knocked off #1 Florida 86-76 to advance to the Players Era 8 championship game.

Kansas: 5-0, #11 AP / #9 Coaches / #3 KenPom

Houston: 5-0, #8 AP / #7 Coaches / #1 KenPom

When: Thursday, November 19, 10:00 PM CST

Where: Michelob ULTRA Arena, Las Vegas, Nevada

Kansas leads the all-time series 7-6, but Houston won the most recent meeting 69-47 in the Big 12 Tournament semifinals last March. That wasn't particularly enjoyable.

Houston is in its 13th season (!!!) under Kelvin Sampson, who has turned the Cougars into one of the most consistently successful programs in the country. They've reached two Final Fours under Sampson, including the 2025 national championship game, and are once again among the nation's best teams. The Cougars opened this season with wins over La Salle, Cal, and Jacksonville State before beating Rutgers 85-73 and Florida 86-76 in Las Vegas.

The Florida victory was particularly impressive. Houston trailed by three at halftime, then outscored the Gators 49-36 in the second half. Dedan Thomas Jr. scored 30 points, and the Cougars held Florida to just 18 points in the paint while scoring 44 of their own.

Kansas has also faced some legitimate competition. The Jayhawks beat Kentucky by 20, handled UNLV by 25, and then needed a second-half comeback to escape West Virginia.

Both teams are undefeated. Both have already beaten quality opponents.

And one of them gets to leave Las Vegas with a 6-0 record.

Through five games, here's how the teams compare offensively (KU / Houston):

Effective field-goal percentage (eFG%): 61.1% / 55.6%

Offensive rebound rate (ORB%): 35.0% / 42.9%

Kansas has been the more efficient shooting team, but Houston has done a better job protecting the basketball and has a considerable advantage on the offensive glass. The Cougars have recovered nearly 43% of their available offensive rebounds, which is a fairly terrifying number considering that KU may be missing one of its best rebounders.

Both teams also get to the free-throw line frequently, but here's a rather alarming difference: Houston is shooting 81.9% from the charity stripe, while Kansas is making just 63.0% of its attempts. That's nearly a 19-percentage-point difference, and in a close game, it could be enormous.

And now we're up to five games of data. Progress!

1. Can Kansas score efficiently against Houston's defense?

Kansas is averaging 92.2 points per game and shooting 55.4% from the field. Those are excellent numbers, and the Jayhawks have demonstrated that they can score against legitimate competition. But Houston isn't West Virginia or UNLV. The Cougars are allowing just 63.6 points per game, holding opponents to 37.8% shooting, and forcing more than 15 turnovers per contest.

And this isn't exactly a new development. Kelvin Sampson has built his program around physical defense, relentless pressure, and making opponents work for everything they get.

Kansas scored 86 points against West Virginia despite making only three of its 11 three-point attempts. That's encouraging, particularly after the Jayhawks struggled from the perimeter against UNLV. But KU also attempted 40 free throws against the Mountaineers, making 27. I'm not sure we should count on another 40 opportunities at the line against Houston.

Tre White scored 21 against WVU, but he needed 18 field-goal attempts to get there. Trey Kaufman-Renn added 19, and Keanu Dawes contributed 18.

The good news is that Kansas found enough offense without Tyran Stokes.

The bad news is that Houston should make generating those opportunities considerably more difficult.

2. Can Kansas survive the rebounding battle?

This might be the matchup that worries me most.

Houston has collected 66 offensive rebounds through five games, compared with 56 for Kansas. The Cougars have outrebounded their opponents by nearly 12 per game and are converting missed shots into additional scoring opportunities at an impressive rate.

Joseph Tugler is the player to watch here. The 6-foot-8 senior is one of the country's best defensive players, averaging 6.8 rebounds, 1.8 steals, and 1.6 blocks per game. Against Florida, he recorded 12 points, 10 rebounds, three steals, and three assists. Delrecco Gillespie provides additional size and rebounding, while Houston's guards are willing to contribute on the glass as well.

Kansas has plenty of size of its own. Dawes grabbed 12 rebounds against West Virginia, and Kaufman-Renn and Christian Reeves give the Jayhawks legitimate frontcourt options.

But Stokes missed Wednesday's game with a bone bruise in his knee, and his availability for Houston remains uncertain. That matters. Stokes averages 8.0 rebounds per game, and Kansas could certainly use his athleticism against a team as physical as Houston.

Even if Stokes plays, the Jayhawks will have to finish defensive possessions with rebounds. Giving Houston two or three chances to score on the same possession seems like a particularly unpleasant way to spend a Thursday evening.

Dedan Thomas Jr., a senior transfer from UNLV, is averaging 20.8 points and 5.2 assists per game. He's shooting 55.0% from the field and scored 30 against Florida, making 10 of 17 shots and all eight of his free throws. He's also committed just six turnovers in five games. That's the sort of efficiency that makes life difficult for opposing defenses.

Houston isn't especially dependent on three-point shooting. Only 24.9% of its field-goal attempts have come from beyond the arc, compared with 33.2% for Kansas. The Cougars prefer to attack inside, draw fouls, and create opportunities around the basket.

Turns out, they're pretty good at it. Against Florida, Houston attempted only nine threes. The Cougars still scored 86 points, while making 25 of 27 free throws and committing just six turnovers.

Kansas just watched West Virginia freshman Miles Sadler score 34 points by repeatedly attacking the defense. Thomas is an even more established offensive threat, and he has considerably more help around him. With Melvin Council Jr. still unavailable, Taylen Kinney and Leroy Blyden Jr. will have their hands full.

Kansas doesn't necessarily need to shut Thomas down. That might not be realistic. But the Jayhawks have to make him work for his points and avoid letting Houston's offense operate comfortably for 40 minutes.

Because this isn't a team you want to spend the entire second half chasing.

Five games into the season, Kansas looks considerably better than I think most of us expected.

The Jayhawks have legitimate scoring options, improved frontcourt depth, and enough offensive versatility to win even when the three-pointers aren't falling. Wednesday's comeback against West Virginia was encouraging, particularly without Stokes. But Houston is a different animal.

The Cougars defend, rebound, take care of the basketball, and have an outstanding point guard. Case in point, they just beat the #1 team in the country, scoring 86 points while shooting 3-for-9 from behind the arc. That's what you call "concerning."

Kansas can absolutely win this game. If White and Kaufman-Renn score efficiently, the Jayhawks hold their own on the glass, and the guards handle Houston's pressure, there's no reason KU can't leave Las Vegas with the championship.

But with Stokes' availability uncertain, Council already sidelined, and both teams playing their third game in three days, I'm having a hard time talking myself into another Kansas victory.

These bodies could have been collecting slow-moving particles for billions of years.

The researchers used neutrino data and heat measurements to rule out some combinations of particle mass and interaction strength that laboratory experiments couldn’t probe.

The work was led by Alejandro Ibarra from the Technical University of Munich, Merlin Reichard from the Korea Institute for Advanced Study and Gaurav Tomar from the Sogang University in Seoul.

About 85% of the matter in the universe is dark matter. We know it’s there because galaxies spin faster than their visible matter can explain and light bends more than it should as it passes them.

Beyond that, very little is known about it. Physicists are stuck in a frustrating loop.

You can’t identify something you can’t see, and you can’t design a way to see it without some idea of what it is. So they make a guess, build an experiment to test it, and so far the answer has always been no.

One guess is that dark matter isn’t completely dark. Its particles might respond to electricity and magnetism very faintly, carrying a tiny fraction of an electron’s charge or acting like microscopic magnets.

The paper describes five such properties. One of them, the “anapole moment,” is a doughnut-shaped kind of magnetism.

Any of the five would let a dark matter particle occasionally bump into an atom.

Underground detectors wait for those bumps. A slow particle taps an atom so gently that the instrument doesn’t notice.

In practice, these detectors are more sensitive to faster dark matter particles.

Planets and stars work the other way around. A fast particle shoots straight through them. A slow one only needs to lose a little speed in a collision before gravity grabs it and won’t let go.

Over billions of years, trapped particles would sink toward the center and crowd together. Once packed tightly enough, they could meet and annihilate, turning into other particles.

Some of those would be neutrinos, which pass through rock almost as if it weren’t there. The rest would deposit their energy as heat.

“In this work, we considered four celestial bodies: Earth, the Sun, Jupiter and white dwarfs,” Tomar said.

“There are dedicated experiments, for example IceCube at the South Pole, looking for neutrinos from the Sun and Earth. For Jupiter and white dwarfs, we considered the heating possibilities.”

For the Sun and Earth, the team used limits from IceCube, a detector built into the Antarctic ice, and from its smaller companion detector, DeepCore.

That included ten years of IceCube data on neutrinos coming from Earth’s core. There was no excess.

Neither Jupiter nor the white dwarfs generate heat through nuclear fusion.

The Cassini spacecraft measured how much heat Jupiter leaks from its interior.

The white dwarfs are burnt-out stellar cores in M4, a cluster of ancient stars. Each one holds about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass in a ball with a radius of roughly 1,250 kilometers.

If a candidate type of dark matter would make these objects give off more heat than they actually do, it can be crossed off.

Earth turned out to be the best test for dark matter with a tiny electric charge. For particles heavier than about ten protons, it set the strongest limits yet.

There are two reasons. First, a charged particle’s pull reaches a long way, so it is easily drawn in. Second, Earth’s core is full of iron, whose atoms each carry 26 protons, which makes collisions more likely.

The white dwarfs set leading limits on two of the more exotic properties, the charge radius and the anapole moment, across a wide range of particle masses.

For very heavy particles, they also beat earlier limits on dark matter’s magnetic dipole moment.

The anapole limits matter for one popular candidate whose only possible electromagnetic property is the anapole moment.

For that candidate, the white dwarfs reach places that particle colliders and underground detectors struggle to get to.

Jupiter was a letdown. In general, it didn’t improve on limits that already existed. None of the four bodies beat earlier limits on the electric dipole moment either.

Underground labs sit beneath a kilometer or more of rock to block cosmic rays. If dark matter interacts strongly with ordinary matter, that same rock could slow it down or stop it before it reaches the detector.

The shield could be hiding the very particles the experiment is looking for.

Planets and stars don’t have this problem. The more strongly dark matter interacts, the better they catch it.

There is one important caveat. The more dark matter surrounds a star, the more of it the star can capture, and nobody has measured how much dark matter M4 contains.

The team had to rely on estimates from models.

“We do not know the dark matter density there. We used estimated values, so if those values change, our limits could become weaker or stronger,” Tomar noted.

Tomar suggests two places to look next. One is even colder white dwarfs in regions with more dark matter, where any extra heat would be easier to detect.

The other is beyond the Solar System, where exoplanets could serve as dark matter traps too.

The study is published in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics.

The Common Vitamin Linked To Less Belly Fat

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

People with higher levels of a common vitamin tend to carry less fat around their internal organs.

People with higher levels of vitamin D tend to carry less fat around their internal organs.

This deeper layer of belly fat, known as visceral fat, surrounds organs such as the liver and intestines.

It matters because people with more visceral fat tend to have greater difficulty using insulin to control their blood sugar.

The link with vitamin D became weaker when the researchers took body mass index into account, suggesting that overall body size could explain part of the difference.

One possibility is that vitamin D is spread through a larger volume of tissue in people with more body fat, leaving a lower concentration in the blood.

The researchers reviewed records from nearly 1,300 adults with type 2 diabetes who attended a hospital in Quzhou, China, during a two-and-a-half-year period.

Each person had a blood test for vitamin D and a measurement to estimate their visceral fat.

The researchers divided patients into four groups according to their vitamin D levels and compared the estimated area of visceral fat in each group.

The clearest difference was between the groups with the highest and lowest vitamin D levels, with the middle groups showing a less consistent pattern.

After accounting for BMI and other differences between patients, the researchers estimated that the group with the highest vitamin D levels had about 10 square centimetres less visceral fat area than the group with the lowest levels.

Earlier research using genetic data suggested that a higher BMI can contribute to lower vitamin D levels, with weaker evidence for an effect in the other direction (Vimaleswaran et al., 2013).

As well as being distributed through a larger volume of tissue, some vitamin D may be stored in fat tissue, which could affect how much circulates in the blood.

Everyday habits could also help explain why the two measures vary together.

Someone who exercises outdoors may get more sunlight and carry less belly fat, two differences that could help explain the link.

The hospital records did not capture how much sunlight participants got, how active they were or whether they took vitamin D supplements.

Since vitamin D and belly fat were assessed at the same visit, it remains unclear which changed first or whether both were influenced by other factors.

The study was published in the Journal of International Medical Research (Zhu et al., 2026).

“It was cool and a little windy, but I was seeing deer soon after daybreak,” says Miltroka, a 38-year-old long-haul truck driver from Farmland. “I spotted a good buck far out, but the cover was too thick to shoot.”

As the morning wore on, Miltroka got a chance to take two does, and he dropped them both with his Ruger. He toted both does to his truck using a deer cart, since motorized vehicles are not allowed off-road.

The spot was already paying off, and Miltroka had a friend — who had seen a giant deer using the area before the hunt — to thank for tipping him off about it.
