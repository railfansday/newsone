The pilot of Gilmore Girls reveals Rory Gilmore (Alexis Bledel) as a sweet teenage girl who dreams of getting into Harvard, but it also marks the start of Rory and Dean's relationship timeline. She's leaving her small-town high school behind when she meets new kid Dean Forester (Jared Padalecki). Suddenly, she wonders if she should go to the prestigious private school Chilton after all, which starts a big fight between her and her cool mom, Lorelai (Lauren Graham). While Rory's romances become a point of contention throughout the series, her first relationship with Dean is the catalyst for a lot of the shifts in the show's early dynamics.

The foundation of Gilmore Girls is the relationship between Rory and Lorelai, but the romantic entanglements in the series play a big part in Rory's coming of age and Lorelai's ability to allow her daughter to spread her wings. Gilmore Girls deals with teenage life well and part of that includes Rory and Dean's relationship. From their meeting to the early days of their romance, along with their two break-ups and reunions later on in their lives, this love story is an important part of the show. Whether viewers are Team Dean or not, Rory's first big relationship on Gilmore Girls is hugely momentous.

Rory and Dean's relationship timeline begins in the very first episode of the series, unlike Rory's connections to other characters in Gilmore Girls. Rory and Dean meet each other in the Gilmore Girls pilot and they have a truly adorable scene. Rory makes a joke about Rosemary's Baby and is shocked and thrilled when Dean understands it. After kissing at Doose's Market, which is awkward but cute, and Lorelai inviting Dean over for a movie night, Rory and Dean enter an official relationship. Engineered to contain flames and burn fuel more completely, smokeless fire pits deliver ambient warmth with a lot less smoke in your eyes than a traditional campfire. They achieve this through two points of combustion: The first happens at the bottom, where airflow feeds burning wood; the second occurs at the top, where the fire’s smoke and gas output are burned off. And while no smokeless fire pit is 100 percent smokeless, if you use best practices (like burning dry wood) they come pretty darn close.

We tested smokeless fire pits in our New York and Pennsylvania backyards, building fires rather than judging them on specs alone. Along with smoke output, we considered size, which affects wood capacity and where the heat actually lands, as well as how easy each pit was to empty of ash and store once done, among other factors. We also used them repeatedly over time to see how their finishes held up, because a brand-new pit can look very different after several fires.

Before lighting a single fire, we evaluated the things that can make or break a smokeless fire pit before you ever use them. We assessed the packaging and how well each pit was protected in transit, weighed every model, and evaluated assembly. We read the instructions, noting whether they were clear, complete, and actually useful.

We considered whether it could be disassembled for storage, and what came in the box versus what required an additional purchase. While We had plenty of outdoor space to work with, safety always came first: we set every pit on heavy-duty pavers and kept a fire extinguisher close at hand.

Then we lit them up. We used comparable loads of seasoned firewood to assess how easily each pit started, how much smoke it produced, how evenly it burned, and how much heat it put out. We paid attention to flame height and consistency, too, and whether the fire actually warmed people sitting around it—not just looked good from a distance. We measured logs to see exactly what each pit could accommodate and tested wood pellets where models supported them.

Finally, we evaluated the less glamorous but equally important details: how easy each pit was to tend, how much ash accumulated, how easily that ash could be removed, and what it took to clean and put each model away. And because a fire pit can be beautiful at 7 p.m. and a pain in the ass at 10 p.m., we tested for both.

Whether you want a smokeless fire pit to heat your outdoor space or be more of an ambient seasonal moment is an important consideration. For long nights by the fire with family and friends, you’ll likely want to invest in a larger model that measures 24 inches in diameter. In testing, models that size and up exuded warmth for several feet, and could easily warm four to six people.

They could also handle 14- to 16-inch-long logs. The trade-off: Models this size are a lot heavier and harder to move. If you’re looking for more of a warming accent, narrower fire pits work wonders. You can also maximize some models’ output by adding a heat deflector that sits above the flames and pushes heat out laterally, instead of letting it escape upward.

Ideally, the smokeless fire pit should work right out of the box, with limited-to-no setup, besides setting the pit on a base. It should also break down easily. If you’ll be storing it between fires or seasons, a model that’s easy to disassemble, carry, and put away is key.

The shallower and wider the smokeless fire pit, the easier it is to make a fire from scratch. You simply have more base room to work with to set up your paper, kindling, and wood, and less depth to maneuver to light your fire once ready. No matter your choice, you’ll definitely want a lighter with a long metal wand and a safety catch. None of the models we tested included one.

Burning pellets instead of wood was the closest we got to a truly smokeless fire during our testing. Made of compressed wood waste like sawdust, chips, and shavings, pellets are easy to light, always bone dry, and produce a hotter, more consistent flame than logs. There are trade-offs, though: They lack the classic crackle and pop of a wood-burning fire and that wonderful smell.

Bags of the stuff also typically cost more than a handful of logs at the hardware store, yet burn about the same length of time. But, if you’re allergic to smoke, don’t want your sweaters to smell of it, have wet wood drying out, or want a reliable and even fire every single time, pellets are an excellent way to go. Some fire pits work with them right out of the box; others need an adapter.

Every smokeless pit burns wood. Many smokeless fire pits can also use wood pellets right out of the box. Some, like Solo Stove, need an adapter, so the tiny pellets don’t drop right through the pit’s air holes into the ashcan below. Some portable options may only be wide or deep enough to handle twigs (pellets may be a better option for those).

Pro tip: Wet or green wood will always be slow to burn and emit a lot of smoke; use a moisture meter to suss out whether your logs are dry enough to light up before putting a match to them.

If you plan to use your fire pit regularly, easy ash removal should be a deciding factor. The last thing you want to have to do is pick up and tip a heavy steel drum over a trashcan in order to empty it of ash. Instead, look for a removable ash pan or tray that allows you to lift the ash out cleanly once the pit has fully cooled (usually 12 to 24 hours after a fire has ended). Otherwise, you’ll need a shovel to scoop out the ash.

If you don’t have space to leave your fire pit outside all year, think about who will move it and how often. Even a small pit designed for camping trips can weigh as much as 20 pounds. Fortunately, these options usually come with a carrying bag, so you can sling them over your shoulder. Meanwhile, larger models can top 45 pounds (the 24-inch Breeo weighs just over 60 pounds), which matters when you have to move it under cover or into the garage at the end of the season. Units that come apart make this easier; having a dolly or hand truck on hand helps, too.

Material affects how long your new pit will look the way it does on arrival. While stainless steel looks shiny and modern from the start, heat from the flames will discolor it, and potentially quite quickly, as some testing showed. (You can restore its original shine with a stainless-steel cleaner such as Bar Keepers Friend, but that’s extra work.) Corten-steel usually costs more but forms a protective rust-colored patina that looks attractively weathered over time. Painted or powder-coated steel usually costs less, but can scratch and chip with repeat use.

Whatever finish you choose, keeping your smokeless fire pit empty of ash and covered when it’s not in use will help extend its life. Rainwater sitting in a fire pit full of ash can create a corrosive, alkaline slurry that accelerates rust and can damage the metal. Some models come with a cover as part of their bundle; others charge extra for it. Our reviews note whether a cover is included in every pick.

Smokeless fire pits can be a lot more than a place to warm your hands on chilly nights. In some cases, you can turn them into an outdoor kitchen. Brands like Breeo, Solo Stove, and Cuisinart have grill grates, griddles, pizza ovens, or roasting sticks that let you cook over an open fire. Other add-ons also make a difference, like a cover, if you plan to leave it outside when not in use; a spark screen, particularly if you have toddlers and pets around (though, warning: they can also get hot to the touch); and fire pokers or ashtray lifters, so you don’t get your hands dirty. To find the best dual-fuel generator for your home, campground, or worksite, consider the factors listed below, including fuel efficiency, fuel tank capacity, power output, and control panel configuration.

Dual-fuel generators have an isolated gasoline tank with a fuel capacity that ranges from as small as 2 gallons to more than 10 gallons. The larger the fuel tank on the generator, the longer the generator can provide gasoline-based power. But, as tank size increases, so does the size of the generator.

Fuel capacity does not affect how much propane you can use with your dual-fuel generator. Propane gas is stored under pressure in canisters, which attach to the generator with a hose and pressure regulator for direct fuel consumption.

The power output on dual-fuel generators is measured in watts. Manufacturers’ specs include peak power output and running power output. Peak power output refers to the maximum number of watts the generator can provide at any one time. Running power output equals the average number of watts produced during use.

Dual-fuel generators generally produce a power output from 1,000 watts to over 12,000 watts, depending on their size. Gasoline produces a higher wattage than propane, so many manufacturers of dual-fuel technology break wattage into propane peak and running outputs and gasoline peak and running outputs.

For greater power output needs, users may need to consider a standby generator rather than a portable dual-fuel option.

You can start your dual-fuel generator with an electric start switch or a recoil cord.

Many dual-fuel generators produce 50 to 90 dB of noise, depending on fuel type (gasoline runs louder than propane) and power output. The most common range is between 65 dB and 75 dB, comparable to the sound of normal conversation.

The quietest dual-fuel generators combine dual-fuel technology and inverter technology to create a dual-fuel inverter generator.

Dual-fuel generators are typically large and heavy, with hoses or tanks that make them difficult to carry and move. Thus, some manufacturers include wheel kits for tilting the generator back and rolling it to the desired location.

You may also be able to purchase a wheel kit separately to add to your existing generator or replace an older wheel kit that doesn’t work well anymore. Either way, a wheel kit makes transporting a portable dual-fuel generator much easier.

Lift hook bars are handy on jobsites, allowing workers to attach the generator to a hook-and-pulley system and raise or lower it instead of carrying it up or down stairs or ladders. In industrial settings, this feature helps raise the generator onto a walkway or platform.

A lift hook bar will increase the generator’s weight (and likely the price), so don’t look for a generator with this feature unless you truly plan to use it.

Most dual-fuel generators are between 22 inches long by 22 inches wide by 22 inches high and 35 inches long by 30 inches wide by 30 inches high. They typically weigh between 100 and 250 pounds.

Remember, however, that larger, heavier motors are also the most powerful. You’ll want to find the right balance of size, weight, and power to meet your needs.

Gasoline and propane each have their pros and cons as fuel options. Gasoline is easier to find, but propane costs less. Propane is a better fuel source in hot summer weather, while gas burns better in the cold. A dual-fuel generator lets you choose one or the other based on immediate circumstances.

Using both fuel sources, one after the other, greatly increases a generator’s runtime. Users can run to the gas station to refuel while the generator keeps providing power on propane.

A dual-fuel generator operates like a single-fuel generator, except it can burn propane and gasoline to produce electrical energy. It converts mechanical energy produced by the burning fuels to force the movement of electrical charges and generate electricity.

While some single-fuel generators can be adapted for use with natural gas, this isn’t the case with dual-fuel generators. Unless specially designed, dual-fuel generators do not run on natural gas and cannot be adapted for this purpose.

Propane gas is less expensive than gasoline, nearly impossible to spill, and burns cleaner, resulting in less wear on the engine over time. Running a dual-fuel generator on propane will save money but slightly decrease the machine’s peak and running watts.

Runtime is determined by fuel efficiency and fuel tank capacity. On average, a standard dual-fuel generator runs about 8 to 10 hours on propane.

Choosing the right running headphones can really make your run. With so many options available, it’s important to consider which features are most important for your needs. To help guide your decision, here are some key factors to consider when selecting the perfect running headphones.

In-ear headphones, also known as earbuds, fit directly into the ear canal. They are often compact and lightweight, making them highly portable. In-ear headphones can offer good sound isolation, blocking out some external noise and, depending on the fit, a decent level of bass. They often come with various ear tip sizes to make sure they’re not going to fall out of your ears.

Hook headphones typically feature a design with hooks that wrap around the ears for added stability. This makes them popular for activities like running or exercising, where a secure fit is crucial to prevent them from falling out. Hook headphones can come in various styles, including earbuds with hooks and over-ear, open-ear styles with hooks.

Bone conduction headphones work by transmitting sound vibrations through the bones of your skull, bypassing the eardrums. This allows you to listen to audio while keeping your ears open to your surroundings. This is especially useful for runners who need to be aware of traffic or other environmental sounds.

Open-ear headphones are designed to sit outside the ear, allowing you to hear both your audio and the environment around you. They don't block your ear canal, so you remain aware of your surroundings, which is helpful for safety during outdoor activities. However, they may leak sound, and the audio quality might not be as immersive as other types.

Headphones with storage typically include onboard memory for storing music files directly on the headphones. This eliminates the need to carry a separate device for music playback. This feature is particularly convenient for runners or swimmers who prefer not to bring their phones along.
