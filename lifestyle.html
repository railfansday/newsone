Forget Salmon: This Fast-Cooking Fish Has Fewer Calories

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Salmon's rich intensity also means it generally has more calories, which can mean different things based on someone's dietary needs. If you're conscious of this and still want something that cooks up quickly (and deliciously), try flounder, a leaner, lower-calorie option that never lacks flavor.

Exact calorie amounts can vary quite a bit, especially when it comes to wild versus farmed salmon, but even the leanest ones usually sit between 130 and 200 calories per three-ounce serving. Flounder populations, however, have a more uniform lifestyle and consistently clock in at around 100 calories per three-ounce serving. That brown or gray layer of fat may help a salmon migrate and survive cold waters, but flounder are sedentary ambush predators that don't have nearly the same energy requirements. On your plate, this means a fillet of flounder is lean, clean-tasting, and quite mild compared to a richly marbled piece of salmon.

Because flounder is often thinner and far less fatty than salmon, that also means it cooks exponentially quicker. While a skin-on fillet can widen your margin of error, it's still much easier to render your tender fish into a dense, rubbery mess. Your best bet is to treat it like a lean steak and use a hot and fast cooking method, letting a bit of carryover cooking finish the center rather than trying to reach the perfect temperature in a pan or on the grill.

Flounder is delicate, light, and can easily lose its natural flavor if seasoned too heavily. While it's not exactly a beginner-friendly fish, the best ways to cook it are often straightforward and focus on timing and temperature, not technique. Hands-off methods that require little manipulation or rely on a sauce for flavor are usually ideal and keep your meat moist and tender.

If you haven't tried making a meunière yet, it's a classic seafood dish you should try at least once. Made by combining browned butter, lemon, and parsley, it's just flavorful enough to elevate your flounder without overwhelming it. Just about any French butter sauce, from beurre blanc to béchamel, is great with flounder because it adds a bit of richness and is a great vehicle for other tasty seasonings. Plus, they're flavorful enough that you'll only have to pan-sear each side of your fish, reducing the chance that it'll fall apart or overcook.

Flounder grows more delicate the more it's cooked, so preparation methods that rely on you only handling it when raw are also quite ideal. Baking is a precise method, and you only have to move the fish once to transfer it to a plate. On the other hand, flounder is fantastic when deep-fried, and the breading helps it hold its shape. You'll still want to use a large, slotted tool and a delicate hand when fishing it out, but all you need to do is batter and drop it into oil for a great result.

Forget Blue Jeans, This Fall Is All About Coffee-Colored Denim

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Blue denim might be fashion’s most enduring wardrobe staple, but this fall, I’m developing a taste for something a little richer. Enter a sophisticated palette of espresso, mocha and chocolate-brown jeans, which offer all the versatility of black denim, but with a softer, more luxurious feel. The street style set have already got the memo, of course, pairing their brown jeans–mostly of the wide-legged variety–with Penny Lane coats and oversized bombers. Below, discover how to wear autumn’s most appetizing denim trend.

Back in the early Noughties, we loved a fashion rule. Black and green should never be seen. A statement belt was essential for nipping in the waist. Black and brown make a frown. Thankfully, in 2026, we’re no longer beholden to the style diktats of yore. In fact, pairing coffee-coloured jeans with a black jacket is a failsafe way to ease into the trend.

Why stop at one coffee-colored piece when you could have the whole wardrobe? Once you’ve developed a taste for brown denim, it’s only a matter of time before the shade makes its way into the rest of your outfits. Break things up with a touch of grey, or go all in on head-to-toe tones.

Autumn is the perfect time to experiment with texture (there’s little risk of overheating come November), and brown jeans provide the ideal backdrop for a statement coat or chunky knit. Whether you’re partial to a Penny Lane coat or have been dabbling in grandpa golf jumpers, let your denim do the grounding.

Rather than wishing the cold weather away, embrace autumn layering. The warmth of a brown jean makes the perfect foundation for cosy coats, chunky knits, and oversized scarves.

Sometimes, simplicity is best, and few combinations feel more autumn-ready than an oversized leather jacket and jeans. Opt for a bomber-style topper in olive green or burgundy, both of which pair well with coffee-colored denim.

Tod’s Creative Director Matteo Tamburini Steps Down

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Tod’s creative director Matteo Tamburini has stepped down, the company announced on Tuesday, leaving yet another designer vacancy at the helm of a luxury house. A successor has not yet been named.

“I would like to thank Matteo for his creative and professional contribution throughout his tenure, for his commitment and for the work carried out in close collaboration with the company’s teams. I wish Matteo every success in his future professional endeavours,” group chairman and CEO Diego Della Valle said in a statement.

Tamburini joined Tod’s in December 2023, and showed his first collection for the brand in February for Fall/Winter 2024, which was recognized for its attention to house codes. His Spring/Summer 2027 collection, shown in Milan in September, was his last for the brand.

Tamburini joined the brand from Bottega Veneta, where he was the head designer for ready-to-wear. Prior to his tenure at Bottega, the designer worked at brands including Emilio Pucci and Schiaparelli.

Tod’s Group has been having a tough go in recent years. Prior to delisting in May 2024 through a deal with L Catterton, the company’s last public earnings were looking up: revenues at Tod’s Group were up 11.9 per cent year-on-year to €1.13 billion in 2023, driven by a strong performance in China and the retail channel. Tod’s brand rose by 10.4%. But since going private, the macroeconomic environment has become more challenging, and China demand has softened.

In a release shared by Tod’s Group, which owns Tod’s, Hogan and Roger Vivier, the company said that the decision in line with their decision to “embark on a strategic direction aimed at more decisively communicating the brand’s values, the distinctive character of its codes, and the artisanal quality of its products, as well as the Italian lifestyle”.

“My experience at Tod’s has been an important chapter in my professional journey and an opportunity to bring my creative vision to the brand. I am proud of what we have achieved together and grateful to the Della Valle family for the opportunity they have given me,” Tamburini said in a statement. “Above all, I would like to thank the talented people I have had the pleasure of working with, whose passion and dedication have made this experience so meaningful. I conclude this chapter with deep respect for the work we have accomplished and for the people who made it possible.”

One of Star Trek's Best Characters Almost Didn't Exist Because of Gene Roddenberry

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Even though hundreds of notable actors have graced Star Trek with their presence, only one cast member can claim the distinguished title of "most prolific Trek contributor." With 288 onscreen appearances to his name, Michael Dorn's franchise résumé eclipses even his equally legendary Star Trek: The Next Generation co-stars and their Star Trek: The Original Series predecessors. One exquisite Season 3 episode heralds a crucial turning point for both Lieutenant Worf's evolution and Trek's approach to the Klingons — even though Dorn's record-setting, career-defining portrayal faced behind-the-scenes interference from the start.

Despite Worf's immediate intrigue as the first main Klingon character and the lone Klingon to join Starfleet, he spends Season 1 as an isolated background figure — a symbol of the Federation's peaceful alliance with their former antagonists. Once Worf replaces Tasha Yar (Denise Crosby) as the Enterprise's chief of security, he assumes a pivotal role and demonstrates perhaps the most substantial growth out of the entire ensemble. Fond of prune juice, Klingon opera, romantic poetry, and cosplaying American Westerns, Worf survives The Next Generation's seven-season run and its quartet of feature films, joins Star Trek: Deep Space Nine, and reunites with his original crew in Star Trek: Picard's final season as an elite Starfleet Intelligence agent — complete with white hair and a new propensity for witty retorts, meditation, and imbibing tea.

Yet Star Trek's architect, Gene Roddenberry, almost prevented this austere, enthralling, and multifaceted fan favorite from walking onto the Enterprise bridge. According to interviews collected by Mark A. Altman and Edward Gross for their book The 50-Year Mission: The Next 25 Years, Roddenberry wasn't interested in developing the Klingons beyond their restrictive origins as recurring foils whose aggressive nature employs racially-coded stereotypes. This sentiment led Roddenberry, in the words of creative consultant David Gerrold, to "adamantly" resist "[my suggestion of a Klingon first officer]. Gene vetoed it for four months." Even after Roddenberry conceded, executive producer Rick Berman revealed that "Michael Dorn wasn’t even guaranteed all episodes in the first season."

Six decades after The Original Series premiered, the Klingons are regarded as one of the canon's most prominent, recognizable, and sublimely realized alien species. Before The Next Generation Season 3 — the first installment to hit its creative stride — the spin-off had already opened the door for a more sensitive, if still imperfect, depiction. The character beats and plot threads established in the third season's seventeenth episode, "Sins of the Father," written by Ronald D. Moore and W. Reed Moran from a story by Drew Deighan, reverberate for years to come.

Moore displayed an early aptitude for crafting engrossing Klingon-oriented stories. Moore's collective influence and his collaboration with Moran on "Sins of the Father" expands the Klingon Empire into a culture thrumming with vibrant life. Their spiritual practices, feudalistic government, home planet (seen for the first time in "Sins of the Father"), language, and the immense value they place on battle prowess and warrior honor code surpass archetypal words.

Can a new live-action series maintain fans' passion for Star Trek, or will it be another disappointment?

Likewise, "Sins of the Father" grants Worf's underutilized potential a narrative purpose beyond the metaphorical. His upbringing as an orphan separated from his civilization and adopted by doting human parents exacerbates his conflicted duality. Worf strives to prove his worth by honoring his biological heritage and fulfilling his duty to Starfleet, and refuses to accept that he can't unify that contradictory schism. His Enterprise colleagues gradually accept him, while the Qo'noS-born Klingons dismiss him as a weakling who willingly betrayed his birthright.

In "Sins of the Father," Kurn (Tony Todd), the younger brother Worf believes had perished alongside their parents during the infamous Khitomar Massacre, visits the Enterprise to disclose their connection and employ his sibling's aid. Duras (Patrick Massett), the leader of a rival house and a prominent member of the Klingon High Council, has accused the brothers' late father, Mogh, of forming a treasonous alliance with the Romulans. What befalls an ancestor taints their entire bloodline, so Worf vows to prove his father's innocence. If his claim fails, then the sons of Mogh will both die by execution.

Assisted in person by an unwavering Captain Picard (Patrick Stewart) and from afar by the loyal, evidence-scouring Enterprise crew, Worf uncovers a nefarious conspiracy. Duras is framing Mogh to deflect suspicion from his own father, the real traitor, and Chancellor K’mpec (Charles Cooper), the High Council’s leader, has advanced the lie to avoid sparking a civil war. Worf's challenge was doomed long before he issued it.

Worf can’t fathom betraying his familial responsibility, let alone a major legal body condoning such injustice. Nevertheless, he understands the preventative reason behind their cover-up and accepts discommendation (a non-lethal ceremony where individuals endure public dishonor) as long as the Council spares Kurn's life. As Worf's fellow Klingons declare him a coward and turn their backs, leaving him abandoned and shunned, the outlier who studied his culture from afar models a far deeper comprehension of honor, sacrifice, integrity, and courage than the rulers who spout such values but fail to walk the talk.

Between the meaty material and Les Landau's direction, Dorn's magnetism, gravitas, and pathos enjoy their richest opportunity to shine to date. Roddenberry gave Dorn leeway to develop Worf as he saw fit, and the incisive actor-character synergy that elevates Worf into a series-stealing standout is on full display through Worf's dignified resolve, his steely determination, the strength of his principles, and Dorn's extensive range. Since Worf tends to be a man of few words, the ones he does utter carry lethally significant weight.

Meanwhile, his wordless micro-expressions carry enough gravity to sustain multiple planets. Dorn carries Worf through an array of demandingly complex emotions: repressing his fury over Kurn's needling comments, grappling with the appearance of his lone living relative, solemnly yet proudly shouldering the weight of his parents' legacy, his profound gratitude and admiration for Picard, and reconciling with how the Empire's corrupt leadership has shattered the idealized vision of his people Worf carries.

History repeated itself when The Next Generation's production group defended Season 4's "Redemption," a two-part finale following up on "Sins of the Father." In Moore's 2021 interview with the Hollywood Reporter, he shared: "[Gene] didn’t really see Worf as a primary character. [...] And this was going to be the series' 100th episode on top of it. So, we had to fight somewhat to get the episode going." In true warrior fashion, the team's commitment ensured Worf's victory against every obstacle in his path. "Sins of the Father" guides Worf toward his endgame – an assured and complete identity — as an ethical soldier, a diplomatic ambassador, a father, a devout friend, and a widower. A defining moment in Star Trek history, it's a focus for Worf and Dorn that was long overdue.
