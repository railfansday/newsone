‘You’re the Bacon’: A blunt warning to Realtors about AI

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NAR reports 23% of agents use AI daily and 25% weekly, while 12% do not use it and do not plan to. NAR’s Mike Woodward highlighted risks tied to free tier data use, hallucinations, and potential misrepresentation from AI edited listing photos.  AI Summary

More and more real estate professionals are turning to AI tools to automate rote tasks, manage their client relationship management systems and generate marketing collateral.

According to the National Association of Realtors’ (NAR) 2026 Realtors Technology Report released in September, nearly half of agents now use AI daily (23%) or weekly (25%), while 31% are experimenting occasionally. Only 12% said they are not using AI and do not plan to, down from 32% who had not yet tried AI in 2025.

And while AI may help agents save time or create more sophisticated looking marketing, as Mike Woodward, NAR’s director of data science, put it, quoting Spider Man “With great power, comes great responsibility.”

In a session at NAR’s 2026 NXT conference on Sunday, Woodward explored what AI can do for real estate professionals and the housing industry, as well as what it could do to agents and the industry if not used without proper caution and guardrails.

Woodward began by exploring the business model used by AI companies, because, he argues, you cannot understand the risk until you understand how AI companies make their money.

“There ain’t no such thing as a free lunch,” he says, before reaching for a darker metaphor. Two pigs in a barn admire the free shelter and the great food — right up until breakfast. “For those of you who had bacon this morning, you know how that story ends. You’re the bacon.”

As he put it, large language models are “voracious beasts” for data. Feed a free service your documents and it can ingest them, learn from them and surface what it learned to the next user.

Due to this, for anything real or confidential, pay for the service and tick the data-retention opt-out boxes, which stop the model from keeping or training on your inputs.

If the business model is the first trap, the overconfidence of LLMs is the second. Woodward warns against treating AI like “the Oracle of Delphi” — gods walking among us, dispensing truth.

To prove the point, he ran the same kinds of queries his clients run. He asked Copilot about supermarkets and schools near a Massachusetts town; it took the question too literally, omitting stores and schools in neighbouring towns. He asked Claude to pull up records for his home and five of the seven facts came back wrong.

The lesson for agents, he said, is less about the errors themselves and more about their clients.

Buyers now arrive armed with AI-generated impressions of a town or a property and it is in the agent’s interest to know what those buyers think they know.

Woodward also sought to dispel the belief that one AI model is better than the others, noting that agents need to simply pick out and learn how to make it work for them and their business. In addition to this, he also noted that there were no fancy tricks for prompting, instructing agents to “talk to it like a child”: short sentences, simple words, clear instructions.

But AI isn’t all just doomsday scenarios, according to Woodward, who noted that there were many low-risk and genuinely valuable use cases.

Some of these uses included asking Claude to create a plain-English summary of soil-analysis report and provide a step-by-step plan to fix the issues. He also used AI to create a listing description from a property fact sheet, a task that he said might take an agent 15 minutes, but done in seconds instead.

Additional applications he feels are low-risk include using AI to research companies, build charts and generate presentations that real estate professionals can use as a jumping off point.

The caveat running through all of it: on free tiers, use dummy data, and never feed in anything confidential. A performance review he rewrote for a fictional ex–Air Force employee was his cautionary tale — exactly the kind of sensitive, personal text that should never go near a free or overseas-based model.

Fixing technical faults like exposure, white balance or a tilted horizon are things industry professionals have long done using tools like photoshop. But corrections like these are now accessible to everyone thanks to AI regardless of their proficiency with photoshop.

Corrections like these, according to Woodward are fine, but removing temporary blemishes or even inventing property features like adding a fireplace, moving a window or dropping in a swimming pool can cross firmly into misrepresentation, a breach of the Realtor Code of Ethics.

But even when making image corrections, Woodward warned that sometimes the AI will make changes you never ask for.

That is the real hazard, he argues, especially given that the image goes out under the agent’s name.

“You are responsible,” he said. “You cannot say the dog ate my homework.”

The backlash, he warns, is already here: a recent front-page New York Times article in which real estate professionals “did not look good,” and pages of angry posts on Reddit. State legislatures and New York City are weighing regulation. And AI-detection tools, he believes, work well.

“If you use AI and you don’t confess to it, you’re going to get caught,” he said. “People are going to get sued for what I’m going to show you. Consumers are white-hot angry.”

But there is a legitimate version of all this, which Woodward said his lawyers were the ones to note: showing a buyer how a property could look. Virtual staging can turn an empty rental’s “prison cell” walls into something welcoming. The condition is disclosure, noting that agents should show the original alongside the edited image and label the edit clearly as AI-generated.

For Woodward one of the most important takeaways for agents is that they should pay for the tools they want to use when working with confidential information. In addition he said they should reread the code of ethics, ensure they understand the laws coming to their state and when uncertain consult with their legal counsel.

What’s Fogging Your Brain? Signs of Aging vs. ADHD

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cognitive changes may stem from perimenopause, ADHD, or early dementia, each of which requires distinct treatment.

Q: How do I know whether hormonal changes related to perimenopause are causing my cognitive function problems, or my ADHD is getting worse, or I might be experiencing mild cognitive impairment or early dementia?

ADHD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) exhibit clinically different cognitive deficits that help me differentiate one from the other. While ADHD symptoms involve forgetfulness, inattention, and distractibility, MCI has specific cognitive symptoms of word-finding challenges, misspellings, and the use of generic terms like “stuff” and “things” because of word-finding difficulty. Also, MCI is progressive over two to three years, whereas ADHD doesn’t generally worsen.

In perimenopause, a decline in estrogen leads to cognitive difficulties. In contrast to MCI, we know that the cognitive changes from estrogen decline are not the result of neurodegeneration.

However, the specific deficits related to declining estrogen haven’t been well differentiated from those associated with ADHD. So if you have pre-existing ADHD and you experience heightened cognitive difficulties in perimenopause and menopause, you may think, “My ADHD is getting worse.” But it’s likely not worsening ADHD. It’s declining estrogen.

Okay, so what do you do about it? There is a paucity of research for clinical guidance. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) was not always recommended because it was alleged to increase risk for breast cancer and other illnesses. That claim has now been refuted by the most recent research, which has led to the removal of the black box warning from the FDA on HRT product labels. And so, if I have a female patient with ADHD who is 50 years old and says, “For the past two years, I’ve had cognitive decline,” the question to consider is this: “Do I increase her stimulant medication or do I put her on HRT?”

I am inclined at that point to put her on HRT for three months, keep her stimulant medication dosage stable, and monitor the qualitative changes in her cognitive functioning. If there is improvement on HRT, then the question becomes, “Does she stay on HRT for some extended period of time along with the stimulant medication?” If the HRT doesn’t work or she can’t take HRT for medical reasons, then I might increase the stimulant medication dosage to see if we can’t overcome the perimenopausal changes from the estrogen decline. This advice comes from my clinical experience, and it is unfortunate that we don’t have more research for clinical guidance here.

Q: Could ADHD stimulant medication mask symptoms of mild cognitive impairment?

There is very limited research on stimulants and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). My clinical impression is that stimulants likely improve symptoms of MCI, but do not take that as an endorsement to seek treatment for MCI with stimulants. There is emerging research on MCI and stimulants that has not yet been published. The follow-up question to that is, if you have ADHD and MCI, will treating the ADHD delay the development of MCI or Alzheimer’s? There is no clinical research for me to answer that question. But if this were me, I’d be treating my ADHD, keeping my fingers crossed, and appreciating the fact that the quality of my life on medicine is better than it would be without my ADHD treated.

ADHD should not get worse; however, the experience of ADHD may be worse if life’s demands start to increase and exceed your ability to compensate. However, if you’re having cognitive changes that are beyond the scope of your ADHD, you may have two neurologic processes going on: your ADHD and something else. In this case, your ADHD may, indeed, be getting worse because you have a second neurological process that needs to be evaluated.

David W. Goodman, M.D., is an internationally recognized expert on adult ADHD.

Addleshaw Goddard to Deploy Harvey Firmwide and Co-Develop Legal AI

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

London-headquartered Addleshaw Goddard will provide Harvey’s legal AI platform to more than 3,400 lawyers and business services professionals across Europe, the Middle East and Asia from January, alongside a co-development program covering legal work and client delivery, Harvey announced on November 9, 2026.

Addleshaw Goddard’s Innovation Group will work directly with Harvey’s product and legal engineering teams. Lawyers, business services professionals and clients will help shape and test priority capabilities, establish consistent adoption practices and develop new AI-enabled services, making product feedback and client involvement part of the rollout itself.

Addleshaw Goddard said the collaboration followed a three-month assessment led by its 80-person Innovation Group, which drew on a decade of experience developing and deploying AI and legal technology. During the assessment, 400 partners, lawyers, legal technologists and business services professionals tested leading platforms on real legal and business tasks.

From the outset, AG practitioners worked with Harvey’s product and legal engineering teams to test emerging capabilities and provide detailed feedback on complex legal work and delivery at pace and scale. Managing Partner Andrew Johnston described the agreement as the firm’s most significant technology transformation and said the co-development element takes it beyond a firmwide deployment.

The announcement presents Review Tables as an early result of that work. Harvey’s product team and AG’s Innovation Group further developed the feature, which helps lawyers organize, compare and verify large volumes of information on complex matters. AG’s feedback shaped source checking, preservation of lawyer-reviewed work, sharing and performance, and the firm received early access to AI-led features for creating and updating tables at scale.

A Harvey engineering post published October 22, 2026 explains the underlying workflow. Lawyers can ask natural-language questions across uploaded documents, with rows representing individual documents and columns mapping to questions. Each answer cell includes the model’s reasoning and citations from the source document, allowing reviewers to inspect the material behind an answer.

Rows can be assigned to collaborators, answers verified or locked, and comments added for discussion. Once verified, a table can serve as a reusable, validated source for a later memo or presentation. Those controls let large teams divide review work and retain checked answers instead of recreating the analysis.

Scale has also imposed an engineering constraint. Harvey engineers said loading complete tables in the browser caused lag and, in the largest cases, failures after tables exceeded thousands of rows. Their redesign fetches ordered rows page by page while preserving sorting and filtering, so a large table no longer has to load every row into the browser at once.

Addleshaw Goddard and Harvey plan to meet quarterly with selected clients to identify shared challenges, explore new approaches to legal service delivery and, where appropriate, co-create solutions. The sessions extend the collaboration beyond internal adoption by bringing client requirements into the same co-development model.

Kerry Westland, partner and head of the Innovation Group, described the agreement as a decisive next step in the firm’s buy-and-build AI approach. She identified better outcomes, greater quality and consistency, increased capacity and new ways of working with clients as opportunities, while emphasizing that “human judgement, supervision and accountability” should remain central.

The collaboration also sits within AG2030, the firm’s international growth strategy, in which innovation is a central component. Its practical test is to turn co-development and client input into consistent legal services across the workforce while preserving the professional oversight Westland described.

NYU Langone’s PREVIA Flags PTSD Risk From Emergency Narratives

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NYU Langone Health announced on November 9, 2026, that its PREVIA AI ensemble analyzed emergency-department trauma narratives to estimate one-month post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) risk, reporting 95.4% sensitivity for the best configuration in a 145-patient study. The experimental screener is designed to identify at-risk patients without adding a lengthy mental-health questionnaire to emergency intake.

The Nature Communications paper, also published November 9, reports 78.5% AUROC and 46.8% precision across three cross-validation folds and calls for validation in larger, independent cohorts. Sensitivity measures the share of later PTSD cases detected, precision measures the share of flagged patients who actually developed PTSD, and AUROC summarizes how well the scores separate the two outcomes. That precision means many positive screens would be false positives, potentially adding follow-up staff time, patient burden and resource-allocation costs, although the next step would typically be a brief assessment rather than invasive intervention.

PREVIA is designed around a practical gap: only about 28% of US emergency departments routinely screen for PTSD, and only two-thirds of those use validated questionnaires, according to the study. Standard assessments compete with immediate medical needs when clinician time is typically around 15–20 minutes per patient. The first month after trauma is a critical identification window because many patients are no longer in contact with the hospital by then.

Senior author Katharina Schultebraucks, co-director of computational psychiatry at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, said: “PREVIA can serve as an AI screener that identifies at-risk patients and explains why, without adding any time to intake in busy emergency rooms.”

Research coordinators asked patients at two New York City level-1 trauma centers to describe the event that brought them to the emergency department. Participants were enrolled within 24 hours of trauma and given up to three minutes to respond while an iPad recorded video. Narratives averaged about 374 words. Whisper transcribed patient speech, and native English-speaking research staff reviewed the transcripts. One-month PTSD outcomes were determined using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5, a clinical diagnostic interview.

PREVIA divides each transcript across three layers. In the first, Llama and Mistral independently assess early post-traumatic stress symptoms, reactions during the traumatic event, risk factors and protective factors. DeepSeek reconciles their eight assessments into structured summaries, while the two models’ numerical scores are averaged with equal weighting. A final assessor combines the summaries with age, sex, trauma type and PTSD prevalence information to produce a risk score from 0 to 100 and a short explanation.

The pipeline uses zero-shot prompts, with no parameter training or fine-tuning on the study sample. Cross-validation was used to optimize and evaluate the classification threshold.

The paper reports broadly comparable overall performance between PREVIA and single zero-shot configurations of O3, Llama and Mistral on many metrics, with PREVIA’s clearest advantage in sensitivity. For subthreshold PTSD, meaning clinically meaningful symptoms below the full diagnostic threshold, PREVIA reached 89.7% sensitivity and a 69.4% F1 score, a combined measure of precision and sensitivity.

The study reports a separate comparison of 30 randomly selected narratives, stratified to include nine PTSD cases, in which PREVIA recorded 100% sensitivity and 84.4% AUROC. One emergency clinician recorded 88.9% sensitivity and 83.9% AUROC; both recorded 50% precision. The clinician had more than 24 years of emergency-medicine experience and specialized PTSD research experience. The single-rater design leaves open whether the comparison generalizes across experience levels and clinical settings.

Participants were English-speaking, medically stable adults aged 18–80, and women accounted for 31% of the sample. Medically unstable patients were excluded, potentially omitting some patients at highest risk of chronic PTSD. The authors identify testing in other languages, longer-term PTSD trajectories, comorbidities such as depression, and integration with clinician judgment and emergency workflows as future work.

The authors propose a clinical workflow that pairs a brief audio recording with those demographic and trauma variables from the electronic health record, then returns a risk score and explanation without additional clinician input. The study did not establish that such screening improves patient outcomes. PREVIA ran in a HIPAA-compliant high-performance-computing environment on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Self-hosting supports control over patient data, but adoption depends on hospitals having suitable computing and governance infrastructure.

The public PREVIA repository provides the complete pipeline and prompts for open-weight models that run locally, plus five fabricated narratives for an end-to-end demonstration. Identifiable clinical narratives and psychiatric assessments remain restricted for privacy and consent reasons, so the demo can test operation but cannot reproduce the paper’s quantitative results.

PREVIA’s immediate contribution is an independently testable screening approach built from a routine emergency encounter and clinician-assessed outcomes. Its clinical value now hinges on independent validation: whether larger cohorts preserve its sensitivity, whether services can manage the false positives, and whether hospitals can incorporate recording, follow-up and secure computing without disrupting emergency care.

Pierre Fabre Taps XtalPi’s AI and Robotics for Drug Discovery

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Laboratoires Pierre Fabre and XtalPi announced on November 9, 2026 an agreement to discover novel small-molecule clinical candidates across multiple research programs. XtalPi will contribute integrated AI capabilities and a robotics platform, while Pierre Fabre will select and advance compounds in oncology, potentially dermatology, and rare diseases.

The partners are directing that work toward challenging and historically hard-to-drug targets, which they associate with significant unmet medical need and substantial opportunities for innovative drug discovery.

That focus gives context to the companies’ stated goal of accelerating the identification of high-quality clinical candidates. The emphasis on both quality and speed means the intended output is compounds strong enough to become clinical candidates in areas where discovery has been difficult.

XtalPi will handle de novo design, meaning the creation of new molecular structures, and deliver novel small-molecule therapeutics against undisclosed targets. This places its integrated AI capabilities and robotics platform at the front end of the partnered discovery process, with the resulting molecules moving into Pierre Fabre’s selection work.

Pierre Fabre will apply expertise in disease biology, discovery, translational research, and clinical development to select and advance the most promising compounds. It will also be responsible for subsequent clinical and commercial development. The agreement thus gives Pierre Fabre continuity across the selection decision and the later development path, while XtalPi’s defined responsibility remains the design and delivery of the underlying molecules.

The acceleration goal expressly spans multiple programs in oncology and potentially other therapeutic areas. That scope gives XtalPi’s platform several research settings in which its designs can be assessed using Pierre Fabre’s disease-biology and translational expertise.

The agreement includes an upfront payment, multiple milestone payments, and downstream economic participation.

For Laboratoires Pierre Fabre, the agreement is a strategic addition to its R&D roadmap and strengthens its drug-discovery capabilities. Francesco Hofmann, head of pharma research and development at Laboratoires Pierre Fabre, described XtalPi’s AI expertise as “a critical addition to our R&D Data and AI roadmap and to our discovery engine.”

For XtalPi, the collaboration advances its effort to apply AI and robotics to de novo drug discovery. The two stated rationales are complementary: Pierre Fabre is adding an AI-enabled discovery component to its research roadmap, while XtalPi is applying its platform within partnered therapeutic programs that have a defined route into clinical and commercial development.

The consequential test sits at the handoff between molecular design and development. For targets that have historically resisted drug discovery, the partnership’s value depends on whether XtalPi’s designed molecules become compounds Pierre Fabre considers promising enough to advance, and ultimately whether they meet the companies’ goal of high-quality clinical candidates in areas of unmet medical need.

Ian Ippolito, a former professional software developer, is an investor and serial entrepreneur. He founded Private Investor Club and The Real Estate Crowdfunding Review, where his five-part series on AI and investing begins here.

For years, I dismissed AI as hype. Then in February 2026, it finished in a few hours the Monte Carlo program for our family’s finances that had defeated it for months. Since then many have been surprised at how expensive and scarce AI is turning out to be. Here’s what I found, and how it’s changing where I invest.

(Disclaimer: I’m an investor sharing my personal opinion. I’m not your attorney, accountant or financial advisor, so consult your own professionals before making any financial decisions.)

Before starting companies, I worked as a professional software developer. Later I founded and sold vWorker (formerly Rent a Coder), an early online marketplace for hiring programmers.

Today I run Private Investor Club, where accredited investors share due diligence on private investments.

So when AI companies claimed their tools could write software, I tested the claim myself. In the fall of 2025, I used AI to help build a Monte Carlo simulation for our family’s finances: the kind of program that runs thousands of what-if market scenarios, which many of you have used to test your own retirement plans.

It was miserable. The AI forgot what it had done a few minutes earlier, ignored instructions and broke code that already worked. After a few months of workarounds, I gave up.

By December 2025, I expected AI to join the long list of technologies that never lived up to their hype. And I thought an ugly stock market correction was coming.

In February 2026, an acquaintance mentioned that Anthropic’s Claude Code had improved a lot, so I gave it one more try, fully expecting to be disappointed.

I handed it the half-finished Monte Carlo program. It finished the job in a few hours, and the code was clean and worked.

Then I gave it something harder. In 2024, my wife and I went shopping for a lot to build a home on, and quiet matters a lot to us. So I paid a talented overseas developer $3,500 to build a program that tracks airplane flights over different parts of town. He did excellent work in six weeks.

I gave Claude Code the exact same requirements. It built the whole program in about five hours, for roughly 69 cents’ worth of my $100 monthly subscription. It even caught an error in a data provider’s documentation that I had missed, and it added useful features I hadn’t asked for.

I spent decades learning to program. Watching a machine do that work faster and better than a very good human developer was both thrilling and a little sad.

When AI redoes a $3,500 programming job for 69 cents, it looks like a nearly free substitute for human workers. Plenty of executives had been saying exactly that for years:

The predictions had a real basis. AI is priced by the “token,” a small piece of text the AI reads or writes, and the price of each token really has collapsed. In early 2025, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman estimated that the cost of a given level of AI drops roughly tenfold every year (Three Observations).

But the newest AI models chew through far more tokens to finish each task. So the price per token can keep falling while the total bill keeps climbing.

The research firm Gartner now expects AI programming to end up costing more than the staff it was supposed to replace. In June 2026 it forecast that by 2028, companies will spend more on AI coding than the average developer earns, because models are consuming more tokens and vendors are moving to pay-per-use pricing (Gartner).

Even if tokens keep getting cheaper, AI runs on land, buildings, permits and electricity. And all four are getting scarcer.

Electricity is the other bottleneck. The consulting firm ICF forecasts that U.S. electricity demand will grow about 25% between 2023 and 2030 (ICF). Data centers are competing for that power with electric cars, heat pumps and new factories.

My view is that AI will stay scarce and expensive for a long time. Cheaper chips can’t conjure up power plants, transmission lines or a permitted data center overnight.

So I’m looking at two kinds of investments: the things AI can’t run without (the first three below), and the work AI still can’t do (the fourth). Physician on FIRE has covered the public-market side in Investing In The AI Revolution; my focus here is private deals.

In each area, I’ll start with the deals that didn’t make it through my due diligence, and then the ones that did.

1. The AI companies themselves. Every one of those runaway AI bills is income for whoever sells the AI.

I passed on two fast-growing startups, one claiming its revenue was multiplying about fourfold a month, because I thought the big AI developers, such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, could add what they do as a feature. I also passed on a fund aimed at mid-stage companies, which are riskier (though the winners can pay more).

2. The buildings and networks AI runs on. When billions of dollars of new data centers are blocked or delayed, the ones already approved, powered and operating become harder to replace.

AI Agents Now Design and Run Your Robot-Powered Lab. The Molecules They Engineer Deserve Better Than a Flat Screen.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The lab is quiet at 2 a.m., and that's the strange part.

Not empty-quiet. Productive-quiet. A six-axis arm swings over a rack of vials, pauses, swaps a pipette tip, and lowers itself toward well B7. Somewhere upstream, an agent read a paper that dropped three hours ago, proposed a synthesis route, priced the reagents, and queued the run. Nobody drove in. Nobody's wearing a lab coat. The experiment is already halfway done.

If you've been watching the last year of lab automation, this isn't science fiction anymore — it's a Tuesday. Agents plan. Robots execute. The loop closes without a human in the middle. And the output of that loop — the thing you actually care about — is a molecule. A three-dimensional object with a shape, a surface, a binding pocket, a personality. A thing that only makes sense when you can turn it in your hands.

Which is why I keep coming back to a small, uncomfortable observation: the tools we use to look at these molecules never got the same upgrade.

We spent a decade making the making faster. We left the seeing on a flat screen.

Here's the honest shape of a modern autonomous lab stack, as I've seen it described by the people building them:

That's a real architecture, and the people building it are doing genuinely hard work. Scheduling a robot to run a 96-well plate overnight without collisions is not trivial. Getting an agent to produce a valid protocol — one the hardware will actually accept — is its own research problem.

But notice what's missing from that stack. There's no layer for inspection. No place where a human, or the agent, or the two of them together, sits down and looks at the molecule before committing to the next 96 wells.

The output of a run is a structure file. A .mol. A .sdf. A .pdb. And when a scientist opens it, the default experience is: a flat 2D rendering, or a 3D viewer that spins the thing on a trackpad and gives you no sense of scale, no sense of depth, no sense of this pocket is shallow and that one is deep.

You designed a molecule with an agent. You ran it on a robot. And then you looked at it through a keyhole.

A small molecule is on the order of a nanometer across. Its shape is the entire point — lock-and-key, induced fit, all of it. A binding pocket is a cavity. It has a floor and walls and a rim. When you're deciding whether to keep a candidate or kill it, you're making a judgment about geometry.

On a flat screen, you get a projection. You get shading cues. You get a rotating turntable that your brain has to reconstruct into 3D, and your brain is bad at that for anything more complex than a coffee cup. Ask anyone who's tried to teach stereochemistry over Zoom.

The agents don't have this problem in the same way — they operate on coordinates. But we do. And the humans are still the ones who decide what's worth making.

So the loop has a bottleneck that isn't compute, isn't reagents, isn't robot time. It's human spatial cognition, mediated by a 2D display.

I've been following this space for a while, and the tool that's been sitting in my bookmarks — and, full disclosure, in the product reference for this piece — is Mol3D from Patsnap. The premise is simple and, once you see it, obvious: take the molecule that came out of your pipeline and put it in interactive 3D, on the web, in the browser, without a plugin or a desktop install.

I'm not going to oversell it. It's a viewer. But "just a viewer" undersells what changes when the viewer is 3D, interactive, and web-native:

That last point is the one I keep thinking about. In an autonomous lab, the human's job is increasingly judgment — deciding which of the machine's proposals to pursue. And judgment about a 3D object is better when the object is 3D.

I don't think the answer is "put on a headset." I've watched enough VR demos die on the vine to be suspicious of that pitch. The answer, for most working scientists, is probably closer to: make the 3D view as easy to open as a Google Doc.

That's a lower bar than it sounds, and a higher bar than most cheminformatics tools clear.

If you're building agents that design molecules, or robots that make them, or pipelines that connect the two — the last mile is a human looking at the result. It's worth asking whether that last mile is a flat screen or a window you can walk around.

The lab is going to keep getting smarter. The molecules are going to keep getting more interesting. The least we can do is look at them properly.

Amp Spaces: One Agent to Coordinate Them All — What Changes When You Stop Managing Threads

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 6, Amp launched spaces — a new way to work with coding agents that flips the usual multi-agent workflow on its head. Instead of you creating and organizing threads, a space is a conversation with a single agent whose job is to spawn and coordinate agents for you.

That sounds like a small interface change. It isn't. It moves the human out of thread management entirely.

If you've used a coding agent for anything bigger than a one-off fix, you know the pattern: one thread per task, tabs multiplying, and you acting as the dispatcher — deciding what gets its own thread, what's done, what needs a follow-up. The agent does the work; you do the coordination.

Amp's answer is to put one agent in charge of that coordination. You talk to the space agent. It breaks the work down, spawns threads, and reports back. Amp's own description of the feeling is telling: you operate with a bird's-eye view but can still dive into the weeds when necessary. "It's more like sitting in the captain's chair than down in the trenches."

To start one, you hit the + in the Spaces section of the sidebar, set a title and a project, and tell your agent what to work on. The space agent takes it from there.

The announcement is more convincing than most launch posts because Amp has been dogfooding spaces for weeks, and it gives three concrete examples.

Spaces were built in a space. Tim, Lewis, Nicolay and the rest of the team shared a space called "Spaces v1", dropped feedback, bugs, and new ideas into the chat, and the agent delegated each item into threads. When the team needed to talk, they used comments to chat and tagged in Amp when they were ready. The product shipped out of its own workflow.

A feature launch, run from one space. Lewis ran the launch of service accounts from a single space. From there, he could roll out the feature, monitor X, Slack, and bug reports for issues, fix incoming bugs, and send out emails and refunds to affected users. The space kept monitoring user issues and system health for the rest of the week. That's not a coding task — it's an operations shift, coordinated by one conversation.

Recurring security reviews. Will gave a space a standing goal: review recent changes for security issues. Its first run split the codebase into 16 areas, reviewed each in its own thread, cross-checked every finding in another thread, and kept a summary artifact in the space for Will to review and track. The supervised first run filed 14 issues.

One detail worth noting: the team says they naturally moved communication into the space instead of Slack — taking video calls and discussions there. The space became the room where the work happened, not just a log of it.

Multi-agent systems keep running into the same wall: spawning agents is easy, coordinating them is the actual work. Every framework has its own answer — orchestrators, handoffs, shared memory, message buses — but most of them still make the human the orchestrator, or bury the orchestration in config the human has to maintain.

Spaces takes the opposite bet: the coordinator is just another agent, and you talk to it in natural language. The coordination logic lives in the conversation, not in a YAML file. That has real advantages:

This is a launch announcement, not a benchmark. A few things to watch before treating spaces as the new default:

If you use Amp, the lowest-risk way to try this is to copy the security review pattern: pick a bounded, repeatable goal with a clear artifact at the end — a review, an audit, a migration checklist — and give the space a standing goal with a supervised first run. You get the coordination benefit with a human checkpoint built in, and the summary artifact tells you quickly whether the coordinator is earning its keep.

The bigger question spaces raises is about where the human sits in agent workflows. For two years the answer has been "in the loop, managing threads." Amp is betting the answer becomes "in the chair, managing goals." If the coordinator agent is reliable enough, that's a better job for everyone — including the agents.
