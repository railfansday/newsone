North Korean hackers create memecoins to launder funds from Binance hack: Elliptic

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The blockchain firm traced stolen funds through six new tokens on NEAR, where hackers used liquidity pools to obscure transfers between wallets.

North Korean hackers created memecoins to launder cryptocurrency stolen in the $6.4 billion Binance hack, routing roughly $430,000 through six newly launched tokens on the NEAR blockchain, according to a report from blockchain analytics firm Elliptic.

Between Nov. 5 and 7, the hackers created at least six tokens on NEAR and used liquidity pools on decentralized exchanges to move stolen funds between wallets, Elliptic said. The tokens attracted almost no outside trading and were abandoned shortly after launch.

The hackers used one set of wallets to swap stolen NEAR tokens for the new memecoins, then used another set to sell those memecoins back into the pools for NEAR. The swaps let them move funds without direct wallet-to-wallet transfers, though the transactions remained traceable onchain.

How stolen NEAR moved between wallets through liquidity pools. Source: Elliptic

Elliptic also found that five of the six token creators were funded with proceeds from earlier token sales just two to 13 minutes before the next launch, linking the tokens in a chain of transactions.

“North Korea’s launderers rarely rely on a single technique,” Elliptic co-founder Tom Robinson wrote in the report. “Instead, they layer many cheap techniques, each adding a little more delay.”

Elliptic said the same laundering technique was also used following the $1.5 billion Bybit hack in 2025, when North Korean-linked actors moved at least $24 million through a newly created token on the TRON blockchain.

At the time of Elliptic’s analysis, about 11.8 million NEAR stolen in the Binance hack remained unmoved. Elliptic valued those funds at $43.7 million.

Amazon’s New Kindle Paperwhite Is Here. Here’s What’s Different From the Last Version.

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From slimmer designs to updated displays and charging features, here's what's changed with Amazon's latest e-readers.

Amazon has refreshed its Kindle lineup for 2026, introducing new versions of the popular Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition. Both e-readers have notable design updates, but if you already own a previous model, you might wonder whether it's worth upgrading.

The short answer? It depends on which Kindle you're using. Compared to the 2024 models, the latest Paperwhites offer a sleeker design, updated display technology, and some welcome improvements to everyday usability. The Signature Edition, in particular, gets a few extras that make it feel more like a premium device. Here's what's changed and what's stayed the same.

Amazon Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition

The most noticeable upgrade is the design and appearance. Amazon has made the standard Paperwhite thinner and slightly lighter, reducing its thickness from 7.8 millimeters to 6.8 millimeters. It also weighs approximately 204 grams, compared to the previous model's 211 grams. That might not sound like much, but a slimmer e-reader is always welcome when you're holding it for hours or slipping it into a bag.

The redesigned display construction also creates a more seamless, edge-to-edge look. Amazon uses a new display stack with an oxide thin-film transistor backplane, designed to deliver excellent contrast, deeper blacks, and crisp text. The screen is still 7 inches with a 300-ppi resolution, so don't expect a dramatic jump in sharpness over the 2024 model.

Another small but genuinely useful change is the power button's placement. Instead of sitting along the bottom edge, where it was easy to press accidentally, it's now positioned on the upper-right side.

And if battery life is your biggest concern, there's good news: Amazon still promises up to 12 weeks on a single charge, just like the previous generation. The new model also maintains its IPX8 waterproof rating and adjustable warm lighting.

The Signature Edition receives the same thinner design and updated screen construction, but Amazon has made a few additional changes to distinguish it from the standard Paperwhite.

For starters, it now has an aluminum back instead of the previous model's plastic construction, giving it a more premium look and feel. That upgrade comes with a trade-off, though: At approximately 229 grams, the new Signature Edition is actually a little heavier than its 214-gram predecessor.

The new model also introduces pogo-pin connectors for charging and accessories. These let the Kindle connect to Amazon's new charging stand and a magnetic Page-Turn Cover with physical buttons, which could appeal to readers who miss the page-turn buttons on older e-readers.

One important difference is that the 2024 Signature Edition supported conventional Qi wireless charging, while the 2026 model switches to the new contact-based pogo-pin system.

Amazon also highlights a double-tap feature that lets you advance pages by tapping the side or back of the device, making one-handed reading a little easier.

Otherwise, you'll still get 32GB of storage, an auto-adjusting front light, and up to 12 weeks of battery life. Both generations are waterproof, too.

If you're shopping for a new Kindle rather than replacing an older one, the decision mostly comes down to whether the Signature Edition's premium features are worth an additional $50.

The standard Paperwhite starts at $199.99 and includes 16GB of storage, adjustable warm lighting, and a 7-inch glare-free display.

For $249.99, the Signature Edition doubles the storage to 32GB, adds an aluminum back and automatic brightness adjustment, and supports the new pogo-pin charging accessories. It's also available in Seaglass Green, whereas the regular Paperwhite comes in Graphite.

Both deliver essentially the same core reading experience, so casual readers probably won't miss much by sticking with the less expensive version.

If you already own a 2024 Kindle Paperwhite or Signature Edition and it's working perfectly fine, you probably don't need to replace it. You're still getting the same screen size and resolution, excellent battery life, waterproofing, and access to Amazon's massive Kindle library.

However, if you're upgrading from an older 2021 Paperwhite—or you're shopping for your first e-reader—the 2026 models are worth considering. The thinner design, improved screen construction, and updated controls make the latest lineup more appealing.

For most readers, the regular Paperwhite will be more than enough. But if you read every day, want the premium aluminum design, or like the idea of a case with physical page-turn buttons, the Signature Edition is a worthwhile step up.

When you can spread the flu and when to return to work: 12 facts

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 12 facts explain how long the flu is contagious, tracing when influenza spreads from the day before symptoms to the CDC's fever-free threshold for work and school

The flu can spread before anyone knows they have it. An adult infected with influenza can pass the virus to others about one day before the first fever, ache or cough appears. The same person can keep spreading it after the worst of the illness has passed and they feel ready to get back to normal life. Contagiousness and feeling sick do not follow the same schedule, and that mismatch is what makes the question hard to answer.

So how long is the flu contagious? The answer depends on where a person is in the illness and who that person is. The incubation period, the time between exposure and first symptoms, usually runs one to four days, with an average of about two days. Contagiousness tends to peak in the first three to four days of illness. Most healthy adults can infect others for roughly five to seven days after symptoms start. Children and people with weakened immune systems can shed the virus for longer.

These timelines carry practical weight. They shape when a parent keeps a child home from school and when an employee returns to the office. They also shape when a household can stop taking extra precautions around a vulnerable relative. A person who goes back to work as soon as they feel better may still be carrying enough virus to pass it on. Fever-reducing medicine such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen can also hide a fever that has not truly broken.

The 12 slides that follow trace the flu from first exposure to recovery. They cover the incubation period, the day of spread before symptoms, the peak contagious days and the longer timelines for children and immunocompromised people. They also cover the CDC's 24-hour fever-free guidance for returning to work or school. Other slides explain the main ways the virus moves between people and how antivirals such as Tamiflu fit in. Which flu shot should you get? It depends on age and health

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Types of flu shots differ by age, health and manufacturing, from standard-dose and cell-based vaccines to high-dose shots for seniors and the FluMist nasal spray

Picking a flu shot is less about brand than about birthday. Age drives most of the decision, followed by health status and whatever the local pharmacy or clinic has in stock. The names on the consent form — Fluzone, Flucelvax, Flublok, Fluad, FluMist — mostly describe different ways of building the same kind of protection.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends a flu vaccine every year for everyone six months and older, with a few exceptions. For most healthy people under 65, any licensed vaccine approved for their age is acceptable. CDC does not rank one of those options above another. The guidance shifts at 65. Since 2022, the agency's vaccine advisers have preferentially recommended certain products for that age group. It shifts again for young children, pregnant people and anyone weighing the nasal spray.

The shots themselves changed recently. Before the 2024-25 season, U.S. flu vaccines were quadrivalent, built to protect against four flu strains. Starting that season, they became trivalent, covering three strains, after the B/Yamagata component was removed. The specific strains are not fixed from year to year. Each season's composition is set anew.

The differences among the types of flu shots come down to three questions. How is the vaccine made? Who is it built for? And how does it enter the body? The traditional shot uses killed virus grown in chicken eggs. Flucelvax grows its vaccine virus in mammalian cell cultures instead. Flublok skips the virus entirely, relying on a lab-produced version of a flu protein called hemagglutinin. Fluzone High-Dose and Fluad are designed for adults 65 and older, whose immune systems respond less strongly to vaccines. FluMist is a live, weakened vaccine sprayed into the nose rather than injected.

The seven entries below are a selection of vaccine types and situations, not a ranking. The first six explain who each vaccine is licensed for, how it works and where its limits sit. The last entry covers three common questions. It explains what people with egg allergy can get, which shots suit pregnancy and why some young children need two doses in one season.

The standard-dose inactivated flu shot is the traditional injected vaccine, made from killed influenza virus grown in chicken eggs. Brands include Fluarix, FluLaval and Fluzone, and some of these products are licensed for children as young as six months.

This is the version most people picture when they think of a flu vaccine. Pharmacies, clinics and doctors' offices commonly stock it for a wide range of ages, from young children to working-age adults.

"Inactivated" means the virus in the vaccine has been killed. The shot contains no live virus. It cannot cause the flu in the person who receives it. Instead, it shows the immune system the surface proteins of the season's selected strains. The body learns to recognize those proteins and can respond faster if the real virus arrives.

Manufacturing starts with fertilized chicken eggs. Vaccine makers inject the selected flu viruses into the eggs and incubate them for several days while the viruses multiply. The virus-laden fluid is then harvested. The virus is killed and the antigen is purified before it goes into vials and syringes. This egg-based process has been the main way to make flu vaccine for decades. Methods using cell cultures or lab-made proteins arrived later.

The dose is measured by hemagglutinin, the protein on the virus surface that the immune system targets. A standard-dose shot contains 15 micrograms of hemagglutinin for each strain it covers. Shots built for older adults change that formula, either with more antigen or with an added ingredient.

In the U.S., the FDA selects the strains for each season's flu vaccines, drawing on recommendations from the World Health Organization. Individual brands do not choose their own strains.

The shot is given as an injection into a muscle, usually the upper arm for adults. Infants and young toddlers often receive it in the thigh. Common side effects include soreness, redness or swelling where the needle went in. Some people also have a low fever, headache or muscle aches that pass within a few days.

Age eligibility differs from brand to brand. Some standard-dose products are licensed from six months, while others start at older ages. The pharmacist or the product label confirms which brand fits a given person.

For most healthy people under 65, a standard-dose shot is an acceptable option. Adults 65 and older have separate preferred options from CDC's vaccine advisers. A standard-dose shot remains a fallback for that age group when none of those is available.

Flucelvax is a flu vaccine whose virus is grown in mammalian cell cultures instead of chicken eggs. It is approved in the U.S. for people six months and older. That matches the full age range of CDC's yearly flu vaccine recommendation.

The difference lies in one production step. Every flu vaccine starts with virus strains chosen for the coming season. Makers of most flu shots then grow large amounts of that virus. For traditional shots, that growth happens in eggs. For Flucelvax, it happens in mammalian cells kept in culture.

That choice addresses a known issue with egg-based production. A flu virus grown in eggs can pick up small changes as it adapts. Those changes can leave the vaccine virus slightly different from the virus selected for the vaccine. Avoiding that step is one reason cell-based manufacturing exists.

That rationale is about how the vaccine is made. It is not a finding that Flucelvax protects people better overall. CDC does not rank Flucelvax above other licensed flu vaccines for healthy people under 65.

The cells used for Flucelvax come from a line called Madin-Darby canine kidney, or MDCK, cells. That line was originally derived from a dog's kidney. Cell-based production also does not depend on a large supply of chicken eggs.

The FDA approved Flucelvax in 2012. It was the first U.S. seasonal flu vaccine made using cell culture technology. Its first approval covered adults only. Later expansions brought the age range down to six months. CSL Seqirus makes the vaccine.

The broad age approval means a parent and a baby can receive the same product. Whether a particular pharmacy or clinic stocks it depends on location.

Flucelvax is not among the shots singled out for older adults. In 2022, CDC's vaccine advisers preferentially recommended high-dose, adjuvanted or recombinant vaccines for adults 65 and older. An older adult can still receive Flucelvax when none of those preferred shots is available.

Flublok is a recombinant flu vaccine, which means it is made without the flu virus itself and without chicken eggs. It contains a laboratory-produced version of hemagglutinin, a protein found on the surface of the influenza virus.

Hemagglutinin is the part of the virus the immune system learns to recognize after vaccination. It sits on the outside of the flu virus and helps the virus attach to the cells it infects. Antibodies that recognize this protein can help block that step. A shot that delivers the protein alone gives the body that target without exposing it to any virus.

The other U.S. flu vaccines all begin by growing influenza virus. Flublok skips that stage. Its hemagglutinin is made in insect cells that carry the genetic instructions for the protein. The protein is then purified for the vaccine rather than harvested from a grown virus. The finished shot contains no live or killed virus.

Flublok was first licensed for adults only. In 2025, the FDA extended its approval to people aged nine and older. That expansion is recent. Children younger than nine need a different licensed vaccine that covers their age. Several injected options, including Flucelvax, are approved from six months.

For adults 65 and older, Flublok is one of three vaccine types that CDC's advisory committee, ACIP, preferentially recommended in 2022. The other two are the high-dose and adjuvanted shots. That makes Flublok the only one of the three preferred types that is also licensed for people under 65.

Flublok is also an option during pregnancy. CDC advises pregnant people to get an injected inactivated or recombinant shot rather than the live nasal spray. Flublok, as a non-live recombinant vaccine, fits that guidance.

For healthy people under 65 who fall within its approved age range, CDC does not rank Flublok above other licensed, age-appropriate flu vaccines.

Fluzone High-Dose is a flu shot licensed only for people 65 and older. Each dose carries four times the antigen of a standard-dose shot: 60 micrograms of hemagglutinin per strain, compared with 15.

Hemagglutinin is the protein on the surface of the flu virus that the immune system learns to recognize. A flu vaccine presents this protein to the body, which then builds antibodies before a real infection arrives. The amount in each dose helps set how strong a signal the vaccine sends.

The higher dose exists because immune systems change with age. Older adults respond less strongly to vaccines than younger people do. A standard dose can prompt a weaker antibody response in a 75-year-old than in a 30-year-old. Fluzone High-Dose aims to close part of that gap by giving the immune system more of the target protein to react to.

Across the three strains in the current trivalent formula, a high-dose shot holds 180 micrograms of hemagglutinin in total. A standard-dose trivalent shot holds 45 micrograms.

The age limit is firm. Adults under 65 choose from the shots licensed for their own age group instead. The extra antigen targets the weaker vaccine response seen later in life, not a general need for a bigger dose.

The product shares the Fluzone brand name with a standard-dose shot. At a pharmacy or clinic, the "High-Dose" label is the part that identifies the version built for older adults.

Fluzone High-Dose is one of three vaccine types that CDC's advisory committee, ACIP, preferentially recommended in 2022 for adults 65 and older. The other two are the adjuvanted shot Fluad and the recombinant shot Flublok. The recommendation does not favor one of the three over another.

The strain composition of the high-dose shot, like that of every other flu vaccine, is set anew for each season. A dose from a previous year does not substitute for the current season's shot.

Fluad is a flu vaccine for adults 65 and older that adds an ingredient called MF59 to strengthen the body's immune response. MF59 is an adjuvant, an oil-in-water emulsion mixed into the shot alongside the flu antigen.

An adjuvant works differently from simply putting more antigen in a dose. The antigen is the part of the vaccine the immune system learns to recognize. In flu vaccines, that is mainly the hemagglutinin protein on the virus surface. An adjuvant does not add more of that target. It helps provoke a stronger reaction to the antigen that is already there.

That distinction separates Fluad from Fluzone High-Dose, another shot built for older adults. The high-dose vaccine raises the amount of antigen per strain. Fluad keeps a standard amount of antigen and relies on the adjuvant to do more of the work. Both designs address the same problem: the body's response to vaccines weakens with age.

Adjuvants are not unique to flu shots. Aluminum salts have long served as adjuvants in other routine vaccines. MF59 is built around squalene, an oil the human body also produces.

The FDA first licensed Fluad in the U.S. in 2015. It was the first seasonal flu vaccine containing an adjuvant to win approval in the country.

Fluad is an inactivated vaccine. It contains no live virus and cannot cause the flu. It is given as an injection into the muscle, usually in the upper arm.

In 2022, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, which advises CDC, preferentially recommended three kinds of flu vaccine for adults 65 and older. Those were high-dose, adjuvanted and recombinant vaccines. Fluad is the adjuvanted option in that group. The committee did not rank the three against one another. An older adult can receive whichever of them is on hand.

The preference does not mean older adults should go unvaccinated when a preferred shot is out of stock. If none of the three is available, CDC guidance says any age-appropriate flu vaccine should be used instead.

Younger adults and children are not eligible for Fluad. They choose from other licensed flu vaccines suited to their age.

FluMist is a needle-free flu vaccine sprayed into the nose, and it is approved for people aged two through 49. It contains live attenuated influenza viruses. These viruses are weakened so they do not cause flu illness in healthy recipients.

That design sets the spray apart from every injected option. Flu shots contain inactivated virus or a single lab-made flu protein. The nasal spray instead uses viruses adapted to grow at the cooler temperatures inside the nose. They can replicate there, but they grow poorly in the warmer lungs.

AstraZeneca $AZN makes the spray. Each dose is split between the two nostrils, with half sprayed into each side.

The age window is firm. FluMist is not licensed for babies and toddlers under two. It is not licensed for adults 50 and older either. Both groups need an injected vaccine instead.

Some people inside that age range should also skip the spray. It is not recommended for pregnant people. It is also not advised for people with weakened immune systems. The vaccine contains live virus, which is the reason for that caution.

CDC lists other conditions that rule it out. Children and teens taking aspirin or other salicylate medicines should not get it. Children aged two to four who have asthma, or who have wheezed in the past 12 months, should get a shot instead. People who recently took flu antiviral drugs should check with a clinician first. Those medicines can stop the vaccine virus from replicating.

The spray has a history of shifting guidance. CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices advised against using it in the 2016-17 and 2017-18 seasons. That decision followed weak performance against one influenza A strain. The committee restored it as an option for the 2018-19 season.

The newest change concerns who gives the dose. In 2024, the FDA approved FluMist for self-administration or for administration by a caregiver. That approval has made home delivery possible. Eligible people can get a flu vaccine without visiting a pharmacy or clinic.

For healthy people in the eligible age range, CDC treats the nasal spray as one acceptable option alongside age-appropriate shots. The agency does not rank the spray above or below them.

People with an egg allergy can receive any licensed flu vaccine that suits their age. CDC says they need no special precautions beyond those that apply to any vaccination.

That guidance covers egg-based shots as well. Standard-dose brands such as Fluarix, FluLaval and Fluzone are produced with chicken eggs. Under CDC guidance, an egg allergy does not rule them out.

Some people with an egg allergy still prefer a vaccine made without eggs. Two licensed options meet that preference. Flucelvax is approved for people six months and older. Flublok was first licensed for adults, and the FDA extended it to people aged nine and older in 2025. Choosing either one is a personal preference, not a requirement under CDC guidance.

Pregnant people should get an injected flu vaccine. CDC guidance points to either an inactivated shot or a recombinant shot during pregnancy. In practice, that includes standard-dose brands, the cell-based Flucelvax and the recombinant Flublok.

The FluMist nasal spray is not recommended during pregnancy. It contains live virus, unlike the injected options. Fluzone High-Dose and Fluad do not apply here either. Both are licensed only for adults 65 and older.

Young children can need more than one flu vaccine dose in a season. Children aged six months through eight years who have not previously received enough flu vaccine doses need two doses that season. CDC says those two doses should be given at least four weeks apart.

Children in that age group who already have enough prior doses need only one dose for the season. From age nine, one dose per season is the rule regardless of history. Parents unsure of a child's vaccine history can check it with the child's health care provider.

Age also limits which vaccines a child can receive. Flucelvax and some standard-dose brands are licensed from six months. FluMist starts at age two, so children under two need an injected shot. Flublok is not an option until age nine.

10 flu warning signs that mean it's time to see a doctor

These flu warning signs, drawn from CDC guidance for adults and children, show when flu symptoms call for a doctor or emergency care rather than rest at home

For most healthy people, the flu is an illness to ride out at home. The harder part is spotting the moment when rest and fluids are no longer enough.

Influenza usually arrives fast. The typical pattern is a sudden fever, cough, sore throat, body aches and fatigue. Recovery usually takes about two weeks. In some patients, the illness takes a more serious course and leads to complications such as pneumonia.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publishes a list of emergency warning signs for exactly that moment. The CDC states that these symptoms can signal complications and need urgent medical care. Some signs apply to adults, some to children and several to both. A few look different depending on age. Breathing trouble in a child, for example, can show up as fast breathing or ribs that pull in with each breath. Other signs, such as bluish lips or face and any fever in a baby younger than 12 weeks, appear only on the child list. The guidance also covers changes in alertness, hydration and fever patterns.

Timing also matters for treatment. Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, sold under the brand name Tamiflu, work best when started within 48 hours of the first symptoms. A doctor decides whether a prescription is appropriate.

Some people face higher odds of serious illness from the start. The CDC's higher-risk groups include adults 65 and older and children under five, especially those under two. Pregnant people and those up to two weeks postpartum are also on the list. So are people with chronic conditions such as asthma, heart disease or diabetes, and people with weakened immune systems.

The 10 flu warning signs below are a selection from the CDC's guidance for adults and children. Each slide explains what the sign looks like, who it applies to and what to do next. None of these signs is a diagnosis on its own. Each one signals that a patient needs professional medical attention.

If any of these signs appear, call a doctor right away. When symptoms are severe, call emergency services.

Shortness of breath or difficulty breathing while sick with the flu is a reason to get emergency medical care, not to keep resting at home. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it among its emergency warning signs of flu in adults.

This sign is separate from the ordinary symptoms of influenza. A cough, a sore throat and a stuffy nose are common with flu and usually ease as the illness runs its course. Shortness of breath is different. It describes a struggle to get enough air, even while sitting or lying still.

In children, the CDC describes the warning sign in slightly different terms. It lists fast breathing or trouble breathing as an emergency sign. It also lists a pattern in which the ribs pull in with each breath.

That rib movement is often called retractions. The skin between or just below the ribs sinks inward as the child inhales. It happens when the chest muscles work harder than normal to pull air into the lungs.

The CDC's wording for children centers on what an adult can observe: the speed of the breathing and the movement of the chest. A young child may not be able to describe feeling short of breath.

Breathing trouble during flu can point to pneumonia or other lung involvement. Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs in one or both lungs. Those sacs can fill with fluid or pus. That makes it harder for oxygen to reach the bloodstream.

No single breathing change confirms a specific diagnosis. A doctor can assess the cause, often by listening to the lungs. Where needed, the doctor may order tests such as a chest X $TWTR-ray or a pulse oximeter reading of blood oxygen.

The CDC applies this warning to adults and children alike, with the wording adjusted for age. In both groups, the recommended response is the same. Get emergency medical care right away rather than waiting to see whether breathing improves on its own.

A child with flu whose lips or face turn bluish needs emergency care right away. Call emergency services or go to an emergency room rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

The CDC lists bluish lips or face as an emergency warning sign of flu in children. The agency's list of emergency warning signs for adults does not name it as a separate item. For parents and caregivers, that makes it a child-specific sign to watch for during a flu illness.

The bluish tint reflects low oxygen in the blood. When the body is not getting enough oxygen, the lips and skin can take on a blue or grayish cast. The medical term for this discoloration is cyanosis.

Flu is a respiratory illness. It infects the nose and throat, and it can also reach the lungs. When breathing is affected, less oxygen may reach the bloodstream. A color change in the face or lips is one outward sign of that problem.

Where the color appears matters. Central cyanosis shows up in the lips, the tongue and the inside of the mouth. Peripheral cyanosis affects the hands and feet. Blue fingertips or toes can come from cold or from reduced circulation to the limbs. A blue tint in the lips or tongue is more closely tied to low oxygen in the blood.

The change can show up around the mouth, on the lips or across the face. On darker skin, a bluish tint can be harder to see. Looking at the lips, gums and tongue can make the change easier to notice.

A bluish color is a reason to act, not a diagnosis. It does not on its own say what is causing the low oxygen. Finding the cause is the job of the medical team that examines the child.

Parents do not need to wait for other symptoms to appear before acting. The color change on its own is reason enough to get emergency help.

Pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that persists while someone has the flu calls for emergency care right away. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it as an emergency warning sign of flu in adults.

The key word in the CDC's description is persistent. Body aches are a common flu symptom and part of a typical illness. This warning sign is different. It refers to pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that stays rather than easing.

The adult sign covers two parts of the body. Pain or pressure in either the chest or the abdomen fits the description. Symptoms do not need to appear in both places.

Some people with flu also have vomiting and diarrhea. The CDC notes that these stomach symptoms are more common in children than in adults. A brief upset stomach of that kind is not the same as abdominal pain or pressure that will not go away.

The CDC keeps a separate list of emergency signs for children. Chest pain appears on that list. The abdomen is named only in the adult wording.

Flu can affect organs beyond the airways, including the heart. The CDC names myocarditis, an inflammation of the heart muscle, among the serious complications flu can cause.

No single episode of chest pain points to one cause on its own. Chest discomfort can arise from the lungs, the heart, the chest wall or other structures. A clinician has to examine the patient to tell them apart.

The CDC places this sign in its emergency category, not among symptoms to manage at home. The appropriate response is to call emergency services or go to an emergency department. Waiting to see whether the pain fades is not part of the guidance.

The CDC notes that its warning-sign lists are not all-inclusive. The agency advises contacting a medical provider about any other symptom that is severe or concerning.

Sudden dizziness, confusion or being unable to wake up are emergency warning signs of flu in adults, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Anyone showing these signs needs medical care right away. That means calling emergency services or going to an emergency room.

These signs concern how alert a person is and how clearly they think. That sets them apart from the cough, fever and body aches that most people with flu manage at home.

Fatigue is a normal part of flu, and many people sleep more than usual while sick. The CDC's warning sign describes something different. It refers to a person who cannot be woken, not someone who is simply tired or drowsy.

The CDC's adult sign pairs dizziness with confusion. Its wording describes dizziness that comes on suddenly during the illness.

A person who is confused may not recognize that anything is wrong. A family member, roommate or caregiver may be the one who notices the change and calls for help.

For children, the CDC describes this warning sign through behavior. Its list includes a child who does not wake up or who does not interact when awake. A child who is awake but does not respond to parents or others nearby fits that description.

The CDC also lists irritability as a warning sign in children once it crosses a specific line. That line is a child so irritable that they do not want to be held.

Young children often cannot explain how they feel. Changes in how a child responds to a parent's touch or voice are one way a caregiver can see that the illness has become more serious.

The CDC does not tie these signs to a single cause. No one of them confirms a specific diagnosis. Its guidance treats sudden changes in alertness or behavior during flu, in adults or children, as reasons to get urgent medical attention.

A seizure during a bout of flu is an emergency warning sign that calls for emergency medical care right away. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists seizures among its emergency warning signs of flu in both adults and children.

The CDC keeps separate warning-sign lists for adults and for children. Several items on those lists take a different form depending on age. Seizures are one of the signs that appear on both lists.

The guidance attaches no duration or count to this sign. It does not say a seizure must last a set length of time before it counts. It also does not say a seizure must happen more than once. A single seizure during a flu illness fits the CDC's description.

The CDC groups seizures with its emergency signs. It does not place them among symptoms that can be managed at home. Anyone who sees a person with flu have a seizure should call emergency services.

The CDC also states that its warning-sign lists are not all-inclusive. It advises people to contact a medical provider about any other symptom that is severe or concerning.

The sign is defined by its timing. The CDC lists a seizure as a flu warning sign when it happens while the person is sick with flu.

An adult with the flu who stops urinating needs medical care right away, under emergency warning-sign guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). In children, the CDC flags no urine for eight hours, a dry mouth or no tears when crying as signs that call for emergency care.

These signs can point to dehydration. The body is not taking in enough fluid to replace what it loses. Dehydration can become a complication of flu. It tends to develop when fever, poor eating and drinking, or vomiting continue without a break.

Fever plays a direct role. A raised body temperature increases the fluid lost through sweating and breathing. A sore throat, nausea or exhaustion can also lead a sick person to drink far less than usual. Vomiting adds to the problem by expelling fluid before the body can absorb it.

Children face added exposure. The CDC notes that vomiting and diarrhea can occur with flu and are more common in children than in adults. Both drain fluid quickly. Young children can also become dehydrated faster than adults.

Urine output is one of the simplest measures to track at home. For babies and toddlers in diapers, caregivers can count wet diapers through the day. A diaper that stays dry for eight hours meets the CDC's warning threshold for children. For older children and adults, a long stretch without needing the bathroom is the signal to watch.

Tears and the mouth offer further checks in children. A child who cries without producing tears may be short on fluid. A dry mouth is another clue. The CDC groups both with missing urine under the same dehydration warning sign.

Other common dehydration clues include unusual thirst, very dark urine and lightheadedness on standing. These do not appear on the CDC's emergency list by themselves. They can still tell a caregiver that fluid intake is falling behind what the body needs.

A doctor can assess how much fluid a patient has lost. Treatment can range from drinking an oral rehydration solution to receiving fluids through a vein.

Severe muscle pain, or severe weakness or unsteadiness, is an emergency warning sign of flu in adults, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Anyone with these symptoms during a flu illness should contact a doctor or seek emergency care.

The line between an expected symptom and a warning sign here is one of degree. Body aches are a standard part of influenza. The sign on the CDC list is narrower. It covers muscle pain the agency describes as severe, not the general soreness that comes with a typical case.

The same distinction applies to weakness. The CDC lists fatigue among the common symptoms of flu. Feeling drained during the illness is part of a typical bout. The warning sign describes something more serious: weakness or unsteadiness the agency calls severe.

An adult who struggles to stand or keep their balance while sick with the flu fits that description. The CDC treats these changes as a reason to get medical help, not as part of an ordinary recovery.

For children, the CDC describes the sign through behavior. Its guidance lists muscle pain so severe that a child refuses to walk. The adult list names the pain itself. The child list frames it as a change in what the child is willing to do. Young children often cannot explain how much something hurts. A refusal to walk gives parents and caregivers something they can see for themselves.

The CDC does not tie this sign to one specific cause. Severe muscle symptoms on their own do not tell a patient or parent what is wrong. A doctor can examine the person and decide what care the situation needs.

The CDC also notes that its warning-sign lists are not all-inclusive. It advises contacting a medical provider about any other symptom that is severe or concerning.

A fever or cough that eases and then comes back, or gets worse, means it is time to contact a doctor right away. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists this pattern among its emergency warning signs of flu in both adults and children.

The warning lies in the course of the illness, not in how severe any single symptom feels. A person may have several days of fever and cough, start to feel better and then relapse. That relapse can point to a problem beyond the original viral infection.

One possibility is a secondary infection. Influenza damages the cells lining the airways. That damage can leave the lungs more open to bacteria. Bacterial pneumonia is one complication that can follow this sequence. Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus are among the bacteria commonly involved in pneumonia after flu.

The cause matters for treatment. Flu antivirals act on the influenza virus and do not treat bacterial infections. Antibiotics act on bacteria and have no effect on the flu virus itself. A clinician can examine the patient and may order a chest X $TWTR-ray or other tests before choosing a treatment.

The sign applies to children as well as adults. A child who seemed to be recovering and then develops a new or higher fever fits the same pattern. So does a child whose cough deepens after it had begun to fade. Parents can note when the fever first broke and when it returned. That timeline is useful to share with a pediatrician.

A rebound does not confirm any particular diagnosis on its own. A medical evaluation is how the cause gets identified.

A simple written record helps any clinician. Daily temperature readings, the times symptoms changed and a list of medicines taken give a clear picture of the illness. Fever reducers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen can lower a temperature reading. Noting each dose and its time keeps the record accurate.

This relapse pattern differs from the tiredness and mild cough that can linger for a while after flu. What sets it apart is a clear return or worsening of symptoms after a period of improvement. That change is the signal the CDC flags.

A long-term health condition that gets worse during a bout of flu is a signal to call a doctor or seek emergency care. The CDC lists the worsening of chronic medical conditions among its emergency warning signs of flu in adults.

This sign differs from most others on the CDC's list. It does not name one particular symptom, such as chest pain or a seizure. It describes a change in an illness a person already lives with. That change is judged against how the condition usually behaves.

Flu can make chronic health problems worse, according to the CDC. The agency points to asthma and heart disease as examples. People with asthma may have asthma attacks while they are sick with flu. People with chronic heart disease may find that flu triggers a worsening of their condition.

Diabetes follows a similar pattern. The CDC notes that flu can raise blood sugar levels. Illness can also reduce appetite, and eating less can make blood sugar drop. Either shift can make diabetes harder to keep under control.

Children are covered too. The CDC's emergency warning signs for children include the same entry on worsening chronic medical conditions. A child with asthma, heart disease or diabetes falls under this sign in the same way an adult does.

This sign stands on its own in the CDC's guidance. It does not depend on any other warning sign being present. A person with an ordinary cough and fever whose chronic condition is slipping still meets the CDC's criteria.

The CDC's guidance does not tie a worsening chronic condition to any single flu complication. It treats the change itself as the warning. A person who notices it can call a doctor or emergency services. So can a caregiver who sees it in someone else. Neither needs to wait for the flu to run its course before acting.

Any fever in an infant younger than 12 weeks is an emergency warning sign of flu, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). So is a fever above 104°F in a child, and either one calls for immediate medical care.

Both thresholds appear in the CDC's list of emergency flu symptoms in children. They are separate signs. A child does not need to show both before a parent calls a doctor or emergency services.

The infant sign has no temperature cutoff in the CDC's wording. For a baby younger than 12 weeks, the presence of any fever is the warning sign. How high the thermometer reads does not change that.

The second sign uses a fixed threshold: a fever above 104°F. On a Celsius thermometer, that line sits at 40°C. The CDC's wording gives no age range for this sign. An older child whose temperature climbs past that mark meets the same sign as a younger one.

Neither threshold appears on the CDC's list of emergency warning signs for adults. Both belong to the agency's guidance for children only.

Babies in their first 12 weeks also have no vaccine protection of their own. The CDC recommends a yearly flu vaccine for everyone six months and older, so infants this young are not yet eligible.

The CDC notes that flu vaccination during pregnancy helps protect a baby for several months after birth. The agency also recommends vaccination for people who live with or care for infants too young to receive the vaccine.

A fever is not always part of flu. The CDC points out that not everyone with the illness runs a fever. A child without one can still show other emergency warning signs on the agency's list.

The two temperature signs give parents a clear point for action. When a baby younger than 12 weeks has any fever, the next step is a call to a doctor or emergency services. The same applies when a child runs a fever above 104°F.
