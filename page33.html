Applied Materials vs. Dell Technologies: Comparing Stable vs. Accelerating Revenue Between These Artificial Intelligence Companies

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Applied Materials has stabilized near $7 billion in quarterly sales, while Dell's revenue has surged past $47 billion -- a widening gap that raises questions about diverging growth trajectories.

Applied Materials (AMAT +11.37%) primarily generates its revenue by designing, developing, manufacturing, and selling the highly specialized engineering materials, technical services, and precision fabrication tools utilized by global electronics customers to manufacture complex integrated circuits.

It recently expanded its institutional research network by entering into multiple collaborative technology agreements with major university engineering programs, while additionally formalizing an arrangement to increase its strategic financial equity investment in BE Semiconductor Industries.

Dell Technologies (DELL +5.41%) primarily generates its operational revenue by designing, producing, and selling a broad assortment of enterprise-grade infrastructure hardware, client computing devices, complex data storage equipment, and complementary software applications alongside extensive global technical support services.

It introduced a newly updated portfolio of specialized server hardware platforms designed specifically to handle intensive local workloads, and it simultaneously rolled out an entirely new line of advanced personal computing workstations while expanding its cybersecurity software integrations.

Comparing the revenue trajectories of Applied Materials and Dell Technologies quickly helps investors identify the faster growing company amid the artificial intelligence boom. From the end of 2025 into 2026, both have experienced rapid quarter-over-quarter sales growth, indicating customer demand has accelerated.

This acceleration is structurally driven by the explosion in enterprise artificial intelligence infrastructure spending, moving from steady linear growth to aggressive exponential scaling in 2026. Examining these two companies offers investors a masterclass in how the AI supercycle impacts different layers of the technology stack.

Applied Materials provides tech fundamental to constructing the advanced components required for AI, and that specialized role has allowed the company to not only achieve record revenue in its fiscal third quarter, ended July 26, as sales soared 25% year over year, but also to command a gross margin of more than 50%.

Dell delivers far greater revenue, and like Applied Materials, its fiscal 2027 Q2 income of $47 billion was also a record, representing massive 58% year-over-year growth. However, its gross margin was a much slimmer 20.9%, demonstrating to investors that Dell can capture enormous market share quickly, but faces compressed pricing power relative to key component suppliers such as Applied Materials.

How A Salesman Accidentally Convinced Shaquille O'Neal To Buy 3 Ridiculously Expensive Cars

Shaquille O'Neal doesn't do small or understated. The 7-foot-1 four-time NBA champion has an instantly recognizable brand. Oh, he's worth around $500 million, too. Naturally, as with many other well-to-do star athletes, he has a penchant for flashy cars. Problem is, he seldom fits in them without some contortions and modification. It's what drove him to have a Lamborghini lengthened into a Franken-Gallardo. That stretched supercar isn't the only time Shaq did some silly stuff with expensive cars.

In what was almost certainly a prideful purchase, O'Neal bought three outrageously expensive cars on the spot. Why? All it took was one condescending question from a less-than-respectful Rolls-Royce dealership salesman, and Shaq bought the lot. Just like that, the champ and business mogul had three extra opulent cars he says "I never drive."

Don't believe it? One of them, a 2004 Rolls-Royce Phantom, just sold on Bring-a-Trailer. If that wasn't enough, the once-undisputed heavyweight champion of the world, Mike Tyson, may have had a hand in nudging the Diesel to buy three ultraluxe cars instead of a solitary sedan. Of course, it's not difficult to get carried away when shopping for Rolls-Royces; just look at the one-off Phantom inspired by "Goldfinger."

Some 30 years ago, Shaquille O'Neal signed a groundbreaking $120 million deal to don Los Angeles Lakers colors and put away his Orlando Magic jersey. Several years later, he wandered into a Rolls-Royce showroom with his friend Alex and started browsing the hand-built grandeur.

Now, O'Neal had money to spare, but he wasn't quite up for indiscriminate spending. "I like to spend money, but I don't like to waste money. So I don't want to spend $400,000 for a car," O'Neal recalled in a Revolt interview. He went about asking a salesperson about the prices of the showroom's many Roll-Royces. That's when the salesperson said the wrong thing. Or the right thing, considering the commission on three hilariously expensive cars.

In O'Neal's book, "Shaq Uncut: My Story,"  he recalls the question as especially disrespectful, be it accidental or deliberate. The salesperson gave the Lakers sweatpants-clad O'Neal a glance and, per Basketball Network, asked, "Are you sure you can afford it, sonny boy?" That did it. Shaq lost it. "I said, 'What the f*** did you just say to me?'" So, he pointed around the showroom. "I want that silver hardtop one, and I want the green one, and give that red one over there, too."

One of those cars, the silver hardtop O'Neal selected, was a 2004 Rolls-Royce Phantom. That car sold in November 2026 on Bring-a-Trailer for $62,000. Not exactly the $320,000 or so O'Neal likely paid for it when it was new in 2004. Although he was shopping in a Rolls-Royce dealer, Shaq's book mentions a handful of Bentleys as part of the splurge. Whichever British luxury marque made O'Neal's list of picks, it was quite the spendy visit to a dealership.

Shaq's financial flexing and what is likely the most impressive commission that Rolls-Royce salesperson has ever made makes for a pretty crazy story. Believe it or not, it gets even more wild from there. According to O'Neal's book, "Iron" Mike himself, Mike Tyson, was in the mood for Bentley shopping just as the Laker found himself at the dealership.

Shaq recalled seeing Tyson picking out a couple of Bentleys and the need to out-do the champion pugilist. "Next thing I know Tyson picked out two. He's buying two of these cars, so you know what I've got to do. I've got to buy three!" Though it's not clear what ratio of Rolls-Royce to Bentleys made up his impulse shopping spree.

Be it Iron Mike or the mouthy salesperson who drove Shaq to pull the trigger, the outlandishly expensive price tag for the purchase hit him after. "A million dollars gone in 10 minutes," the massive athlete once lamented in an interview with Vanity Fair. Frankly, that's not the only time O'Neal has had the rotten luck with luxury cars. After spending the money to have it customized to fit his considerable frame, his Lucid Air Coupe ended up in a salvage yard less than two years later. Bummer.

Superman Already Confirmed The Real Reason DC's Heroes Will Always Be Stronger Than Marvel's

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The heroes of the DC Universe are naturally more powerful than those of the Marvel Universe, regardless of how hard the Avengers try to keep up with the Justice League. The Justice League and the Avengers are both the ultimate gathering of their respective universes' greatest superheroes, assembled to save the world and the cosmos no matter what it takes. Both superhero teams have also expanded way beyond their original lineups to include characters whose abilities reach extraordinary levels, to the point that they occasionally become threats themselves.

The enormous popularity of the Marvel Cinematic Universe has introduced millions to some of Marvel Comics' most extraordinary power feats, although the movies have occasionally simplified or substantially altered the original stories. Thanos' infamous Snap from The Infinity Gauntlet became a pop culture staple thanks to Avengers: Infinity War, and Scarlet Witch is now famous for her reality-altering abilities thanks to WandaVision and Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness, which have echoed her actions from House of M. Thor has also become synonymous with extraordinary physical strength and divine weaponry, though the God of Thunder has accumulated even bigger feats of power in the Marvel Comics multiverse.

However, Marvel Comics' characters are inherently a few notches below DC's characters in terms of power, all thanks to the very nature of both realities.

In JLA/Avengers #2, when Thor unleashes Mjolnir against Superman, the Man of Steel catches the hammer, leading the God of Thunder to ask how it can be possible. Superman responds that "the dials go up to eleven" in his world and delivers a punch that knocks Thor unconscious. Although the Avengers of the time have Scarlet Witch, Vision, Quasar, Ms Marvel, Jack of Hearts, Hercules, and Thor in their ranks, the Justice League easily overshadow Marvel's mightiest heroes with Superman and Wonder Woman alone, not to mention Martian Manhunter, the Flash, Captain Atom, Aquaman, Green Lantern, and Zatanna.

The DC multiverse also possesses an elaborate hierarchy of increasingly abstract realms, including the Sphere of the Gods, the Monitor Sphere, the Source Wall between realities, and the Overvoid, with entities that exist beyond ordinary space-time and perceive entire universes as comparatively insignificant concepts. Marvel certainly has its own equivalents, including the Living Tribunal, Eternity, the Beyonders, and the One-Above-All, but DC particularly emphasizes the idea of successive planes of existence that transcend everything beneath them. DC takes the concept even further with Darkseid, whose true form exists on a scale so immense that his fall into reality threatens every single timeline across the multiverse.

Individual DC characters have reached absurdly elevated levels. Superman's Prime One Million and King Omega forms make him virtually invincible, and his Cosmic Armor fusion with Ultraman makes him the physical embodiment of superhero storytelling. The Flash has achieved similar feats, like running so fast he singlehandedly reboots the DC multiverse in Crisis on Infinite Earths, or being so fast he's basically everywhere at once in Kingdom Come and Ryan North and Gavin Guidry's 2026 The Flash run. Marvel has certainly produced comparable cosmic spectacles, particularly through the Beyonder, God Emperor Doom, and the Maker's manipulation of the multiverse, but the scale and effort of their actions is noticeably different.

While competition inspires the DC and Marvel Universe to get creative with power levels, focusing too much on them introduces the risk of pushing away what makes each character compelling. For instance, the Flash often claims the title of the fastest speedster in fiction, but in doing so, he also renders every villain and threat inoffensive. If the Flash can solve every problem on Earth and still have time to spare, all the slower speedsters' adventures automatically become more interesting. Likewise, Batman's best feats of skill are unforgettable, but they affect his fame as a regular human hero standing shoulder to shoulder with gods.

Marvel's answer to Superman is at the very top of the MCU's power hierarchy, but these superhero characters would all take Sentry down.

Marvel could try to catch up to DC's power levels, but the problem remains the same. Storm has earned her place as the most powerful mutant and the most powerful of the thunder gods, even higher in the Marvel Universe's power rankings than Eternity and Infinity at one point. Yet, Ororo Munroe inevitably needs to lose that power in order to participate in the X-Men's conflicts without solving all their problems all by herself. Although the DC Universe is naturally stronger than the Marvel Universe, there's no need for Marvel's heroes to power up. After all, battles aren't always won through sheer force alone.

GOFO White Paper Highlights Structural Shift in U.S. Last-Mile Delivery

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The white paper examines changing carrier dynamics, fragmented e-commerce demand and strategies for shippers navigating an evolving parcel market.

LOS ANGELES, Nov. 9, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- GOFO, a technology-driven global last-mile delivery carrier, highlights a structural shift in the U.S. parcel market in its industry white paper, How Fragmenting E-Commerce Is Rebuilding the U.S. Last Mile, first introduced at PARCEL Forum 2026 in September. Drawing on third-party market research, industry data and customer case studies, the report examines how changing strategies among established carriers and increasingly fragmented e-commerce demand are creating room for a broader range of delivery networks and reshaping carrier selection for shippers.

According to ShipMatrix data cited in the white paper, U.S. domestic parcel volume reached 23.9 billion packages in 2025, an increase of 0.4% year over year (ShipMatrix, 2026). Amazon Logistics surpassed USPS as the nation's largest domestic parcel carrier by volume, delivering 6.7 billion packages, up 9.9%, compared with USPS's 6.6 billion (ShipMatrix, 2026).

Amazon's expansion represents a significant development in a market historically dominated by UPS, FedEx and USPS. Meanwhile, UPS has been reducing lower-value Amazon volume, FedEx is emphasizing higher-value shipment segments, and USPS continues competing for lightweight e-commerce deliveries while adjusting its pricing and service mix.

On the demand side, e-commerce is becoming increasingly fragmented. According to eMarketer estimates cited in the report, Amazon accounted for approximately 40.5% of U.S. retail e-commerce sales in 2025, with the remaining 59.5% distributed across other marketplaces, retailer websites and direct-to-consumer channels (eMarketer, 2025). This diversification is creating more varied shipment patterns, fulfillment arrangements and delivery requirements.

As traditional carrier strategies and e-commerce demand evolve, alternative delivery networks are expanding their presence in the U.S. parcel market. ShipMatrix's "Other Carriers" category, which includes retailers' own delivery networks and independent regional carriers, reached approximately 2.5 billion packages in 2025, with reported growth of 13% (ShipMatrix, 2026).

The white paper identifies a range of emerging operating models, including cross-border specialists, technology-enabled urban delivery platforms and super-regional networks. Rather than following a single standardized approach, these carriers serve different geographic markets, shipment profiles and fulfillment needs.

This evolving landscape is giving shippers a broader range of delivery options. The report recommends evaluating carriers based on total transportation costs, geographic coverage, service reliability and technology integration, while maintaining diversified carrier relationships to reduce dependence on individual networks.

Customer Case Studies Highlight the Importance of Carrier Readiness

Drawing on GOFO's experience with Dr. Squatch and ShipMonk, the white paper illustrates how alternative carriers can support shippers when existing delivery arrangements are disrupted.

According to the case studies, Dr. Squatch expanded its relationship with GOFO from a monitored pilot to a core carrier arrangement after an existing provider became unavailable at short notice. During the transition, the average calendar transit time improved from 3.75 to 3.15 days.

ShipMonk faced an unplanned carrier transition within its Economy delivery tier. GOFO became operational within ShipMonk's Virtual Carrier Network in 10 days and completed onboarding across its network nodes within 35 days. Over a comparable off-peak period, average transit time improved from 2.56 to 2.10 business days.

Both cases illustrate how advance carrier integration and diversified delivery options can support service continuity during unexpected disruptions.

The white paper concludes that U.S. last-mile delivery is undergoing a long-term structural realignment rather than a temporary market cycle. As delivery networks diversify, operational flexibility, real-time shipment visibility and technology integration are expected to become increasingly important in carrier evaluation.

For shippers, adapting to this environment will require delivery strategies that balance cost, coverage, reliability and flexibility across different fulfillment models.

The full white paper, How Fragmenting E-Commerce Is Rebuilding the U.S. Last Mile, provides additional market analysis, customer case studies and a detailed carrier evaluation framework. It is available on GOFO's website.

Hundreds protest US Senate bill that would gut key section of National Historic Preservation Act

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Protesters, including members of tribal nations, at the US Capitol on 9 November to protest the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

On Monday (9 November), hundreds of protesters gathered on the West Lawn of the US Capitol in Washington, DC, to protest the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA, or senate bill S.5653), which would gut a key section of the National Historic Preservation Act. Representatives from more than 100 tribal nations, members of congress, leaders from the Association of African American Museums, the DC Preservation League, the American Institute of Architects and the Archaeological Institute of America gathered to protest a bill that would remove the requirement for federal agencies to notify and consult with the public and tribal nations about potential harms to historic sites, as stipulated in Section 106, Title III of the National Historic Preservation Act.

The BAAJA, described by New Mexico congresswoman Melanie Stansbury at the protest as a “dirty deal”, was created without tribal consultation or public input. According to California representative Jared Huffman, the 500 new pages of legislative text, which he described as “the most sweeping legislation we’ve seen on energy and environmental policy in decades”, was released just 48 hours before the Senate was set to vote on it Monday evening.

“They scheduled this vote for tonight to try to prevent something like this,” Huffman told protesters gathered on the Capitol lawn; organisers estimated around 800 people attended. “To try to prevent the American people from having a say, from looking under the hood of this sweeping legislation and understanding what’s actually it, but you came anyway.” Holding signs with illustrations of President Donald Trump’s planned Triumphal Arch in flames and “Nix 106”, protesters gathered for over two hours to listen to more than 15 speakers who argued that BAAJA would seriously threaten US history.

The 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

Greg Werkheiser, a leader of the Section 106 Coalition and co-founder of Cultural Heritage Partners, kicked off the protest. “We gather at the seat of American democracy, where the laws of this nation are written, to affirm one simple principle: the people deserve a voice in decisions that affect the places that we love,” he said. “That is the promise we have come here today to defend.”

Along with removing the requirement for federal agencies to engage with the public, the BAAJA would also make consultation with tribal nations optional, narrow what types of activity trigger a federal historic preservation review, restrict the definition of what constitutes a historic site, and remove any consideration of sensory, indirect and cumulative effects of changes to historic and sacred sites, essentially allowing federal agencies to take over tribal and state historic preservation offices that do not abide by limits and deadlines that the new bill would set. The BAAJA would even restrict what legal action people and organisations can take against the destruction of historic and sacred sites.

“For tribal nations,” Arizona congresswoman Adelita Grijalva said, “the fight is more than historic preservation. It’s about sovereignty. It’s about self-determination. It’s about the fundamental right to protect our ancestral homelands, culturally significant sites, and traditions that have existed since time immemorial.”

Protesters at the 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

In its current form, Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires federal agencies to consider the impact of federally funded building projects and requires that people receiving federal funds or permits consult with tribal nations. If passed, S. 5653 would make that consultation—the requirement that tribal nations participate in decision-making—optional.

“Our sacred places are living places of prayer, our responsibilities to them do not end at the reservation boundary,” Harry Antonio Jr, the governor of the Pueblo of Laguna in New Mexico, said at the rally. “You do not have to share our prayers to understand why those places deserve respect. Section 106 gives our governments a way to bring those concerns into federal decisions.”

Senators in favour of the bill have argued that its passage would lead to more economic opportunities. Articulating a position held by most rally attendees, Grijalva said: “Economic development does not require sacrificing our history, our culture or our sovereignty. We can create jobs without destroying sacred places. We can create jobs without sacrificing Mother Earth, our land, our water.”

Protesters at the 9 November rally at the US Capitol against the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act Photo © Emma Cieslik

Rashida Tlaib, the congresswoman from Michigan, said the bill would be used to fast-track projects with severe environmental consequences, including data centres and gas pipelines. “This bill strips away what little tools we already have to defend ourselves from corporate polluters,” she said.

The senate’s vote on the BAAJA coincides with Trump’s push to build a 250ft-tall arch between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. On 5 November, protesters gathered at its proposed site and spoke against it during a National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC) meeting. Although the NCPC voted to approve the arch, a Washington, DC judge then immediately blocked construction for 14 days.

“The court orders stopping construction of those projects would not be possible if this BAAJA legislation becomes law,” Huffman said. “If this becomes law, the administration will use that in those pending court cases to make it much, much harder to stop Trump from advancing those and other vanity projects.”

Speakers urged protesters to call their senators, and implored senators to vote down the bill or to table a vote until the newly elected members of congress are sworn in in January.

Adding limestone to farmland is helping to store more carbon dioxide than it releases

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agricultural liming in the Mississippi Basin removed more carbon dioxide than it released, after accounting for acid from fertilizer and pollution.

Limestone is rock made partly of carbon, so spreading it on farm fields sounds like a way to put carbon dioxide into the air. Climate accounting rules treat it that way.

However, a new study finds that, over the past century in the Mississippi River Basin, the limestone that farmers spread has removed more carbon dioxide than it released.

The old math leaves out the acid. Fertilizer and air pollution add acid to soil and water. That acid releases carbon dioxide, whether or not anyone spreads lime.

Measured against a basin with the same acid and no limestone, the lime has worked as a carbon sink.

Geochemist Tim Jesper Suhrhoff of the Yale Center for Natural Carbon Capture led the work with colleagues at Georgia Tech and elsewhere.

The team paired a computer model of soil chemistry with a century of river records from one giant watershed.

In an interview with Earth.com, Suhrhoff said the result adds to what farmers already gain from lime.

“Liming may provide a public climate benefit on top of the benefits farmers already get from healthier soils and better yields,” he said.

Many farm soils slowly turn acidic, and crops grown in acidic soil tend to yield less. Farmers fix that by spreading finely ground limestone or a similar rock called dolomite, a practice known as agricultural liming.

Between 1935 and 1950, lime use in the U.S. rose from about 1.1 million tons (1 million metric tons) a year to about 22 million tons (20 million metric tons).

Most of it went to the Mississippi River Basin, the land drained by the Mississippi and its tributaries. It holds about 65 percent of the cropland in the contiguous U.S.

When limestone dissolves in acidic soil, it gives off carbon dioxide. So the default method from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) treats all the carbon in applied lime as emitted.

The U.S. national inventory assumes about half of it escapes.

When nitrogen fertilizer and manure break down in soil, they produce acid. Burning coal and oil sends sulfur and nitrogen pollution into the sky, and it comes back down as acid.

Rivers carry carbon dissolved in a form called bicarbonate. When acid reaches that water, it turns some of the bicarbonate back into carbon dioxide, which escapes into the air.

Across the basin, acid from farming and pollution far outpaced the lime. By 2015, about half of all the acid added since 1900 hadn’t been offset by lime, mostly on land that sees little liming.

In parts of the Corn Belt, though, decades of liming matched or exceeded the acid.

In 1980, the team’s model estimated, acid alone would have released about 22 million tons (20 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide.

Adding lime cut that to about 17 million tons (15 million metric tons). The IPCC and U.S. methods came in lower, at roughly 8 million and 4 million tons (7 million and 3.5 million metric tons) respectively.

So the standard methods assign the emissions to the lime and miss most of what the acid does.

“I was also struck by how much excess acidity has been added to the system over the last century, implying that agricultural CO₂ emissions from acidification may have been substantially larger than we usually account for,” Suhrhoff told Earth.com.

To check the model, researchers pulled more than seven decades of water chemistry from five U.S. Geological Survey stations between Vicksburg, Mississippi, and Belle Chasse, Louisiana. Samples from the early 1900s filled in the start.

The team compared the bicarbonate that the river carried once liming took off with what it carried before. They traced the extra to about 440 million tons (400 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide. This was removed between 1900 and 2015.

That’s roughly 90 percent of what the lime could have removed if all of it had reacted in the ideal way.

The soil model, built up from conditions across the basin, estimated about 240 million to 410 million tons (220 million to 370 million metric tons). Two different methods reached nearly the same answer.

“I was surprised by how well the bottom-up modeling and top-down river data converged, and that we could actually see these trends at the scale of the entire Mississippi River Basin,” Suhrhoff said.

When liming first ramped up in the late 1930s, the model shows a brief burst of carbon dioxide before removal began. Calcium from dissolved lime attaches to soil particles and releases acid stored there.

That stored acid has to be neutralized before much of the lime’s effect reaches the river.

In the river records, carbon left the basin decades after the limestone went down. The authors warn that carbon removal programs that credit lime as soon as it’s spread could overstate the near-term benefit.

Liming is a close relative of enhanced weathering, which means spreading crushed rock on fields to draw carbon dioxide out of the air.

“This is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to trace more than 120 years of historical records that can tell us whether enhanced weathering works,” said Noah Planavsky, a corresponding author at Yale University.

“I would caution against assuming that the Mississippi result translates directly everywhere,” Suhrhoff said.

The early emissions, the delay and the amount of carbon removed all vary, he added. They depend on how much lime is used, the local soils and how water moves.

Where acidic soils hold back harvests and farmers can’t afford lime, he said, subsidies or pay-for-practice programs could widen access. Three of the authors advise CREW Carbon, a company that adds lime in wastewater treatment plants rather than on farm fields.

For most of the Mississippi’s own tributaries, the records aren’t long or continuous enough to separate lime’s effect from everything else.

The authors call for more regional studies wherever long, detailed records exist. Spreading more lime than the basin’s farmers did would need its own look at local soils, water and that early burst of emissions.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
