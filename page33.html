United Airlines Remembers Your Bad Flight And May Upgrade You Next Time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

United Airlines appears to be quietly remembering when passengers suffer through a bad trip and then surprising some of them with a complimentary Economy Plus seat on a future flight. It costs United very little when the seat would otherwise remain empty, but it communicates something valuable to the passenger: we remember what happened to you.

A passenger recently shared an interesting message from United Airlines with aviation insider JonNYC.

The traveler had missed a connection about a month earlier and then received the following message before another United flight:

“We know your previous travel experience may have faced some challenges, and we want to make your journey more comfortable.”

United moved the passenger to Economy Plus, offering additional legroom at no charge.

United:personally, dunno if this is new/true/previously reported:

"think this could be of interest to you- UA IT has pulled some more dark magic, systems are starting to remember a bad IROP a pax went through (I misconnected a month ago). Received this before my flight… pic.twitter.com/isS2SvsQcj

What makes the gesture especially interesting is that the passenger had no MileagePlus elite status. The upcoming flight was lightly booked, which presumably made it easier for United to offer the seat without giving up revenue.

JonNYC described it as United’s technology performing more “dark magic,” and I think that is a fair description. The system appears to have connected an earlier operational disruption with a future reservation, then identified an inexpensive opportunity to improve the next journey.

Reports of similar gestures stretch back at least to 2025, so this does not appear to be entirely new. One United Silver member reported receiving an Economy Plus assignment after an eight-hour delay, even after United had already provided a $75 travel credit.

But United has not publicly announced this as a formal benefit, and we do not know precisely how passengers are selected (not that I would expect otherwise with such a “Surprise and Delight” program, lest it become an entitlement).

With advancing technology, airlines already know when they have inconvenienced you.

They know when your flight was delayed, when you missed a connection, and when you were rebooked. They also know whether you have another reservation and which seats remain available on that flight.

Yet customers are often forced to explain the entire situation again in a complaint, including information the airline already has in its own systems. That creates more work for the passenger after the airline has already disrupted the trip, though I’m sadly not expecting carriers to offer proactive compensation, even when delays are within its control.

United appears to be using its data more intelligently, like with its ConnectionSaver technology, that can help some passengers without inconveniencing others.

I have written before about United introducing technology that saves callers from repeating information when they are transferred from an automated system to a human agent. I also welcomed the addition of an app feature that watches earlier flights for disrupted passengers while preserving their confirmed reservations.

This Economy Plus gesture reflects a similar approach: use the information already available to reduce friction and improve the passenger experience.

An Economy Plus seat is not a first class upgrade. The passenger receives more legroom, but the onboard service remains the same as regular economy class.

Even so, it has value. United charges for these seats, sometimes quite a bit depending upon the route and seat location. Receiving one unexpectedly can make a future flight noticeably more comfortable.

The economics also make sense for United. If the flight is lightly booked and the Economy Plus seat would otherwise remain empty, the marginal cost of assigning it to an inconvenienced passenger is essentially zero.

The value to the passenger can be much greater than the cost to the airline.

I also appreciate that the message explicitly connects the better seat to the previous experience. A random seat reassignment might go unnoticed, while a clearly explained gesture tells the passenger that United remembers what happened and wants another chance to deliver a better trip.

That is smart service recovery…I wonder if the system is smart enough to do this when passengers have to be moved anyway due to weight and balance reasons? (why let a good opportunity go to waste?)

This is not a published benefit, and passengers should not expect a complimentary Economy Plus assignment every time something goes wrong. We do not know what level of disruption qualifies, how much empty space must be available, or whether United considers the passenger’s fare or travel history…and that’s the point, lest it become an expectation.

And of course United must be careful when selecting a seat to avoid a familiar complaint on Delta Air Lines. Moving someone from a regular economy aisle seat to an Economy Plus middle seat will not strike every passenger as an improvement. Ideally, the system should preserve the passenger’s aisle or window preference whenever possible or offer the upgrade rather than automatically moving the traveler.

Furthermore, if a MileagePlus Premier is expecting an entire row and suddenly has a seatmate, I can understand there will be annoyance (though that’s a sort of entitlement that is not helpful either)…in the past I’ve passed on upgrades to domestic first class in order to stretch across multiple seats in economy class.

Still, those limitations do not diminish the appeal of the idea. This is a discretionary gesture, not something passengers can demand, and that element of surprise is part of what makes it effective.

When airlines discuss service recovery, the conversation often centers on travel credits or miles. Those can be appropriate, particularly after a serious operational failure. But compensation need not be limited to those forms.

A better seat, lounge invitation, meal voucher, or another small benefit can communicate recognition when it is offered thoughtfully. The key is identifying something the passenger will appreciate that the airline can provide at a modest cost.

United has become particularly skilled at using technology to address operational problems. ConnectionSaver is the best example, intelligently holding certain flights when connecting passengers can reach the gate without causing a significant delay.

United appears to be quietly offering complimentary Economy Plus seats to some passengers whose previous trips went poorly, including travelers without elite status.

The gesture will not erase a missed connection or make every passenger forget a miserable journey. But it shows that United remembers the disruption and recognizes an opportunity to do something helpful. An empty Economy Plus seat costs United almost nothing. Used this way, it can generate far more goodwill than another automated apology email…it’s a positive step.

Juventus captain Locatelli opens up on ‘one of the most difficult moments of my career’

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Manuel Locatelli has admitted he is going through one of the hardest periods of his career as he recovers from a serious knee injury.

Speaking to TuttoJuve, with quotes via CalcioMercato, the Juventus captain was answering questions from young supporters in the club’s traditional Junior Report feature.

Locatelli has been out since the 1-1 draw with Milan on 6 September. He suffered a sprain to his right knee and underwent surgery to repair the lateral meniscus, and he isn’t expected back in full training before March 2027.

“This is definitely one of the most difficult moments, because I hurt my knee and I have to stay away from the pitch for a long time, so I’m living life in a very different way,” Locatelli said.

“Another difficult moment was when we didn’t manage to go to the World Cup. When you lose, and lose like that, it’s a moment of suffering. But in the end, in football you win and you lose. The important thing is to improve and give everything you’ve got.”

The midfielder insisted his rehabilitation is going well.

“I feel good, in the sense that I’m working well. I know I have to wait, and unfortunately that sometimes happens in our lives as athletes,” he said. “I’m approaching each day differently and doing more work in the gym. I miss playing football so much, but I’ve learned to accept this situation, and I know I have to do my best to come back well.”

Locatelli joined from Sassuolo in 2021 and has since taken on the armband. He credited the leaders who came before him.

“I was lucky when I arrived at Juventus to have great captains ahead of me. Chiellini was still here, and Bonucci, then I had Danilo. It was very important for me to learn their attitude from them, always being present and available,” he said.

“I think the secret is dedication, the desire to work and improve, but above all being willing to listen to others.”

Locatelli also remembered his own childhood heroes.

“I was already a Juventus fan as a child, so I had Nedvěd, Buffon and Del Piero. Then of course I admired Totti too. They were my idols, the players I grew up with,” he said.

“Sometimes children see us that way, and that gives us a responsibility. We need to be an example, not just in our technical play during the match but in our attitude on and off the pitch. It’s a beautiful and important responsibility.”

His absence has been a blow for Luciano Spalletti, who is also without Kenan Yildiz and Khéphren Thuram.

And a rogue black bear—the one night he’s late to a barbecue—kills his mom and puts his brother in a wheelchair. So now we have the man, we have the means, we have the motivation.”

Instead of spiraling, he uses his depression as fuel to take on Macho. Only with the help of several experts and a commitment to a plan does the idea of fighting the bear seem, well… still ridiculous. But somewhat more attainable than going into the woods carrying nothing but rage.

For Finkel, an M&F contributing writer and editor who witnessed fight camps with UFC legends such as Brock Lesnar, Randy Couture, and Frank Mir, the novel is more than a tribute to the ’80s action adventure genre. There’s also a bit of strategy readers can incorporate into their own goal-setting, without actually having to grapple a grizzly.

“Turn your grief or stress into a healthy challenge,” he said. “A lot of people, they’ll start triathlons. They’ll do Ironmans to get over something, to occupy their mind. In this case, it’s not an Ironman or a marathon. It’s to fight a bear.”

Whether it’s losing weight, running a marathon or staying true to a 12-week training program for a BJJ tournament, each of these will have its own set of obstacles that may appear overwhelming at certain points. Like Bridger, with the right help and strategy, even the most ridiculous of challenges can become attainable—on paper at least.

1. Choose a Goal that Gives Your Training Direction

A clear target can turn scattered and inconsistent workouts into preparation with purpose. Whatever your type of activity you’re into, select an event or milestone that excites you, then break it into manageable steps. Study its requirements, plan your training, then execute and track your progress.

“You take a task and whether it’s impossible or not—or you think it’s impossible—and you train all your energies toward it,” Finkel says. “Yours may be completing an Ironman or marathon. Brick’s was fighting a bear.”

2. Find the Obstacle—and Make a Plan for It

No matter what your skill level is, there will come a point in which your progress stalls. When it happens, don’t assume that the solution is simply to work harder. Take inventory of what may be the cause of your plateau. Is it a physical weakness? Technique? Consistency? Recovery? Brick understood the challenge and searched for an opening. For your fitness goals, it could mean hiring a coach, making program adjustments, or even giving yourself more time to build your skillset.

“You got to know your enemy, and you have to be humble,” Finkel says. “He looks until he eventually finds the Achilles’ heel. Maybe there is always a way.”

3. Work on Weak Points While Still Enhancing Your Strengths

A smart plan is a combination of making improvements while also capitalizing on your primary abilities. Brick used his own size and MMA skills as the foundation while also putting in the work in the “classroom” to search for new ways to victory. For you, build on your strengths, too, from choosing a training style that fits your abilities, then working and progressing on the secondary skills that will help get the job done.

“Lean into your strength,” Finkel says. “Brick’s not an in-his-prime Mike Tyson knocking out guys in the first round. Every fight is now a grind for him. I even did this myself—I was trying to do triathlons, and I realized the only thing I actually like is swimming. So I just became a really fast swimmer and forgot the two parts I hated. As soon as I leaned into the thing I was best at, I enjoyed it so much more. So pick one and be really good at it.”

A go-it-alone strategy heavily reliant on brute strength and past personal achievements ends in disaster for nearly all of us. For Brick, filling the gaps for this task meant incorporating experts who knew the right tendencies and could spot openings and weak spots the untrained eye would overlook. So when it comes to goal achievement, it helps to find a coach, training partner, or knowledgeable source who can add to your preparations, spot problems, and offer candid feedback.

“Brick had a zoologist and a wrestling coach explaining the ways it can’t work,” Finkel says. “Being creative is one thing, but being resourceful within that creativity can really make a project like this work.”

An outrageous goal will take longer than you expect, and there will be stretches where it looks like you’re losing. Brick’s MMA motor keeps him moving forward when every fight becomes a struggle. He keeps working his way toward that small but decisive opening he knows is there but isn’t quite visible yet.

“He doesn’t quit,” Finkel says. “Brick might not be the most technically sound guy. He might not even be the strongest. He’s the guy with the motor, the size, and the will to keep figuring things out until he can exploit the weakness. Most people’s goals die right at the point where it stops feeling good—keep grinding until it does.”

New Zealand’s Twin-Engine Toyota Celica Is Essentially Unstoppable

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

A fairly conventional overlanding Celica build quickly spiraled into a monster with two engines, dual gearboxes, and semi-amphibious capability.

The art of modifying cars is gleefully rule-free. Sure, there are never-ending hoops to jump through for street legality, but actually building your perfect vehicle is merely a matter of dreams. For Kiwi Gareth Wadsworth, who goes by @garethcars on social media, those dreams meant stuffing two engines and two transmissions into a single sixth-generation Toyota Celica. Seems easy enough, right?

Wadsworth’s twin-engine Toyota Celica has made literal waves on social video in the past few weeks, as the Christchurch resident demonstrated its remarkable wading abilities in a series of viral videos. The basic recipe is a pair of Toyota’s 2.0-liter 3S-GE engines—one mounted in the front and one mounted in the rear—mated to a pair of manual transmissions and fed to individual differentials.

With so many moving parts, Wadsworth went analog with the controls; a pulley system attached to the original clutch pedal also operates the clutch for the rear transmission, and the throttle pedal is divided into an upper portion for the front engine and a lower portion for the rear engine. If you press directly in the middle of the accelerator, both engines will rev up (somewhat) in sync. Wadsworth emphasized that it is two completely separate drivetrains that require two of everything (two batteries, two fuel tanks, two ECUs, two radiators) and can be driven separately or simultaneously.

But this Celica build wasn’t always this extreme. In fact, the initial version was supposed to be a simple overlanding setup for a university off-road club trip in 2020. He initially off-roaded the Celica with a simple four-inch lift kit and 31-inch tires, but Wadsworth wanted to try something more extreme—and he didn’t want to abandon the existing platform.

“I had this Celica that I bought crashed, and I didn't have anything to do with it. I was a student. I didn't have the money for a four-wheel-drive, so I ended up building it into a four-wheel-drive,” Wadsworth said to Road & Track. “I paid $800 for the car, and then I sold the wheels and tires that were on it. So it was effectively a free car just sitting around, and I got some suspension left over from other projects that I threw in it, and the engine came from a crashed car I found upside down off the side of the road.”

After a year of mud-slinging fun, Wadsworth decided to go all-in on the dual-engine swap. The rear engine transplant only took two weeks, with most of the work happening after school and during a series of all-nighters. A day or two after the build was complete, Wadsworth and friends trailered the dual-engine Celica to an off-road area and began testing its abilities. Some limitations were quickly revealed.

“It was just the stock intakes for a few trips. It did have a snorkel on the outside for the front engine. Once it was twin-engine, it had enough power that we could hit the water faster, and that meant that the bow wave would keep going over the roof and filling the front engine's snorkel. We had one trip on the White Bear River that was in flood, and we hydrolocked the front engine twenty-three times, and we got sick of having to pull the spark plugs out, blasting the water out, and trying to get it running again. And so that's why I moved the intake to the glove box,” Wadsworth said.

Wading is one of the twin-engine Celica’s best tricks, as it is fast enough to glide onto the water and light enough to float through. The Celica has floated through currents that have sunk heavier 4x4 trucks, Wadsworth said. But the twin-engine Celica doesn’t just excel at water crossings, either. Because the gearboxes run independently, Wadsworth can tune the gear ratios of either end to suit his needs.

“There's no weird handling from the differences between the front and rear. It's just whichever engine's got the least load on it speeds up to catch up with the other one. They're effectively a fifty-fifty split,” Wadsworth said. “Because of the biasing, I quite often run almost exclusively rear-wheel-drive, and I only add front when I need it for traction. It's quite a unique feeling. It's so much fun, and probably the main reason I've still got it is being able to dynamically shift between front, rear, or four-wheel-drive and throttle biasing on the fly. In a mud pit, I can sit around doing donuts with the front in neutral, and then I can put the front-end gear in and let the clutch out slowly on the front to transition from donuts to power slides.”

And it isn’t even too hard to drive—relatively speaking. Wadsworth said that his mates can hop in and romp it with minimal coaching, at least in this configuration. Shifting the two gearboxes can get awkward, but the rear transmission shifter is placed near the traditional shifter, so you don’t have to reach far. Before the pulley clutch system, Wadsworth wired the rear clutch through the parking brake, which required a gentle touch to keep from stalling.

But it wasn’t always so accommodating. “On the original build, we didn't have time to connect any of the controls, so it had two drivers back then. And we didn't have a throttle, so the strap for tightening the hood was tied onto the rearview mirror and back to the rear throttle body, and you pulled it like a train horn,” Wadsworth said. “We certainly had a lot of difficulty driving it when we first built it, because it required two people to communicate—but it didn't have any exhaust, so we couldn't hear each other, and we had no total control on the rear; it was either fully on or fully off. And you had to get up out of your seat, turn around, and put it in gear for the rear engine.”

Even after six years of ownership and five years with two engines, Wadsworth plans to continue driving the twin-engine Celica as long as possible. Initially, he considered adding four-wheel steering using a power steering motor from a Nissan Leaf, but he said the time under the knife wouldn’t be worth it. The more time spent off-road, the better.

Tidal announces ban on AI-generated music; 27 million tracks will be deleted

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI songs account for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s catalog, but it’s about to be zero.

Most streaming music providers have opted to label AI-generated content on their platforms, and until now, Tidal was one of them. However, the company is about to take a harder stance. Tidal announced that in the coming weeks, it will fully ban AI music because of the harm it does to human artists.

Tidal debuted its initial AI policy in July 2026, which included labeling and demonetizing all songs created entirely with AI. That wasn’t enough to stem the tide of AI slop, though. Over the past 90 days, Tidal reports that 43 percent of new uploads were completely AI-generated. On November 6, new uploads to Tidal hit a whopping 60 percent AI-generated.

As things stand, Tidal hosts 27 million tracks labeled as AI, all of which were obviously added in the past couple of years. That accounts for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s total catalog. On December 9, Tidal will delete all those songs. The streamer notes this will have essentially no negative impact because 96 percent of AI-labeled tracks got zero plays in the past 90 days. Throughout its announcement, Tidal is careful to stipulate that it’s only talking about music that has been fully created by AI. Artists can still use AI tools as long as the final product contains human elements.

Credit: Tidal Credit: Tidal

Some people don’t particularly mind AI music, but the team at Tidal has come to believe the harm to artists is too great. AI models can pump out random noises faster than humans can create things of real value, which can mislead listeners and siphon royalty payments away from creators. There’s also an issue with AI uploaders actively attempting to steal royalty payments. Tidal says it has already removed about 66,000 tracks from its service for impersonating artists, which was against its AI policy even before this update.

As of December 9, individual uploaders and distributors will no longer be allowed to add AI music to Tidal. However, they could lie, couldn’t they?

Tidal hasn’t specified how it detects AI music, but it will probably rely on watermarking. Almost every AI song on Tidal (94 percent) comes from Suno, which added watermarking earlier this year. Tidal also cites Google Lyria as a source of AI tunes, which has SynthID labels embedded in the waveform. Deezer claimed it has a licensable AI music detector with a false-positive rate of less than 0.01 percent, but it’s unclear if Tidal is using that or something similar.

That will take care of most AI music, but this approach won’t detect everything. There are open models for music generation that you can run locally on a GPU, and some cloud-based providers aren’t bothering to watermark their outputs. Even if Tidal is using a separate detection system, new models may be able to evade it—it’s always going to be a cat-and-mouse game. This will be a problem with verifying provenance in all types of media in the future. For the time being, at least, Tidal’s policy should clear out most of the AI slop.

The people directing robots to make these songs will have plenty of other options after Tidal blocks AI. Spotify, YouTube Music, Deezer, and Apple Music all allow AI music on their platforms with labeling. Spotify has removed millions of AI tracks from its collection based on user feedback. Other streamers haven’t specified how much slop (if any) they’ve cleared out.
