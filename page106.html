Before ‘Band of Brothers,’ Tom Hanks Produced a Forgotten HBO Miniseries That Aged Perfectly

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the most impressive miniseries of all time is 2001's Band of Brothers. Executive produced by Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, the series is a compelling war drama that follows Easy Company, a part of the U.S. Army's 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment during World War II. Taking home 20 Emmy nominations and seven wins (including one for Outstanding Miniseries), Band of Brothers still holds up as being an authentic and powerful depiction of extraordinary historical events. But this wasn't the first time that Hanks had a hand in producing a show revolving around a true story. Just three years before, Hanks would also produce an HBO miniseries that is a must-watch today, especially for sci-fi fans who love the great beyond.

In 1865, science fiction writer Jules Verne penned a novel called From the Earth to the Moon. More than a century later, a team of Hollywood heavyweights would borrow the title for a 12-part television series depicting some of our country's most famous astronauts. The 1998 series was produced by Hanks, Ron Howard, Brian Grazer, and Michael Bostick — figures who created some of the most influential films of the 1990s. The miniseries tells the story of the Apollo program, which NASA ran during the 1960s and 1970s.

Although the episodes are dramatized, with stunning visuals and special effects by Ernest D. Farino, each episode explores one of the risky missions these astronauts undertook. Hanks introduces each episode, and then the viewer gets to dive into each mission, which is told from different perspectives. This allows every episode to showcase a variety of themes. A particularly brilliant installment is "The Original Wives Club" in Episode 11, which was directed by Oscar winner Sally Field. This episode shows how the wives of these astronauts lived through the Apollo program, leading to a behind-the-scenes look at the stress they endured while their husbands were put in danger, as well as the pressure they experienced in such public-facing positions.

From the beginning stages of the Space Race with the Soviet Union to a pseudo-documentary-style narrative in Episode 12 of the final lunar Mission (Apollo 17), every hour is as gripping as the last. While we're able to celebrate the triumphs of these astronauts as they travel where no man has before, From the Earth to the Moon never shies away from also depicting the tragedies that befell these men. We get to witness the horrific fire onboard Apollo 1 and the infamous Apollo 13 disaster, which ensures that the series is always honest in showing how truly dangerous these missions were. Although it's horrible to think of these traumas occurring to real people, the authentic storytelling makes the series even more harrowing and fascinating to watch.

The writers of From the Earth to the Moon were also intent on not just rehashing events that had already been depicted in movies prior to its release. For example, the 1983 film The Right Stuff portrayed the events of Project Mercury, so it's just briefly summarized in Episode 1. And then there's the 1995 movie Apollo 13, which was also directed by Howard and starred Hanks.

To avoid any repetition, and to ensure viewers still felt invested in the storytelling, From the Earth to the Moon tells the story of the mission from the eyes of the media covering the event. This type of creative storytelling allows the miniseries to feel fresh. While there were a few characters invented for the series, enough famous real-life figures, including Jim Lovell (Tim Daly), Buzz Aldrin (Bryan Cranston), Neil Armstrong (Tony Goldwyn), and Alan Shepard (Ted Levine), allow the show to fall into a familiar, yet captivating authenticity.

At the time of its release, From the Earth to the Moon was a critical darling, bringing home 17 Emmy nominations and three wins, including Outstanding Miniseries (just like Band of Brothers would do a few years later). It would also cement Hanks as someone who could tell historical stories in a respectful and intriguing way (paving the way for future projects such as The Pacific and Masters of the Air).

What makes From the Earth to the Moon so compelling nearly three decades later is its ability to capture both the wonder of space exploration and the enormous personal risks behind humanity's greatest achievements.

From the Earth to the Moon is available to stream on HBO Max in the U.S.

The pros and cons of using an Amazfit smartwatch

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazfit watches are easy on your wallet, but they can’t do everything.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

Amazfit smartwatches are widely considered to be good alternatives to the bigger brands. They're generally cheaper, with some models like the well-reviewed Amazfit Bip 6 available for as little as $80. But as you would expect, there are concessions to be made to justify the lower price.

The line from the Chinese company Zepp Health includes a wide range of smartwatches to suit any lifestyle, from casual wearers to serious athletes. However, they run on a proprietary OS that limits app access, and some models have limited storage. The companion app also isn't the most intuitive.

On the plus side, Amazfit smartwatches focus on AI-powered software using its Zepp Coach and offer long battery life, with some models lasting for up to 25 days per charge. You can also find deals on Amazfit smartwatches, which are already affordable to begin with.

Before you commit, it helps to know what you're getting and what you're giving up. Here's why so many people consider Amazfit in the first place.

Amazfit smartwatches are sleek, with designs that resemble other popular models. You'll find square-shaped options like the Bip 6 and Active 2 (Square) that look like an Apple Watch. There are also round-faced ones like the Amazfit Active Max and the Amazfit T-Rex 3, which is designed for outdoor adventurers and resembles a Garmin watch in its aesthetic.

Another advantage is cross-device compatibility. You can pair an Amazfit smartwatch with either iPhone or Android since it uses its own operating system and isn't tied to either. Many trusted outlets also rank Amazfit watch models among the best budget smartwatches, citing accurate health tracking along with their stylish designs. They also note that integrations like the AI Coach, Bluetooth calling and Alexa command support are top-notch.

Amazfit smartwatches also offer over 100 sports modes, from strength training and running to gardening and Tai Chi. And they have all the important sensors you'd want for data, including heart rate, sleep, temperature, GPS and more.

Amazfit is a solid budget smartwatch brand you can continue to use even if you swap phone platforms. There's also no subscription tier for any of the data it tracks and reports, and these watches are a step up from the best cheap fitness trackers, with larger screens for at-a-glance information and control on your wrist.

Amazfit smartwatches run on the proprietary Zepp OS, so you can't access third-party apps the way you can with Wear OS watches. Instead, you're limited to a few productivity apps and games from the Zepp Store. The watches do integrate with third-party platforms like Strava, TrainingPeaks, Komoot and Runna, but you can't access Google Wallet, Google Maps, Uber, Starbucks, Gmail or banking apps. There's no Spotify app either, though you can listen on your phone and use the watch to control playback or use a remote app to mirror Spotify on the device.

Beyond apps, the software isn't as intuitive as others or as easy to navigate as those of better-known brands. You'll see unfamiliar terms like PAI, Amazfit's personal activity score and Biocharge, which is basically its equivalent of a readiness score. Some models have limited storage, which could cause issues with storing workout data or apps. The Amazfit Bip 6, for example, has 512MB of storage.

On longevity, Amazfit offers only three years of software and security support for its smartwatches. They won't stop working after that point, but it's worth factoring into the decision if you plan to hold onto the smartwatch for a while.

For a more intuitive experience with better app support, you might prefer going with affordable models from brands like Fitbit and Garmin instead, or even an older-model Samsung Galaxy Watch or Apple Watch, even if they're in their last years of software support. While Amazfit smartwatches aren't the best smartwatches you can buy, you will get reliable health tracking, long battery life and sleek designs for a great price, with a few caveats you might be able to live with.

Alabama Power spent more than $6 million on ads last year — and customers can be billed for half of them until 2029

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This article originally appeared on Inside Climate News, a nonprofit, non-partisan news organization that covers climate, energy and the environment. Sign up for their newsletter here.

On a busy election day, the Alabama Public Service Commission requested that Alabama Power voluntarily stop billing customers, effective immediately, for television commercials and other ads it buys to promote various aspects of its business.

Alabama Power is the largest electricity provider in Alabama, with territory covering about two-thirds of the state. Though it is a monopoly, the company spends millions each year on advertising, touting its reliability, customer service and other aspects of its business.

In recent months, the company has launched a series of television commercials touting a rate freeze it announced late last year amid heavy criticism over rising electricity prices.

A state law passed earlier this year will prohibit Alabama Power from including the costs of those commercials in customers’ electric bills, meaning the powerful utility will have to find all of the dollars in corporate profits. But the new law won’t take effect until 2029.

Currently, Alabama Power is allowed to bill customers for half of its non-public safety advertising costs.

During Tuesday’s meeting, PSC President Cynthia Lee Almond moved to “request” that Alabama Power voluntarily begin excluding those costs immediately.

“I don’t see any reason why we shouldn’t go ahead and take steps now to begin that process, rather than waiting until Jan. 1, 2029,” Almond said during the meeting.

Almond then made her motion, which was not on the meeting agenda, but came after the regular report from the commission’s electricity division.

“I move that the commission request that Alabama Power consent to exclude all advertising expenses from retail rate setting until after Dec. 31, 2028, when the new law takes effect,” Almond said. “Assuming Alabama Power consents, this interim measure would be reflected in its [Rate Stabilization and Equalization] filings for 2027 and 2028.”

Commissioners Jeremy Oden and Chris Beeker IV voted yes after a brief discussion.

According to the company filings with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, Alabama Power spent more than $6 million in general advertising last year, roughly $4 per customer. It’s unclear from the federal filing how much of that total was passed on to customers or how much might be impacted by the new prohibition.

Alabama Power did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Daniel Tait, executive director of Energy Alabama, said the move was a boon to Alabama Power customers, who faced some of the highest electric bills in the country in recent years.

“We are grateful for the leadership of President Almond and the full commission for acting to end customers paying for Alabama Power’s advertising,” Tait said. “Once in place, Alabama will have one of the strongest customer protections in the nation on this front.”

This will be among the last actions of the commission under its current construction before a major overhaul takes effect.

In January, the commission will expand from three members to seven. Outgoing Gov. Kay Ivey has appointed four new commissioners, who will take office in January, including the PSC’s two first-ever Black members.

Oden and Beeker will also leave the commission in January after being defeated in Republican primaries. Cullman County Sheriff Matt Gentry will take Oden’s Place 1 seat, and former state auditor and PSC Commissioner Jim Zeigler will replace Beeker in Place 2.

Zeigler, a perennial candidate in Alabama across several decades, will return to the commission 48 years after leaving the PSC. He served one term from 1975-1979, during an era of skyrocketing energy costs and protracted political battles over electric rates between Alabama Power and then-Gov. George Wallace.

Almond will be the sole remaining member of the current commission.

The new seven-member commission will be overseen by the state’s first secretary of energy, who will be selected by Gov.-elect Tommy Tuberville. The new secretary will set the commission agenda, and overruling the secretary will require a vote of five of seven commissioners.

Tuberville has not yet announced his pick for secretary of energy. The only statutory requirement is that the secretary “must have experience in utility regulation or energy policy or a related field.”

Golf In Comfort Year-Round In This HVAC-Equipped Golf Cart From Tractor Supply

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

If you're an avid golfer but find the time spent between holes a bit uncomfortable, Tractor Supply has something that will make your life a lot nicer. Available right now for $15,849 is the Massimo MVR Cargo Max HVAC Lithium, an enclosed electric vehicle that, as its name suggests, includes integrated heating and air conditioning. Tractor Supply has it listed under its "Golf Carts" section of vehicles, but per its product description, its intended use is a lot wider.

The MVR is said to be "built for farm chores, property maintenance and recreation," and meant to let its owner "tackle property projects in comfort." And in that context, it really does remind me of a kei truck, especially with its power-dump cargo bed that uses a hydraulic mechanism for easier unloading.

With marine-grade vinyl seating for two, it support total loads of up to 1,100 pounds, and has an estimated driving range (that's how far you can drive with it, not a golf thing) of up to 45 miles, thanks to a 51.2-volt 105Ah battery. A 5.0 kW (6.7 horsepower) AC motor lets it hit a top speed of 15 mph. Golf carts can be powered by electric or gas, and each have their own pros and cons, but having this one at your local golf course would certainly add to the serenity both inside and outside of the cart.

To help it navigate less-than-ideal terrain, there's 12 inches of ground clearance and 23-inch all-terrain tires fitted all around. Tractor Supply currently lists the MVR Cargo Max HVAC in just two colors: Pearl White and Ultra Red, but Massimo's site also lists it in Midnight Silver, Deep Blue, and Glacier Blue.

Sixteen grand for a "golf cart" may sound like a lot, but, again, I feel like the Massimo MVR Cargo Max is only a golf cart on a technicality. You can definitely use it as fairway transport, sure, but it's really a utility cart meant to transport cargo and up to two people around all sorts of private property. And with HVAC, it'll keep you comfortable doing so all year round. You may even be able to take it around your gated community, if that's the sort of thing they all. Whether or not you can legally drive it on public roads, however, is highly dependent on where you're at.

"Not since the infamous debut of Daikatana has the release of a single game generated so much incensed indignation from early buyers." Our review of Pool of Radiance: Ruins of Myth Drannor didn't hold back. The 2001 dungeon crawler contained, our review said, "A multitude of outrageous bugs," including ones that corrupted saves, made quest items disappear, and most infamously deleted system files when you uninstalled the game.

SNEG's rerelease, available now on Steam and GOG, doesn't have those issues. It adds widescreen, controller support, adjustable combat speed, tooltips, an action bar, and sharpened visuals. If, like me, you skipped this one back in the day because of its reputation for being a hassle to play—even with the eventual patches and community fixes—now it's smooth and problem-free. Don't get the wrong idea, though. Ruins of Myth Drannor isn't Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines. This isn't an RPG masterpiece held back by its buggy release. It's a simplistic turn-based dungeon crawl that's mostly notable for being a spiritual successor to the first Gold Box D&D game, Pool of Radiance, and for adapting the then-new third edition of D&D's rules.

The 3.0 rules were a huge step forward at the time. Any character could choose any class, and Armor Class went up instead of down—you can't imagine how much of a drag it was explaining to new players that lower AC was better than high AC, and ideally you wanted a negative number. It also formalized the central mechanic of rolling a d20 and adding a number to beat a difficulty score. If you've played 5th edition, you'll recognize a lot of its DNA here.

Which is why it's a shame Ruins of Myth Drannor isn't a complete adaptation of those rules. You can't play a wizard—sorcerer is the only option for arcane casters—or a halfling or a gnome. You choose which class to improve when you advance a level, but don't get to decide where your skill points go or what feats to take. A couple of years later, The Temple of Elemental Evil would adapt the 3.5 rules update much more fully. Ruins of Myth Drannor feels rudimentary by comparison.

It's also not much of a story. Set 10 years after the events of Pool of Radiance, a new magical pool has opened in the ruined elven city of Myth Drannor so the evil sorceress and dracolich on the box art can get up to some shenanigans, and Elminster has sent a party of adventurers in to stop them. There's one fun twist to this otherwise bog-standard plot, and it's that you're not that first party of adventurers. You're the B-team sent as backup, but a magic portal mishap means you arrive too late to save the A-team and have to take on their quest.

Which explains why four level-one nobodies now have to clear out hundreds of orcs and skeletons from samey dungeon rooms. It's a slow-paced slog, especially in Myth Drannor's main halls, earning a drip-feed of experience points and gold coins from zombies and ghouls, hoping an orc doesn't score a crit and drop one of your party members in a single blow.

Novo Nordisk Just Delivered Great News for Investors. Is a Comeback Finally Underway?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Novo got a much-needed boost from its latest earnings report.

Are things finally starting to look up for Novo (NVO +0.03%)? The Denmark-based pharmaceutical leader has had a rough past couple of years, but its third-quarter financial results were strong enough to send the stock up 6% post-earnings. That's great news for shareholders, but can Novo maintain this momentum?

At first glance, Novo's third-quarter results didn’t seem particularly impressive, as some of the company’s longtime growth drivers underperformed. For instance, sales of Ozempic, the company's famous diabetes medicine, were 29.7 billion Danish kroner (about $4.5 billion), down 3% compared to the year-ago period. The company's weight-loss medicine, Wegovy, recorded sales of 20.4 billion DKK (about $3.1 billion), roughly flat compared with the prior-year quarter.

However, several other medicines helped pick up the slack, and arguably none was more important than Novo's oral Wegovy. The company launched this weight loss pill -- the first of its kind to earn approval -- in January. The Wegovy pill has helped attract many patients who were hesitant to start treatment with injectables. Novo reported that, as of June, oral Wegovy had surpassed three million prescriptions, and more than 80% were for patients who had never taken GLP-1s before. In the third quarter, oral Wegovy generated 5.4 billion DKK (about $810 million) in revenue.

Novo's total net sales were 79.2 billion DKK (about $11.9 billion), up 6% year over year. The company's net income dropped by 16% year over year to 16.7 billion DKK (about $2.5 billion), largely due to non-cash impairment charges. Still, Novo’s results beat analyst estimates, and the pharmaceutical company also raised its guidance for the fiscal year 2026.

It now expects revenue to be flat (at the midpoint) year over year, while the previous guidance implied a 3% year-over-year drop at the midpoint. It's no wonder Novo's shares jumped following this beat-and-raise quarter. But what's next for the drugmaker?

There are several catalysts ahead that could help Novo improve its financial performance. Let's consider three of them. First, the company could make meaningful progress with oral Wegovy. The medicine has now been approved in several other countries, including Germany and the U.K.

These markets might not be as lucrative as the U.S., but they should move the needle for the company. Novo will also likely attract brand-new GLP-1 patients in these regions. The company is also looking to earn label expansions for oral Wegovy. Novo has submitted an application to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for oral Wegovy as a potential treatment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis.

The original injectable version of Wegovy is approved for this indication, but the pill offers a more convenient option. It could be successful in this niche and continue driving Novo's sales higher. Second, Novo will launch new products to strengthen its position in the diabetes and weight-loss markets.

It is inching closer to approval for CagriSema, a medicine that beat Wegovy in a head-to-head Phase 3 clinical trial for weight loss. CagriSema also posted strong clinical trial results in diabetes. This medicine could help Novo steal some market share from its biggest competitor, Eli Lilly (LLY -0.79%).

Third, Novo has several pipeline candidates that could improve its approved lineup over time. The company's Phase 3 pipeline features zenagamtide, a promising diabetes and weight-loss medicine that is being developed in subcutaneous and oral formulations.

The weight loss market is growing rapidly, and this product, along with others, may help Novo ride that tailwind for a long time, even as competition intensifies. Novo may still experience some clinical setbacks, but after the beating it took over the past two years, the company is slowly staging a comeback. In my view, the stock is attractive following its third-quarter results.

Over Pinchot Pass — John Muir Trail Day 10

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The night before, at Rae Lake, I ate some kind of buffalo stew, which was supposed to provide about 900 calories.  It is funny how flavor (usually) takes a backseat to caloric value on the trail.  I am just so conscious that I am exerting myself — a lot — and need to maximize my fuel intake.

After dinner I had my headlamp on in the tent, probably dictating my notes, and was not aware that Evelyn (or Serena?) was taking pictures of my tent in the darkness under a starry sky.

Night camping at Rae Lakes (photo courtesy of Evelyn or Serena)

My sleep started deep and sound.  Then I did wake up, coughing a bit, and I thought, “Oh, no!”  But it was still much less than before, and I was very tired, so I slept again.  I vaguely remember dreaming about how some people were organizing something-or-other and I was confused how it was going to work.  Maybe it meant I was wondering how to manage this journey?

Around 4:40 in the morning, when I got up to pee, I checked the Garmin and saw the time.  I thought, “Well, I think I’m not going to go back to sleep.”  So I didn’t; instead I fetched my food from the bear box, and ate my breakfast.  But I felt sluggish, like I was swimming through molasses.

How sluggish?  Well, I rose at 4:40 but I did not get on the trail until 7:00 a.m.  Despite everything, it was only thirty minutes later than the previous day, but it was far from efficient.  I said goodbye to Evelyn and Serena; they were heading southbound, and I was heading northbound.  I left the crowded campsite and started on my way.

One consequence of the Rae Lake crowds is that it was hard to find a private place to dig a cathole.  I decided to wait for a better place.  About a half hour down the trail, I pulled off and found a spot that was quite lovely and dug my hole and did my business.  In the middle of it, a deer appeared in the meadow next to me and gazed at me curiously.

Cat hole in the foreground, amazing view in the background!

Less than a half mile further, I discovered a beautiful camping site with a bear box and no crowds at all.  I thought, “Oh my gosh, if I’d just known and been willing to take the risk to go a little further yesterday, it would have been a much nicer situation.”  It was too late for me, but I resolved to tell any southbound hikers I encountered that they would be more comfortable stopping at the last camp site before the main Rae Lake location.

More beautiful and fewer crowds — the place to be!

As I looked at the long day ahead of me, I realized it would be 6-7 miles, all downhill, followed by 8-9 miles all uphill, followed by a mile or two of downhill to my next camp site at Lake Marjorie.  In elevation I started at 10,500 feet, would drop to 8,500 feet, and then would cross Pinchot Pass at 12,200 feet.  I planned for a long day.

I did about four miles on that nice downhill to start the day when I encountered a small stream I had to cross.  In general, these stream crossings had not been too challenging, frankly.  I brought my water shoes explicitly because I thought I would wade through river crossings, perhaps as much a thigh-deep, and there hadn’t been any like that – at least so far.

This one was no different, a two-stone hop across with a couple of different options as to route.  That, it turned out, was the problem.  As I prepared to take my second step I decided to go via a different sequence of stones, and I turned slightly to change direction.  It was a slight turn, nothing notable about it at all.  Except… Suddenly I felt a sharp pain in my left knee.

I let out a yowl and finished crossing.  But I was worried.  Clearly, I had injured something. The only question was, what was it and how badly was it damaged?

I was still able to move it, so that was a good first sign.  Working it gently, it barked at me in certain angles; but just going straight ahead, it seemed not to bother me too much.

I decided to take an Ibuprofen at the bottom of the hill to prevent swelling from whatever it was that had happened.  Five minutes later I changed my mind, thinking, “Why am I waiting  to take the medication?  I just sustained the injury.  I should take the ibuprofen immediately, right NOW.”  And I did.

I continued down gingerly, favoring my knee a bit, worried that this might be something serious, and not knowing whether it would be or not.

My main distraction was lizards.  Yes!  I had observed no reptiles at all above 10,000 feet, but coming lower they started to appear.  I think they were most likely Western Fence Lizards, but they could also have been Sagebrush Lizards.  Because of my focus on my knee, I did not photograph them, but I resolved to do so the next time they appeared.

Finally, I reached the bottom of the downhill.  There was a suspension bridge to cross there, and it was one of the bouncier ones I had ever been on.  With a GoPro I would gladly have filmed myself crossing it, but with my hand-held phone it was too easy to imagine it flying out of my hand into the rushing waters below.

From solid ground I took a couple of pictures of the bridge anyway.

Then I crossed to the other side and began the big uphill.  I was facing around 3,600 feet of elevation gain, while worrying about my stupid knee.  I also worried that the trail might feel kind of endless, and it did feel kind of endless.

Along the way I started leapfrogging another hiker named Amy.  Our hiking styles almost made it inevitable.  I would go fast but then stop and rest; she kept a steady pace.  So, I would go by her moving faster, and she would move by me when I stopped.  Then I would resume and pass her again, and the cycle would continue.  At one point, when I was ahead, I saw a grouse.  I wondered if it would still be there when she came by.

The good news in all of this was that either the Ibuprofen was working, or my knee was not badly injured, or both.

Amy and I had both set Lake Marjorie, on the other side of Pinchot Pass, as our destination for the night.  This was the first camping space about a mile and a half below the summit.

We kept ascending.  The one advantage was that the ascent took place over many miles, so it was rarely going up very steeply.  But over the course of the afternoon, it was feeling quite relentless.  Only the series of waterfalls along the way kept me engaged and appreciative.

…another waterfall falling in perpetuity!

Approaching Pinchot Pass I ran into a guy coming downhill toward me.  We chatted and I learned that, like me, he was 69 years old.  He seemed very demoralized.  He said he felt like he was shuffling, like he could barely even do it.  He said he was planning to get off at Kearsarge Pass and probably would not return.  I said, “Well, what you should do is get off at Kearsarge and go to Independence and then spend a day there doing nothing.  After a day off trail, your mind will be clearer, and then you can decide if that’s the end for you, or if you do actually want to come back.”  That was my recommendation; I don’t know if he took it.

Nearing the top, there were many rocks patterned with white striations.  I wanted to turn to Sam and ask the geologist to explain what had caused them.  But of course, he was back in Boulder by then.

You can see the white stripes in the foreground rocks, but also in the background cliff face. Quartz maybe? Sam could have told me…

The end of the trail up to the pass was a series of switchbacks.  You couldn’t really see where they were leading until, suddenly, ta-da!, you had reached the saddle of the pass.

It was a really, really hard day.  I started at seven a.m. and I got to the pass at exactly five p.m.  If you do the math, that’s ten hours.  And I still had the mile and a half to go down to the lake.

The views over the lakes as the sun was setting were beautiful.  Amy finally caught up to me again, at a stream where I stopped to top off my water supply before making camp.  That late in the afternoon, we were both worried that the camping spots might already be filled up.  But when I saw a truly beautiful location, on nice flat sandy ground, I said, “That’s the one for me!”  Amy kept going to the next spot further down the trail, but I called it a day.

Arriving at Rae Lake the previous day, I was so tired it was difficult to function.  On this occasion, at Lake Marjorie, I set up calmly and reasonably efficiently, and it just felt much more natural than it had up to that point.  I pitched my tent, soaked my food, and set up my sleeping system, of course.  But I also went down to the lake shore where I washed my socks and washed my feet.  While I had my feet in the lake’s icy cold water, I thought, “Oh, I need to ice my knee too.”  So, I went a little deeper into the lake and got my knee in the icy cold water as well.

Unfortunately, my dinner was disgusting.  Some of these freeze-dried meals work great for a cold-soaker, but some really need boiling water to be edible.  I just wish I had kept track of which was which for future hikes!  Dinner was the low point of the day (other than my knee tweak), but I ate it for the calories.

Early Evening by Lake Marjorie. Notice my tent to the right of the boulder and — at that point — nothing to the left of the boulder.

Sitting in my tent pitched next to a large boulder it was getting colder-and-colder at our high elevation.  It was definitely a night for my sleeping bag liner!  In deep twilight I heard a large animal moving around on the other side of the big boulder.  Initially I was quite spooked, but when I checked I saw that a late-arriving hiker was setting up camp there.

For a moment I was annoyed that my delicious privacy had been disturbed.  Then I overcame that feeling and went over to welcome the newcomer and see if they needed anything.  It was a solo hiker named Sally, born in China but now an Australian citizen, also NOBO on the John Muir Trail.  She was friendly and we chatted for a few moments before I returned to my tent to call it a night.

It had been a 17-mile day, my longest yet on the trail.  Sleep came easily.
