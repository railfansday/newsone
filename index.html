With the moon still plump and full, I set out through the mountains from the restful town I’d slept in.

It was daylight, and as I carefully descended the path strewn with loose rocks, I listened to the book that taught me about the Road to Santiago.

In the town before my destination, I planned to stop and eat breakfast. The cafe cats caught my attention as I approached. As the whole street came into view, I saw my Italian brother smiling at me. I ran to him and gave him a hug.

We always managed to stumble upon each other without planning, and I loved it.

When we made it to the city of Ponferrada, we went directly to the donativo albergue—San Nicolás de Flüe Pilgrims Hostel—to wait for it to open. The courtyard was large, with many trees, a large wooden totem, and a vine-covered sitting area. I went into the church nearby looking for a restroom. What I found, though, made me take a seat in the pew.

Within those tiny walls, an extraordinary mural was painted on the ceiling, depicting scenes from the Camino de Santiago, the four cardinal directions, the elements, and the stars. It stirred both the artist and the spiritual side within me.

When I went back to where we were relaxing, our Argentine brother had arrived. The three of us called ourselves “Familia Real”, the name emphasizing the bond we had created.

Once again, without planning, we were all together.

As a religious donativo albergue, I was placed on the woman’s side of the building. When I saw the name of the room I’d been assigned to, I couldn’t help but smile. “Cruz del Ferro.”

That place would forever hold a special place in my heart.

The brothers and I went on a side quest to a bodega overlooking the Castle of the Knights Templar.

After the siesta hours ended, I went to the Castle of the Knights Templar to embark on the tourist part of the day. I’d made it a few feet into the castle walkway when I saw my pilgrim friend below on the street. He was a musician who carried a flute with him everywhere, taking full advantage to play and sing and jam at every opportunity. I yelled down, asking him to play, but that was already his plan.

A man he met in the city joined him with a violin and I was entranced.

Standing on a castle wall, listening to music from artists I knew, with the city and mountains stretching out behind them, felt surreal.

That moment will forever be stamped into my memory.

After each song ended, I clapped from my perch on the castle wall, and the pilgrim smiled, skipping around with pride. His passion for music poured out of him with such a burning fire, and I felt lucky to witness it.

I knew I had a lot more to see, though, so I went on my way.

Exploring the exterior, I went level after level, corridor after corridor. I don’t like big houses, but now I want to live in a castle.

The secret passageways and rooms reminded me of the forts I used to build as a child.

And being on top of that tower overlooking the castle, the city, and the mountains?

I learned more about medieval culture than I ever had in my life. I explored an exhibit of a collection of books. The texts on display were all shapes and sizes, some as thick as my head, some smaller than a deck of cards.

Had it not been for exhaustion hitting me, I could’ve stayed for several more hours.

After 2.5 hours, though, I knew it was time to move on.

The day had mirrored the life I’d grown accustomed to on the Camino.

Agent instructions: in the repo or in the tool?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are two places to put the standing rules you want a coding agent to follow: in a file in the repo, or built into the tool that runs the agent. I've ended up with both, and they trade off differently.

In the repo, the rules are versioned. They show up in pull requests, the whole team sees them, and they travel with a clone. The cost is that you write and maintain them per project, and they're only as good as the last person who edited them.

In the tool, they work in every project and for every agent with no setup. The cost is that they're invisible: nobody reviews a diff of them, and they can clash with how a particular repo does things.

Styx does the second kind. It builds in guidance from a few public repos: Ponytail for coding, Impeccable for interfaces, design-critique for reviewing a design, and architecture-designer for system design. Only the core rules are included, each applies only to that kind of work, and your project's own design system and rules come first.

That last part is the rule I'd defend: when the two disagree, the repo wins, because it knows the project and the tool doesn't.

Do you agree, or do you let the tool override the repo?

Your Mac has been keeping a file on your family

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since macOS 13 shipped in 2022, every Mac has run two daemons whose only job is to build a graph about you. Who you are, who you know, how those people relate to you and to each other, how you communicate, where you go, what you do and when. The daemons are called knowledgeconstructiond and intelligenceplatformd, and the closest thing Apple has ever documented about them is a two-sentence man page.

A security duo going by The Bishop and The Knight took the system apart in October and published the anatomy. The graph lives in ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform, fifty-one plaintext SQLite databases protected by macOS's Full Disk Access permission. The core one, graph.db, is a triple store: 181 entity classes, 278 predicates, and a confidence score on every row. What goes into it is basically everything the OS can see. Contacts. Photos, with faces. Calendar titles and attendees. Family Sharing, including the age of every member. The emergency contact in your Medical ID. Events and people Siri Suggestions digs out of your mail and messages. Apps donated through App Intents. Beside the graph sit tables for who appears with whom in your photos, a communication log without content, location and movement, and a stream of device signals: screen locked, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging, ambient light.

Then there is the part the HN headline picked up: a pair of XGBoost classifiers, shipped as CoreML models, that decide who counts as family. They read 144 features and output a probability for seventeen relationship columns per person, recomputed every day. Twenty of the features are identity: same last name, shared home address, an iCloud Family flag, age difference. Forty are communication patterns. Eighty-four come from photographs.

One shipped feature name is worth reading twice: photosPersonPosteriorProbabilityGivenSaturdayNight. A real input to a real classifier, and its job is deciding whether someone is your sister.

The scores look mundane until you look at what produced them. A mother scored 0.960, the ceiling of that column. Nobody typed it. The post counts nine independent signals that land on the same person: the partner column, a composite score, a household entity, the Family Sharing token, an iCloud flag, top twelve-month contact, a Photos face cluster, a Contacts record, a reachable handle. The model joined them and concluded "partner" for someone the owner had never labeled.

The joins are where it stops being about you. From one store, the authors worked out that there is a child in the household and roughly how old, which daycare the child attends along with both phone numbers, where the family will be and between which hours on a given Thursday, and which password manager is installed. A relationship is intelligence one level above the rows it was inferred from.

Deletion does not fully retract any of it. The live graph drops a contact, but a second table, expired_stable_graph, keeps the retracted rows with their confidence and sources, about forty-eight hours by the authors' measurement, longer on their own machines. The scorecard still scored two people a day after the graph had dropped them, people it knew only from a calendar invitation.

The authors also run a thought experiment that applies to any personalized system: what happens when this file ends up in the wrong hands, paired with a local model that has had its guardrails stripped. They call the graph a privacy single point of failure, and they play the scenario out in a second post.

To be fair to Apple: everything happens on device. The databases sit behind the Full Disk Access permission, so other apps cannot read them without the user approving it. Apple's researchers published a paper on the graph techniques back in 2023. And the same store exists on iOS, where a forensics tool called iLEAPP has been reading it since September.

The fair criticism is narrower and harder to wave off. The depth of the inference is undocumented. The kinship classifier is mentioned nowhere. And the graph does not just hold your data; it holds data about everyone who has ever emailed you, appeared in your photos, or been invited to a calendar event, and none of those people were asked.

The authors open-sourced a dashboard called iKnow that reads your own graph locally, if you want to see the file your Mac keeps on you.

Pydio Cells access needs a PAT, workspace, and public-share inventory

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pydio Cells combines identity, file storage, workspaces, OAuth clients, and public sharing. A person who reaches the login page may be blocked before seeing any files, while a leaked personal access token or public link may bypass the browser flow entirely. An exposure assessment that only records the login page leaves the important paths out.

On 2026-11-08, ZoomEye count-only observations returned 0 matches for app="Pydio Cells", 1,470 for title="Pydio Cells", and 2,397 for http.body="Pydio Cells". The query scope was sub_type=all, page 1, and page size 1. The title and body counts are close enough to be useful leads, but neither proves that a host is configured with public shares or weak tokens.

Pydio documents personal access tokens as an alternative to the standard OAuth flow. A token can replace a bearer access token when calling the REST API. It can also be restricted to a specific node with a scope in the form node:NODE_UUID:PERMISSION, where the permission can include read or write.

That scope is useful only when it is applied to the right node and the underlying user still has access to that node. The token should not be treated as an independent grant of filesystem authority. If the user is removed from a workspace or loses a role, the token should lose the effective access as well. Review both the token's scope and the roles that determine the user's workspace ACLs.

This Bitcoin Developer’s Idea Would Make Quantum Attacks a Losing Bet

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This Bitcoin Developer’s Idea Would Make Quantum Attacks a Losing Bet

Bitcoin developer Cory Fields has an unusual idea for dealing with future quantum thieves: Make them risk their own Bitcoin every time they try to steal someone else’s.

In a November 4 post to the Bitcoin development mailing list, Fields outlined “quantum landmines” and two types of auctions designed to turn attacks on vulnerable wallets into expensive gambles. He called the ideas “thought experiments,” not a completed Bitcoin upgrade.

Bitcoin owners use a secret private key to authorize transactions. A matching public key lets the network check their signatures without learning the secret.

If the whole idea seems too hard to understand, we used AI to generate a video to help you grasp it:

Today’s computers cannot realistically calculate that private key from the public one. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could use a mathematical technique called Shor’s algorithm to do it, potentially letting a thief spend coins as if they owned them.

Some older Bitcoin outputs have exposed public keys, and certain newer formats reveal them too. Other addresses hide the key until someone spends, so the risk is not identical for every wallet.

Researchers call the hypothetical arrival of a computer capable of these attacks “Q-Day.” No publicly demonstrated machine can steal Bitcoin this way today, and nobody knows exactly when one might exist.

Imagine someone owns 1 BTC in an old, potentially vulnerable wallet. Before Q-Day, this person creates a tiny, separate quantum-safe Bitcoin output containing a hidden proof that they controlled the original private key while quantum theft was still impossible.

Think of it as a sealed ownership receipt. Outsiders can see the small output, but not the evidence tucked inside it, or even that it is a trap.

Now imagine a future thief uses a quantum computer to recover the user’s old private key. Under Fields’s suggested rules, the thief cannot simply take her 1 BTC: They must add 1 BTC of their own from a quantum-safe wallet as collateral.

Both coins enter a restricted output worth 2 BTC. The thief must wait through a challenge period—30 days in Fields’s example—before spending it freely.

The wallet owner notices the transaction and reveals the pre-Q-Day proof. The network then allows the owner to move the full 2 BTC to the quantum-safe address they committed to earlier: their original coin, plus the thief’s collateral.

The attacker has no way to know in advance which old wallets contain these hidden receipts. Even a successful quantum key recovery could become a 1 BTC loss.

The wallet owner would also need matching collateral to initiate a normal post-Q-Day move of her own legacy coin. If they have no spare BTC, the same rule intended to hurt thieves could make moving her savings difficult.

Landmines only work when an owner prepares one before Q-Day and reveals it in time. They cannot protect coins whose keys are already lost, including some of the early Bitcoin holdings widely believed to be inaccessible.

Fields proposes a second mechanism called a staked auction. Before Q-Day, someone could lock up quantum-safe Bitcoin and secretly register a bid against a specific old wallet they expect thieves to target.

Suppose an apparently abandoned wallet holds 50 BTC. A future attacker tries to move it and posts another 50 BTC as collateral; during the auction period, someone who committed a bid in advance can challenge the attempted theft.

In Fields’s example, a successful challenger claims part of the attacker’s collateral while the original vulnerable coins are destroyed rather than awarded to the bidder. The point is to deprive thieves of a reliable payday, not return the coins to a missing owner.

His third idea, a sats auction, requires no advance stake. Instead, a vulnerable-coin spend would trigger a contest in which the highest bidder gets the coins minus their bid, while the runner-up pays their bid and gets nothing.

That resembles a notorious auction game where two bidders keep raising their offers because neither wants to be the person who loses money for nothing. A thief might avoid starting a contest that could cost more than the coins they hoped to steal.

One proposed response to quantum risk is to restrict spending from outdated wallets after a migration deadline. That might prevent theft, but could also permanently lock out legitimate owners who never moved their coins.

Fields is exploring a different trade-off: Keep old coins potentially spendable, but make attempted transfers costly and open to challenges. Bitcoin cannot tell an honest owner from a quantum thief if both can produce the same valid signature.

The trade-off is severe, considering that honest owners might need large collateral deposits, inactive holders could miss their chance to object, and auction rules might burn coins belonging to people who are still alive and simply offline.

Bitcoin would first need a quantum-safe way to hold and spend funds. Wallets would then need to support hidden ownership commitments, while Bitcoin’s consensus rules would have to enforce collateral, waiting periods, special recovery transactions, and auctions.

Developers would also need to agree on when the new rules activate, how much collateral is required, and what happens to losing bids or leftover funds. Fields believes some mechanics could be introduced through a soft fork, a type of Bitcoin rule change, but has not supplied the detailed design.

None of these protections exists in Bitcoin today. Fields has published no implementation or activation schedule, and his original post leaves the auction payouts and even the desired outcome of an auction unresolved.

They were both caught on recently installed remote wildlife camera traps, an incredible tool for scientists and award-winning wildlife photographers alike.

As SFGate reports, the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing, which has been in the works since the National Wildlife Federation (NWF) and Santa Monica Mountains Fund began raising money for it in 2014, isn’t slated to have its ribbon-cutting ceremony until early December. Construction is ongoing, so it isn’t even technically a crossing yet.

Wildlife doesn’t wait. The project, which includes two bridges crossing the 10-lane Ventura Freeway, includes 13 acres of habitat restoration. Birds and bugs have already enjoyed the growing reclaimed habitat above the busy highway, while a western fence lizard was the first to go on the bridge’s surface itself.

The two coyotes were the first large mammals spotted on the structure itself, but a mountain lion and her two cubs, a mule deer, and bobcats have all been spied walking next to the wildlife crossing.

“To everyone who wondered how wildlife would know where to go without signs… apparently, they’ve figured it out,” writes the Facebook account for P22 Mountain Lion of Hollywood, an influential mountain lion that was tragically euthanized in 2022. The famous big cat made a lasting impact on California’s wildlife conservation efforts, inspiring many to get involved and help make projects like the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing possible.

For scientists, the new wildlife crossing bridge offers many interesting things to learn.

“This is more than just about whether animals use the crossing,” said Seth Riley from the National Park Service’s Santa Monica Mountains National Recreation Area team. “We want to understand how effective it is in reconnecting an ecosystem that has been divided by the freeway for decades. That is a much bigger scientific question and answering it will take years.”

Riley and other team members will learn a lot from cameras set up on and near the wildlife crossing, and the public will get to see many of these photos. For security reasons, there won’t be real-time public feeds from the site, though. Scientists will study how the wildlife crossing affects wildlife diversity, genetic diversity, habitat evolution, how light and sound affect animals, and more.

It’s an exciting time to be a wild animal near Highway 101. And a decidedly safer one.

Thank God Star Wars' The Mandalorian Replacement Didn't Happen

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Nearly a decade after it was canceled, it was clearly a blessing that the Star Wars movie that was meant to replace The Mandalorian never got made. For nearly seven years now, The Mandalorian has been the flagship Star Wars show on Disney+. It's by far the franchise's most popular series, even with the box office failure of The Mandalorian and Grogu, and it even spawned its own mini-franchise, the Mandoverse.

Though The Mandalorian has become a powerhouse of Star Wars at large, Disney and Lucasfilm didn't originally think it was going to be such a huge success. Beyond the sequel trilogy of Star Wars movies, Disney and Lucasfilm didn't really know what to do with the franchise. After two spinoff films, Rogue One and Solo, there still wasn't a viable alternative to yet another trilogy.

If things had gone just a little bit differently, The Mandalorian may not have happened. One of the many previously announced and later canceled Star Wars movies may have taken its place. If that had happened, Star Wars as a whole would have been significantly worse off. For possibly the first time, The Mandalorian has proven that it was actually for the best that Disney and Lucasfilm failed to finish a project they announced.

Years before The Mandalorian premiered in 2019, Disney and Lucasfilm were planning on making a standalone Star Wars movie based on Boba Fett. The now-canceled Boba Fett movie was announced in 2014 and was originally supposed to be a spinoff film akin to Rogue One. Over the years, the Boba Fett movie lost its director, got shuffled around behind the scenes while Solo: A Star Wars Story was being made, and ultimately got canceled in 2018.

The cancelation of the Boba Fett movie was disappointing at the time. Boba Fett has long been a fan-favorite character, and the movie was a chance to finally use him to his fullest potential decades after The Empire Strikes Back. However, it's now clear that canceling the Boba Fett movie was the best decision Disney and Lucasfilm could have made, as it made The Mandalorian possible. Put simply, The Mandalorian never would have been created if the Boba Fett film hadn't been axed.

Since Star Wars Episode VII: The Force Awakens hit theaters in December 2015, but the franchise has moved onto even better things on smaller screens.

Both of the possible outcomes of the Boba Fett movie being released would have led to Din Djarin not being created. If it was a hit, Star Wars would have doubled down on Fett, meaning there was no need for Djarin. As the failure of The Book of Boba Fett proved, there's really only room enough in Star Wars for one helmeted hero at a time. If it was a flop, Star Wars would have moved away from Mandalorians entirely to focus on a different part of the franchise. There's no way around it: if the Boba Fett movie happened, The Mandalorian wouldn't have.

The cancelation of the Boba Fett movie ended up being a massive blessing in disguise. That's because the creation of The Mandalorian was absolutely crucial for Star Wars, and not just because it's a great show. The Mandalorian came at exactly the time Star Wars needed it most, and it ended up rejuvenating the franchise and ushering in an era of live-action shows on Disney+.

The Rise of Skywalker premiered just a month after The Mandalorian started, and it marked a significant low point for the franchise. The sequel trilogy was already divisive and the subject of the so-called culture war, especially after The Last Jedi divided the fan base into those who loved and those who hated it. Solo: A Star Wars Story had also become the franchise's first box office bomb in 2018. The Rise of Skywalker then unified those fans, but it mostly unified them in a shared hatred of the trilogy's final chapter.

Star Wars was in a spiral at the end of 2019, and it may have only gotten worse if The Mandalorian hadn't been there to absorb some of the blow. For the first time since Disney purchased Star Wars, The Mandalorian managed to be nearly universally acclaimed by both camps of the culture wars. It was a resounding hit that drew in old and new fans of the franchise and of Baby Yoda. It also proved the viability of live-action Star Wars shows, which Disney would focus on for the next six years.

The Mandalorian certainly didn't solve all of Disney and Lucasfilm's problems, but it was more than enough to keep Star Wars healthy for nearly a decade. Instead of taking a hiatus to lick its wounds after two underwhelming movies back to back, Star Wars was huge from 2020 to 2025. The franchise produced nearly a dozen live-action and animated shows in that time. While the fanbase was largely still divided, The Mandalorian gave viewers something to come together on.

While it is possible that Boba Fett's solo movie could have been a rejuvenating force for Star Wars, there's almost no chance it would have had the same impact as The Mandalorian. The main reason for that is because Boba Fett is a legacy character from the original trilogy, while Din Djarin was a wholly new creation. As popular as Boba Fett was back in the 2010s, he also carried some baggage.

The Mandalorian was a hit precisely because it was so broadly accessible. You didn't need to watch a minute of Star Wars to understand the show. As long as you had a vague understanding of the Force, Yoda, and the Empire, you were all set. Viewers weren't even required to know what a Mandalorian is, as Din Djarin was part of a secretive and traditionalist sect. Anyone and everyone could tune in to see Baby Yoda and a space cowboy go on adventures together.

Boba Fett, on the other hand, would have been immediately polarizing. Whether the solo movie required extensive knowledge of the franchise or not, it would be perceived by the public as a very Star Wars-y story. That alone would make it very hard for the movie to find a wide audience, especially with the public perception of the franchise being as bad as it was in the wake of The Rise of Skywalker. Even if the Boba Fett movie was the best Star Wars movie ever made, Disney and Lucasfilm would have had a hard time getting people to see it.

The Mandalorian also had much more room for growth than Boba Fett did. Din was a new character that could go in any direction, he was part of a new, mysterious, and intriguing sect of Mandalorians that wasn't beholden to established lore, and he was in a standalone story. Boba Fett, on the other hand, had a set personality, a set backstory, and very little intrigue left. There's simply no way Boba Fett's movie would have been as important to Star Wars as The Mandalorian was.

Despite its storied past, it's no secret that The Mandalorian has been struggling in recent years. The Mandalorian season 3 is universally viewed as the worst installment in the show. There's still no word on The Mandalorian season 4 after three years and running, and The Mandalorian and Grogu lost tens of millions of dollars at the box office. Star Wars itself also seems to be leaving Din and Grogu behind to shift its attention back to the sequel era.

The struggles The Mandalorian has endured since 2023 obviously aren't great, but they also don't detract from the show's overall impact on Star Wars at large. As previously mentioned, Star Wars would be a fraction of the franchise it currently is without this show. Nearly a dozen Star Wars shows wouldn't have been made, the fanbase would be even more divided than it already is, and Disney and Lucasfilm would likely still be trying to pivot away from the sequel trilogy.

The Mandalorian is, by a wide margin, the single most important Star Wars show ever made. Nothing can take away the magic of those first two seasons or the impact they had on the franchise at large. That's why it was for the best that the Boba Fett solo movie never got made. Without it, The Mandalorian got its chance to change Star Wars forever and for the better.

14 Everyday Home Items You Need to Replace More Often Than You Think

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

These overlooked swaps can make your home cleaner, safer, and fresher.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Your home might be a showcase of timeless design, with stylish accessories, custom artwork, and high-end furniture. But when the everyday things that keep a household functioning are left unattended to for long enough, the space may be less clean and organized (and safe!) than it looks.

“Even the most gorgeous spaces lose their charm if we forget to update essentials,” says Linda Hayslett, principal designer at LH.Designs. “Keeping your home looking and feeling fresh isn’t just about design—it’s also about the little things.”

Caring for your home means looking beyond the interiors. Giving neglected areas the attention they need can keep germs and grime at bay, and replacing certain items can also protect your health and safety. Consider your carbon monoxide alarm: If an aging unit fails during an emergency, the consequences could be deadly.

To help you keep everything in good shape, we asked experts which 14 household items you should replace as soon as possible. Take a look—you can thank us later!

Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors lose their sensitivity over time, which could delay detection during an emergency. The timeframe for when you should replace these will vary by manufacturer. Look up the model you have online to be sure—even a fairly new alarm should be tested once a month. The batteries should be replaced once a year.

Mold and mildew can build up on shower liners, especially in damp environments. A 2024 study by SafeHome.org showed that shower curtains had the most germs of any other surfaces in the bathroom, including the toilet seat. While Hayslett recommends washing them every three months, the material can eventually become brittle over time.

Dingy towels are the antithesis of luxe. Towels lose their absorbency and softness after years of washing.

“Avoid using too much detergent, which can build up on fibers,” Hayslett says. “Use a little vinegar during the rinse cycle to keep towels soft.” If your bath towels feel scratchy or start to smell musty despite washing, it’s time for a refresh.

To prolong the life of your towels, make sure you let them dry properly between uses to prevent mildew growth, noted Vyanca Soto of Market Studio Interiors. And you should definitely swap them out if you notice discoloration from your skincare products.

You likely spend plenty of time in your kitchen and bathroom each day. Make sure you take a few minutes to examine the grout lines around your bathtub and kitchen countertops.

“Grout breaks down over time, and you can end up with water damage to the drywall,” says Rebekah Zaveloff, cofounder and creative director at Imparfait Design Studio. “If you look and there are spots that have pulled away or deteriorated, it’s time to regrout.”

A sagging mattress is a clear sign it’s time for a new one. Mattresses lose support and can accumulate dust mites, allergens, and even mold, which is certainly not a dreamy way to slumber.

“Flip your mattress every six months [unless it's a one-sided design] to help maintain its structure and extend its lifespan,” Hayslette advised.

A mattress protector can extend its lifespan as does regular vacuuming to remove dust and debris. Soto recently bought a bed vacuum cleaner and says, “It works like a charm.” She also uses it on her dog’s bed to help it stay clean and smell fresher.

The lifespan of a pillow mainly depends on its material, whether that be foam, feathers, or another type of synthetic fill. But once a pillow of any kind loses its shape, it's time to say goodbye. Like mattresses, pillows can harbor dust mites, dead skin cells, and sweat over time. You can prolong a pillow's life with a pillow protector—Soto likes the Serta Power Chill—but you should still swap those out every year or so.

When to replace: two to three years for sheets; five to 10 years for duvets

Bed linen care and personal hygiene dictate the condition of your linens. Similar to pillows, sheets and duvets can accumulate dust mites, allergens, and bacteria, especially if you allow kids or pets into your bed.

“Over time, duvets lose their fluffiness and insulating properties due to regular use and washing,” says Rebecca Bobroff of Rebecca Bobroff Design.

If you’re prone to night sweats or use a lot of skin-care products, you may notice that your sheets become quickly discolored. Along with regular washing, you may want to stick to white sheets you can bleach over colors, which will look dingy even when they're clean.

“At a minimum, wash pillowcases once a week,” says Danielle Hurhula, head of home merchandising for Quince. “I’d recommend bumping washes up to twice a week if you suffer from allergies or asthma, sleep with hair or skincare products, or have sensitive or acne-prone skin.”

HVAC maintenance experts, including Energy Star, recommend checking filters monthly and replacing air filters every three months to help your system function properly and keep dust and allergens out of your home.

If you remove your filters and they look extra dirty, you may want to up the frequency. Doing so will not only improve indoor quality but keep your HVAC system from overworking and wasting energy, which could shave a few years off its life.

Water filters can accumulate bacteria, minerals, and other contaminants. This can affect the taste and quality of your drinking water.

“The lifespan of a filter cartridge is influenced by the level of contaminants and particulates in your water,” says Eric Roy, Ph.D., founder and chief scientist at Hydroviv. “Sink filter cartridges should be replaced approximately every six months and are designed to handle up to 720 gallons of water.”

You should also watch for a decrease in the flow rate, which indicates the filter is saturated.

When to replace: five years (or as recommended by the manufacturer)

You likely know that fridge water filters should be replaced every six months, but water lines can build up gunk or start leaking over time.

“Inspect the line for cracks or discoloration every year,” Soto suggests. “Flush it with vinegar and water every six months to prevent mineral buildup and ensure clean, clear water. My mom taught me this and when you see the color of the water when you flush it—you'll never stop doing it.”

Using sharp knives on wooden and plastic cutting boards can create grooves that embed bacteria, even after a dishwasher cycle. One study showed that plastic surfaces have the longest bacteria survival time, and scraping wooden boards with a knife significantly reduced bacterial presence.

Soto disinfects cutting boards with a vinegar solution and uses bleach for plastic boards. “Sand down wooden boards occasionally to remove deep grooves and re-oil them to maintain the wood,” she says.

Sponges harbor bacteria, especially when you use them to clean up raw food such as eggs and meat. In fact, salmonella can thrive on a sponge for more than seven days. Hayslette’s tip: Microwave damp sponges for one minute to sanitize between uses, but toss them after two weeks max.

Keep sponges as dry as possible, or better yet, replace them with dish brushes as these are less likely to breed bacteria, according to the International Journal of Food Microbiology. We like the Curio dish brush because it stands upright on a magnetic pedestal and you can replace the brush heads.

When to replace: three to five years (or sooner if scratched)

The nonstick coating on your cookware can wear off or become damaged over time. This decreases a pan's efficacy and also makes it a health hazard—the coating may start to flake off into your food.

“To extend their life, always use nonmetal utensils and hand-wash your pans instead of placing them in the dishwasher,” Hayslette says.

Not only do latex anti-slip rug pads lose cushioning over time, but they can also start to disintegrate.

“They get tacky and sticky on the floor, which can damage the floor's surface,” Zaveloff explains. Pull up your rug once in a while and if you see that the rug pads are turning dark yellow, start shopping for new ones.
