‘He Needs To Be Layered’: Bond Girl Léa Seydoux Shares the Most Important Traits for the Next 007

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What it takes to play James Bond, from the actor who’s played his most influential love interest.

If there’s one Bond girl who has influenced the course of 007 history more than anyone, it’s Dr. Madeleine Swann. Played by French actor Léa Seydoux, who is this month’s ELLE UK cover star, psychotherapist Dr. Swann disrupts the James Bond narrative in a way that no previous lover has done: She challenges his misogyny.

Appearing in the last two films in the series—Spectre (2015) and No Time to Die (2021)—Dr. Swann has a long-term relationship with secret agent 007, eventually telling him that he’s the father of her daughter, making Seydoux’s part in the legendary franchise unique.

Asked whether she would consider a return to the franchise, now under the creative direction of Amazon MGM Studios and without Daniel Craig as 007, the actor said: “I really loved working with Barbara Broccoli and her brother Michael [G. Wilson]. It was one of the best experiences, a pure delight. And I don’t know, it will be different.”

She has already worked with Denis Villeneuve, the director attached to the next Bond film, in Dune: Part Two. “He’s a great choice, let’s see. I’m curious how they will reinvent it. But for me, it will never be the same because it was Barbara’s father who initiated the Bond movies. Now, I don’t know,” she told ELLE. “It became American….I hope they will keep the British spirit.”

As for casting Seydoux in the role, former Bond producer Barbara Broccoli said that her scenes in No Time to Die still move her to tears. “When we were filming the goodbye scene at the Italian train station, Léa approached it with such subtlety—she grasped her stomach to express her pain, but also the secret heartbreak that she was carrying James’s child. It moved me to tears then and continues to move me each time I watch it.”

As for the question of who will play Bond, she won’t be drawn into the speculation. (This was before Robert Pattinson was announced to take on the mantle on November 16.) Seydoux admits that finding the right person to replace Daniel Craig is difficult. “He needs to be layered, you know? And that is something that’s hard to find in young actors.”

Léa Seydoux appears next in Gentle Monster, which begins streaming on Netflix on November 18.

HIV Treatment Is Prevention

Posted November 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

HIV treatment, antiretroviral therapy (ART), has seen dramatic advancement in the past four decades. HIV is now a chronic condition. When people take their ART as prescribed and maintain an undetectable viral load and a high T-cell count, life expectancy for those living with HIV approximates that of those who are uninfected (Trickey et al., 2023).

In addition to the direct health benefits of maintaining an undetectable viral load, there is an additional prevention benefit. When someone living with HIV has an undetectable viral load, they are unable to transmit HIV through sex (Eisinger et al., 2019). Public health campaigns promoting ART adherence have called attention to this phenomenon, terming it Undetectable = Untransmissible or U=U.

We might assume that prevention messaging that draws on U=U would be most effective among people living with HIV who are partnered with someone who is uninfected. Having an undetectable viral load would decrease or eliminate the potential for sexual transmission of HIV to their partner. Alternatively, people living with HIV may be generally motivated to take their ART and maintain an undetectable viral load when they are having sex with partners who may be uninfected regardless of relationship status.

In two recent studies (Hillesheim & Starks, 2026; Starks et al., 2024), our team examined relationship status group differences in ART adherence and viral detectability in large samples of sexual minority male adults.

Ordinarily, we expect people in relationships to have better health outcomes compared to single people (Kiecolt-Glaser & Wilson, 2017). In both of these papers, findings suggested this was true for sexual minority men living with HIV in non-monogamous relationships and those whose partners were uninfected. These groups were more likely to be adherent to ART and report an undetectable viral load compared to single sexual minority men. Presumably, these are groups where men may be most likely to consider preventing transmission of HIV to sexual partners.

There was also a warning sign in both papers. Sexual minority men in relationships where both partners were living with HIV were significantly less likely to be adherent to their ART and achieve an undetectable viral load. For these men, prevention motivation may be of limited relevance. It may be important to emphasize the personal health benefits of HIV treatment to maximize HIV outcomes in these subgroups.

Effective HIV treatment not only prolongs the lives of people living with HIV, but it also eliminates the potential for sexual transmission. This prevention motivation may explain why rates of ART adherence and achieving an undetectable viral load are higher among sexual minority men in non-monogamous relationships and those with uninfected partners. We may need different messages, emphasizing the personal health benefits of HIV care, to reach subgroups who have low levels of sexual HIV prevention motivation.

6 Superhero Movies From the 2000s That Are 10/10 Masterpieces

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The 2000s were huge years for superhero cinema—arguably the biggest for the landscape of all time. It was in this decade that superhero movies, as audiences know them today, were defined, and the standards were set. The years 2000 to 2009 are some of the most significant years and helped birth some of the most popular franchises in cinematic history as a whole.

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man series, the X-Men franchise, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight series and so much more all got their big breaks in this era. Without these abundantly important 10 years, superhero movies would not even be close to where they are today. Their significance wouldn't even be close to what they are, and events like Avengers: Endgame most likely would not have ever happened or had the success they did without these foundational projects that are, to simply state, masterpieces.

Spider-Man is the most profitable superhero on the planet—he has been for quite some time now—and that meant that no one was surprised that, when the technology was finally there, he'd get a live-action movie. Come 2002, fans were finally treated to Sam Raimi's Spider-Man, which seriously rocked the world. Sure, X-Men came out just a few years earlier, but Spider-Man really helped drive home and fully prove that comic book characters could work on the big screen and the Hollywood landscape.

This 2002 flick was an all-time smash hit and proved that superhero movies could be genuinely amazing financial investments for big studios, with the project grossing an astounding about $825 million worldwide, with a phenomenal $114.8 million opening weekend. It was the first film to ever cross $100 million in its opening weekend and if that's not proof of how great this movie is, then what could be? The performances across the board from especially the likes of Willem Dafoe are wonderful, the directing from Raimi shows how unashamed he was in the source material, and the CGI work was revolutionary for the times.

The first Blade movie was a big moment for comic book movies, being one of the first ones to ever be rated-R and be a success. So, when it came time for the sequel, Blade II, the stakes were high. Thankfully, legendary director Guillermo del Toro and leading man Wesley Snipes, delivered a project that was a great success. It turned the franchise from what the franchise was in Blade to a high-speed, thrilling, gothic, and all-around fire flick.

Guillermo del Toro continues to be a masterclass of a director here with his emphasis on practical effects, making the more horror-based elements of the project sell better than they ever could in CGI. Snipes brings his everything to the role—as per usual—and keeps Blade feeling like a character that the audience genuinely wants to keep watching.

The first X-Men movie was incredibly important. It established these amazing characters and began to send a great message about the effects of discrimination and bigotry. Mutants became the talk of the town, and Hugh Jackman stepped into the boots of Wolverine for the very first time. However, it's highly agreed upon that the sequel, X2: X-Men United, is the superior film for a plethora of reasons.

This is, without a doubt, the Star Wars: Episode V — The Empire Strikes Back for the X-Men franchise. The stakes are raised significantly, and the themes and messaging are more prominent than ever before. It has one of the best opening scenes in a superhero movie—Nightcrawler (Alan Cumming) infiltrating the White House to attack the President (Cotter Smith), brings more classic X-Men characters to the forefront, has a great villain in William Stryker (Brian Cox), and so much more.

2008 was arguably the biggest year in superhero cinema for quite some time because of two major movies: The Dark Knight and none other than the beginning of the MCU itself, Iron Man. Brought to life by the amazing Jon Favreau in the director's chair and Robert Downey Jr. as the Armored Avenger, this 2008 project birthed one of the most popular and profitable cinematic franchises ever put to screen—one that still runs in massive success to this very day.

This served not only as a major comeback for Downey Jr.'s career, but was a huge step in CGI for filmmaking, as well. Iron Man looks perfect in pretty much every shot, and is still looked back on to have better-looking CGI than some modern Marvel films. The biggest standout, however, is the performance from Downey Jr. He solidifies himself at Tony Stark and continued to play him for over 10 years after the release of Iron Man, now even returning to the MCU again in 2026—albeit as a new character, Doctor Doom. This movie is the majority reason the superhero movie landscape has become what it is today.

Four people, four completely different ideas of what it means to have somebody in your corner. Here is who picked up, what they would ask of you, and what you would get back.

You want the friend who does not leave. Steve is the one who turns up at the door without being asked, who remembers what you said you were worried about, and who has never once made you audition for his attention.

You want the friend who is genuinely exhausting and completely worth it. Tony will forget your birthday, then build you something at four in the morning that you did not know you needed, and hand it over as though it were nothing.

You want the friend who replies in four seconds and apologises for it. Peter is earnest in a way that is almost unfashionable, and he will take on far more than he can carry rather than admit that he is struggling.

You want the friend who insults your shoes, fixes your phone and then quietly rearranges your entire life for the better. Shuri does not do soft reassurance, and that is the reason you would believe her when she says you are fine.

The other half of the two major super-films from 2008 is the unforgettable The Dark Knight by super-director Christopher Nolan. This project took a far more gritty and grounded take on the character of Batman (Christian Bale), and set a new style for superhero movies. Even the Spider-Man franchise took a note from this movie's book, with The Amazing Spider-Man also taking a grounded take—which everyone believes is because of the success of The Dark Knight.

Much like Iron Man, The Dark Knight's biggest highlight is within a masterful performance . . . that of Heath Ledger as the Joker. This performance has gone on to be one of the most impactful, defining, and memorable performance in cinematic history as a whole. The Dark Knight is genuinely one of the most impactful superhero movies ever made.

There's no question that when it comes to superhero films that are masterpieces, Spider-Man 2 must enter the conversation. Raimi's big sequel is genuinely known as one of the best movie sequels period. It takes everything that was good about Spider-Man, enhances those things, and adds to them as well. This feels like a borderline perfect superhero movie, and is still considered one to this day. It's so beloved and astounding that it's fair to say it won't be forgotten for quite some time.

With great acting from almost every cast member, iconic action (who could forget the train scene?), great visuals (both in cinematography and visual effects), a heartfelt-yet-heartbreaking screenplay, and a once-again mesmerizing score from Danny Elfman, Spider-Man 2 deserves all the praise it's gotten over the decades. What's so interesting and well-done about Spider-Man 2 is the fact that before anything, it's a Peter Parker (Tobey Maguire) movie—not a Spider-Man movie. The heart and soul of the story lies within Peter and his struggle between his two identities, and it isn't solely fueled by the external conflict—which is more of what Spider-Man did a few years prior. This allows the second entry in this franchise to feel more like a character study before anything else.

Knit Health raises $35m from NEA for Med-BEAM, a world model for healthcare

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Knit Health’s Med-BEAM predicts the care patients need next from billions of clinical decisions. Knit says it was up to four times more accurate and 250 times cheaper than frontier AI models.

Knit Health’s co-founders, from left: Jonas Knecht, co-CTO; Jonathan Kolstad, CEO; Maya Petersen, chief scientist; and Ted Robertson, COO.

Knit Health, a startup building an AI model that predicts what care patients will need next, has raised a $35m Series A led by NEA. Town Hall Ventures, SignalFire and existing investors joined the round, Knit Health said in a statement on Monday.

Chief executive Jonathan Kolstad first disclosed the round to Will Maddox at Axios Pro. The money will fund the rollout of Med-BEAM across health systems and other healthcare groups. Knit calls the model a world model for healthcare.

Scientists from the University of California, Berkeley founded Knit in 2025. It has raised $46.6m so far. Earlier seed and pre-seed backers include Frist Cressey Ventures, Uncork Capital, Moxxie Ventures, Coalition Operators and The Aligned Fund.

According to Knit, much of healthcare’s key information never makes it into notes. It sits in the orders, referrals and diagnoses that clinicians make, and in what happens next. That makes it hard for large language models to tell where a patient is heading, the company said.

Med-BEAM learns from billions of real healthcare decisions and hundreds of millions of patient records that follow people over time. It predicts the care a patient is likely to need and shows how different treatments could change their course.

Knit said it trains only on data it has clear, documented rights to use. All of that data is fully de-identified before it reaches the company. Med-BEAM checks its predictions against real clinical outcomes. That lets it flag when there is too little evidence to be confident.

“Healthcare still operates too often on what amounts to chance: which doctor you see, which day you arrive, which hospital you enter,” Kolstad said in the statement.

Knit compared Med-BEAM with a group of models from frontier AI labs. Each had to predict the clinical actions for hospitalised patients over the next 24 hours. Knit picked no outcome in advance.

Med-BEAM was up to four times more accurate, 100 times faster and 250 times cheaper than the models tested, Knit said. The company added that its model’s confidence matched real outcomes more closely.

Blake Wu, a partner at NEA, said one model could power “dozens of critical workflows”. He listed care management, patient navigation, clinical trial recruitment, specialist scheduling, hospital-at-home care and treatment-response prediction.

Jordan Belfort’s new AI voice startup, Tone, wants to sell like the ‘Wolf of Wall Street’

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Years ago, as questions mounted about WeWork’s soaring valuation, I sat down with co-founder Adam Neumann. We were negotiating a stage interview, and he asked to meet me twice in the weeks leading up to it, once at a WeWork in San Francisco and again at his office in New York.

I knew full well that the numbers that SoftBank in particular had assigned to WeWork made little sense, but I came away understanding why investors wanted to throw in their lot with him.

It’s similarly easy to understand why people are enchanted by Jordan Belfort, the self-proclaimed “Wolf of Wall Street.” Belfort famously pleaded guilty in 1999 to securities fraud and money laundering tied to Stratton Oakmont, the Long Island brokerage where salespeople pushed penny stocks in pump-and-dump schemes while Belfort acquired a Southampton mansion, a 167-foot yacht, and at least one helicopter. (He destroyed the latter two while high on Quaaludes and cocaine, as depicted in Martin Scorsese’s famous adaptation of Belfort’s autobiography, “The Wolf of Wall Street.”)

You might think someone who spent 22 months in prison and was ordered to pay $110.4 million in restitution to 1,513 victims wouldn’t get another shake. The victims ultimately received “far less” than they were owed, TMZ reported in July, noting that Belfort completed his court-ordered restitution obligations in late April of this year.

But Belfort, whose energy has a galvanizing quality, keeps resurfacing, including in recent years as a Miami-based sales consultant and one-time host of crypto workshops at his Miami Beach home. (A New York Times reporter attended a weekend workshop several years ago where the price of admission was one bitcoin or its equivalent value at the time of $40,000.)

Now, he is moving into the intersection of what he knows best — sales — and the hottest trend on the planet, AI, building out a voice-based AI platform. The secret sauce is that it imbues its voices with the kind of tonality that wins deals, he says, for example, by phrasing declarative statements as questions (which “builds engagement”) and even whispering at times (which “implies scarcity of information”).

Belfort says the platform, called Tone, is already being used by companies that have more sales leads than their human teams can handle. Now, in a move that could also help expand its training data, Tone is launching a self-service platform for content creators and authors who want to turn their books into “expressive AI-generated narration.” Users can select from Tone’s AI voices or, as a voice-cloning feature rolls out, create a clone of their own voice.

It’s easy to argue the market is already saturated with AI voice startups and technologies, and Tone comes with added risk: Belfort’s co-founder in the business is longtime investor Shervin Pishevar, who has a controversial past of his own. We also can’t independently verify that the technology works as described (neither co-founder is an engineer).

But if my time with Neumann taught me anything, it’s that doubts about the numbers rarely get in the way of a good pitch. When Belfort looks for investors to back him in this new venture, finding believers won’t be too hard.

Here is part of our conversation, edited for length and clarity.What have you built?

It’s a very unique way of going about building a voice, and it stems from a system of influence and persuasion I’ve been teaching since the late 1980s. It’s not just what you say, but how you say it. When you’re not in person, body language collapses into tonality. There are about 15 very powerful tonalities that people use all the time. The phrase I invented for this was “goal-oriented communication.” If I’m trying to be perceived as an expert, I apply tonality strategically.

It started that way. As a non-engineer, I went to first principles: How would I teach a human to do this? One tonality at a time. I have 30 years of super high-quality data , and I started teaching the model. It was a breakthrough moment, because it not only sounded exactly like me, it had nailed the tonality. Then I taught it a second one, and then how to link them. The key to all of this is data. Even the littlest bit of corrupt data dramatically negates the progress you’ve made.

How did you and Shervin Pishevar come together?

Shervin and I have been personal friends for a long time, and we were out for dinner one night. I had been pitched by an AI company that said it had an AI that could do selling. I listened to it. It was awful. But it put the idea in my head. I thought I was just going to teach models how to sell, but I couldn’t find a voice that was good enough. Necessity is the mother of invention, so we built our own.

Who are your customers, and how do they use it?

We have law firms and health insurance companies. We do a lot in polling and automobiles. It will take you through an entire health insurance sale. It asks qualifying questions, like what type of insurance you want, your budget, your ages. Then it chooses a plan and explains the features and benefits. Our latency is like 600 milliseconds. If the payment is too high, it’ll say it can bring the payment down and explain how to raise your deductible. If you don’t like the plan, it swaps you to a different one. It does literally the whole entire sale.

How does the AI handle a sales call that goes off script?

It never says “That’s a great question,” unless it really is. I hate that. It’s so ingratiating. In any sale there’s a happy path, how we’d want the perfect conversation to go, and every last word can be mapped out. But people have questions and concerns, and they take you off the straight line. So the trick is a model that can carry that perfect conversation, handle anything thrown at it, answer elegantly and return to the straight line, without breaking rapport and without hallucinating — or, as a salesperson, lying. We’ve trained the model to do that.

Have you generated any revenue from Tone yet? Have you raised funding for it?

Revenue, probably about a million bucks — $700,000, maybe. We’ve raised friends and family and seed, about $10 million.

I want to address the elephant in the room. You have a fraud conviction. Pishevar has faced misconduct allegations that he denies. Why should a bank or a hospital or a consumer brand trust this product coming from the two of you?

All I can say for myself is that I did make mistakes. It was a very, very long time ago, and I’ve spent the last 20-plus years teaching people all over the world. Where is it used most effectively? Low-dollar sales opportunities? I’d think the more money someone is spending, the more human contact they’d demand.

I didn’t. Believe it or not, I don’t watch or read anything about me, good or bad. I never read a comment. When you’re well-known, you have to live by who you are inside. I go by my own internal compass.

'Tokenization Is Not the Finish Line': Jupiter to Let Users Borrow Against Bullish Shares

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

'Tokenization Is Not the Finish Line': Jupiter to Let Users Borrow Against Bullish Shares

Jupiter, the largest trading platform on Solana, is bringing Bullish's shares onchain as collateral, as part of a push to put tokenized assets to work.

The company will launch a dedicated, permissioned market on its lending platform, Jupiter Lend, starting with Bullish's NYSE-listed BLSH stock, Jupiter tweeted on Monday. More issuer tokens will follow, though the market won't be available in all jurisdictions, it added.

A NYSE-listed company is bringing its own shares directly to Jupiter Lend.

Today at Breakpoint, we announced our partnership with @Bullish to launch a dedicated, permissioned $BLSH market.

For the first time, Issuer Tokens can be used as collateral inside Solana’s… pic.twitter.com/rWWqwOcWmK

— Jupiter (@JupiterExchange) November 16, 2026

Unlike many tokenized stocks, which give holders a claim on a share, Bullish's issuer-sponsored token "is itself the share," Jupiter COO Kash Dhanda said in a presentation at Solana's Breakpoint conference in London.

Users will be able to borrow against them with fully onchain liquidations in an "institutional-grade lending market," and integrations with qualified custodians, he said. Bullish acquired Equiniti, one of the world's largest transfer agents, earlier this year, Dhanda noted.

Dhanda framed the announcements around what he called the "post-tokenization era." Most tokenized assets today are "parked, not productive," he said.

Of the roughly $460 billion in tokenized assets tracked by data platform RWA.xyz, only about $39.5 billion is actually onchain and can be moved off the issuer's platform, and just $3.4 billion is used in DeFi, according to DeFiLlama, Dhanda said, a "utilization gap" of 99%.

"There is this myth, and this is the thing that's misunderstood: 'If you put it on chain, they will come'," he said. "This is not the field of dreams," he added, arguing that, "Tokenization is not the finish line. It is actually the starting line."

Jupiter launched two other products alongside the Bullish market. The Jupiter Global Passport lets users verify their identity to buy permissioned assets, while keeping their information as private as possible using zero-knowledge data sharing, Dhanda said. It is live with KYC verification, with onchain credit scores and proof of wealth to follow, and the company is exploring licenses in different jurisdictions.

Jupiter Liquidity Services, meanwhile, will help asset issuers design their markets, seed initial capital, find market makers and integrate with Jupiter's routing so that their tokens are available across the platforms that use it. "You integrate once, you will be available everywhere," Dhanda said.

"Shallow credit markets don't drive liquidity on chain," he added. "If you cannot do more with your onchain asset than you can do with the off-chain version of it, you're probably not going to switch."

Jupiter says it has handled more than $500 billion in trading volume so far this year and paid out $162 million in yield. The Breakpoint announcements follow the company's launch last week of token baskets, yield vaults and a rebuilt portfolio, and come as other venues bring tokenized stocks to Solana. Trading platform Formation has notified the SEC of plans to trade tokenized U.S. stocks on the network.

Numa, a San Francisco startup that sells AI software to car dealerships, has raised a $45M Series C, the company announced on Monday. Donald Trump greeting Jensen Huang in September this year

It has created a crisis for one of the world's most valuable firms, which is a key part of the AI industry and its huge expansion.

But now fellow tech bosses, including SpaceX's Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, chief executive of Fortnite-maker Epic Games, have backed a campaign to get Nvidia's board to "Bring Jensen Back".

The pair were among more than 175,000 signatories of an open letter calling for the board to reinstate Huang while an ongoing internal probe is conducted.

President Trump gave his views on Huang's future on Monday, saying he "would certainly not mind" seeing the former boss given his job back.

"I guess maybe one lesson is more important than another, but I would certainly not mind seeing that," he told reporters as he left the White House.

The president's comments come after the "Stand with Jensen" campaign group, which claims to be made up of current and former Nvidia employees, partners, shareholders and supporters, demanded the return of the former chief executive.

They expressed concerns the ex-Nvidia boss "was removed without meaningful due process".

Huang had been expected to weather the storm following the revelation of an affair involving Alice Huang, alleged to be a Chinese spy.

However, he resigned last Thursday after a second extra-marital relationship emerged, allegedly with an executive at Nvidia who reported to him.

Robert Pattinson's James Bond Casting Continues An Unfortunate Trend For Nicholas Hoult

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert Pattinson is officially the man of the moment. 2026 was already his year, of course, considering his acclaimed turns in everything from A24's "The Drama" to Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" to "Primetime" — with still another big appearance to come in December with "Dune: Part 3." That's on top of reprising his role as Bruce Wayne/Batman in Matt Reeves' "The Batman Part II" and, as of today, his official casting announcement as James Bond. But can we take a moment to pour one out for the equally-as-talented character actor doomed to be the runner-up far more often than he deserves? We're talking, of course, about Nicholas Hoult.

We've sort of been dreading this exact outcome the moment we first heard about the various finalists up for the world-famous MI6 agent. In all fairness, neither Nicholas Hoult nor his agent (nor his bank account) should shed any tears based on his career trajectory to this point. He's made an impression in a number of smaller roles over the years (check out "The Order" from 2024 for great example of that), all while working with some of the best directors around. He's even waded into the blockbuster arena a few times, first in the 20th Century Fox "X-Men" movies and most recently as Lex Luthor in James Gunn's DC Universe.

But, despite all that success, we have to point out how the "Mad Max: Fury Road" scene-stealer continues to get the short end of the stick. Adding insult to injury, this isn't even the first time he's watched Pattinson specifically land the coveted role he was eyeing. So here's to Nicholas Hoult, the perpetual underdog who'll always be a star in our hearts.

"Always the bridesmaid, never the bride" is probably too harsh to describe what's going on with Nicholas Hoult lately but, man, it reaches a point. Despite proving himself to have one heck of a screen presence throughout the last several years, whether it be as an agent of chaos in Yorgos Lanthimos' "The Favourite" in 2018 or as a leading man in 2019's "Tolkien" biopic or even the horror-heavy "Nosferatu," Hoult has clearly been aching for the unambiguous breakout role that would make him a household name among general audiences. With all due respect to his meme-factory performances in "The Menu" or "Superman," well, neither of those have quite cut it. (Although that one photoshoot for "The Great" alongside Elle Fanning still leaves rent-free in our minds. You know the one.)

Sadly, that trend continues with the James Bond announcement. Real ones know it all started when Hoult was in contention for the title role in Matt Reeves' "The Batman," reportedly the only other name considered along with Robert Pattinson. But wait, it gets worse. After losing out on that crown jewel for superheroes, Hoult managed to find himself in the running for Superman a few short years later for James Gunn's reboot — which he ended up falling just short of, as well, though he picked up the role of supervillain Lex Luthor in a fun bit of meta casting. Third time isn't the charm, however, as he's now once again looking up to Pattinson as our newest Bond. How much is one man supposed to endure???

We love you, Nicholas Hoult, even if Hollywood's casting directors apparently have it out for you.
