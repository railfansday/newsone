Pope Leo urges Argentina's Milei to put people before the economy

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pope Leo XIV has urged Argentina to protect democracy and ensure economic growth benefits the poor, delivering a message to President Javier Milei over austerity, social welfare and political divisions.

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Argentina on Sunday, urging to protect democracy and ensure economic growth benefits the poor.

The pope's remarks were widely watched for criticism of President Javier Milei's harsh austerity programs and cuts affecting social services.

While Leo did not address the government's policies directly, he said "the economy must be at the service of the people, rather than people being at the service of the economy."

Talking to Milei and other government officials, he pointed to the plight of those who, "despite hard work, nonetheless struggle to meet their household's needs."

With regards to democracy, he said it was "part of Argentina's heritage that needs to be safeguarded."

The visit to Buenos Aires marks the second leg of the pope's South America tour and is the first papal visit to Argentina in nearly 40 years.

The 71-year old pontiff, who underwent surgery for lung cancer less than a month ago, arrived from Uruguay and is scheduled to visit several cities in the Catholic-majority country before continuing to Peru on Wednesday.

Milei's policies have in the past drawn strong criticism from Argentina's Catholic Church, particularly cuts affecting social services, pensions, disability assistance and public universities.

The Argentine leader, a close ally of US President Donald Trump, has defended his aggressive spending cuts as necessary to stabilize the country's ailing economy and curb inflation.

Annual inflation has fallen from more than 200% to approximately 33% under his administration, but households continue to face financial pressure, and there's been anger over public-sector layoffs and business closures.

In the past, he agressively dismissed criticsm from the Catholic Church, going as far as calling Leo's predecessor, Pope Francis, an "imbecile." He had later though softened his tone and met with Francis in Rome.

On Sunday, Milei struck a significantly more conciliatory note, praising Francis as an "exceptional figure" and the "most important Argentine in history."

Pope Leo also stressed the need to safeguard democracy in the country.

"Those who exercise authority have a duty to act with prudence, to acknowledge the limits of the power entrusted to them, and to direct their decisions toward the good of the entire community."

The Pope's words carry particular significance ahead of Argentina's presidential election in 2027, in which Milei has said he will seek another term.

The president's aggressive rhetoric and frequent attacks on journalists and political opponents are widely seen as part of an increasing political polarization in the country.

Leo told Milei that strengthening democracy required a "willingness to listen" and the "careful use of words."

His visit marks the first time a pope has traveled to Argentina since John Paul II's trip in 1987. It also brings renewed attention to the legacy of Francis, the country's first pope, who died in April 2025 without returning to his homeland during his 12-year papacy.

How to use your laptop as a second monitor

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Converting the device is not as easy as simply plugging in an HDMI cord, but here's what does work.

Whether you're gaming, working or simply browsing the web, adding a second monitor is one of the best ways to upgrade your workflow. Mirroring the display, which shows the same desktop on both screens, can be handy for presentations, while extending the display gives you one wider desktop. Extending the desktop into additional screen space means more visible tabs, easier multitasking and increased productivity. But making this change does not necessarily require buying a fancy setup. If you have a spare laptop lying around at home, you can make a few small tweaks to use dual monitors without going all-in on new hardware.

Converting your laptop into a second screen is fairly straightforward but not necessarily as simple as plugging in an HDMI cable. First you need to check your computer's compatibility, consider wired versus wireless options and tweak some settings on your system to smoothly extend or mirror the display. So how, exactly, do you turn your laptop into a second monitor? Here are the steps for Windows, Mac and Linux, as well as wired options via HDMI connections.

If both your primary computer and the spare laptop you want to use as a second monitor are running Windows 10 or 11, extending or mirroring them is quite simple due to the operating systems' built-in wireless casting tools. These rely on Miracast, a functionality that enables connections between devices via Wi-Fi and requires only a quick workflow to set up.

To activate it, go to your spare laptop, open Settings, then System, then Projecting to this PC. Click Optional features, then View features, then select Wireless Display to add it. Once it installs, return to the same page and set it to Available everywhere, and open the Wireless Display app.

Now, you're ready to mirror or extend your display. On your main computer, press the Windows key + K, select the spare laptop from the list of available displays, enter the PIN and allow keyboard and mouse input. You can then choose to duplicate your screen or extend it, and you can switch anytime with Windows key + P. Keep in mind, this method of connecting your devices does require Wi-Fi on both machines, and you may experience some lag. Therefore, while it's great for presentations or organizing large numbers of tabs, it may not be the best choice for gaming.

If both of your devices are Macs, you can use AirPlay to connect the two, but only if the MacBook is from 2018 or later. Once you've confirmed your device is compatible, go to the MacBook you want to use as the second screen, open System Settings, select General, then AirDrop & Continuity. Turn on AirPlay Receiver and then under Allow AirPlay for, select Current User. Make sure both MacBooks are signed into the same Apple ID to ensure a smooth connection. On your primary device, open System Settings, then Displays and click the plus sign from the dropdown menu to select your secondary device from the list. Decide if you want mirroring or extended display, which can be selected under settings. Like Windows' Miracast, Apple's AirPlay works over Wi-Fi and can thus have a slight delay. For a wired option with less lag, see the HDMI section below.

If connecting between two different operating systems, whether between a Windows PC and a MacBook or anything involving Linux, you will need software like Deskreen, a free, open-source tool that runs on Wi-Fi. To connect, make sure both computers are on the same Wi-Fi network, and download Deskreen on the main device. Deskreen will offer a QR code and URL. Open this on the spare laptop's browser to connect the devices. Deskreen mirrors the display by default. To extend to the spare laptop, you will need a cheap virtual display adaptor, or a "dummy plug," in your main computer's HDMI port. Like the other systems, any software that connects devices over Wi-Fi will have some lag, so consider the wired option below for gaming or other activities that rely on smoother connections.

Most laptop HDMI ports are video-out only, so plugging two devices together with a cord will not directly allow you to extend or mirror displays. To work around this, get an HDMI-to-USB video capture card, which can be plugged into both laptops. Plug your main device's HDMI cable into the card, then plug the card's USB end into the spare laptop. The capture card will function as a webcam, so you can view the feed in your laptop's camera app or a free video app like OBS Studio, which runs on Windows, Mac and Linux.

If you are gaming and want to play a console without a TV, you can connect a PlayStation or a Nintendo Switch to the laptop in the same way. Again, there may be a small amount of lag but usually less than Wi-Fi-based connections, and it's unlikely to trip you up unless you are a competitive gamer. If none of these uses appeal to you, you can always use your old laptop to make a smart home hub.

The Chilean filmmaker, who passed away on Friday aged 52, leaves behind a diverse but cohesive body of work including 'Gloria,' 'The Wonder' and the Oscar-winning 'A Fantastic Woman.'

When it was announced, in 2017, that the Chilean writer-director Sébastian Lelio was to helm a U.S. remake of his 2013 breakout film “Gloria,” admirers of the original — and indeed of Lelio himself — had cause for concern. English-language do-overs of world cinema successes frequently go awry; furthermore, we had the cautionary tale of Dutchman George Sluizer, who directed a hopeless 1993 remake of his own impeccable 1988 chiller “The Vanishing,” to remind us that even having the original filmmaker in charge was no guarantee against a travesty. The news came at a crucial moment in Lelio’s career. The first of his two films to premiere in 2017, the vibrant, heartfelt transgender character study “A Fantastic Woman,” was riding a wave of festival and critical success that began with a Best Screenplay win at the Berlinale, and culminated with an Oscar for Best International Feature (or Best Foreign Language Film, as the award was still called then) — the first ever win for a Chilean film in the category, sealing a resurgence of the country’s cinema also being led by such contemporaries as Sebastián Silva and Lelio’s own producer Pablo Larraín.

The second, “Disobedience,” was Lelio’s English-language debut — another story of queer identity, but an altogether different tonal and stylistic proposition. A sober portrayal of an illicit romance between two women from North London’s strict Orthodox Jewish community, it was sensitively observed and beautifully acted by leads Rachel Weisz and Rachel McAdams, with a restraint that was occasionally countered by moments of frank, charged sensuality. The contrast between “A Fantastic Woman” and “Disobedience” confirmed Lelio as a particularly empathetic and versatile teller of women’s stories — and “Gloria,” a warm and unsentimentally joyous comedy of a fiftysomething divorcée’s reawakening, was also good one. But why go back, when his career was barreling forward?

As it turned out, Lelio knew better than his doubters. The remake, which eventually premiered in 2019 as “Gloria Bell,” hewed close to the template of the original, largely inheriting its pleasures and poignancies. But if that makes it sound redundant, it proved a canny and fascinating exercise in performer-as-auteur influence: It was clear that Lelio, self-evidently a champion of women on screen, was interested to see how a wholly different actress would change the tone and temperature of his original story.

A lot, we learned. Paulina García was a funny and resilient force of nature in the 2013 original; in “Gloria Bell,” Julianne Moore brought a yearning, lonely fragility to the material that gave even the film’s uplifting trajectory an undertow of melancholy. The remake got less than its due — Moore, at the very least, deserved some awards attention that year — but it sits beside “Gloria” as an illuminating companion piece rather than a competing interpretation.

I lead with the two Glorias because their respective successes improbably prove a truth about Lelio, a restlessly curious and compassionate filmmaker who passed away, far too soon, on Friday, aged just 52: Even when ostensibly making the same film twice, he never made the same film twice.

His 2005 debut “The Sacred Family” was confident and a bit scrappy, its handheld jaggedness far removed from his later polish, its droll comic tale of a bourgeois man falling for his son’s girlfriend also distinct from the searching feminist portraiture that would define his later career. But it was lively and promising, made in a semi-improvised fashion that he carried forward into his next, more complex film, the troubled youth study “Navidad,” which made its way into the 2009 Cannes Directors’ Fortnight program. But his third film, 2011’s “The Year of the Tiger,” was a considerably more sophisticated effort: an exquisitely understated exploration of faith, guilt and trauma in the wake of Chile’s devastating 2010 earthquake.

“The Year of the Tiger,” which competed at Locarno, was too soulful and richly considered a work on its own terms to be seen as a bid for arthouse-major status — but that’s how it worked out anyway, even if the film didn’t get quite the global exposure it deserved. But by the time “Gloria” came along two years later, the word on Lelio was out and expectations were high: The film was selected for the main competition at Berlin and was an immediate, ebulliently received crowdpleaser. If Larraín had established himself as the dark prince of new Chilean cinema with spiky, perverse, politically bristling works like “Post Mortem” and “No,” Lelio was poised to become its great humanist: his touch light but not soft, his perspective probing but not abrasive.

“A Fantastic Woman,” four years later, delivered on that promise. Propelled by trans star Daniela Vega’s extraordinary, emotionally unguarded performance, its story of a grieving trans woman spurned by the prejudiced family of her dead lover arrived at a vital tipping point for the visibility of transgender narratives and artists in popular culture, and delicately essayed an arc of emphatic, even triumphant self-definition amid a climate of isolation and rejection — all without feeling glibly or opportunistically issue-driven. It was to Lelio’s credit that he was fairly self-effacing in his promotion of the film, letting Vega — with whom he had closely developed the protagonist, aligned with her experience — be its chief ambassador and spokesperson.

“A Fantastic Woman” earned Lelio the most acclaim and kudos of his career — though his finest film as a director, to my mind, was comparatively shortchanged. A period drama set in 19th-century Ireland and adapted from a novel by “Room” author Emma Donoghue, “The Wonder” didn’t outwardly look or feel much like a Lelio joint — with its austerely repressed milieu shot, by Ari Wegner, with such a damp, chilly verdigris finish you could practically sense moss growing on the screen as you watched. Yet its story of feminine resolve and dueling moral and spiritual convictions meshed well with the director’s interests, as did Florence Pugh’s tenacious, stout-hearted performance; the film’s earthy folktale trappings stood in exciting contrast to its stark formalist construction.

If “The Wonder” was an ambitious and grandly realized reach, I was less sold on the huge stylistic swing of Lelio’s most recent film, last year’s political musical “The Wave,” which returned him to his homeland. Inspired by a national spate of #MeToo-related feminist student protests that shut down universities across Chile in 2018, it was rhetorically heavy-handed but composed and choreographed with dynamic aplomb, positively thrilling to the spectacle of massed bodies in motion: I wrote in my review that it made one eager to see Lelio tackle more musical fare on screen, never thinking that the film would be his last.

In one of “The Wave’s” more playful flourishes, the fourth wall is broken to acknowledge Lelio’s own presence as the director of the movie — and to ask, pointedly, what a male director was doing on this expressly feminist project anyway. It’s a wink-wink joke that more seriously encapsulated the generous, conscientious, community-minded approach that Lelio brought to his filmmaking: His auteur signature was subtly distinctive, yet he never made himself the star of his work.
