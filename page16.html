How much real power monarchs still hold in 20 countries

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Countries where monarchs hold real power include Gulf absolute monarchies and European principalities, and this guide to 20 nations compares them with limited and ceremonial thrones

A crown can mean a seat at the head of the cabinet table or a place on a postage stamp. In many of the world's remaining monarchies, the second description fits better. The king or queen opens parliament, signs laws drafted by others and stays out of party politics. A smaller group of rulers still governs in fact. They appoint ministers, veto legislation and dissolve elected assemblies.

This guide covers 20 monarchies, from countries where monarchs hold real power to several where the throne's authority is limited or purely symbolic.

At one end sit the absolute monarchies. In Saudi Arabia, the king serves as both head of state and prime minister. The Quran and the Sunna act as the country's constitution. Oman's sultan also holds the prime minister's post. Brunei's sultan has ruled since 1967 and personally holds the finance and defense portfolios. Eswatini's King Mswati III presides over a system in which political parties cannot contest elections. Vatican City is an absolute monarchy of a different kind. Its pope is elected and holds legislative, executive and judicial authority.

The middle is more varied. Qatar's emir holds wide executive authority under a 2004 constitution. In the United Arab Emirates, the hereditary rulers of seven emirates choose the federal president. Jordan, Bahrain, Kuwait and Morocco have constitutions and elected chambers, but their monarchs keep levers that elected officials lack. Those levers include appointing prime ministers or cabinets, naming members of an upper house and dissolving parliament. Thailand's king reigns under a constitution and is shielded by a strict lese-majeste law. Tonga's king still formally appoints the prime minister. In Europe, Liechtenstein's prince can veto laws and dismiss the government, powers that voters confirmed and widened in a 2003 referendum. Monaco's prince shares lawmaking with an elected council. Malaysia rotates its king among nine hereditary state rulers. The officeholder appoints as prime minister the lawmaker who appears to command a majority.

The far end of the spectrum holds the contrast cases. Cambodia, Bhutan and Luxembourg each keep a monarch whose formal role is limited, with day-to-day governing handled by elected leaders. Japan's emperor is defined as a symbol of the state and has no governing power under the 1947 constitution.

The countries here are a selection, not a ranking. Each slide describes one monarch's constitutional or practical role.

Saudi Arabia is an absolute monarchy in which the king is head of state and the final authority over government, law and the courts. The Basic Law of Governance, issued in 1992, sets out rule by the Al Saud family and names the Quran and the Sunna as the constitution.

The Basic Law is not a constitution in the Western sense. King Fahd issued it by royal decree. No elected assembly drafted it, and no voters approved it. Its first article declares that the kingdom's constitution is the Quran, Islam's holy book, together with the Sunna, the recorded practice of the Prophet Muhammad. The document itself works as a framework for organizing the state under those texts.

Royal authority runs through every branch. The Basic Law names three state authorities — judicial, executive and regulatory — and makes the king the point of reference for all of them. It also makes the king president of the Council of Ministers, the body that acts as the cabinet. New laws take effect through royal decrees rather than votes in a parliament.

Succession stays within one family. The Basic Law limits the throne to the sons of the kingdom's founder, Abdulaziz ibn Saud, and their descendants. Abdulaziz proclaimed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932. Every king since his death has been one of his sons. In 2006, King Abdullah created the Allegiance Council, a body of senior princes, to take part in choosing future crown princes.

Saudi Arabia has no elected national legislature and no political parties. The Consultative Assembly, known as the Shura Council, studies proposed laws and offers advice. The king appoints its members, and its role is advisory. Voters elect a share of the seats on municipal councils, a practice that began in 2005. Those councils handle local services rather than national policy.

The king also carries a religious title. Since King Fahd adopted it in 1986, Saudi monarchs have used the title Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques. The title refers to the mosques in Mecca and Medina, Islam's two holiest sites. Millions of Muslims travel to those cities each year for the Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages.

Oman is an absolute monarchy, and Sultan Haitham bin Tariq sits at its top as both head of state and prime minister. He has ruled since January 2020, when he succeeded Sultan Qaboos bin Said after Qaboos died.

The country's written framework is the Basic Statute of the State, first issued by royal decree in 1996. It works as Oman's constitution, but it places final authority with the sultan rather than with any elected body.

The 2020 transfer of power showed how that framework operates. Qaboos had no children and never publicly named an heir. The Basic Statute gave the Ruling Family Council a set window to choose a successor. If the family could not agree, a sealed letter written by Qaboos would decide the matter. The council chose to open the letter, and it named Haitham, a cousin of the late sultan.

Before taking the throne, Haitham served as Oman's minister of heritage and culture. He also chaired the committee drafting Oman Vision 2040, the country's long-term economic plan.

His predecessor set the template for the job. Qaboos took power in 1970 by removing his father, Sultan Said bin Taimur, and ruled for almost 50 years. During that time he kept key portfolios under his own control, including defense, foreign affairs and finance.

Haitham has adjusted parts of that arrangement without giving up control. In August 2020, he appointed separate ministers to run foreign affairs and finance. In January 2021, he issued a revised Basic Statute that created the post of crown prince. His eldest son, Theyazin bin Haitham, was named to it. That gave Oman a fixed line of succession it lacked under Qaboos.

The sultan governs by royal decree, and those decrees carry the force of law. He commands the armed forces and appoints the cabinet. Ministers serve at his discretion.

Oman also has a two-chamber body called the Council of Oman. Citizens elect the members of the Majlis al-Shura, while the sultan appoints the members of the State Council. Neither chamber can override him, and political parties are not permitted.

King Mswati III has ruled Eswatini since 1986. He holds supreme executive authority in a system where political parties cannot compete for power. The small southern African kingdom is widely described as Africa's last absolute monarchy.

Mswati was 18 when he was crowned. He succeeded his father, King Sobhuza II, who died in 1982 after a reign that began in 1899. Between Sobhuza's death and Mswati's coronation, regents governed the country for about four years.

The king appoints the prime minister and the cabinet. He also names most members of the Senate and some members of the House of Assembly. Legislation needs his assent to become law. Elected lawmakers can debate and pass bills, but the final decision on national policy rests with the throne.

The country's political structure is known as the tinkhundla system. Candidates for the House of Assembly stand as individuals in local constituencies called tinkhundla. They do not run as representatives of parties. The arrangement traces back to a 1973 decree by Sobhuza II. That decree suspended the constitution adopted at independence and banned political parties.

A new constitution took effect in 2006. It describes the tinkhundla system as resting on individual merit as the basis for election to public office. It does not provide a route for parties to put forward candidates or form a government.

Royal authority also reaches beyond the formal machinery of the state. Much of the country's land is classed as Swazi Nation Land. The king holds it in trust, and chiefs allocate it to families. The queen mother, who carries the title Indlovukazi, holds a formal position alongside the king in Swazi tradition.

The monarchy sits at the center of national ceremonies. Incwala is a kingship ritual held each year. Umhlanga, the reed dance, draws large numbers of young women to the royal residence.

In 2018, the king announced that Swaziland would be known as Eswatini. The change marked 50 years of independence from the U.K. Eswatini is landlocked and borders South Africa and Mozambique. Mbabane serves as the administrative capital. Lobamba is the royal and legislative capital.

Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah has ruled Brunei since 1967 and leads its government in person. Besides serving as head of state, he is prime minister, defense minister and finance minister.

That arrangement places the main levers of the state in one office. In many monarchies, the king appoints a prime minister who then directs the cabinet. In Brunei, the sultan fills that post himself. Choices on public spending, national security and policy direction rest with the person who sits on the throne.

Hassanal Bolkiah became sultan in October 1967. His father, Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddien III, abdicated in his favor. A formal coronation followed in 1968. Brunei was a British protectorate at the time. It became fully independent from the U.K. on January 1, 1984. The sultan took the prime minister's post at independence.

The legal framework for his authority dates to Brunei's 1959 constitution. That document grants the sultan supreme executive authority. After an armed revolt in December 1962, a state of emergency was declared. The government has renewed it every two years since then. Emergency rule allows the sultan to govern by decree.

Brunei does have a Legislative Council. Its members are appointed by the sultan rather than chosen by voters. The council was suspended in 1984 and reconvened in 2004. The country has held no national legislative election since 1962.

The sultan's role also extends into religion. He is the head of Islam in Brunei. The national ideology is Melayu Islam Beraja, which translates as Malay Islamic Monarchy. It ties national identity to Malay culture, the Islamic faith and the throne.

That religious authority has shaped the law. Brunei began phasing in a Sharia penal code in 2014. Its later stages took effect in 2019. The code was introduced under the sultan's authority, without a vote by an elected body.

Oil and natural gas revenue funds much of the state. Brunei levies no personal income tax on its citizens. The government also provides free public education and subsidized health care.

Qatar's emir, Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, holds wide executive authority under the permanent constitution the country adopted in 2004. The constitution leaves the main levers of government in his hands.

Tamim took power in June 2013, when his father, Sheikh Hamad bin Khalifa Al Thani, abdicated in his favor. Hamad had come to power himself in 1995. He replaced his own father, Sheikh Khalifa bin Hamad Al Thani, while Khalifa was out of the country.

The constitution makes rule hereditary within the Al Thani family. Succession passes through male descendants, and the emir names an heir apparent from within the family. The Al Thanis have led Qatar since the 19th century. Their rule predates the end of the country's treaty relationship with Britain, which came with full independence in 1971.

The emir's executive powers are broad. He appoints the prime minister and the cabinet ministers, and he can dismiss them. He is commander-in-chief of the armed forces. He represents the state at home and abroad. He also sets its general policy with the help of the Council of Ministers.

Lawmaking runs through the emir as well. The Shura Council reviews draft laws and the state budget. The emir ratifies laws and can return a bill to the council for reconsideration. The constitution also lets him issue decree-laws in urgent cases when the council is not in session. He can dissolve the council by decree.

Islam is the state religion under the constitution. Islamic law, or Sharia, is named as a main source of legislation. Qatar has no political parties.

That structure separates Qatar from parliamentary monarchies such as the U.K., where an elected House of Commons determines who forms the government. In Qatar, the prime minister and ministers are jointly responsible to the emir. The ruler, not a legislative majority, decides who governs.

The 2004 charter replaced a provisional constitution that Qatar had used since 1970. The permanent document set out the state's institutions in more detail. It kept final executive and legislative authority with the emir.

The United Arab Emirates chooses its head of state through the Federal Supreme Council, a body made up of the hereditary rulers of its seven emirates. The council elects the president from among its own members, and every president since the federation formed in 1971 has come from Abu Dhabi's ruling family.

The seven emirates are Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Umm Al Quwain, Fujairah and Ras Al Khaimah. Six of them joined the federation in December 1971. Ras Al Khaimah followed in early 1972.

Each ruler inherits power in his own emirate. Together, the seven sit as the Federal Supreme Council, which the constitution names as the country's highest federal authority. The council sets general policy, ratifies federal laws and elects both the president and the vice president.

The presidential term is five years and can be renewed. Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, ruler of Abu Dhabi, served as the first president until his death in 2004. His son, Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan, succeeded him. After Sheikh Khalifa died in 2022, the council elected his half-brother, Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan.

The voting rules give the two largest emirates extra weight. Substantive council decisions need the approval of five of the seven rulers. That majority must include the rulers of Abu Dhabi and Dubai. As a result, either of those two rulers can block a major federal decision.

Dubai's ruler has long held the posts of vice president and prime minister. Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum has filled both roles since 2006.

The country also has a Federal National Council with 40 seats. Half of its members are elected by a limited electorate whose members are selected by the rulers. The other half are appointed. The chamber reviews draft laws and debates policy. Its role is advisory, and it cannot override the rulers.

The federal system leaves significant power at the emirate level. Each emirate controls its own natural resources, including oil, and runs much of its own local government. Abu Dhabi holds most of the country's oil reserves. The city of Abu Dhabi also serves as the federal capital.

Kuwait pairs an elected legislature that holds real oversight powers with a hereditary emir who can shut that legislature down. The emir has the constitutional power to dissolve the National Assembly by decree, and he also appoints the prime minister who leads the government.

The framework dates to the constitution of 1962, adopted the year after Kuwait gained independence from Britain. The document defines the state as a hereditary emirate. Succession is limited to the descendants of Mubarak Al Sabah, who ruled Kuwait from 1896 to 1915.

The National Assembly has 50 members chosen by voters. Cabinet ministers who were not elected also sit in the chamber as ex officio members. That arrangement gives the government a block of votes inside parliament without contesting a single seat.

Kuwaiti lawmakers hold tools that most other Gulf assemblies lack. They can question ministers in formal sessions known as interpellations. They can also vote no confidence in an individual minister, which forces that minister out of office.

The prime minister is treated differently. Parliament cannot vote him out directly. Instead, the Assembly can declare that it is unable to cooperate with the head of government.

That declaration hands the decision to the emir. Under the constitution, he can then either dismiss the prime minister or dissolve the Assembly and call new elections. The ruler, not parliament, settles the dispute.

Dissolution carries constitutional conditions. The decree must state its reasons, and the same grounds cannot be used for a second dissolution. Elections for a new Assembly must follow within two months. If they do not take place, the dissolved chamber regains its full powers until a new one meets.

The Assembly also has a say in the line of succession. The emir nominates a crown prince from among the eligible descendants of Mubarak Al Sabah. That nomination requires approval by a majority of the Assembly's members.

Political parties are not legally recognized. Candidates run as individuals, though organized blocs still form inside the chamber. Women won the right to vote and stand for office in 2005. Every Kuwaiti prime minister has come from the Al Sabah family.

Bahrain's King Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa heads a constitutional monarchy in which he appoints the cabinet and every member of the upper house of parliament. Voters elect only one of the legislature's two chambers.

King Hamad came to power in 1999 as emir, succeeding his father, Sheikh Isa bin Salman Al Khalifa. In 2002, he proclaimed Bahrain a kingdom and took the title of king. That same year, a new constitution set up the current two-chamber National Assembly.

The legislature is split evenly. The Council of Representatives has 40 members chosen in national elections. The Shura Council, or consultative council, also has 40 members. The king names all of them by royal decree. A bill must pass both chambers and then receive the king's ratification before it becomes law.

The arrangement grew out of the National Action Charter, a reform blueprint that Bahraini voters approved in a 2001 referendum. The charter promised a return to parliamentary life. Bahrain's earlier elected assembly first met in 1973 and was dissolved in 1975. The first elections for the new Council of Representatives took place in 2002.

Executive power runs through the throne. The king appoints and dismisses the prime minister and the other ministers. He also serves as supreme commander of the Bahrain Defence Force. The constitution gives him the power to dissolve the elected Council of Representatives.

Members of the royal family have long led the cabinet. Khalifa bin Salman Al Khalifa, the king's uncle, served as prime minister from independence in 1971 until his death in 2020. Crown Prince Salman bin Hamad Al Khalifa, the king's eldest son, then took the post.

The Al Khalifa family has ruled Bahrain since the late 18th century. It is a Sunni dynasty governing a country with a large Shia population. That divide has long featured in Bahraini politics.

The divide came to the surface in 2011. Protesters gathered at the Pearl Roundabout in Manama to demand political reform. In March of that year, Gulf Cooperation Council forces led by Saudi Arabia entered Bahrain at the Bahraini government's request.

King Abdullah II of Jordan appoints and dismisses the prime minister and can dissolve the elected lower house of parliament. Those powers come from the 1952 constitution. It has been amended several times, but it still places the monarch at the center of government.

Jordan's legislature is the National Assembly, which has two chambers. Voters elect the House of Representatives. The king appoints every member of the Senate. The constitution caps the Senate at half the size of the elected house. That arrangement gives the palace a direct hand in choosing one of the two chambers that pass laws.

Legislation needs royal approval. Parliament can override a royal objection, but only with a two-thirds vote in both chambers. The constitution also names the king supreme commander of the armed forces.

The cabinet answers to both the king and parliament. The House of Representatives can bring down a government through a vote of no confidence. The king, however, decides who forms the next one. Jordanian monarchs have changed prime ministers frequently. A new government is a common royal response to economic or political pressure.

Abdullah II became king in February 1999, after the death of his father, King Hussein. Hussein had ruled since 1952. The Hashemite family has led the territory since Abdullah I established the Emirate of Transjordan in 1921. Jordan gained independence from Britain in 1946. Abdullah II named his eldest son, Hussein, crown prince in 2009.

The constitution has changed during Abdullah II's reign. Amendments in 2011 followed protests across the Arab world. They created a Constitutional Court and an independent commission to run elections. Further amendments in 2016 moved in the other direction on some appointments. They let the king name the crown prince, the army chief and the head of the General Intelligence Directorate. None of those appointments now needs a countersignature from the prime minister.

The monarchy also carries religious weight. The Hashemites trace their lineage to the Prophet Muhammad. Jordan's special role at Muslim holy shrines in Jerusalem is recognized in its 1994 peace treaty with Israel. That custodial role gives the throne a regional dimension beyond its domestic powers.

King Mohammed VI is Morocco's head of state and its highest religious authority. He has reigned since 1999. His title, Commander of the Faithful, gives the throne religious standing alongside its executive powers.

Mohammed VI took the throne in July 1999 after the death of his father, King Hassan II, who had ruled since 1961. Both belong to the Alaouite dynasty, which has governed Morocco since the 17th century. The family traces its lineage to the Prophet Muhammad. That claim underpins the monarchy's religious role.

The title Commander of the Faithful, or Amir al-Mu'minin in Arabic, is written into Morocco's constitution. It makes the king the guardian of Islam in the country. He presides over the Superior Council of Ulemas, the body that issues official religious opinions.

The current framework dates from a constitution approved by referendum in July 2011. It followed street protests that spring, part of the wider wave of demonstrations across North Africa and the Middle East. The new text expanded the role of the elected government and parliament.

Under the 2011 constitution, the king must appoint the head of government from the party that wins the most seats in the House of Representatives. That rule narrowed his choice over who leads the cabinet. He still appoints ministers on the head of government's proposal and can dismiss them.

The king keeps several major powers. He chairs the Council of Ministers, which deliberates on major policy and draft laws. He is supreme commander of the Royal Armed Forces. He can dissolve either house of parliament, or both, after required consultations. He also presides over the council that oversees the judiciary.

Parliament has two chambers. The House of Representatives is directly elected, while the House of Councillors is chosen indirectly. Elected governments handle day-to-day administration. Security, defense and religious affairs remain closely tied to the palace.

Some of the main social reforms of Mohammed VI's reign began with the throne. The king announced a revision of the family code, known as the Moudawana, in a 2003 speech to parliament. The new code took effect in 2004. It raised the minimum marriage age for women to 18 and placed new limits on polygamy.

Thailand is a constitutional monarchy in which King Maha Vajiralongkorn holds a legally protected status. Article 112 of the Criminal Code, the country's lese-majeste law, punishes insults to the king with prison terms. Vajiralongkorn, also known as Rama X $TWTR, has reigned since 2016.

He succeeded his father, King Bhumibol Adulyadej, who died in October 2016 after a reign of 70 years. Bhumibol was the longest-reigning living monarch in the world at the time of his death. Vajiralongkorn's formal coronation took place in May 2019. He is the 10th king of the Chakri dynasty, which has held the Thai throne since 1782.

Thai kings have not ruled as absolute monarchs since 1932. That year, a group of civilian and military officials known as the People's Party carried out a revolution that ended absolute rule. It also gave the country its first constitution. Thailand has since adopted many constitutions, often after military coups. The current charter dates from 2017.

The constitution places the monarch above ordinary politics. It describes the king as enthroned in a position of revered worship. It also bars anyone from exposing him to any accusation or legal action. On paper, the king exercises legislative power through parliament, executive power through the cabinet and judicial power through the courts.

Royal authority also shows up in appointments and formal approvals. The king chooses the members of the Privy Council, an advisory body of up to 18 members plus a president. Bills need royal assent before they become law. Most royal acts on state affairs must be countersigned by a minister. That requirement links the crown's formal decisions to the cabinet.

Article 112 is the clearest legal expression of the monarchy's standing. It makes it a crime to defame, insult or threaten the king, the queen, the heir apparent or the regent. Each offense carries a prison sentence of three to 15 years.

The law differs from ordinary defamation rules in two practical ways. Any person can file a complaint, not only someone directly involved. Courts can also impose a sentence for each separate count. A defendant facing several charges can therefore receive a very long combined term.

Calls to amend or repeal Article 112 have been a recurring issue in Thai politics and protest movements. Together, the constitutional protections and the criminal law make open criticism of the king a legal risk inside Thailand.

Liechtenstein's reigning prince can veto any law and dismiss the government. Voters widened those powers in a 2003 referendum. Few other monarchs in Europe hold comparable authority.

Liechtenstein sits between Switzerland and Austria, with Vaduz as its capital. It has about 40,000 residents. The principality was created in 1719, and the House of Liechtenstein has supplied its head of state ever since.

The constitution divides power between the prince and the people. The Landtag, a 25-member parliament, passes legislation. No bill becomes law without the prince's sanction. That veto also reaches measures approved by voters in a referendum. The prince can dismiss the government and dissolve the Landtag.

The 2003 vote grew out of a long dispute between Prince Hans-Adam II and parliament over who held the final word. The prince proposed constitutional amendments that confirmed and extended his authority. He also said the princely family might leave the country if voters turned the package down. A clear majority approved it in March 2003.

The Venice Commission, the Council of Europe's advisory body on constitutional law, raised concerns before the vote. It warned that the changes could move Liechtenstein away from European democratic standards. The amendments took effect after the referendum passed.

The reform also added checks that run in the other direction. Citizens can bring a motion of no confidence in the prince through a popular initiative. They can also start a process to abolish the monarchy by referendum.

In 2004, Hans-Adam II handed day-to-day duties to his son, Hereditary Prince Alois, who acts as regent. Hans-Adam II kept the title of reigning prince.

The veto became a public issue in 2011. Before a referendum on legalizing abortion, Hereditary Prince Alois said he would veto legalization if voters approved it. Voters rejected the proposal.

A citizens' initiative followed in 2012. It proposed stripping the prince of the power to block referendum results. Voters rejected it, and the veto stayed in the constitution.

Prince Albert II of Monaco holds executive power and shares legislative power with the elected National Council. Under the principality's 1962 constitution, the monarch is a working part of government, not a figurehead.

Albert II became sovereign prince in April 2005 after the death of his father, Prince Rainier III, who had reigned since 1949. The House of Grimaldi has been tied to Monaco since 1297. That year, François Grimaldi seized the fortress on the Rock. The constitution makes succession to the throne hereditary within the Grimaldi line.

Monaco adopted its constitution in December 1962 and revised it in 2002. The text states that legislative power is exercised jointly by the prince and the National Council. In practice, the government drafts most bills in the prince's name. The council then debates them and votes. A bill cannot become law without the council's approval. It also cannot take effect without the prince's sanction.

The National Council has 24 members elected by Monegasque citizens for five-year terms. The 2002 revision strengthened its role, including its ability to put forward its own legislative proposals. The council also votes on the state budget. That vote gives elected members a check on public spending.

Executive authority belongs to the prince. He exercises it through a government led by the minister of state, whom he appoints. The minister of state heads a small Council of Government. That official answers to the prince rather than to the National Council. France has historically had a role in choosing the minister of state. That role reflects the close treaty relationship between the two countries.

Judicial power is also linked to the throne. The prince delegates it to the courts, which deliver justice in his name. He can also dissolve the National Council after consulting the Crown Council, an advisory body. New elections must then be held.

The result pairs an elected legislature with a hereditary ruler who still directs the executive. Monaco covers roughly two square kilometers. Its monarch retains more formal authority than most European royals, whose roles are largely ceremonial.

Tonga is a constitutional monarchy in the South Pacific where King Tupou VI formally appoints the prime minister. Since democratic reforms in 2010, the Legislative Assembly selects the candidate, but the king keeps powers that go beyond ceremony.

The king's full name is ʻAhoʻeitu ʻUnuakiʻotonga Tukuʻaho Tupou VI. He took the throne in March 2012 after the death of his older brother, King George Tupou V. Both were sons of King Tāufaʻāhau Tupou IV, who reigned from 1965 to 2006. The new king's formal coronation followed in July 2015 in the capital, Nukuʻalofa.

He came to the throne with direct experience of government. Before becoming king, he served as Tonga's prime minister from 2000 to 2006, under the title Prince ʻUlukalala Lavaka Ata.

Tonga's political system rests on a constitution adopted in 1875 under King George Tupou I. He founded the current royal line after unifying the islands in the 19th century. The kingdom was never formally colonized. It spent 1900 to 1970 as a British protected state before regaining full control of its foreign affairs.

For most of its modern history, power sat with the monarch and a small hereditary nobility. Commoners elected only a minority of the Legislative Assembly. The king chose the cabinet. Pressure for change grew in the 2000s. In November 2006, pro-democracy protests in Nukuʻalofa turned into riots that destroyed much of the capital's central business district.

The 2010 reforms followed. They gave voters a majority of the seats in the Legislative Assembly. A block of seats remains reserved for representatives chosen by the nobles. The assembly now picks the prime minister, whom the king then appoints.

Those changes were limited in scope. The king retains the authority to dissolve the Legislative Assembly, along with other powers set out in the constitution. That combination places Tonga between the Pacific's parliamentary democracies and the world's absolute monarchies.

Malaysia's king, the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, is elected for a five-year term by the Conference of Rulers from among the nine hereditary rulers of the Malay states. The king has real discretion over who becomes prime minister.

The arrangement dates to independence in 1957, when the Federation of Malaya brought several royal houses under one federal crown. The constitution did not raise a single dynasty above the others. It let the state rulers take turns on the national throne instead.

The nine rulers come from Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Perak, Perlis, Selangor and Terengganu. Their titles differ. Seven are sultans, Perlis has a raja, and Negeri Sembilan's ruler is styled Yang di-Pertuan Besar. Penang, Malacca, Sabah and Sarawak have appointed governors rather than royals. Those governors do not vote for the king.

The election takes place by secret ballot within the Conference of Rulers. In practice, the throne has moved through the royal states in a broadly settled order. The same body also chooses a deputy king, who stands in when the monarch is abroad or unable to serve.

Sultan Ibrahim of Johor became Yang di-Pertuan Agong in January 2024. He is the 17th person to hold the office.

Most of the king's formal duties are carried out on the advice of the cabinet. The constitution sets aside a few areas of personal judgment. Under Article 43, the king appoints a prime minister from the Dewan Rakyat, the lower house. The appointee must be the member who, in the king's judgment, is likely to command the confidence of a majority.

That choice carries the most weight when an election or a party split leaves no clear winner. In those situations, the king can consult party leaders and lawmakers before deciding whom to appoint.

The king may also withhold consent when a prime minister asks for parliament to be dissolved. That discretion, set out in Article 40, means an early election is not automatic.

Other royal roles carry less political weight. The king is supreme commander of the armed forces. He is head of Islam in his own state, in the federal territories and in Penang, Malacca, Sabah and Sarawak. He also grants pardons for offenses in the federal territories, acting with a pardons board.

The constitution limits the king's hold over legislation. Under Article 66, a bill passed by parliament becomes law within 30 days even if royal assent is not given. A 1993 constitutional amendment also removed the rulers' personal legal immunity. It created a special court to hear cases involving the rulers.

Cambodia's King Norodom Sihamoni holds a limited, largely symbolic role, and day-to-day political power rests with the prime minister. Article 7 of the 1993 constitution states that the king shall reign but shall not govern.

Sihamoni was born in 1953 and has reigned since 2004. He took the throne after his father, King Norodom Sihanouk, abdicated. Before becoming king, he spent much of his adult life abroad. He taught classical dance in Paris and served as Cambodia's ambassador to UNESCO.

Cambodia's crown does not pass automatically from parent to child. A body called the Royal Council of the Throne selects each monarch from descendants of three earlier kings: Ang Duong, Norodom and Sisowath. Candidates must be at least 30 years old. The council includes the prime minister, leaders of the legislature and the heads of the country's two main Buddhist orders.

The monarchy itself has not been continuous. It was abolished after a 1970 coup. It stayed abolished through the Khmer Rouge period and the Vietnamese-backed government that followed. It returned in 1993, when a new constitution restored Sihanouk to the throne.

Under that constitution, the king's duties are mostly formal. He serves as head of state and holds the title of supreme commander of the armed forces. He signs the royal decree appointing the prime minister and cabinet after the National Assembly approves them. He can also grant pardons and amnesties.

Those powers operate inside a system run by elected officials. Hun Sen held the prime minister's post, at times sharing it, from 1985 until 2023. His son, Hun Manet, then became prime minister. The king has no independent authority to choose the head of government or to set national policy.

That places Cambodia near the ceremonial end of the range of monarchies. The constitution describes the king as a symbol of the unity and eternity of the nation. Buddhism is the state religion under the same document. Executive authority belongs to the Council of Ministers, which answers to the National Assembly.

Bhutan's King Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck has reigned since 2006 under a system that has been parliamentary since 2008. An elected prime minister and cabinet run the government. The king's formal powers are limited, but he keeps several defined constitutional levers.

The king's title is Druk Gyalpo, usually translated as Dragon King. He is the fifth ruler of the Wangchuck dynasty, which has held the throne since 1907. His father, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, abdicated in his favor in December 2006. The formal coronation followed in November 2008.

Bhutan's move to democracy came from the throne itself. The fourth king pressed for a written constitution and set out a path to multiparty elections. Bhutan held its first National Assembly elections in March 2008. The Druk Phuensum Tshogpa party won 45 of the 47 seats. Jigme Thinley became the country's first elected prime minister. The constitution was formally adopted in July 2008.

Under that framework, the king does not chair the cabinet or direct government policy. The constitution still gives him specific roles in lawmaking. When parliament passes a bill, the king can return it with amendments or objections. If the two houses then pass it again in a joint sitting, he must grant assent. That gives the throne a power to delay legislation, not to block it outright.

The king also shapes the makeup of the upper house. He appoints five of the 25 members of the National Council. The other 20 are elected, one from each of the country's districts. He also appoints senior judges and the heads of constitutional bodies through procedures the constitution sets out.

The same document places limits on the monarch himself. It requires the king to step down at age 65. It also lays out a process by which parliament can move to force an abdication. The final decision in that process goes to voters in a national referendum.

The constitution defines the Druk Gyalpo as head of state and the symbol of unity of the kingdom and its people. It also writes Gross National Happiness, an idea associated with the fourth king, into the document as a goal of state policy.

Vatican City is an absolute elective monarchy, and the pope is its sovereign. He holds full legislative, executive and judicial authority over the state, with no parliament or independent court able to overrule him.

The Fundamental Law of Vatican City State sets out this arrangement in its first article. That text names the Supreme Pontiff as sovereign of the state. Pope Francis issued the current version in 2023, replacing a text from 2000.

In daily practice, the pope delegates much of his authority. The Pontifical Commission for Vatican City State, made up of cardinals he appoints, handles legislation. The commission's president also heads the Governorate, which runs the state's administration. Vatican courts judge cases in the pope's name. The pope can still reserve any matter for himself.

The monarchy is elective rather than hereditary. When a pope dies or resigns, the College of Cardinals gathers in a conclave inside the Sistine Chapel. Only cardinals under the age of 80 may vote. Pope Paul VI introduced that age limit in 1970. A candidate needs a two-thirds majority of the voting cardinals to win.

A pope normally reigns for life. Resignation is possible but rare. Benedict XVI stepped down in 2013. He was the first pope to resign since Gregory XII in 1415.

The state itself is small. It covers about 44 hectares inside Rome, which makes it the smallest independent country in the world by area. It became a sovereign state under the Lateran Treaty of 1929, signed with Italy. That agreement settled a dispute left open since Italian forces took Rome in 1870.

Vatican City is distinct from the Holy See, though the pope leads both. The Holy See is the central government of the Roman Catholic Church. It conducts diplomacy and exchanges ambassadors with other countries. It also holds permanent observer status at the United Nations. Vatican City is the territory that secures the Holy See's independence.

Security follows the same sovereign structure. The Pontifical Swiss Guard, founded in 1506, protects the pope personally. The Vatican Gendarmerie handles policing within the state.

The pope holds two separate roles. He is the spiritual leader of a global church. He is also the temporal ruler of a small state, and that civil authority applies only within the Vatican's own territory.

Luxembourg's grand duke is a head of state with a limited constitutional role. That role became smaller after a 2008 dispute over euthanasia. A constitutional change completed in 2009 meant laws passed by parliament no longer needed the monarch's personal approval to take effect.

Luxembourg is the only grand duchy left in the world. It is a parliamentary democracy. Day-to-day government rests with a prime minister and cabinet who answer to the Chamber of Deputies, a single-chamber parliament of 60 elected members.

Grand Duke $DUK Henri began his reign in 2000, succeeding his father, Grand Duke Jean. Under the constitution as it stood then, the grand duke had to sanction each law, a formal act of approval. For decades that step was treated as routine.

The routine broke in 2008. Lawmakers backed a bill legalizing euthanasia and assisted suicide under strict conditions. Henri, a Catholic, made clear that his conscience would not let him approve it.

His position created a constitutional problem. A monarch who withheld approval could, in principle, block a law that elected lawmakers had passed. That would hand an unelected head of state a direct say over legislation.

Luxembourg's political leaders chose not to force the grand duke to act against his stated beliefs. They changed the constitution instead. The amendment ended the requirement that laws receive the grand duke's sanction before taking effect. The euthanasia law then came into force in 2009.

The outcome differs from the way neighboring Belgium handled a similar clash. In 1990, King Baudouin of Belgium refused to sign a law liberalizing abortion. The Belgian government declared him temporarily unable to reign and passed the law without him. Parliament then restored him to the throne. Belgium left its constitution unchanged. Luxembourg rewrote the rule itself.

The grand duke remains Luxembourg's formal head of state and represents the country abroad. Political decisions, however, sit with the elected government and parliament. In the 2008 dispute, lawmakers resolved the clash between the monarch's conviction and the parliamentary majority by narrowing the crown's role in lawmaking.

Japan's emperor holds no governing power. The 1947 constitution defines him as the symbol of the state and of the unity of the people. That makes Japan a contrast case on this list rather than a country where the crown still rules.

Article 1 says the emperor derives his position from the will of the people, with whom sovereign power resides. That wording reversed the earlier order. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 had placed sovereignty in the emperor himself and described him as sacred and inviolable.

Article 4 draws the line in plain terms. The emperor performs only the acts in matters of state that the constitution lists, and he has no powers related to government. Article 3 adds that each of those acts requires the advice and approval of the cabinet. The cabinet, not the emperor, is responsible for them.

The listed duties look substantial on paper. Under Article 6, the emperor appoints the prime minister. He must appoint the person the Diet designates, however. He also appoints the chief judge of the Supreme Court, as designated by the cabinet.

Article 7 covers the rest of the formal role. The emperor promulgates laws, cabinet orders, treaties and constitutional amendments. He convokes the Diet, dissolves the House of Representatives and proclaims general elections. He receives foreign ambassadors and awards honors. In each case, elected officials and the cabinet make the decision. The emperor's act is a formality.

The constitution was drafted during the U.S.-led occupation after World War II. It was promulgated on November 3, 1946, and took effect on May 3, 1947. Months before that, on January 1, 1946, Emperor Hirohito issued a rescript disavowing the idea that the emperor was divine.

The limits extend to the throne itself. The emperor cannot change the rules of succession. Those rules sit in the Imperial House Law, which the Diet passes and amends. When Emperor Akihito wished to step down, the Diet passed a special law in 2017 permitting his abdication.

Akihito stepped down on April 30, 2019. His son Naruhito became emperor the next day, on May 1. It was the first abdication of a Japanese emperor since Emperor Kokaku gave up the throne in 1817.

That sequence shows where authority lies in Japan. The monarch's own retirement required an act of elected lawmakers. The crown carries a long line of succession and a ceremonial public role, while governing decisions rest with the Diet and the cabinet.

From Louis XIV to Brunei's sultan: 20 rulers who held thrones for decades

Meet 20 of the longest-reigning monarchs in history and today, from Louis XIV and Elizabeth II to Brunei's sultan, who held thrones across empires and eras

A single reign can outlast empires, wars and entire political systems. Elizabeth II took the British throne in 1952 and reigned until her death in 2022. Louis XIV became king of France at age four and died about 72 years later, in 1715. Reigns of that length turn one person into a fixed point while governments, borders and technologies change around them.

This list gathers 20 of the longest-reigning monarchs in history and in the present day. It is a selection, not a complete ranking. Sixteen entries are historic reigns that ended through death, abdication or deposition. The other four belong to monarchs who were still on the throne as of this article's writing. Among them is Hassanal Bolkiah, Sultan of Brunei since 1967. He is the longest-serving current monarch as of this article's writing.

The reigns covered here belong to sovereign monarchs, and the dates are those commonly recorded. Several of these rulers took the throne as children. Sobhuza II of Eswatini became paramount chief as an infant in 1899, and regents governed during his early years. Pedro II of Brazil was five when he became emperor. The Kangxi Emperor of China was seven. Where a reign began under a regency, the slide says so. A long formal reign and a long stretch of personal rule are not always the same thing.

Some names that appear on other lists are left out. Pepi II of ancient Egypt is traditionally credited with an exceptionally long reign, but its length is disputed. No figure is given for him here. The early emperors of Japan whose reigns come from legend are excluded for the same reason. The one ancient reign that does appear, that of Ramesses II, carries an approximate length. Chronologies of that period differ.

The slides run roughly from the longest documented historic reigns to the four rulers still on the throne. Each one covers how a reign began, how long it lasted and what it spanned. Those events range from the American Revolution and two world wars to the fall of Brazil's empire and the abdications that ended reigns in Denmark and the Netherlands.

Sobhuza II of Eswatini, then known as Swaziland, holds the longest officially recorded reign of any monarch. It is usually given as more than 82 years, running from his accession as an infant in 1899 to his death on Aug. 21, 1982.

Sobhuza was born in 1899, the same year his father, Ngwane V, died. Too young to govern, he reigned through regents in his early years.

His grandmother, Labotsibeni Mdluli, led that regency as queen regent. She held the role for roughly two decades. In that period she managed relations with British officials and kept the throne secure for her grandson.

Sobhuza spent part of the regency in school. His education included the Lovedale mission institution in South Africa's Eastern Cape. He took full powers around 1921, as a young man in his early 20s.

Swaziland was a British protectorate for much of his reign. Britain controlled its foreign affairs and much of its administration until independence. For that reason, lists of long reigns often treat Louis XIV of France separately. He is described as the longest-reigning sovereign of a fully independent state with well-documented dates.

Land dominated the early part of Sobhuza's rule. A 1907 partition set aside about one-third of the territory for Swazi use. Much of the rest went to concession holders and the colonial administration. In the early 1920s, Sobhuza traveled to London to press the Swazi case over land.

Swaziland gained independence from Britain on Sept. 6, 1968, with Sobhuza still on the throne. The change turned a protectorate's paramount chief into the head of a sovereign state.

In 1973, he suspended the independence constitution and banned political parties. From then on, he governed with wide personal authority. The monarchy became the center of political power in the country.

His reign began during the Second Anglo-Boer War, which started in October 1899. It went on to outlast British rule over Swaziland by 14 years.

Louis XIV reigned over France for about 72 years, from May 14, 1643, to Sept. 1, 1715. That is the longest reign of any sovereign of a fully independent state whose dates are well documented.

He became king at age four, after the death of his father, Louis XIII. A regency held real authority in those early years. His mother, Anne of Austria, served as regent. Cardinal Jules Mazarin ran the government as chief minister.

The young king grew up during the Fronde, a series of revolts against royal authority between 1648 and 1653. Nobles and the Parlement of Paris challenged the crown's power. In 1649, the royal family left Paris for safety at Saint-Germain-en-Laye.

Mazarin died in 1661. Louis then declared that he would govern without a chief minister. That decision marked the start of his personal rule. Ministers such as Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who oversaw finances and trade, answered directly to the king.

The nickname "the Sun King" — le Roi Soleil in French — comes from the solar imagery Louis adopted. As a teenager, he danced the role of the rising sun in a 1653 court ballet. The sun later became his personal emblem.

The Palace of Versailles, outside Paris, is the reign's best-known building. Louis expanded his father's hunting lodge there into a vast palace. He formally moved the court and the government to Versailles in 1682. Its Hall of Mirrors became a setting for royal ceremony. Leading noble families were expected to attend the king at court.

Louis also fought a series of long and costly wars. The last of them, the War of the Spanish Succession, ran from 1701 to 1714. It ended with Louis's grandson Philip confirmed as king of Spain. At home, Louis revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, ending legal toleration for French Protestants.

Louis outlived both his son and his eldest grandson. When he died at Versailles, the crown passed to his great-grandson, Louis XV, who was five years old. That meant France began a second long reign the same way the first had started — with a child king and a regency.

Elizabeth II reigned from Feb. 6, 1952, to Sept. 8, 2022, a span of 70 years and 214 days. That makes her the longest-reigning British monarch on record.

She became queen while on an overseas tour in Kenya. Her father, George VI, had died in his sleep at Sandringham House in Norfolk. She was 25 years old.

Her coronation came more than a year later, on June 2, 1953, at Westminster Abbey in London. It was the first time a British coronation service was broadcast on television. Millions of people watched it live.

Her role extended well beyond the U.K. She was also head of state of several Commonwealth realms. These are independent countries that kept the British monarch as their sovereign. Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Jamaica were among them.

The list of realms changed over the decades as some countries became republics. Barbados, for example, replaced her as head of state in 2021. She also held the separate title of Head of the Commonwealth, an association made up mostly of former British territories.

Her reign overlapped with 15 British prime ministers. The first was Winston Churchill, who was in office when she took the throne. The last was Liz Truss. The queen appointed Truss at Balmoral Castle in Scotland two days before her own death.

She passed the previous British record, held by her great-great-grandmother Victoria, on Sept. 9, 2015.

Her reign was marked by a series of jubilees. She celebrated her Silver Jubilee in 1977, her Golden Jubilee in 2002 and her Diamond Jubilee in 2012. In June 2022, she became the first British monarch to mark a Platinum Jubilee, for 70 years on the throne.

She married Prince Philip in 1947. He died in April 2021, at age 99.

Elizabeth II died at Balmoral on Sept. 8, 2022, at age 96. Her eldest son succeeded her immediately as Charles III. His coronation took place at Westminster Abbey on May 6, 2023.

Bhumibol Adulyadej reigned as king of Thailand from June 9, 1946, until his death on Oct. 13, 2016, a span of about 70 years. Known formally as Rama IX, he is the longest-reigning monarch in Thai history.

He was the ninth king of the Chakri dynasty, which has ruled from Bangkok since 1782. His regnal name, Rama IX, marks that place in the line of succession.

Bhumibol was not raised as an heir. He was born on Dec. 5, 1927, in Cambridge, Massachusetts, where his father, Prince Mahidol Adulyadej, was studying at Harvard. His elder brother, King Ananda Mahidol, died of a gunshot wound at the Grand Palace in Bangkok in June 1946. Bhumibol succeeded him at age 18.

The new king did not take up his duties at once. He went back to Switzerland to finish his studies in Lausanne. A regency handled royal affairs while he was abroad. He married Sirikit Kitiyakara in April 1950, and she became Queen Sirikit. His formal coronation followed on May 5, 1950.

Bhumibol reigned as a constitutional monarch. Thailand's absolute monarchy had ended with the 1932 revolution, when he was a small child. During his reign the country went through repeated military coups and a long series of constitutions. The king held no executive power, but his standing gave him political weight.

That weight was visible in May 1992. After deadly clashes between soldiers and protesters in Bangkok, he summoned Prime Minister Suchinda Kraprayoon and protest leader Chamlong Srimuang to a televised audience. Suchinda resigned soon afterward.

He also sponsored royal development projects focused on irrigation, water management and farming. In northern Thailand, the Royal Project encouraged hill communities to replace opium poppies with other crops. Outside official duties, he played jazz saxophone and composed music.

Thai law gives the monarchy strong legal protection. Article 112 of Thailand's criminal code makes defaming or insulting the king, queen, heir or regent a crime punishable by up to 15 years in prison per count.

Bhumibol died at Siriraj Hospital in Bangkok at age 88. His son, Maha Vajiralongkorn, succeeded him as Rama X $TWTR. After a year of official mourning, the royal cremation took place in Bangkok in October 2017.

Johann II ruled Liechtenstein from Nov. 12, 1858, to Feb. 11, 1929, a reign of about 70 years. No other prince in the Alpine principality's history has held the throne for as long.

He was born in 1840 at Lednice, a Liechtenstein family seat in Moravia. He inherited the title after the death of his father, Alois II. Johann II never married and had no children. When he died, the throne passed to his younger brother, Franz I.

Liechtenstein's ruling princes of that era did not live in the principality. Johann II spent most of his life in Vienna and on the family's estates in what is now the Czech Republic. He died at the family's palace in Valtice. A reigning prince would not make Liechtenstein his permanent home until Franz Joseph II moved there in 1938.

His tenure covered two major constitutional changes. A constitution granted in 1862 created an elected parliament, the Landtag. A second constitution, adopted in 1921, gave the people and parliament a larger share of power while keeping the prince as head of state. That 1921 document, amended several times since, remains the foundation of Liechtenstein's government.

The principality disbanded its small army in 1868. Liechtenstein has not maintained a military since.

The reign also spanned a reorientation of the country's foreign ties. From 1852, Liechtenstein shared a customs union with Austria. After Austria-Hungary collapsed at the end of World War I, the principality turned toward Switzerland instead. It signed a customs treaty with its western neighbor in 1923. The Swiss franc replaced Austrian money as the country's currency in the early 1920s.

Johann II was known in German as "Johann der Gute," or Johann the Good, a name linked to his charity and patronage. He was a major art collector who expanded the princely collections. He gave large numbers of paintings and other works to museums in Vienna and elsewhere in central Europe. In Vaduz, he paid for the construction of the neo-Gothic Church of St. Florin. That church became the cathedral of the Archdiocese of Vaduz in 1997.

Franz Joseph I ruled the Habsburg lands from Dec. 2, 1848, until his death on Nov. 21, 1916, a reign of just under 68 years. He governed the Austrian Empire and then Austria-Hungary, and he died during World War I.

He became emperor at 18, in the middle of the revolutions that spread across Europe in 1848. His uncle, Emperor Ferdinand I, abdicated. His father, Archduke Franz Karl, gave up his own claim, and the crown passed directly to the young archduke.

His early decades brought military defeats that redrew the empire's borders. Austria lost Lombardy after its defeat by France and Piedmont-Sardinia in 1859. In 1866, Prussia defeated Austria at the Battle of Königgrätz. That short war ended Habsburg leadership among the German states.

The loss to Prussia led to the Compromise of 1867, which turned the empire into the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary. Hungary gained its own government and parliament. Franz Joseph was crowned king of Hungary that year and remained emperor of Austria. The two halves shared one monarch, one foreign policy and a common army.

Vienna changed shape during his reign. In 1857, he ordered the old city walls torn down. The cleared land became the Ringstrasse, a boulevard later lined with the parliament building, the city hall and the state opera house.

His family suffered a series of violent deaths. His brother Maximilian, who had become emperor of Mexico, was executed by firing squad in 1867. His only son, Crown Prince Rudolf, died by suicide at the Mayerling hunting lodge in 1889. His wife, Empress Elisabeth, known as Sisi, was assassinated in Geneva in 1898.

After Rudolf's death, the emperor's brother Karl Ludwig stood next in line. When Karl Ludwig died in 1896, his son, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, became heir. On June 28, 1914, Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo. The city was the capital of Bosnia, which Austria-Hungary had annexed in 1908. On July 28, 1914, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia, the start of World War I.

Franz Joseph died at Schönbrunn Palace in Vienna at age 86, with the war still underway. His grandnephew, Charles I, succeeded him. The Habsburg monarchy collapsed in 1918 at the end of the war, and Austria and Hungary became separate states.

Ramesses II ruled Egypt for about 66 years in the 13th century BC. He was the third king of Egypt's 19th Dynasty and the son of Seti I.

Later generations called him Ramesses the Great. The Greeks knew him by a version of his throne name, Usermaatre, which became Ozymandias. The English poet Percy Bysshe Shelley used that name for his 1818 sonnet about a ruined statue in the desert.

His best-known military campaign was against the Hittite Empire, which controlled much of what is now Turkey and northern Syria. In the fifth year of his reign, he fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at Kadesh, on the Orontes River in present-day Syria. Neither side won a clear victory. Ramesses still had the battle carved into temple walls as a triumph.

Years later, Egypt and the Hittites settled their rivalry with a formal peace treaty, concluded with the Hittite king Hattusili III. Versions survive in Egyptian hieroglyphs at Karnak. Others survive in Akkadian cuneiform on clay tablets from the Hittite capital, Hattusa. The agreement is one of the earliest known peace treaties. A replica of one tablet hangs at U.N. headquarters in New York.

Building was a major feature of the reign. Ramesses commissioned the rock-cut temples at Abu Simbel in Nubia, including a smaller temple dedicated to his chief wife, Nefertari. In the 1960s, an international campaign led by UNESCO cut the temples into blocks and moved them to higher ground. The work saved them from the waters rising behind the Aswan High Dam. He also built a new capital in the Nile Delta, Pi-Ramesses, and a mortuary temple at Thebes known today as the Ramesseum.

Ramesses outlived many of his children. His eventual successor, Merneptah, was his 13th son. Ramesses also celebrated the Sed festival, a royal jubilee traditionally held after 30 years on the throne. He then repeated it at shorter intervals.

His mummy was found in 1881 in a cache of royal remains at Deir el-Bahari, near Luxor. In 2021, it was moved in a public procession to the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo.

Queen Victoria reigned for 63 years and 216 days, from June 20, 1837, to Jan. 22, 1901. That was the longest reign of any British monarch until her great-great-granddaughter, Elizabeth II, passed it on Sept. 9, 2015.

Victoria became queen shortly after her 18th birthday, following the death of her uncle, William IV. She was already an adult. No regency was needed. Her accession also ended the link between the British crown and Hanover. Hanover's succession law did not allow a woman to inherit while a male heir was available. The German kingdom passed instead to her uncle Ernest Augustus.

In 1840, she married her first cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. The couple had nine children. Albert died in 1861. Victoria wore black for the rest of her life. She also withdrew from much of her public role for years afterward.

Her children and grandchildren married into royal houses across Europe. Her grandson Wilhelm II became the last German emperor. Her granddaughter Alexandra married Nicholas II and became the last empress of Russia. Those family ties earned Victoria the nickname "the grandmother of Europe."

The imperial title came in 1876. Parliament passed the Royal Titles Act that year, adding Empress of India to her style. Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli backed the measure. The title was proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar on Jan. 1, 1877.

The change built on an earlier shift in how India was governed. After the uprising of 1857, the British government took direct control from the East India Company in 1858. The new title gave the monarch a formal place at the head of that rule. Victoria never traveled to India.

Her reign spanned the spread of railways, the telegraph and industrial cities across Britain. It also covered a large expansion of the British Empire. The period now carries her name as the Victorian era. Britain marked her Golden Jubilee in 1887 and her Diamond Jubilee in 1897.

Victoria died at Osborne House, her residence on the Isle of Wight. Her eldest son succeeded her as Edward VII.

Hirohito reigned as emperor of Japan from Dec. 25, 1926, to Jan. 7, 1989, a span of about 62 years. He is the longest-reigning Japanese emperor whose dates can be verified historically.

His reign carries the era name Showa, usually translated as "enlightened peace." In Japan, emperors are referred to after death by the name of their era. Inside the country he is formally known as Emperor Showa. Hirohito is the name used most often abroad.

He was born on April 29, 1901. He took the throne at age 25 when his father, the Taisho Emperor, died. He had already served as regent since 1921 because of his father's poor health. His formal enthronement ceremony took place in Kyoto in November 1928.

The first two decades of the Showa era covered Japan's military expansion across Asia. That period included the invasion of Manchuria in 1931 and full-scale war with China from 1937. It also included the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941. The extent of Hirohito's personal responsibility for wartime decisions remains a matter of historical debate. He was not charged at the Tokyo war crimes trials.

On Aug. 15, 1945, a recorded radio address in his voice announced Japan's surrender. It was the first time most Japanese people had heard the emperor speak. On Jan. 1, 1946, he issued a statement rejecting the idea that the emperor was a living god.

The postwar constitution took effect on May 3, 1947, and redefined his role. It describes the emperor as the symbol of the state and of the unity of the people. It gives him no powers related to government.

The rest of his reign coincided with Japan's reconstruction and rapid economic growth. He opened the 1964 Tokyo Olympics. In 1975 he became the first reigning Japanese emperor to make a state visit to the U.S. Outside his official duties, he was a marine biologist who published research on small sea creatures called hydrozoans.

Hirohito was succeeded by his son Akihito, whose accession began the Heisei era.

The Kangxi Emperor ruled China for 61 years, from 1661 to 1722. That is the longest reign of any Chinese emperor in the historical record. He was the fourth emperor of the Qing dynasty and took the throne as a child of seven.

His personal name was Xuanye. He was the son of the Shunzhi Emperor, the first Qing ruler to govern China from Beijing. The Qing were a Manchu dynasty that had captured Beijing in 1644. The empire Kangxi inherited was still tightening its hold over the country.

The new emperor was too young to govern. Four regents ruled in his name at the start of the reign: Sonin, Suksaha, Ebilun and Oboi. Oboi came to dominate the group. In 1669, the teenage emperor had Oboi arrested and took direct control of the government.

The largest military challenge of his early rule was the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, which began in 1673. The three feudatories were large territories in southern China held by Han Chinese commanders and their heirs. Those commanders had received the lands as rewards for helping the Qing conquest. Wu Sangui was the most powerful of them. When Kangxi moved to reduce their power, they rebelled. Qing forces had put down the revolt by 1681.

In 1683, Qing forces took Taiwan from the Zheng family regime. The Zheng family had used the island as a base of resistance to the dynasty. Taiwan then came under Qing administration.

On the northern frontier, Kangxi's government signed the Treaty of Nerchinsk with Russia in 1689. The agreement set a border between the two empires. It was the first treaty China signed with a European power. In the 1690s, Kangxi personally led campaigns against Galdan, leader of the Dzungar Mongols.

The emperor also sponsored large scholarly projects. The Kangxi Dictionary, completed in 1716, became a standard reference work for Chinese characters. He made six southern inspection tours, traveling through cities in the Yangtze River region.

Kangxi died in December 1722. His fourth son succeeded him and ruled as the Yongzheng Emperor. The length of Kangxi's reign later became a benchmark for his grandson, the Qianlong Emperor.

The Qianlong Emperor of China's Qing dynasty reigned formally for 60 years, from 1735 to 1796, then gave up the throne rather than outlast his grandfather, the Kangxi Emperor. He kept effective control of the government after stepping down and held it until his death in 1799.

The abdication was a gesture of filial respect. Kangxi had ruled for 61 years, the longest reign in the Chinese historical record. Qianlong chose not to surpass that mark. He passed the imperial title to his son, who reigned as the Jiaqing Emperor.

The handover was formal rather than practical. Qianlong took the title Taishang Huang, usually translated as "retired emperor." He continued to direct major decisions from the palace. His reign therefore has two end dates: 1796 on paper and 1799 in practice. The 60-year figure refers only to the formal reign.

Qianlong, whose personal name was Hongli, came to the throne in 1735 after the death of his father, the Yongzheng Emperor. He was an adult at his accession. His grandfather had taken the throne as a child.

Qing territory reached its greatest extent under Qianlong. Campaigns in the 1750s destroyed the Dzungar Khanate and brought the region later known as Xinjiang under Qing control. The emperor counted those wars among his Ten Great Campaigns, a set of military expeditions he promoted as the achievements of his reign.

He also sponsored the Siku Quanshu, or Complete Library of the Four Treasuries, a vast compilation of Chinese texts. The same project served to identify and destroy writings considered hostile to the dynasty.

In 1793, he received a British embassy led by George Macartney and sent by George III. Qianlong turned down British requests for expanded trade and a permanent diplomatic presence in China. His reply to George III stated that China had no need for British manufactured goods.

His final years brought unrest at home. The White Lotus Rebellion broke out in 1796, the year of his abdication, and was still under way when he died. His retirement also coincided with the dominance of Heshen, a favored official at court. Within weeks of Qianlong's death, the Jiaqing Emperor had Heshen arrested on corruption charges and ordered him to take his own life.

George III reigned over Great Britain, and later the U.K., for 59 years and 96 days, from Oct. 25, 1760, to Jan. 29, 1820. His reign spanned both the American Revolution and the Napoleonic Wars.

He became king at 22, succeeding his grandfather George II. His father, Frederick, Prince of Wales, had died in 1751 before he could inherit the crown. George III was the third king from the House of Hanover. He was also the first of them born in Britain and raised with English as his first language.

The conflict with the 13 American colonies dominated the 1770s and early 1780s. The Declaration of Independence in 1776 listed a long series of grievances aimed directly at him. Britain formally recognized American independence in the Treaty of Paris in 1783.

The shape of his realm also changed. The Acts of Union 1800 joined Great Britain with Ireland. The merged kingdom came into being on Jan. 1, 1801, and George III became its first monarch.

War with Revolutionary and then Napoleonic France ran through much of the second half of his reign. The British navy won the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Napoleon's final defeat at Waterloo came in 1815.

By then, George III was no longer governing. He suffered repeated episodes of severe mental illness. A crisis in 1788 and 1789 set off a political fight in Parliament over who should exercise royal power on his behalf. He recovered that time. After a further collapse, a regency began in 1811. His eldest son, the future George IV, governed as Prince Regent for the last nine years of the reign. The cause of the king's illness remains disputed.

Outside politics, George III took a close interest in farming and agricultural improvement. That interest earned him the nickname "Farmer George." He also built a large library, later given to the nation and now held by the British Library as the King's Library.

He married Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz in 1761, and the couple had 15 children. His reign remains the longest of any male British monarch. Only two British rulers have reigned longer: Victoria and Elizabeth II, both of them his descendants.

Pedro II ruled Brazil for about 58 years, from April 7, 1831, until a republican coup removed him on Nov. 15, 1889. He inherited the throne at age five, and regents governed the country during the early part of his reign.

The boy emperor came to power because his father, Pedro I, abdicated in 1831 and left for Europe. Pedro II was born in Rio de Janeiro on Dec. 2, 1825. He stayed behind as the male heir of the Empire of Brazil, which had declared independence from Portugal in 1822.

A series of regencies ran the government through the 1830s. That decade brought provincial revolts in several parts of the country. In 1840, Brazil's parliament declared the young emperor of age at 14, earlier than the constitution required. His formal coronation followed in 1841.

His personal rule lasted almost five decades. In 1843, he married Teresa Cristina of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. Their daughter Isabel became heir to the throne.

Brazil fought the Paraguayan War, also called the War of the Triple Alliance, from 1864 to 1870. Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay fought together against Paraguay. The conflict ended in an allied victory and left Paraguay devastated.

Slavery shaped the later years of the reign. The Law of Free Birth of 1871 declared children born to enslaved mothers free. Full abolition came with the Golden Law on May 13, 1888. Princess Isabel signed it while acting as regent during one of her father's trips abroad.

Abolition cost the monarchy support among plantation owners. Army officers and republicans then moved against the crown. Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca led the coup that proclaimed the republic in November 1889. The imperial family left for exile in Europe within days.

Pedro II had a wide interest in science, languages and scholarship. He visited the U.S. in 1876 and attended the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia. There he saw Alexander Graham Bell demonstrate the telephone.

He died in Paris on Dec. 5, 1891, without returning to Brazil. He was the country's second and last emperor.

Wilhelmina reigned over the Netherlands for about 57 years, from 1890 to 1948. For five of those years, during World War II, she and her government operated from exile in London while Germany occupied her country.

Her reign began in childhood. Wilhelmina was born in The Hague in 1880 and became queen in November 1890, at age 10, when her father, King William III, died. She was his only surviving child. The crown therefore passed to her.

She could not rule on her own at first. Her mother, Queen Emma, served as regent until 1898. That year Wilhelmina turned 18 and was inaugurated in Amsterdam's Nieuwe Kerk, taking up the full powers of the throne.

She was the first woman to reign over the Netherlands in her own right. In 1901 she married Duke $DUK Henry of Mecklenburg-Schwerin. Their only child, Juliana, was born in 1909.

The Netherlands stayed neutral during World War I. After the fighting ended in 1918, the deposed German emperor Wilhelm II crossed into the country. The Dutch government declined to hand him over to the Allies. He lived at Doorn until his death in 1941.

World War II brought a different outcome. German forces invaded on May 10, 1940. Within days, Wilhelmina left for Britain, and the Dutch cabinet followed her to London. The main Dutch army surrendered shortly afterward.

The government in exile operated from London for the rest of the war. The queen spoke to her occupied country through Radio Oranje, a Dutch-language program broadcast from Britain. Those speeches made her a public symbol of resistance in the occupied Netherlands.

She returned to Dutch soil in 1945, as the country was liberated. Three years later, in September 1948, she abdicated in favor of Juliana. She then took the title Princess Wilhelmina.

Her successors followed the same route off the throne. Juliana abdicated in 1980 in favor of her daughter Beatrix, and Beatrix abdicated in 2013 in favor of her son Willem-Alexander.

Wilhelmina published a memoir, "Eenzaam maar niet alleen" ("Lonely but not alone"), in 1959. She died in 1962 at Het Loo Palace in Apeldoorn.

Rainier III ruled Monaco from Nov. 9, 1949, to April 6, 2005, a reign of about 55 years. During that time, the small Mediterranean principality changed its economy, its constitution and even its physical size.

Rainier was born in 1923 and inherited the throne from his grandfather, Prince Louis II. His mother, Princess Charlotte, had given up her own right of succession in his favor. That decision placed him directly in line to rule. Before he became prince, he served with the French army during World War II.

Monaco is the second-smallest sovereign state in the world, after Vatican City. When Rainier took the throne, its economy depended heavily on the Monte Carlo casino. He worked to widen that base toward banking, tourism, conventions and real estate.

That effort brought him into conflict with the shipping magnate Aristotle Onassis. Onassis held a controlling stake in the Société des Bains de Mer, the company that runs the casino. In 1966, the Monegasque state took majority control of the company.

Rainier became known as "the Builder Prince." Land reclaimed from the sea added new ground to the principality. The Fontvieille district is the most visible result of that work.

He also changed how Monaco was governed. In 1962, he granted a new constitution that limited some of the prince's powers. The same year brought a dispute with France, under President Charles de Gaulle, over French residents using Monaco to avoid French taxes.

His marriage drew worldwide attention. In 1956, he married the American actress Grace Kelly. She had appeared in films including High Noon and Rear Window, and she won an Academy Award for The Country Girl. She left her film career to become Princess Grace.

The couple had three children: Caroline, Albert and Stéphanie. Grace died in 1982 after a car crash on a road near Monaco. Rainier did not remarry.

After his death in April 2005, his son succeeded him as Albert II.

Margrethe II reigned as queen of Denmark from Jan. 14, 1972, to Jan. 14, 2024, a span of exactly 52 years. She ended her reign by abdicating in favor of her elder son, who became King Frederik X $TWTR.

She came to the throne after the death of her father, Frederik IX. Her path to the crown depended on a change in Danish law. Under the succession rules in force before 1953, only men could inherit the throne. That year, Danish voters approved a new Act of Succession in a referendum. It allowed a woman to succeed when the monarch had no son. Frederik IX had three daughters, and Margrethe was the eldest.

Prime Minister Jens Otto Krag proclaimed her queen from the balcony of Christiansborg Palace in Copenhagen. Her role was constitutional. Political power in Denmark rests with the elected parliament, the Folketing, and the government it supports.

She announced her decision to step down in her televised New Year's Eve address on Dec. 31, 2023. In the speech, she pointed to her age and to major back surgery she had undergone earlier that year. The handover took place on the anniversary of her own accession.

Voluntary abdication had been almost unknown among Danish monarchs. No Danish king or queen had willingly given up the throne since Eric III in 1146. Elsewhere in Europe, monarchs in the Netherlands, Belgium and Spain had abdicated in 2013 and 2014.

Her 52 years place her reign second in Danish history, behind Christian IV, who ruled from 1588 to 1648. After Elizabeth II died in September 2022, Margrethe was Europe's longest-reigning sitting monarch. She was also the only reigning queen in the world until her abdication.

Outside her official duties, she worked as an artist. In the 1970s, she produced illustrations for a Danish edition of J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings, published under the name Ingahild Grathmer. She also designed costumes and scenery for ballet, theater and television productions.

With her husband, Prince Henrik, she translated Simone de Beauvoir's novel All Men Are Mortal into Danish. Henrik, a French-born diplomat she married in 1967, died in 2018.

Hassanal Bolkiah has been Sultan of Brunei since Oct. 5, 1967, when his father abdicated in his favor. As of this article's writing, he is the longest-serving monarch still on a throne anywhere in the world.

His father, Omar Ali Saifuddien III, was Brunei's 28th sultan. Hassanal Bolkiah became the 29th. He was born on July 15, 1946, so he was 21 when he succeeded. His formal coronation followed on Aug. 1, 1968.

He took the throne while Brunei was still a British protectorate, a status that dated to 1888. Britain kept responsibility for the country's defense and foreign affairs for years afterward. Full independence came on Jan. 1, 1984. He has therefore ruled both as a protected sovereign and as head of a fully independent state.

Brunei sits on the northern coast of Borneo. On land, it is bordered by the Malaysian state of Sarawak, and its coastline faces the South China Sea. The country's wealth rests largely on oil and natural gas. Those resources have funded the state and the royal household for decades.

The sultan is head of state, and he is also prime minister. During his reign he has held other cabinet posts as well, including defense and finance. Brunei describes its political system as a Malay Islamic Monarchy. The sultan holds executive authority, and he is also the head of Islam in the country.

In 2019, Brunei brought the later phases of its Syariah Penal Code into force. The code drew international criticism over the punishments it set out, including for same-sex relations. The sultan later said the country's existing moratorium on the death penalty would also apply to the code.

Brunei joined ASEAN in January 1984, shortly after independence. In 2017, the sultan marked his golden jubilee, 50 years on the throne.

His standing among living monarchs changed after Elizabeth II died on Sept. 8, 2022. She had reigned since 1952, more than 15 years before he became sultan. With her death, he became the longest-serving current monarch.

Carl XVI Gustaf has been king of Sweden since Sept. 15, 1973, when he succeeded his grandfather, Gustaf VI Adolf. He was still reigning as of this article's writing, and no earlier Swedish monarch has held the throne as long.

He was born at Haga Palace outside Stockholm on April 30, 1946, and was 27 when he became king. The crown skipped a generation. His father, Prince Gustaf Adolf, died in a plane crash at Copenhagen's airport in January 1947. The infant prince then stood next in line after his grandfather. He became crown prince when Gustaf VI Adolf took the throne in 1950.

His reign began during a period of constitutional change. Sweden's new Instrument of Government, adopted in 1974 and in force from 1975, removed the monarch's remaining formal political powers. The king no longer takes part in government decisions. The speaker of the Riksdag, not the monarch, proposes the prime minister.

What remains is a ceremonial and representative role. The king serves as head of state, opens the annual session of the Riksdag and makes state visits abroad. Each December, he presents the Nobel Prizes at the ceremony in Stockholm. The Nobel Peace Prize is awarded separately in Oslo.

In 1976, he married Silvia Sommerlath. The two met at the 1972 Summer Olympics in Munich. They have three children: Victoria, Carl Philip and Madeleine.

The rules of succession changed during his reign as well. A change to the Act of Succession that took effect in 1980 introduced absolute primogeniture. Under that system, the eldest child inherits the throne regardless of sex. Carl Philip, born in 1979, had been crown prince from birth. The change passed that position to his older sister, Victoria, who became heir apparent.

The king belongs to the House of Bernadotte. The dynasty traces its line to Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte, a French marshal under Napoleon. Bernadotte was chosen as Sweden's crown prince in 1810 and became King Charles XIV John in 1818. Carl XVI Gustaf's royal motto translates as "For Sweden — with the times."

Sweden marked his golden jubilee in September 2023, 50 years after his accession. Outside his state duties, he serves as honorary chairman of the World Scout Foundation.

Mswati III has been king of Eswatini since April 25, 1986, when he was crowned at 18. As of this article's writing, he remains on the throne. He holds broad executive, legislative and judicial authority in one of the world's few remaining absolute monarchies.

He was born Makhosetive Dlamini on April 19, 1968. That was a few months before the country gained independence from Britain in September of the same year. He is one of the many sons of Sobhuza II. His mother, Ntfombi Tfwala, later served as regent and became queen mother.

The throne stood empty for about four years after his father's death in 1982. Regents governed in the meantime. Queen Dzeliwe held the role first. Ntfombi replaced her in 1983 after a power struggle inside the royal family. The crown prince was a student at Sherborne School in England when he was called home to be crowned.

Eswatini's political system places the king at its center. He appoints the prime minister and the cabinet. He can dissolve parliament, and he names a share of the members of both legislative chambers. Political parties cannot compete in parliamentary elections. Candidates run as individuals through local constituencies known as tinkhundla.

A new constitution took effect in 2006. It replaced the arrangement in place since the independence constitution was suspended in 1973. The document kept the king's central powers intact.

In April 2018, Mswati III renamed the country from Swaziland to the Kingdom of Eswatini. He announced the change during celebrations marking 50 years of independence. The anniversary coincided with his own 50th birthday. Eswatini means "land of the Swazis" in siSwati.

Custom also shapes the monarchy. The queen mother, known as the Ndlovukati, holds a traditional co-ruling role alongside the king. The king presides over the annual Umhlanga, or reed dance, and the Incwala ceremony, two central rituals of Swazi kingship. He has married many wives under customary practice.

His rule has faced open challenges at home. Pro-democracy protests broke out across the country in 2021, and security forces cracked down on demonstrators.

Eswatini is a small, landlocked kingdom bordered by South Africa and Mozambique. Mbabane serves as the administrative capital. Lobamba is the royal and legislative capital.

Harald V became king of Norway on Jan. 17, 1991, following the death of his father, Olav V. He remained on the throne as of this article's writing.

His accession continued a royal line that dates to 1905. That year, Norway dissolved its union with Sweden. A Danish prince was then chosen as king and took the name Haakon VII. Harald is Haakon VII's grandson and the third monarch of that line to reign over an independent Norway.

Harald was born on Feb. 21, 1937, at Skaugum, the royal estate outside Oslo. His parents were Crown Prince Olav and Crown Princess Märtha, who was born a Swedish princess. Norway is a constitutional monarchy. The king's duties are largely ceremonial. Political power rests with the elected parliament, the Storting, and with the government. Harald formally presides over the Council of State, where government decisions are approved.

I’d thru-hiked the PCT and the CDT. I was fine doing the AT as a two-part LASH. I had nothing to prove.

Unlike Killington in 2024, getting off the AT this time wasn’t a surrender. It was a conscious choice. I would be back next summer, but I would return as a section hiker. And to my surprise, that mattered. A lot.

Many of us deal with depression after getting off trail. I am a member of this sad clan. On trail I made new friends, shared parts of myself that hadn’t seen the light of day in years, shed pounds, and became crazy-fit while eating like a joyful pig.

Being on trail expanded my life and gave it purpose by shrinking it. The daily routine of miles and chores, the resupply stops of splurge and cleansing, and the slow accretion of accomplishment.

But now, back home and staying, that bigger life has abruptly ended. The better version of myself, the one who crushed miles despite the bad joints, is slowly fading.

I’ve been following Guru on Instagram, the guy in the kilt I hiked the 100-Mile Wilderness and summited Katahdin with. Seeing his photos of autumn sunsets, fall-stripped trees and leaf-strewn trails, I can smell the damp leaves, feel the cold air. I think, “That could have been me.”

Getting through the Whites showed me what my old body could do. My confidence only grew as I clambered through the Mahoosucs, the Bigelows, and hard sections with no name. I flew over the roots and rocks of Maine, nearly ecstatic. When I climbed Katahdin, I was at my peak.

There are no guarantees I’ll come back and finish. Not for lack of desire, but because of my waning ability. My arthritis, my stamina, and who knows what other shoe might drop between now and next July.

I now believe I should have kept going. It’s not just that I can’t trust my body; it’s like I broke a promise to myself.

When I set out to hike the Triple Crown in 2022, the subtext read “thru-hike.” My dad’s passing, and my AT attempt in 2024 that followed, changed the rules. It seemed that just finishing, no matter the method, was all that mattered.

I am a thru-hiker. An identity of which I am ridiculously proud. I’ve had my face wind-blasted raw below whooshing turbines outside Tehachapi, been chased by wildfires through Oregon, and dragged a norovirus-weakened body through the Cascades. On the CDT, I pushed through joint pain requiring oral steroids in the Wind River Range and dunked frost-stiffened shoes into the Gila River so I could jam my frozen feet into them. To get off trail 6,000 miles into my Triple Crown, even by choice, now feels like a betrayal of what I set out to do.

It took me a long time to write this post. I’d planned to keep writing my way to Katahdin, a story I still need to tell. But I had to come clean first. Thank you for following me this far.

Utopias have a tendency to become less interesting as they become real.  At first, they are exhilarating. They overturn conventions, ridicule authorities, and embarrass the present. Their authors anticipate what everyone else has not yet learned to desire. But sometimes, they happen. Archigram. Its visions of technological freedom, mobile architectures, continuous connectivity, and cities emancipated from fixed institutions were among the twentieth century’s most energizing propositions. Their irreverence was inseparable from their architects’ intelligence and biological vitality. Young minds were having enormous fun imagining the demolition of established orders, their hearts racing at possible worlds.

Now consider permanent connectivity. Work finds us everywhere. Platforms promise choice while concentrating power. Technologies celebrate freedom while organizing surveillance, extraction, segregation, and uninterrupted availability. The promise of mobility encounters selective and segregation-making fortified borders. The promise of flexibility encounters a housing crisis that displaces millions. Sophisticated computation accompanies warfare and the destruction of cities. Networks promising universal knowledge also industrialize disinformation.

What once looked like liberation can become an infrastructure of domination. Utopia did not simply fail. Its realization changed the politics of its promises. A utopia does not carry its politics intact into the future.

Today’s crises emerge through interactions among computational systems, biological processes, economic institutions, and planetary ecologies. Housing is shaped by financial algorithms, property regimes, migration, and bodies struggling to survive. Climate instability connects atmospheric processes, energy infrastructures, industrial production, governance and predictive computation. Biodiversity loss involves agriculture, logistics, microbial ecologies, and land finance. Warfare combines automated detection with the destruction of bodies, habitats, and infrastructures.

These are not separate technological, social, and environmental problems. They are posthuman enactments in which computation and biology, sensing and decision-making, are inseparable. We struggle to imagine our participation in transformations whose production exceeds what we can sense. Yet we participate constantly. This makes experimentation at the thresholds of sensing an architectural question.

Experimenting now means entering the conflicts through which reality is being produced, rather than imagining alternatives from a safe distance. It means challenging what computational systems recognize, what institutions exclude, what bodies endure, and what other forms of life make possible. It means transforming not only what we know, but who and what can produce knowledge, intervene in decisions, and redirect their consequences. The radicality of architecture lies in making reality itself a site of collective experimentation, where the power to sense, contest, and transform is not merely imagined or proposed but enacted and bodyenvironmentally lived.

Like many Americans, I recently watched You Can See Everything, the documentary that follows Elizabeth Holmes through the weeks before she reported to prison. I left the theater fascinated and bewildered. Throughout the movie, I kept asking the same question the filmmakers seem to be asking: Am I being deceived, or am I missing something because I don't know enough?

I have asked myself that question about Holmes before. In July 2013, I was the Deputy Commissioner for the New York City Health Department, where I oversaw the city's infectious disease programs and the public health laboratory. City Hall asked me to meet with a promising new lab-testing company; a few weeks later, I joined an hour-long video call with Holmes, her team, and our lab director.

I still have my notes. Holmes and her colleagues told us that their company, Theranos, had developed a more convenient way to test blood in small amounts that would cut testing costs by 30 percent.

A wide range of tests had been validatedFederal and New York State regulatory approvals were completeThey secured a Department of Defense contract. Testing was scheduled to launch at New York Walgreens pharmacies within months.

My colleague and I asked repeatedly about the underlying science and data. Every time, we got the same answer: they could not disclose business secrets, but everything worked and was approved. Rest assured.

When the call ended, my colleague and I were dumbfounded. Nothing we knew about collecting specimens, validating tests, or getting regulatory approval made their story plausible. And yet I doubted myself. Holmes spoke with such composure and certainty and had such powerful connections and investors that I kept wondering: Did she know something about science or lab regulations that I didn't?

We offered to evaluate the device in our lab before any wider use. Neither side ever followed up. When the Wall Street Journal published its first investigation into the company in October 2015, I called my colleague, and we both said the same thing: "We knew it!" Most of what she said in that meeting was not true.

After watching the movie, I kept thinking about that incident and how it raised a hard healthcare problem: knowing when to trust your own expertise and when to defer to someone else's.

We assume that people who sound certain know more than we do. This is particularly common when checking a person’s claims is difficult or costly. That describes the Theranos meeting almost exactly. We could not check her claims because she said her data was confidential, and she spoke with absolute certainty.

While the lack of data made me suspicious, I fell prey to something known as “the halo effect,” where people with credibility in one domain are often thought to be credible experts in other domains. Theranos had funders and board members who had extremely prestigious backgrounds in government and the military. Even though none of them had a background in laboratory medicine, I assumed they had done sufficient due diligence on the technology.

A big challenge in health: The body is so complex that none of us can be experts in everything. As a physician, if I order an MRI on a patient, I rely on the radiologist to interpret it correctly. When I order a blood test, I rely on laboratory technicians to use validated methods and equipment. I could not do my job if I had to personally verify that everyone knows what they are doing and is doing it correctly. The need to trust others is even greater for patients, because most of them will not be medical experts.

The problem, of course, is that humans are imperfect. Medical errors are, unfortunately, quite common. Someone did something wrong—errors of commission; someone forgot to do something—errors of omission. And, because medical knowledge is always growing, something correct 10 years ago may be incorrect now. I always encourage people to ask their healthcare providers questions and, with major health decisions, to seek second opinions.

How do you decide when and whom to trust? Here are a few questions you can ask whether you are a patient talking to a doctor or interpreting health claims online.

First, is this an area where you have real knowledge and experience? If so, give your own judgment more weight than you are inclined to, even when the person across from you is more confident than you are.

Second, does their authority come from the relevant field? A famous name, a large social media following, or impressive business credentials do not mean they understand how a vaccine works or what a blood test measures.

Third, is the other person offering evidence or only certainty? Consider asking, “What is the evidence that supports this approach? Is this what expert guidelines recommend?” Trustworthy experts can explain their reasoning or point you to high-quality studies or published guidelines to check their claims.

Fourth, has anyone independently checked? Has it been approved by the FDA or another government regulatory agency? Or do major professional organizations recommend it?

We need our health experts and institutions to see everything—the methods and the data—to make sure medications, vaccines, and other health interventions are safe and effective. But when someone asks for our trust and won’t back it up with data, we need to consider trusting our own judgment or asking for a second opinion.

What was really behind Wednesday's monster rally? Trump quickly making peace

📅 2026-11-04  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The stock market skyrocketed on Wednesday on the heels of a decisive sweep for Democrats in the 2026 midterm elections. But that was not necessarily the only driver of the climb.

"President Trump's acknowledgement of the Democrats' victory in the midterms and his expressed openness to collaborating with the opposition have helped markets to rally on hopes that contestation of the results and more adverse polarization can be avoided," said Thomas Hempell, Generali Investments senior economist.

Trump told The New York Times and The Wall Street Journal overnight Tuesday that he accepted the results and would work with Democrats. Those headlines coincided with a rebound in stock market futures.

In the lead-up to this year's midterms, some on Wall Street grew increasingly concerned about the prospect of Trump contesting the election results. The Department of Justice's Alaska election lawsuit led to fears over delays with the outcome that would exacerbate the uneasiness in the market that's typically removed once the elections conclude.

CNBC's Fed Survey showed that among 30 fund managers, strategists and economists, 63% believe that the extended uncertainty around the balance of power created by legal challenges to the election results posed "some" market risk. That was a slight increase from 61% in September. Additionally, 14% believed it represented "significant" market risk, which was up from 7%.

Now, investors are breathing a sigh of relief over Trump's acceptance of the results, which should "act as a break on extreme policy outcomes and bring down risk premia," so long as the president's decision remains, said George Saravelos, head of FX Research at Deutsche Bank.

Trump has a history of rallying markets, as well as driving them lower, with his announcements. Jay Woods of Freedom Capital Markets, who noted that the rebound in stocks overnight was partially due to the president's "initial tone and reaction to the results," said the focus will shift to whether Trump's latest move will actually have staying power.

"I think Trump is going to try to do and say the right thing, but at two in the morning, he may change his mind," the chief market strategist said. "He is an X factor."

Woods added that he's "encouraged" by Wednesday's massive rally, but he noted that there are still headwinds that could plague the market over the coming weeks — such as concerns about inflationary pressures as a result of higher energy prices from the conflict in the Middle East.

"How Trump reacts to it will help us navigate those headwinds," he said.

If Trump "continues to play nice" with Democrats, Osaic's Philip Blancato believes the market is going to have a "very strong" end to the year.

Stocks historically tend to rally after the midterms anyway, but this time around, some were worried the solid run-up in the S&P 500 this year was going to potentially hinder those gains. After Trump's reaction, Blancato doesn't think the S&P 500 hitting 8,000 by year-end is totally out of the question, saying there's "a lot predicated on him, but not completely."

The broad market index gained more than 1% on Wednesday, with its closing price implying a roughly 4% gain to that level.

However, investors should be prepared for any future volatility if his rhetoric does not remain the same, the chief market strategist cautioned.

"The president's always going to be a wildcard," he said. "Let's not let one day make a year. This is an instant reaction to this thing. There's still plenty of things to be hashed out."
