Is There a Hookup to Relationship Pipeline?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yes, it’s true: my relationship started off as a hookup. My boyfriend—who I now love, am fully committed to, and have way too much fun in Costco with—used to send me 2AM “WYD” texts and avoided any sort of conversation about the future.

Yes, I made it out of the situationship trenches. I’ll hold for applause.

While I would like to pretend that I’m better than everyone else, the reality is that there is no secret method to turning your situationship into a real relationship. Sometimes—like in my case—it’s just science.

A 2018 study revealed that sexual desire can actually encourage people to engage in behaviors that help initiate romantic relationships. Through different activities like slow dancing, researchers found that sexual attraction made people want to get physically closer, mirror each other’s behaviors, become more emotionally responsive, and even become more helpful.

We often hear of relationships that begin with emotional connection and then turn into something more romantic (and eventually physical), but the other side of the coin exists—and I’m proof of it.

“Sometimes physical attraction is what initially brings two people together, and that attraction creates opportunities to spend more time together, get to know each other, and potentially discover a deeper emotional connection,” licensed psychologist Dr Matthew Leahy tells Cosmopolitan. “What starts as something purely physical can open the door to something much more meaningful.”

I started hooking up with my now-partner purely because he was hot. Turns out, he was great in bed, too. And because we enjoyed having sex so much, we just ended up spending a lot of time together—so that we could have more of said sex, of course. Eventually, in between water breaks and refueling with Uber Eats orders, we got to know each other. I remember being shocked at what a great guy he ended up being—you know, when we actually took the time to get off each other and talk. Eventually, the booty calls turned into regular phone calls, and late night hookups turned into week-long sleepovers.

But our romantic relationship wasn’t inevitable just because we were sexually attracted to each other. We had to have a lot of conversations about our values, our hopes, and most importantly, what we wanted out of a relationship.

There were also a lot of check-ins I had to do with myself. The sex was great, so I had to constantly make sure I wasn’t confusing a sexual connection for an emotional one (AKA being “dickmatized”). Dr Leahy explains that one way to do this is to ask yourself objective questions about the other person: Are they asking you questions? Is the communication strong? Are they paying attention to you? Do you have things in common besides sexual desires?

“I think it’s good to always take a step back, have some self-awareness, and truly ask yourself: Is this going somewhere? Do I want it to go somewhere, or am I OK with it the way it is?”

Coming to terms with the reality of the situation is hard, but the hardest part of it all? You have to actually put yourself out there—especially if you really want to be with that person.

“Communicating how you truly feel about somebody and what you’re looking for can happen at any time in a relationship,” Dr. Leahy says. But when you know you’re falling hard for someone, the sooner you express those things the better.

I think it’s important to take a beat and remind you that developing feelings for someone you’re constantly being sexually intimate with is totally normal. I know we’re doomed to a dating era of nonchalance, but there’s nothing wrong with liking someone you’re exchanging bodily fluids with—especially if you’re spending a lot of time together.

You just have to know the difference between when your hookup is veering into a romantic connection and when it’s purely physical.

Before you send this article to your situationship, there’s a reality check that needs to happen. Movies like Friends With Benefits and No Strings Attached made us believe that any situation where we’re hooking up with someone we like as a person is bound to lead to us falling in love. I’m sorry to tell you this isn’t true, and he won’t be waiting for you with a flashmob.

I had situationships before my current boyfriend that went nowhere but down the toilet. While the sexual chemistry was there, nothing else quite was.

So how do you tell when something is just a hookup versus the potential of something more?

“It’s important to pay attention to how the other person responds when you communicate what you’re looking for,” Dr. Leahy says.

My partner and I actually stopped talking for a while after I first initiated a conversation about wanting more than just sex. He was very honest with me and said that he wasn’t in the place in his life to pursue a commitment. I had my answer and could decide what I wanted to do with that information. Knowing that I couldn’t just continue to hook up with him without my feelings getting complicated, I cut things off.

“If you’ve been clear about wanting something more and the other person isn’t showing that same interest, you have to be willing to recognize that,” Dr. Leahy says.

We went our separate ways and grew as people. Years later, we reconnected and found ourselves on the same page.

While I’d love to tell you that this is how it always works out in the end—it’s simply not true. Unfortunately, sometimes no matter how much we like someone—despite having intense sexual chemistry—nothing comes of it. Even if you both do have romantic feelings, you both need to be in a place where you want to pursue a relationship. And had I let myself linger in the liminal space of hooking up with someone I had romantic feelings for, I wouldn’t even be in the right headspace to be in the healthy relationship I’m in now—even if it was with someone different.

“Sometimes we get caught up in hoping a relationship will become something different, rather than accepting what it actually is,” Dr. Leahy says. “You can’t convince somebody to want the same kind of relationship you do, and that’s an important part of knowing when it might be time to move on.”

So if you’re currently hooking up with someone you’re starting to develop feelings for, maybe it’s time to have a talk with yourself, and then them. You’ll either end up in a relationship, or better off for knowing that they don’t feel the same way.

Could Your GLP-1s Make You Blind? Here’s What to Make of the FDA’s Latest Notice.

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

For years, there have been rumblings and even research about a rare but serious condition that’s been linked to taking a GLP-1 medication. Now, the Food and Drug Administration is doing something about it.

The FDA just released a drug safety communication to warn patients and healthcare providers about the potential increased risk of developing non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) when you take a GLP-1 medication. NAION is a rare condition that can lead to vision loss in one eye, and it can be permanent.

Again, this isn't the first time concerns have been raised about the risk of NAION. But it is the first time the FDA has issued a warning about it.

Given that up to 11 percent of Americans currently take a GLP-1 for weight loss, this news stands to impact a lot of people. How concerned should you be if you're one of them? Eye experts and a doctor who prescribes GLP-1s break it down.

Meet the experts: Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system; Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute; and Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

The FDA's notice is designed to flag the potential risk of NAION to people who use GLP-1s. The FDA also notes that the agency will require all manufacturers of GLP-1 medications to add information about the risk of NAION to the Warnings and Precautions section of their labels.

"Patients and caregivers should be aware of a potential increased risk of NAION, a rare condition that can result in acute, painless, decreased vision in one eye, including permanent vision loss, with GLP-1 RA use," the notice reads.

The agency cited a 2024 study published in JAMA Ophthalmology as well as its own meta-analyses and retrospective cohort study in the notice. They also noted that “NAION cases have been reported for all currently marketed GLP-1 RA products.”

However, the complication remains rare: incidents from the FDA’s meta-analysis were just 6.4 events per 100,000 per year for patients using the medications.

The warning is just that—a warning to patients and physicians that this could happen, explains Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system. "Most outcome-related issues in medicine should be thought of as being probabilistic—there is a certain chance that events can occur," he says. "This warning indicates that the FDA has reason to believe, at least at this time, that the probability of NAION occurring is higher in patients who take GLP-1 RA drugs."

But Dr. Rizzo stresses that there's no evidence that these drugs cause NAION—just that there's a link.

So…why issue a warning if it's not proven that GLP-1s cause NAION and it's so rare? "The severity of a complication matters alongside its frequency," says Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute. "Even a rare risk deserves attention when it can permanently affect vision." The warning is likely designed to give healthcare providers a heads-up that it could be NAION if a patient has symptoms, he explains.

"It's their duty to report any adverse effects, just so people are aware of them," says Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

While NAION is scary, doctors say it's important to put the data in perspective. "It's extremely rare, but it can happen," Dr. Ali says. Given how much GLP-1s can help with blood sugar management, weight loss, and downstream health effects, Dr. Ali says that the risk of NAION is "not a reason not to seek these medications."

Dr. Rizzo agrees. "I essentially never discourage the general use of GLP-1 RA drugs," he says. "Ultimately, the risk-to-benefit assessment must be decided by the patient, but physicians should provide sufficient information to the patients to better inform their decisions."

People should stay aware of the risk without panicking, Dr. Youssefzadeh says. "It's also worth remembering that the people most likely to be prescribed these drugs—people with diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnea—already carry a higher baseline risk for NAION, so the risk isn't starting from zero," he adds.

As I get ever closer to finishing my hike, I must take on the northern Pennsylvania section notorious for its endless rocks.

A problem for future me. That is what I thought to myself as I finished my hike up and over, and then up and over again, Mount Moosilauke. I had finished the Whites, that meant the rest of the Appalachian Trail would be a cake walk. There was just one problem, northern Pennsylvania. Consistently rated the worst part of the AT not because of steep climbs, but because of the rocks. Throughout my time on the AT, I’d heard tales of this section, and some rather tall ones at that. I pushed the thought out of my mind. It would take me weeks to get there. Including New Hampshire, it was six states away. Well, now I am officially starting northern Pennsylvania. The problem for future me has become the problem for current me. To make matters worse, there was rain in the forecast overnight into tomorrow. It wasn’t just any old rocks I’d be contending with, it would be wet rocks. Lucky me.

I originally planned to drive across the Delaware River to the Dunnfield Creek Trailhead where I’d turned around yesterday. Since it was a lighter mileage day I decided to keep my van where it was and walk north to the trailhead, turn around and continue past the van southbound. This would add some miles to today, but trim them from tomorrow. It would also save a bit of toll expense since hikers aren’t charged for crossing the bridge. I made my way onto the bridge, briefly crossing back into New Jersey and along River Road. I was back at the van before 9:00AM and ready to tackle the climb up Mount Minsi.

On the bridge through the Delaware Water Gap.

Briefly back into New Jersey before leaving for the last time.

The trail passed the small Lenape Lake before ascending the south side of the Delaware Water Gap. Eureka Creek flowed across the Appalachian Trail which continued climbing up Mount Minsi until it reached a few rocky outcrops presenting hikers with a view high above the Delaware Water Gap. I had a perfectly clear view of Mount Tammany, with its rock slides corkscrewing upward. The trail turned along the ridge, relieving me of any more vertical steps. A dirt road made for easy hiking while passing by great views of the valley to the southeast. Soon the trail turned off the dirt road and I had my first encounter with the rocks of northern Pennsylvania. While small, they stretched on for far longer than any other rocky patch on the AT. I couldn’t hike at my usual pace, I would have twisted my ankle in no time. I paid extra close attention to navigating through the rocky maze, moving left and right to find the best spots to put my feet. The constant back and forth made it a chore to keep my momentum up. Still, it was more of an annoyance than a dreadful experience, at first.

The trail follows a gravel road briefly after Lenape Lake.

Looking across the Delaware Water Gap at Mount Tammany.

Crossing the road at Wolf Gap took a bit of courage as there is no line of sight down either side of the crest. I waited for the passing cars to clear and then listened for any new traffic approaching before jogging across the roadway. I was now within striking distance of Wold Rocks. As is customary, I stayed on the AT over Wolf Rocks, even though there is a bypass in case the weather is rainy and windy. That might be the case tomorrow, but all I had today were blue skies. This was the first scramble of the day, which only lasted a minute or so. What followed was a sloped rocky ridge with views to the northwest. Honestly, if it weren’t for the reputation of this section of the trail, I wouldn’t have thought the rocks were that bad, and it made sense for the trail to come up here for the view. I am sure more challenging terrain lay ahead.

Sure enough, this was about where things got more trying. The trail became something of a dense field of jagged rocks sticking up from the earth. The trail went on like this for mile after mile with only brief reprieves to be found. I was managing, but I cringed when thinking of what it would be like tomorrow in wet conditions. I fought my way to Wind Gap, a gap in the ridge that was thankfully not all that windy today. I checked in on a few more viewpoints before continuing the trek about 2 miles past the Leroy A. Smith shelter. Knowing there was finite daylight remaining I turned around to investigate the shelter.

Following the ridge in northern Pennsylvania.

I was struggling to decide where to spend the night. I was going to reach the Leroy A. Smith shelter with some daylight left. If the shelter was empty it might be a good idea to stay in it. I’d avoid a wet tent, but I’d have a longer hike back to the Delaware water gap. I also have had a lingering feeling ever since I passed on staying at the secret shelter that aid be able to get a much earlier start heading out from a shelter than my tent. Packing up camp requires light and warmth. It’s possible to pack up with just my headlamp, but there are a lot of things that could be accidentally left behind like tent stakes. It would be much easier with everything in plain sight in a shelter. If the goal was to finish hiking as early as possible, it might make more sense to stay in the shelter rather than hike a few miles up the trail and camp.

In the end, the decision was made for me. Someone had set up in the shelter already. Two dog bowls suggested that they either had a dog or unconventional eating habits. I dropped my backpack at the shelter and headed down to the spring. I encountered a man and a young dog at the spring. Both seemed friendly enough. The dog reminded me a lot of the dogs I used to foster when I lived in Maryland. It looked tough on the outside, but after a few good ear scratches it melted into a pup as gentle as they come.

I drank a liter of water at the spring and collected two more to get me through the morning. The pipe spring gushed water like a champ. It was quite a hike up from the spring. I noticed that water is rarely on the trail in this section because the mountain is so narrow. I’ll need to expect fewer streams right in front of shelters and more steep treks for water over the next week or so.

View from the trail near the Leroy A. Smith Shelter.

Back on the trail I had limited time to find a camping spot before light ran out. It seemed cruel that now that I was finally back in a state that allows camping along the trail with few restrictions, the areas near the trail rarely seemed fit for tenting. The trail was rocky, and so was all the flat ground near the trail. I was only sure of one spot about 2.5 miles back, I wasn’t going to make it there unless I wanted to retrieve my headlamp from my pack, which I was starting to consider. Before I could, I spotted what logged to be a suitable spot. I turned off the trail and proceeded to walk over the area, checking for sticks and rocks. It felt decent, so I rolled out my tent and felt the ground with my hands to see if there would be any lumps where my tent floor would be. I removed a few more rocks and sticks but was soon satisfied that the spot would work. I finished pitching the tent while my water filtered in the background. It wasn’t easy to get the stakes into the ground. A couple were more stuck between rocks than buried in the ground, but they seemed like they would hold. I went to bed hoping that the precipitation superintendent would cancel the rain tomorrow.

American Cancer Society revises guidelines, urges routine PSA screening for prostate cancer

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Annalisa (Nalis) Merelli is focused on boys’ and men’s health: She looks into reproductive health, trending treatments, screening approaches, mental health, and everything in between.

In a major change to screening guidelines, the American Cancer Society recommended on Thursday that American men between ages 50 and 69 at an average risk of prostate cancer should test their prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood levels every two years.

The prior guidelines, released in 2010, left testing for average-risk men to their discretion, after assessing cancer risk with a physician.

Similarly, PSA testing recommendations were strengthened for high risk groups — Black men and men with a strong family history of prostate cancer. They are now encouraged to get PSA-based screening yearly starting at 45. Black men in particular disproportionately die from prostate cancer, and the guideline authors said they hope that increased surveillance will reduce the number of Black patients presenting with cancers at advanced stages.

Then, starting at 70, the decision on whether to continue screening becomes individualized as the balance of benefit and harm is not as clear after that age. Screening should be interrupted after 80, or in men who are not expected to live another 10 years.

The changes reflect recent findings that screening through PSA levels reduced disease specific mortality for certain men, bringing the indicator back into specialists’ good graces. Once a preferred biomarker to identify risk of prostate cancer, routine PSA screening had fallen out of favor — and was dropped from the guidelines in 2010 — for its lack of specificity, which resulted in many men undergoing follow-up procedures when they had no cancer, or very slow-growing tumors.

The U.S. Preventive Service Task Force, a federally appointed panel of outside experts, has not changed its guidelines since the new evidence became available in 2025, and continues to recommend against routine, population-wide PSA screening, suggesting instead individualized decision-making. But the panel, whose recommendations determine the preventive services that insurers must cover, has announced an upcoming update.

The cutoff for PSA levels to be considered high is established as 3.0 nanograms/milliliter or higher for men aged 45 to 69 years old. This is an important clarification, said James Eastham, chief of urology at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. “Most PSA blood tests in the United States, when they’re reported, say under 4.0 [ng/ml] is normal, but it really should be under 3,” he said. “I think [the update] will hopefully help primary care physicians refer folks a little bit earlier.”

To limit the risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment, the guidelines also now recommend two elevated PSA readings, followed by an MRI scan and/or testing for other biomarkers of prostate cancer, with the aim of reducing the number of patients undergoing biopsies. “We just wanted to give a little bit of flexibility for folks who cannot easily obtain an MRI,” said William Dahut, chief scientific officer at the ACS and a contributing author of the guidelines, about including both biomarkers and MRIs as a possible step following elevated PSA levels. However, he noted that an MRI would still be recommended prior to biopsy, should a biomarker test come back abnormal.

Though there are a variety of biomarkers, the guidelines don’t recommend one over the other. Dahut said ACS researchers  intend to look more closely at biomarkers to establish which may be more effective in predicting a more aggressive cancer.

“I think the new guidelines are certainly a balanced approach for early detection of prostate cancer,” said Eastham. “There have been many men undergoing unnecessary biopsies, and many low risk cancers that were subsequently overtreated,” he said. By recommending some steps between an elevated PSA and a biopsy, he said, the new recommendations reflect the evidence that while being a good biomarker, PSA alone would identify too many men as biopsy candidates.

Finally, the guidelines emphasize the importance of active surveillance in men with slow growing cancer: “If you screen more men, you’re going to find more men with early-stage disease, and we need to be strong in our position that that does not mean that you need to rush for therapies or that you need to have therapies at all.”

For Eddie Baynes, a 56-year-old prostate cancer survivor and advocate, the new guidelines “hit the nail on the head” when it comes to recommending routine PSA screening. As a Black man, he knew he was at higher risk of developing prostate cancer, yet it wasn’t until the fraternity he belonged to organized a screening event that he, at 52, started getting regular PSA tests with his bloodwork. Amid soaring PC hardware prices, gaming monitors are getting cheaper and cheaper. Here's why, and what to buy

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hello, good local-time-zone-adjusted afternoon, and let's just say it's been a while. But I come back to you now at the turn of the technological tide. Well, sort of. Sadly I'm not here to predict an end to the ridiculous AI-fuelled chip crisis. That whole mess has a couple of years to run, surely.

Instead I speak of screens, where there is actually some very good news. In short, have you seen how cheap PC gaming monitors are? I mean remarkably, almost stupendously, cheap. I'm talking £60 / $60 for an entry-level 1080p panel with triple-digit refresh rate, sub-£200 / $250 for high-refresh 4K gaming. Heck, even OLED monitors now start with a "2" in both currencies.

Also bear in mind we've had a fair old bit of inflation in recent years. A 4K panel bought today for £200 is equivalent to paying roughly £150 back in 2020. All the while, that same £200 / $200 right now won't buy you a current-model gaming graphics card at all, even the most feeble example, and will barely secure a basic dual-channel 16GB DDR5 memory kit. Youch.

So, I think we can all agree monitors are cheap in the current chip-catastrophe context. The immediate question is why? The answer isn't all that complicated, actually. Most obviously, the display market isn't being devoured by the AI craze. All those data centres are stuffed with GPUs, memory chips and NAND flash, not monitors.

By way of example, one of the largest new AI data centres on Earth is Microsoft's new Fairwater site in Mount Pleasant, Wisconsin. It's thought to pack hundreds of thousands of Nvidia Blackwell GPUs. The cost doesn't bear thinking about, but those GPUs are built into what's known as "headless" server racks. In other words, they have no screens at all. Ultimately, the key compute hardware is all managed remotely, in which capacity the Fairwater facility is reported to currently have around 375 employees. If we generously assume each has two PC monitors, that's only 750 for the entire site.

AI isn't really driving demand on the end-user side of the equation, either. Put it this way: you don't need to buy a new phone, laptop or PC to access the latest models. At the same time, the display market is suffering from something of a glut of overcapacity. Chinese companies built out huge capacity in LCD panel manufacturing over the past decade, to the extent factories are now running underutilised. Meanwhile, even the OLED market is arguably oversupplied.

In fact, Samsung and LG, the two big noises in large OLED panel manufacturing (as opposed to OLED displays for tablets and phones), have been shifting emphasis away from TV panels to PC monitors because the latter offer better margins. Meanwhile, the AI boom has almost definitely meant lower demand for new PCs. Certainly, the big research houses point to cratering PC sales thanks to higher component costs. Long story short, that's oodles of capacity combined with weakening demand. Like I said, the explanation for cheap monitors isn't exactly rocket surgery, if you know what I mean.

What is a bit more complicated is making sense of what's available today in the gaming monitor market. With so much choice, where exactly is the very best value to be found? And are there any remaining pain points?

Monitor specifications, to be sure, can be tricksy at the best of times. But if you're looking for some blanket rules to fling casually over the whole LCD monitor market - I'll be digging deeper into OLED tech another day - try these for size. Look for, at minimum, 300 nits brightness and 1ms response times. Refresh rates are a slightly more complicated call, but for the vast majority of games, 144Hz is plenty. For "esports-grade multiplayer fare like Counter-Strike or Dota 2, or I think a refresh in the high 200s will get the job done for most. If you'll benefit from more than that, you almost certainly know who you are already.

Oh, and heavily favour IPS over VA panels. Exceptions exist: there are some decent "Rapid VA" panels these days and Samsung, in particular, does some nippy VA models. But a lot of the cheaper stuff is pretty slow and smeary, making VA a risky buy. Finally, don't worry too much about claimed HDR capability, save for the fact that HDR 400 certification at least means the monitor can do 400 nits. Unless a monitor has local dimming, it won't be truly HDR capable.

Anywho, one immediate and rather ironic issue is the growing mismatch between monitors and GPUs. For sure, it's great you can grab a high-refresh 4K monitor for such little money. It's almost hard to believe that you can have this 27-inch IPS MSI for sub-£200 (you're looking at $250 for the same model in the US), and it even has a 288Hz 1080p dual mode.

I actually favour 32 inches with my 4K gaming, and that's naturally a little pricier, especially if you insist on an IPS - which again, you probably should. But even $350 for that kind of panel (here's a comparable model for £340 in the UK) is a fraction of what you'd have paid for a similarly specced model a few years ago. And it probably wouldn't have included dual-mode functionality.

Here's the problem, though. How are you going to drive the thing, what with its eight million-plus pixels? After all, the cheapest RTX 5090 on Newegg right now is over $6,500. So high-end Nvidia GPUs now cost about as much as a decent used car.

Of course, you might be surprised just how effective the likes of an AMD RX 9070 XT or Nvidia RTX 5070-class GPU is at 4K thanks to upscaling technologies including DLSS and FSR. The latest transformer model upscalers really do knock out pretty stellar image quality. Add in a bit of frame gen and you have a compelling combination of visual sharpness, reasonably low latency and nice smoothframe rates, even in the most demanding of games. Perhaps the one thing you can't do is enable full path tracing in a game like Cyberpunk 2077 and still expect a slick experience, but standard ray tracing remains an option.

The bigger snag, unsurprisingly, is that you can no longer bag a 5070 at (let alone below) its original £539 / $549 MSRP. Instead, you're looking at the thick end of £800 / $900. That's a bit of an ask for pairing with a £200 monitor, isn't it?

I'd therefore argue that, for pure gaming purposes, the bang-for-buck sweet spot remains just where it's been sat twiddling its thumbs for the better part of a decade, namely 1440p. That applies to both its vanilla 16:9 aspect variety and my personally preferred ultrawide format, the latter being so very immersive and also pretty nifty for daily multi-tasking. Indeed, the 27-inch 1440p segment even brings the vibrant colours and infinite contrast of OLED panels into a reasonably affordable window.

By way of example, £179 (or $150) is enough for a circa 200Hz, IPS 1440p panel with super low (by IPS standards) pixel response, even if the 250-nit brightness is lower than I'd like. Meanwhile, as I write these words, pricing for big-brand 27-inch 1440p gaming OLEDs kicks off at about £290 in the UK and roughly $270 in the US. Admittedly, those entry-level OLEDs come with some compromises, which I'll cover in a forthcoming post. But what you might call 'proper' OLED panels can be had around the low £300 / $300 mark - not throw-away money, for sure, but relatively accessible.

The 34-inch 1440p ultrawide segment is a bit trickier, however. The good news is that conventional LCD models can be had for relative peanuts, with prices starting at about £220 or $249 for game-focused, high-refresh models. But those two example both use a VA panel, which usually means inferior pixel response.

IPS models are a little thin on the ground when it comes to 34-inch ultrawide, but sneaking in below £300 / $300 is generally possible. The real bummer is OLED, where going ultrawide invokes a massive premium. The best price I can find on a big-brand 34-inch ultrawide today is $629 for this Asus model in the US and £648 for this MSI option in the UK.

That's awfully toppy, especially when you consider that you can grab a 49-inch, 5,120x1,440 ultrawide OLED for barely any more money. I do actually quite like those 49-inch ultrawides, but I have to concede the ergonomics are challenging. However, 4K OLEDs in 27-inch and 32-inch formats can also be had for as much as £100 / $100 less than the current cheapest 34-inch 1440p ultrawides, which doesn't make much sense to me.

Again, with 4K, there's the challenge of keeping all those pixels updated. If you already have a sufficiently mighty graphics card, though, I a 32-inch 4K OLED can make a fantastic all-round option, and something like this MSI model for £549 would be absolutely lovely. You get good pixel density and lots of space for daily desktopping, plus OLED visuals and a large enough panel to be really get you into your games.

It's just a pity that 34-inch OLED ultrawides remain so weirdly expensive, it being a format that ticks so many different boxes. As for my own personal money-no-object pick, well, I'm typing these words on the new Alienware AW3926QW, the 39-inch 5K2K beast with a Tandem WOLED panel and RGB subpixels.

One could argue it's a relative steal at £1,079 or $1,100. I mean, it's pretty much peak current PC gaming display technology, and it's about half the price of what Nvidia's second-tier RTX 5080 graphics card is going for lately. Assuming you can even find one in stock.

At this point, the one major category I haven't covered is LCD monitors with mini-LED full-array local dimming. Cards on the table, I'm not a huge fan of mini-LED monitors. The detailed reasons for that are, perhaps, the subject for another day. But I've reviewed countless models and have yet to see one that didn't come with pretty serious compromises.

That said, the technology has gotten a lot more accessible in the last 12 to 18 months, to the extent that 27-inch 1440p models start at just over $250. I've had that specific model in and came away pretty impressed. It's very, very punchy, and even factoring in the limitations of mini-LED versus OLED when it comes to lighting control and speed, I cannot deny the temptation.

Just for completeness, one further exception to the general rule of cheap gaming monitors involves ultra-high refresh monitors aimed at serious esports aficionados. I'm talking about panels like this 1,000Hz LG I sampled elsewhere. At £800 / $1,000 for a 25-inch 1080p IPS panel it's inevitably a niche proposition, and poor value by normal standards.

I think a much better compromise for just about everyone, short of an actual esports pro, is to grab a larger, higher resolution monitor that offers one of those windowed 1080p modes running at about 500Hz or thereabouts. That way you've got a nice high-res panel for both work and play, as well as that ultra-low latency mode for when you want to dance around Dust 2 doling out the headshots or whatever.

Incidentally, I realise I've gone in a bit hot and heavy with the OLED content here, the implicit assumption being OLED is all that, and notably without addressing the obvious objections like the risk of burn-in. But all that, I hope you'll be pleased to hear, is very much the subject for my next RPS post on all things OLED.

Until then, all I can say is thank goodness PC monitors provide relative respite from the parlous state of much of the rest of the PC component market. It's absolutely brutal out there if you're building a gaming PC, but at least you'll get a lot of screen for your money.

Why AI Could Change The Way Utilities Modernize Their Customer Information Systems

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Steve Dawson is the founder and CEO at POWERCONNECT.AI.

​For decades, electric, water and gas utilities have approached Customer Information System (CIS) modernization with a familiar strategy: When technology becomes outdated, replace it.

That decision can initiate lengthy procurement processes, complex data migrations, investments and years of implementation. Yet artificial intelligence (AI) offers another possibility: improving how employees and customers interact with enterprise systems without necessarily replacing the platforms responsible for billing millions of customers.

The next generation of CIS modernization may focus less on replacement and more on unlocking the value already embedded in utility technology.

Many utilities operate CIS platforms that have served them reliably for decades. These systems manage customer accounts, billing, payments, meter readings and service orders. They also contain rate structures, business rules and integrations that would be costly to replicate.

Nevertheless, customer service representatives often navigate multiple screens and applications to answer a question. The billing engine may work correctly while its user experience falls short of modern expectations. That distinction matters. Does a utility need an entirely new CIS or a better way to interact with the one it already has?

Application programming interfaces (APIs) let applications exchange information and perform authorized operations. A modern user interface can use APIs to retrieve billing histories, account balances, payment records, meter data and service requests, while the existing CIS remains the system of record.

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) adds another capability. This open standard enables AI applications to connect with external tools and data sources through a consistent interface. APIs supply underlying system functionality; MCP can expose approved capabilities to AI assistants and agents.

For example, a utility might offer tools for checking an account balance, reviewing consumption or retrieving service order status. An AI copilot could use those tools, subject to the employee’s permissions, without requiring a custom connection for every new AI experience.

MCP is not a replacement for APIs, and neither guarantees interoperability or security by itself. Utilities still need integration architecture, authentication, authorization, monitoring and clear ownership of each transaction.

Imagine a customer calling because a service transfer has not been completed. Today, a representative may need to open the CIS, locate a service order, check field activity and contact another department.

With a copilot embedded in a redesigned interface, the representative could ask: “What’s delaying this customer’s service transfer?”

The copilot could gather authorized information from connected systems, identify an outstanding inspection or pending work order and present the next step for review. It could also explain which source supports its answer, making the response easier to verify.

This approach can support billing inquiries, payment questions and high-consumption investigations. The objective is not to let AI invent answers or bypass established workflows. It is to reduce navigation and give employees useful context when needed.

Copilots generally help people interpret information and prepare actions. Agentic AI can go further by coordinating steps across systems. A governed agent might collect information needed for a service request, validate required fields, prepare an update and route it for approval. Connected through APIs or MCP tools, it could help bridge billing, payment and field operations applications.

But greater capability brings greater responsibility. High-impact actions should require approvals, with safeguards against incorrect data, unauthorized requests and unexpected behavior. Human oversight must match the operational consequences of the task.

Utilities handle sensitive customer information and operate critical services. Introducing AI should not mean copying entire customer databases into another platform.

Where architecture allows, an intelligent interface can request only the information necessary for an authorized task, leaving core records within existing systems. This may reduce duplication, although temporary processing, logging, vendors and model-provider retention policies still need careful review.

Security controls should include encryption, role-based access, audit trails, tool permissions, prompt-injection defenses and transaction validation. The same employee who cannot change an account in the CIS should not gain that power simply by asking an AI assistant.

A general-purpose chatbot may write fluent answers without understanding utility operations. Accurate billing explanations can depend on rate schedules, meter intervals, estimated reads, seasonal usage and local policies.

That is why AI must be grounded in authorized source data and utility-specific rules. Responses should distinguish verified facts from suggestions, and representatives should be able to inspect the evidence behind recommendations.

A polished answer is not necessarily a correct answer. For utilities, trust depends on traceability and operational accuracy.

Timing also matters. By the time an organization releases an RFP for a new CIS, leadership may already have determined that replacement is the answer. Alternative approaches can be harder to consider once requirements and budgets reflect that assumption.

Utilities should carefully assess API readiness, integration constraints, user experience, security and AI opportunities before deciding whether replacement is necessary. Some aging platforms genuinely lack vendor support, essential functionality or a viable security posture. Those systems may need replacement.

Others may support incremental improvements that preserve billing logic, minimize migration risk and provide value sooner. A disciplined assessment should compare both paths against outcomes, cost and maintainability.

The traditional CIS will likely remain central to accurate billing, financial transactions and customer records. What changes is the way people access those capabilities. Modern interfaces can simplify everyday work. APIs can connect systems. MCP can standardize access to approved AI tools. Copilots can explain information, while agentic workflows can coordinate controlled tasks.

Success should be measured by resolution times, accuracy, customer satisfaction, employee productivity and reliability, not by how much software gets replaced. Before asking, “When should we replace our CIS?” Utility leaders should ask, “What could we improve with systems we own?”

Modernization doesn’t begin not with replacement but rather with a more intelligent way to use what is already there.
