The Customer Paid. Then Silence: Five Updates a Small Business Forgets to Send

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The customer paid on Monday. By Friday, silence. The update lives in your head, not in a system — you meant to send it, you know exactly what it would have said, and the week filled up before you sent it. The customer is not angry yet. They are doing something worse: they are wondering. Wondering whether the money arrived, whether the work started, whether the date still holds, whether anyone is there at all. A paid customer who hears nothing starts to feel like a forgotten one, and a forgotten customer is one message away from becoming someone else's customer.

This article was drafted with AI assistance. It is written in plain English for people who run businesses, not for people who build software, and it makes no claim about any particular product. It is about the question this morning's post asked — what update is still waiting? — and it works through five updates a small business forgets to send, using the tools people already use, so you can check my working and tell me where I got it wrong. This is the twenty-seventh morning we have written about this gap, and the gap is still the same: the work gets done, and the telling does not.

A small business does not have a follow-up problem. It has five follow-up problems, in five places, and each one looks small on its own. The confirmation is only a sentence. The progress note is only a line. The delay warning is only a message. But a customer who gets none of the five hears nothing at all, and nothing at all sounds exactly like nobody cares — even when the work is going perfectly.

Here are the five updates, numbered, each one with the tools people already use. Each one starts with something ordinary. Each one ends with the same question: who was supposed to hear this?

The money arrived on Monday. The customer knows they paid; what they do not know is whether you know. A payment confirmation is the shortest message in business — we got it, thank you, here is what happens next — and it is the one most often skipped, because the person who should send it already knows the money arrived. The customer does not know that you know. For three days they carry a small doubt: did it go through, did it go to the right place, did anyone notice?

WhatsApp is where this message belongs, because WhatsApp is where the customer already is. A sentence in the same thread as the order, the same day as the payment, closes the loop before the doubt starts. A paper receipt does the same job for the customer standing in front of you — it is proof in their hand, and it costs ten seconds. The confirmation is not paperwork. It is the moment the customer stops wondering and starts trusting, and it takes less time than the wondering does.

The job is in progress. Someone is working on it, the parts are ordered, the first step is done. The customer knows none of this, because nobody told them. From their side, the silence after payment looks identical to the silence before anything happened. They paid on Monday, and by Wednesday they have no evidence that anything changed — so they send the message every owner dreads: "Just checking in — any news?"

That message is not a complaint. It is a customer doing your job for you, because the update never arrived. A short note on the day the work starts — we have started, here is the rough shape of the week, I will message again when the next step lands — turns a week of wondering into a week of waiting, and waiting is comfortable when someone said what is happening. Gmail does this well for the customers who live in email; WhatsApp does it better for the ones who live in chat. The tool matters less than the timing: the update has to arrive before the customer has to ask for it, or it is not an update at all.

This is the update that does the most damage when it is missing, and the one owners put off longest, because nobody enjoys sending bad news. The date moved. The part is late, the diary slipped, the supplier has not called back. The customer still has the old date in their head, and every day that passes without a new one makes the old date feel like a promise that was broken on purpose.

A delay notice sent early is not bad news. It is respect. "The date has moved to Thursday, here is why, here is what I am doing about it" turns a broken promise into a kept one — the customer was told, in time, by the person doing the work. A delay notice sent late, or never, turns the same delay into a discovery: the customer finds out on the day the job was supposed to happen, from the silence, and no explanation after that sounds like one. Send this one first, before the others, and send it the moment you know. The customer can forgive a moved date. They cannot forgive finding out last.

The work is finished. The order is packed, the repair is done, the document is ready to collect. The customer does not know, because the finished job is sitting in the same silence as the unfinished one. This is the strangest of the five, because the hard part is over and the only thing left is a sentence — and the sentence is the thing that gets forgotten, because the person who finished the job has already moved on to the next one.

"It's ready, here is how to collect it, here is what to check" is a message that ends the job properly. Without it, the customer waits for a call that never comes, and the finished work waits on a shelf for a customer who does not know to come. The notebook on the counter holds the jobs that are done; the message in the chat is the one that tells the customer. A finished job nobody is told about is a job that is not finished yet, in the only way that matters to the person who paid for it.

A week later, nobody has asked. The job was done, the money was paid, the customer went quiet — and the silence after a finished job reads, from the customer's side, exactly like the silence after a forgotten one. They do not know whether you remember them, whether you would notice if something went wrong, whether they are a customer or a transaction that closed.

The check-in is the cheapest message of the five, and the one with the longest memory. "How did it go? Anything I should fix?" takes one sentence, and it is the sentence that turns a one-time buyer into a returning one — not because of what it says, but because it proves someone was still thinking about them after the money was in. It is also the sentence that finds the small problem before it becomes the lost customer: the thing that was slightly wrong, the question that never got asked, the customer who was quietly unhappy and would never have said so unprompted. A business that checks in hears the truth while it is still small.

This is the kind of work an AI workforce is for — not the clever part, the ordinary part. The payment lands and the confirmation is drafted the same day. The work starts and the note is written before the customer has to ask. The date moves and the warning goes out the moment it is known, not the day the customer discovers it. The job finishes and the "it's ready" is ready to send. A week later, the check-in is drafted and waiting. Five silences become five messages. And nothing is sent before you have seen it. You read the draft, you fix a line if it needs fixing, you say yes — and the customer hears from you the same day, not the next week.

The relief is not that a machine wrote faster. It is that the telling stopped depending on a free minute at the end of a long day. You still decide what counts as ready, what counts as late, and what counts as worth saying. You still approve every word that leaves. The difference is that the drafting is done before you sit down, so the minutes you have are spent deciding — not remembering, at 11pm, the five messages that never went out.

The delay notice goes first, because it is the only one where silence actively does harm. A customer who is not told the date moved will find out on the day the job was supposed to happen, and that discovery costs more than any delay ever could. Tell them the moment you know, and the delay becomes a plan instead of a surprise.

The payment confirmation goes next, because it is the easiest to send and the quickest to earn trust — one sentence, the same day as the money, and the customer stops wondering. "We've started" follows, because it stops the "just checking in" message before it is sent. "It's ready" comes after, because a finished job that nobody is told about is not finished yet. The check-in goes last, not because it matters least, but because it only works once the other four have been sent: a business that never confirmed the payment and never warned about the delay has not earned the right to ask how it went.

That order is my answer. It is also a guess, and it is the part I most want corrected.

After a customer pays, five updates go missing: the payment confirmation, the "we've started" note, the delay warning, the "it's ready" message, and the check-in a week later. Each one is a sentence, each one lives in a tool people already use — WhatsApp, Gmail, a phone call, a paper receipt, a notebook — and the gap is between the job and the telling, and the owner is the glue. Let a workforce draft the five messages as the work happens, and keep the sending in your hands: you pick the order, you approve every word, and the customer stops wondering.

So — what update is still waiting? Which of these five is yours, and which would you send first? Tell me in the comments. I would rather be corrected than agreed with.

A Default-Deny NAT Firewall on OpenBSD pf, Managed with Terraform

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at blog.bsdserver.nl

pf is the best firewall I have worked with: one readable file, atomic reloads, NAT and queueing in the same language as the filter rules. What it lacks is an API. Every tool that wants to change a pf firewall ends up templating pf.conf, copying it over SSH and hoping pfctl -f likes it.

I wrote pfapid to fix that: a small REST API for pf that runs on the firewall itself, with a Terraform provider on top. This post builds a complete NAT firewall with it: a default-deny base, outbound NAT, a port forward to a DMZ web server, a blocklist and QoS on the uplink.

The base ruleset is the part you can't afford to get wrong, so the API can't touch it. Whatever Terraform does, the block and management rules in pf.conf come first.

Tables are lists of addresses that rules refer to as . Changing a table never reloads a rule, so it's the cheap way to add a web server or block a network. A table can hold IPv4 and IPv6 entries side by side:

Each pf_anchor is one anchor, api/, and its rules are loaded as a whole: pf is order-sensitive, so a ruleset is one atomic unit, never rule by rule. Split anchors along ownership: whoever owns the DMZ web server owns dmz-web.

The rdr-to rule changes the destination of connections to the wan address on port 80 or 443 to 10.0.20.10. The same pass rule creates the state, so there is no separate redirect-then-filter step to keep in sync. The redirected packet then leaves on dmz, and the web-out rules let it through: forget those and the port forward silently goes nowhere. Those same rules let LAN clients reach the web server directly.

For IPv6 the server has its own address, so there is nothing to translate: pass it in on wan and out on dmz.

Blocking GPTBot costs you 40% of your AI citations. The data is in.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The most common GEO advice is "check your robots.txt." Most site owners assume they are fine because they never wrote a Disallow rule for GPTBot. The data says 25.8% of them are wrong.

I ran a pre-registered calibration study in October 2026. The goal was to test whether the GEO score predicts AI citations. It did not. But the study also collected a variable I did not expect to be this important: whether the site blocks at least one AI bot in robots.txt.

341 pages, sampled from the Tranco list, audited with GEO Optimizer 4.19.3. For each page, the tool reads robots.txt and checks whether any of the 27 tracked AI crawlers (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, OAI-SearchBot, and 23 more) are explicitly disallowed. The study also queried Perplexity Sonar with two templates per page and recorded whether the domain appeared in the source list.

The result was clear and not part of the pre-registered hypothesis:

That is an 18.6 percentage point gap. Sites that block AI bots get cited roughly 40% less often than sites that do not.

Most of these blocks are not deliberate decisions to keep content out of AI search. The audit data shows three common sources:

Legacy WordPress security plugins. Wordfence and Sucuri ship default rules that block unrecognized user agents. If you installed one of these in 2019 and forgot about it, GPTBot may be getting a 403 right now.

Inherited robots.txt files. Many sites copy a robots.txt from a template or a previous project. If that template was written before AI crawlers existed, it may not have Allow rules for GPTBot or OAI-SearchBot. The crawler falls through to a wildcard Disallow.

CDN bot protection. Cloudflare Bot Fight Mode, when enabled, blocks non-browser user agents at the edge. It does not touch robots.txt, so the block is invisible to any tool that only reads robots.txt. I found that 34.3% of Cloudflare-fronted sites in a 636-site sample block at least one AI crawler at the HTTP level, compared to 12.0% of non-Cloudflare sites.

In a fresh CDN-level scan of 513 reachable sites, the per-bot blocking rates are:

GPTBot is blocked more than any other AI crawler. 12 sites block only GPTBot and nothing else. That selectivity means someone read an article about OpenAI training data and added a Disallow, without realizing that GPTBot also powers ChatGPT's live web search. You can block training and allow citations by separating GPTBot (training) from OAI-SearchBot (search). Most people block both or neither.

14 sites block both GPTBot and PerplexityBot but allow Claude. That is a pattern: site owners who heard about the two most discussed crawlers and blocked them by name, without checking the full list.

If you have never checked whether AI crawlers can reach your site, this is the first thing to fix. Not llms.txt. Not schema. Not content structure. If the crawler gets a 403, every other optimization is invisible.

The fix is a robots.txt audit. Check which of the 27 AI crawlers are explicitly disallowed. If you use Cloudflare, check whether Bot Fight Mode or a WAF rule is intercepting non-browser user agents. If you use a security plugin, check its bot protection settings.

The calibration study is open. The raw data is in the geo-optimizer-skill repo. 341 rows, no domain names, shuffled order. You can reproduce the analysis.

If you want to check your site, run a free audit on geoready.dev. The audit tests all 27 AI crawlers at the robots.txt level and sends live HTTP requests as GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, and Claude-SearchBot to check for CDN-level blocking.

The AI SDK's MCP drift check doesn't hash readOnlyHint: what that would miss, counted on 14,009 servers

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at modelmarket.dev

Since version 7.0.19 the Vercel AI SDK can fingerprint the tools of an MCP server. You call fingerprintTools when you first trust a server, store the result, and on later connects detectToolDrift tells you which tools were added, removed or changed. The package it ships in, ai, was downloaded 48.3 million times in the week of 12–18 November 2026.

The fingerprint covers three members of a tool definition: the description, the input schema and the title. Annotations are not among them. A tool that said readOnlyHint: true on the day you reviewed it can say readOnlyHint: false and destructiveHint: true later, and the drift check returns three empty lists.

We run HISTOR, a public record of what MCP servers advertise and when it changes, so we counted how often that happens. Between 23 October and 16 November 2026, 524 tools on 189 servers switched readOnlyHint from true to false. For 176 of those tools the fingerprint of the tool would not have moved. For 91 of them, on 32 servers, no tool on the server changed its fingerprint in that read, so the check would have had nothing to report.

The gap itself is not our find. NerdLevelTech showed it in September on a purpose-built server, for the output schema as well, and published a fingerprint over the raw tools/list that closes it. What a test server cannot say is how often this happens on servers people actually run. That is the part counted here.

This is read from the published source of ai 7.0.177 (src/generate-text/tool-fingerprint.ts) and @ai-sdk/mcp 2.0.101 (src/tool/mcp-client.ts). In our words, not the library's code:

Two things follow. Everything outside those three members is invisible: outputSchema, the hint annotations, _meta, icons, execution, vendor members. And the top-level additionalProperties of the input schema is replaced with false before hashing, so a server that changes it from false to true hashes the same.

The choice is defensible. The description and the schema are what the model reads, so they are where an injected instruction goes, and the function's own comment calls them the server-controlled, security-relevant fields. Hints are a different kind of risk: the model does not act on them, the host does. And hosts do. The same AI SDK documentation page shows an approval policy that lets a call run without asking when readOnlyHint is true, next to a note that annotations are untrusted hints. Cursor's Bugbot only uses MCP tools that a server marks read-only, according to Cursor staff. Where a host decides like that, the hint is part of what was approved. The release note is explicit about the division of labour: "Baseline storage and the drift response remain the app's responsibility."

A one-tool MCP server over stdio, read through the SDK's own client: once as reviewed, once after only the hints were edited, once after only the description was edited. The three files are server.mjs, pin.mjs and check_ai_sdk.mjs, with a package.json that pins ai 7.0.177, @ai-sdk/mcp 2.0.101 and zod 4.1.8. Node.js 22 or newer:

The first three lines are what the server sent and the fingerprint the SDK computed. With the hints reversed the fingerprint is the same string, and detectToolDrift has nothing to report. The description edit moves it. The last line is the same comparison over a hash of the whole definition, which is the fix further down.

HISTOR reads the tool list of every remote server in the official MCP registry without credentials, on a schedule, and of npm and PyPI server packages once per published version. Since 23 October it keeps every member of a definition, annotations included. The count below uses the snapshot of 16 November: every read between 23 October and 16 November in which something in a tool definition differed from the previous read of the same server.

The middle column is the per-tool view: the drift check may name another tool of that server, but not this one. The right column is the case where it names nothing.

These are not spread evenly. One PyPI package accounts for 37 of the 91 tools, and two hosts for 54 of the 230. They also stay: of the 176 readOnlyHint moves one had gone back by 16 November, and five of the 720 destructiveHint moves.

One case from the data: a remote server whose four tools all went from readOnlyHint: true, idempotentHint: true to false on both in one read, with nothing else changing. That reads like an author correcting annotations that were wrong. It is also exactly the edit that an approval based on the old hints would want to hear about.

Hints are a small part of it. Of 103,657 reads in which a definition changed, 6,338 would move no fingerprint at all: 6.1 % of reads, on 2,014 of 14,009 servers.

Most of these are _meta and outputSchema, and one host produces 2,805 of the 6,338 by itself. We also left out 174,484 reads in which the only thing that moved was a vendor's per-tool version member; counting them would have made the share look far larger and meant nothing. A hash over the whole definition moves on all of that, which is the cost of covering more: more alarms that turn out to be housekeeping.

FastMCP documents a fingerprint too (the "Tool fingerprinting" page, new in 3.0.0). Its base payload is the tool key and the input schema; the page says FastMCP does not define a single contract hash and leaves description, output schema, annotations and _meta for the author to add. It is meant for a server author checking their own tools in CI.

Used as it stands on the consuming side, it would not move in 10,742 reads where only tool descriptions changed, on 4,191 servers. A description edit is the plain form of tool poisoning, so anyone borrowing the recipe for a gateway should add the description first.

An Abu Dhabi court has ordered Circle and 17 other companies to keep tokens linked to Binance’s Oct. 29 theft frozen while the exchange tries to recover them.

Binance disclosed the order on Nov. 18 and said some issuers, exchanges and other partners ask for a court order before they will release or transfer assets they have frozen.

Tether falls outside the order covering the 18 firms, and the company said on Nov. 2 it had frozen more than $1.5 billion tied to the theft.

Thomas Frank shares his feelings on Crystal Palace appointment

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Thomas Frank is back, and he sounds exactly like a man who has had enough time away to think clearly. Crystal Palace have appointed the 53-year-old on a two-and-a-half-year deal, and his first message was simple. He is refreshed, reflective and ready for another go in the Premier League.

Palace need that. They are bottom of the table, short on momentum and heading into a survival battle that will demand clarity more than noise. Frank arrives after a bruising end to his previous spell at Tottenham, where he was dismissed in February, less than eight months after taking charge. He could have stayed away longer. He chose not to.

There was no attempt to dress it up. Frank admitted the break had been enjoyable. He travelled to Japan and Australia, spent time in Denmark and relaxed with family and friends. As he put it, he had been "visiting friends and family, eating too well [and] drinking too much wine".

He had every reason to extend that time off too. "I was very aware I didn't want to do anything in the summer. I put a statement out there, I needed more time," he said. Then came the line that tells you where his head is now, "I can actually, honestly say I would have been fine if it was three, four, five, six, seven months more, because it's good to be back in the circus, but it's also unbelievably good not to be in, especially if you enjoy life, which I do."

Still, Palace convinced him. The club's internal pitch clearly mattered. Frank singled out chairman Steve Parish, along with Matt Hobbs and Iain Moody, as key figures in persuading him this was the right job at the right time.

That matters because sentiment gets you nowhere at the bottom of the Premier League. Palace need points, quickly. Frank's reign starts with a trip to Coventry City on Saturday, and there is no gentle easing-in period. The task is obvious, steady the team, sharpen the structure and lift belief.

Frank also made it clear that his time out of football was not spent feeling sorry for himself. "If you don't learn from any of your jobs, I don't think you reflect, I don't think you improve, and I don't think you get better," said Frank. "Of course there's a lot of things I have been reflecting over. The details, that will be for another time, but I can say I've learned, definitely."

This is where experience matters. Frank knows the scrutiny, understands the noise and appears entirely unbothered by it. "I think we all have an ego," he said. "Some are better at controlling it than others. For me, it's not the critics, it's more what I can learn, what I can prove, how I can get better."

He was even more direct when discussing outside opinion. "There's a lot of clever people out there, but the interesting thing is they only know 10 per cent of the truth. That's a tenth of the truth at Brentford, there's a tenth of the truth at Tottenham, and that's just the way it is.

"If you can't handle that as a coach or a player, then you shouldn't be in this business, and I'm very happy to be back in the game."

That is the point. No melodrama, no self-pity, no grand promises. Crystal Palace have hired a manager who sounds realistic about the mess in front of him and calm about the pressure around it. Right now, that is a sensible start.
