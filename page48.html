President Trump issued a declaration yesterday (Nov. 3), proclaiming space open to all humanity for exploration, and the right of American citizens to develop and settle the solar system according to United States laws.

"The American Declaration of Freedom in Space," describes space as the "province of all humans" and asserts that the United States will lead humanity's expansion into the cosmos. The proclamation reiterates the administration's goals with NASA's Artemis program to return American astronauts to the moon by 2028 and eventually sending astronauts to Mars, but introduces no new funding, programs or policy directives.

"Our Nation will lead the way in the exploration, development, use, and settlement of these new frontiers — forging an interplanetary civilization founded on the enduring and inalienable rights of freedom and individual liberty," the document says.

Trump invoked America's "frontier spirit," highlighting Apollo 11 astronauts as the first people in history to walk on the moon's surface and robotic rovers exploring Mars as examples of U.S. leadership, and emphasized the country's flourishing commercial space sector helping ensure "American space superiority for all eternity."

"The nation that leads in space will shape the future of humanity," the declaration says, and pledges that America will carry the values of faith, freedom and individual liberty into an interplanetary civilization.

The proclamation also highlights the success of NASA's Artemis II mission in April, which carried four astronauts on a 10-day journey around the moon and set a record for the farthest humans have ever traveled from Earth. The follow-up to that mission, Artemis III, is scheduled to launch next summer (2027), and will test rendezvous and docking operations between NASA's Orion spacecraft and the program's two commercial lunar landers in low Earth orbit.

Artemis IV will be the program's first lunar landing mission, and is targeted for 2028. A December 2025 executive order from the President reaffirmed the goal of meeting that timeline, as well as the deployment of the initial elements for a permanent lunar outpost by 2030.

Trump's declaration yesterday does not establish new NASA programs, allocate additional funding or issue instructions to federal agencies. Its sweeping language, though, does echo some of the principles in the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which guarantees freedom of exploration while prohibiting national sovereignty claims on other planets.

Winter Storm Watch vs. Winter Storm Warning: What’s the Difference?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A winter storm watch and winter storm warning mean different things. Here's what each alert signals and how to prepare for dangerous weather.

Tracking severe weather can sometimes feel like it requires a degree in meteorology. With complex scientific terms and technical jargon, it can be difficult to understand what it all means and what should be done to prepare. That’s when the difference between a winter storm watch and a winter storm warning matters.

Both alerts come from the National Weather Service, but they call for different levels of urgency. Here’s how the alerts work and what homeowners should do when they see one.

A winter storm watch means a significant winter storm is possible, but forecasters aren’t yet sure where or when it will hit or how severe it will be. According to the National Weather Service, watches often arrive a day or two before a storm and can cover heavy snow, sleet, or ice.

They give residents time to prepare, even though the storm may not reach their area. As the forecast becomes clearer, the National Weather Service may upgrade the watch to a warning, issue an advisory, or cancel it altogether.

A winter storm warning means dangerous winter weather is expected or already happening. Heavy snow, sleet, or ice could make travel unsafe and cause other problems, including power outages. The amount of snow or ice needed to trigger a warning varies by location.

“Each office knows the local area and will issue Warnings, Watches or Advisories based on local criteria,” according to the National Weather Service. “For example, the amount of snow that triggers a ‘Winter Storm Warning’ in the Northern Plains is typically much higher than the amount needed to trigger a ‘Winter Storm Warning’ in the Southeast.”

Warnings generally arrive closer to a storm’s arrival than watches, although forecasters can issue one before snow or ice begins falling.

A winter weather advisory means snow, sleet, or ice is expected to cause problems, but forecasters don’t anticipate conditions severe enough for a warning. An advisory may signal slick roads or other travel disruptions. While the weather may be less severe than during a warning, residents should still take precautions and monitor local forecasts.

A winter storm watch gives homeowners time to prepare before conditions worsen. That includes checking heating systems, protecting exposed pipes from freezing, and stocking up on food, medications, and other essentials.

“Your primary concerns at home or work during a winter storm are loss of heat, power and telephone service and a shortage of supplies if storm conditions continue for more than a day,” according to the National Weather Service.

Homeowners should have flashlights, spare batteries, and a charged phone in case the power goes out. Checking smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, locating snow shovels, and stocking up on ice melt can also help.

Once a warning takes effect, homeowners should finish their preparations and avoid unnecessary travel. Staying indoors is generally safest during severe winter weather.

If the power goes out, residents can close off unused rooms and wear extra layers to stay warm. Portable generators should always remain outdoors, away from windows and doors. Grills and ovens should never serve as heating sources because they can create serious safety hazards.

Residents should continue monitoring weather alerts throughout the storm. Once conditions improve, homeowners can check for damage and begin clearing snow and ice from walkways and driveways.

If you want to ride in a driverless Waymo in Austin or Atlanta, you can currently only do that via the Uber app thanks to an exclusive partnership between the two companies. That will change in 2028 because Waymo has decided to go solo in those two cities.

Waymo Co-CEO Tekedra Mawakana told Bloomberg on Wednesday that the company will exit its partnership with Uber in Austin and Atlanta starting in January 2028. The reason? Mawakana said the Uber app doesn’t guarantee a driverless ride. You can enable autonomous rides via settings in the Uber app, but the chances of one showing up depend on how many Waymos are actually nearby. If there's no Waymo nearby, riders are matched with human drivers.

Uber offered to add a dedicated robotaxi ride option through its service so that customers would match with driverless cars more easily, but Waymo reportedly declined that.

“In the early days, we would partner because we didn’t have the scale,” Mawakana told the outlet. "...in that partnering, it was a little bit of a crapshoot," she added.

Customers were now demanding a more consistent experience, Mawakana said. That’s why, starting in January 2028, Waymos in Austin and Atlanta will operate the same way they’ve been operating in over two dozen other U.S. cities: via Waymo’s own dedicated app. With that, current partner Uber will also become a direct rival in those two cities.

The whole point of this partnership was to help Waymo scale and give riders exposure to its cutting-edge driverless technology. Uber is the go-to ride-hail app for millions of riders already. The company also has vast experience in fleet operations and logistics.

Waymo, by comparison, is far smaller but is growing quickly. It’s a fully-owned subsidiary of Google parent Alphabet, which is valued at over $3.5 trillion as of writing. Waymo currently conducts over 500,000 paid driverless trips each week. It’s already operational in 15 U.S. cities and plans to add over a dozen more in the coming months. It's in a comfortable lead in the robotaxi race in the U.S., far ahead of Tesla and others.

It now seems more confident about going solo in Austin and Atlanta. However, it’s still partnering with Lyft for driverless rides in Nashville, although that partnership isn’t exclusive. Waymo also serves Nashville through its own app.

Uber also has grand ambitions when it comes to robotaxis. The company plans to have 700 robotaxis online by the end of this year and 2,000 by the end of 2027. It has emerged as a broker of robotaxi technology, inking deals with several companies including Rivian, Lucid, Wayve, and others to offer their driverless vehicles on the Uber app.

Two Hikers Dead in Bear Attack in British Columbia, Leaving Officials to Look for Answers

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A young grizzly in British Columbia (Photo: Colleen Gara / Getty Images)

Two hikers are dead after a suspected bear attack on Indigenous lands in British Columbia, provincial wildlife officials say. The incident comes just a year after a bear encounter in the same area left four people seriously injured.

Rescuers discovered one body on the afternoon of Nov. 2 near the Snooka West Trail in Bella Coola, leading to a search for a second, missing victim.

That search concluded late Tuesday night when searchers found the second hiker dead. Officials with the B.C. Conservation Officer Service (BCCOS) believe both were attacked by a grizzly bear, and efforts are ongoing to locate and trap any bears in the area for assessment.

Authorities and family members have since identified the pair as 48-year-old Jennifer Balcarras and her daughter, 20-year-old Selah Reed.

“It is not yet clear whether more than one bear was involved,” BCCOS Investigator Jeff Tyre told Backpacker. “Any captured bear will be assessed. If it is determined to have been involved in the attack, it will be dispatched in accordance with provincial human-wildlife conflict response guidelines to protect public safety.”

Family members first reported Balcarras and Reed overdue on Sunday, Nov. 1. Police and SAR operators found the first human remains on Monday afternoon, and called BCCOS due to evidence that suggested an animal was involved.

The victims’ remains have since been returned to their families, and the Snooka West Trail remains closed.

In the wake of the deaths, tribal authorities of the Nuxalk Nation, the First Nations people who live in the Bella Coola valley, expressed their support. Samuel Schooner, elected Chief of the Nuxalk Nation, said in a statement that the tribe understands firsthand the trauma this incident will inflict on the victims’ family members and on the survivors of last year’s attack.

That incident occurred near Highway 20 on Nov. 20, 2025, when a grizzly attacked a local school group taking a lunch break. Adults were able to repel the animal with bear spray and a bear banger, but not before it severely injured four people, including three children. The four individuals were rushed to the hospital in varying conditions, and seven others required care at the scene.

No one died, and investigators continued to search for grizzlies in the area, eventually determining three bears could have been involved. Over the following two weeks, officers captured eight adult and juvenile grizzlies for forensic testing. All eight animals were eventually collared and relocated after they could not be forensically connected with the attack.

A total of 24 officers were involved in the response to that attack. Some reportedly drove 14 hours overnight to hand-deliver samples to the Alberta Wildlife Forensic Laboratory. BCCOS described both the response and the incident as extraordinary.

“In my 34 years of experience, I have not seen an attack like this with a large group of people,” Kevin Van Damme, with BCCOS, told Global News at the time. “So this is extremely rare and I think the public needs to understand that part.”

As 2025 field operations concluded, the Conservation Office reported decreasing bear sightings and pointed out that grizzlies in the area typically transition from hyperphagia to denning in or around December.

It’s unclear how long the current search will continue. A spokesperson for BCCOS said investigators were unavailable for comment due to heightened field activity.

“Our thoughts are with the family of the victims during this tragic time,” the agency said. “We are in contact with the family to offer any support we can as we continue to investigate this incident.”
