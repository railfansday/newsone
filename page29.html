Festo Features at Automation Fair a Robust Suite of Pneumatic Safety Solutions

The Festo CIP Safety module and valve terminals help reduce energy consumption and increase machine availability.

MASON, Ohio, Nov. 11, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Festo features a suite of pneumatic safety solutions at Automation Fair, including a preview of its CPX-AP-A CIP Safety module. The module, in combination with the safety features of Festo valve terminals, helps prevent unexpected startup (PUS) and lowers engineering and assembly time for OEMs. Festo Automation Fair Booth #861, Thomas M. The Festo CPX-AP-A CIP Safety module controls and monitors safety I/O, supporting safety functions up to Performance Level e (PL e). This can be used to prevent unexpected startups without depressurizing the machine. Retaining working pressure reduces energy consumption and recharge time and improves machine availability.

CIP Safety brings functional safety onto the industrial network

The Common Industrial Protocol (CIP), managed by ODVA, an independent standards-development organization for industrial automation, provides a common communications architecture for industrial automation networks and is the basis for EtherNet/IP and DeviceNet.

CIP Safety extends the CIP architecture to functional safety, allowing safety controllers, I/O, interlocks, light curtains, and drives to communicate over the same network used for machine control without requiring a standalone network.

By eliminating the need for a separate safety network, CIP Safety reduces wiring and network infrastructure while simplifying machine design, commissioning, and diagnostics.

Integrating safety into the valve terminal

The CPX-AP-A CIP Safety module, scheduled for availability in 2027, enables OEMs to integrate safety close to the pneumatic application. The module can be mounted on Festo's three leading valve terminal families: VTUX, MPA-S and VTSA, allowing machine builders to apply the same safety architecture across valve terminals designed for different application and performance requirements.

Each CIP Safety module provides six single-channel or three dual-channel safe inputs and three safe outputs. Machine builders can use the inputs for safety devices such as E-stops and light curtains, while the outputs can safely control valve zones, pilot air, exhaust valves, and external devices such as a servo drive's Safe Torque Off (STO) function. Up to four modules can be installed on a valve terminal, providing up to 24 safe inputs and 12 safe outputs. Three additional safety solutions in the suite

Festo's valve terminal, the VTUX, has been designed with safety in mind. VTUX valves feature negative overlap spools that allow for safe exhausting of compressed air in case a valve spool is in an indeterminate position.

For those that use safe I/O blocks, such as Allen-Bradley ArmorBlock Guard I/O modules, Festo offers its CPX platform with a VTSA-F-CB valve terminal. This solution delivers three safe zones controlled by an external safety controller. The VTSA-F-CB offers up to a CAT3, PL e, and SIL3 safety level for safe-switch-off and prevented-unexpected startup. It also offers soft start/quick exhaust options.

End Of An Era: Hong Kong’s Iconic “Symphony Of Lights” Show Ending

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Hong Kong is one of my favorite cities in the world. When I think of Hong Kong, the beautiful waterfront and skyline on both sides of Victoria Harbour come to mind. Along those lines, a nightly staple of the waterfront is coming to an end, and I’m sad about it…

Since 2004, Hong Kong has had the “Symphony of Lights” show every single night at 8PM, lasting for around 10 minutes. It holds the Guinness World Record for being the world’s largest permanent light and sound show, as it involves dozens of buildings.

It’s one of the things that contributes to Hong Kong’s electric energy, as you have a sea of people lining up on the waterfront (typically on the Tsim Sha Tsui side, though sometimes also on the Central side) every night to watch it.

Well, after 22 years, the Symphony of Lights will be coming to an end. The last show is expected to take place on Wednesday, November 18, 2026. To mark the end of the era, the final show will feature special pyrotechnics and fireworks.

Why has Hong Kong decided to end the nightly Symphony of Lights show? Officially, it’s a strategic shift in the city’s tourism and event strategy, described as being driven by shifting visitor habits, diminishing appeal, high maintenance costs, and a pivot to seasonal attractions. We’re told that we can expect “something new” in the future, but it doesn’t sound like it’ll be a full replacement for the Symphony of Lights.

I must admit, I’m sad to see this change in Hong Kong. No matter how often I visited Hong Kong, I always made a point of watching the Symphony of Lights, and always found it to be so enjoyable. Hong Kong has one of the world’s most gorgeous skylines, and the nightly show just took that to the next level.

I am curious what the backstory here is. Was the cost of operating this really so high, in the scheme of things? While I can in theory appreciate the concept of diminishing appeal, the nightly show always had a crowd in the thousands waiting to enjoy it — that was true 20 years ago when I first visited Hong Kong, and it was also true when I visited earlier this year.

Hong Kongers will soon have to bid farewell to the Symphony of Lights, the iconic nightly show that has been lighting up the city’s skyline since 2004. Officially, this change is described as being due to shifting visitor habits, diminishing appeal, high maintenance costs, and a pivot to seasonal attractions.

I’ve enjoyed this show every time I visited Hong Kong, so I’m sad to see this development. The skyline is still incredible and leaves me in awe every time, but there’s no denying that the nightly show added just a little bit of extra sparkle.

What do you make of Hong Kong’s nightly Symphony of Lights ending?

Roll-A-Rack debuting low-profile, manufactured-on-site solar racking

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Roll-A-Rack, a developer of patented, on-site-manufactured solar racking systems, will introduce SunChord, its new multi-purpose ground-mounted system, at RE+ 2026 in Las Vegas.

SunChord is a low-profile solar racking designed to use less land than conventional fixed-tilt racking or trackers. It is composed of two components: gutter channels and clamp brackets that attach to solar panels. SunChord assembles using what Roll-A-Rack has dubbed its “snap-slide-secure” system.

“The next generation of solar technology needs to do more than produce clean electricity. It needs to make better use of land, minimize environmental impacts and create value beyond the energy generated,” said Don Scipione, founder of Roll-A-Rack. “SunChord reflects our vision of harmonizing sun and earth, combining solar energy production with rainwater harvesting and soil conservation in a system that can unlock new opportunities for solar development on land where conventional approaches fall short.”

Roll-A-Rack claims an array using SunChord can capture 95% of rain that falls it using its racking channels to redirect the water. The company sees potential for the racking in agrivoltaic applications, sites with land constraints, on brownfields and where ground disturbance should be limited.

SunChord is assembled on-site using a roll-forming device similar to producing roof-edge gutters. SunChord was developed with support from the U.S. Department of Energy’s Integrated Energy Systems Office and the USDA National Institute of Food and Agriculture. The company has two issued U.S. patents, a patent application pending, wind-tunnel ratings and UL 2703 certification.

Roll-A-Rack is planning to supply SunChord for up to five pilot projects. Interested EPCs and developers can contact the company at [email protected].

PUT checked the project space, DELETE didn't: cross-tenant deletes in the CommCare HQ API (GHSA-hq38-78vf-f4cj)

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CommCare HQ is the server side of CommCare, Dimagi's platform for building offline-first mobile apps for frontline workers. One instance hosts many project spaces, and the project space is the tenant boundary. Mobile workers are the field users of a project's apps. Lookup tables hold reference data that a project's apps and forms read.

A delete from another tenant takes away a project's field user or the data its forms depend on.

The API is built on django-tastypie. Each request names a project space in its URL, /a//api/... (the code calls a project space a domain). Authentication checks that the caller has the required permission in that project space. It says nothing about the object the request points at. That second check is left to each resource's own methods.

The mobile-worker resource, at commit 2ef6f1c from 2026-08-13:

The update handler loads the user and asserts it belongs to the URL's project space. The delete handler loads the user by ID alone and retires it.

The lookup-table resource has the same shape. Like its read handler, its update handler raises NotFound when the table's project space doesn't match the URL's. Its delete handler filters by ID only:

So a caller with permission to edit mobile workers through the API in project space X could send DELETE /a/X/api/v0.5/user// with the ID of a mobile worker in project space Y, and the request would pass authentication in X and retire the user in Y. The advisory lists the affected routes for users, lookup tables and rows across the v0.5, v0.6, v1 and v2 API paths.

The URL's project space authenticated the caller. The read and update handlers checked that the object belonged to it; the delete handlers didn't.

I didn't run a full CommCare HQ. On 2026-08-13 I ran a harness that drives django-tastypie 0.15.1, the version CommCare HQ pins, through its real request handling. Into it I copied CommCare HQ's obj_delete method from commit 2ef6f1c, and obj_update up to its project-space check, with the class names swapped for in-memory stand-ins. The harness seeded one synthetic mobile worker in domain_Y, and caller A made the request as a user of domain_X.

These are the delete lines of the harness's output, as recorded in my run notes (the harness's separator lines removed):

The delete from domain_X went through and marked the domain_Y user deleted. It returned 204, not the 202 in the handler, because tastypie returns 204 after a delete. A second harness ran the same delete for a lookup table and a lookup-table row, and both were removed.

The harness also tried the update as a control. On review, that step never reached CommCare HQ's code: the stand-in request object lacks a method tastypie calls on every PUT, so the PUT failed with a 500 before obj_update ran. It doesn't show the update check working. The check is in the source above, and the advisory describes only the deletes as affected.

Honest boundary: the harness ran tastypie's real request path, but with stand-ins for the request object, the database, authentication, CommCare HQ's base-resource dispatch check (which looks only at the caller's own project space), and retire() (a reduced copy that marks the user deleted). I didn't send HTTP requests to a running CommCare HQ. The impact on a live instance is as the advisory states it. The data was synthetic.

Fixed in commit 3570c0ff9c7, through PR #38029, merged on 2026-08-14. Each delete now looks the object up within the URL's project space:

Per the advisory, a foreign object ID now returns 404 instead of being deleted. The advisory asks self-hosted instances to deploy 3570c0ff9c7 or later. For anyone who can't upgrade right away, it suggests blocking the /a//api/ paths at the proxy, since per-user permission changes don't help when the attacker controls their own project space.

If you self-host CommCare HQ, make sure you've deployed 3570c0ff9c7 or later.

Airdrop Scams, Decoded — Dusting, Drainer Links and Fake Claims

Tokens you never asked for showing up in your wallet is not luck — it's a fishing lure cast at scale. The dust itself is harmless. What it invites you to do next is the attack.

Educational guide · reviewed August 2026 · not financial advice

Open almost any active wallet and you'll find them: tokens you never bought, NFTs you never minted, names like "5000USDT-voucher" or a famous project's ticker with a website baked into it. Sending them costs the scammer almost nothing, because on most chains a single transaction can spray a worthless token to thousands of addresses at once. The economics only work because a tiny fraction of recipients does the one thing the token exists to provoke — and that action, not the airdrop, is what empties wallets. When it has emptied one, the first hour after a wallet drain decides whether anything is salvageable.

This guide separates the three layers of the scheme — the dust, the destination, and the signature — so the next unexpected "reward" in your wallet reads as what it is: unsolicited advertising for a trap. Wallet software filters some of that noise and none of the consequence — is Phantom safe sets out what the app itself actually guards.

Paste its mint address before touching it — mass-dusted scam tokens tend to light up a scan immediately.

Legitimate projects airdrop for a reason you can articulate: rewarding past users, decentralizing governance, bootstrapping a network. Scammers airdrop for a different reason — an unsolicited token is the cheapest way to place a clickable message inside your wallet UI, past every spam filter that guards your inbox. The token's name is the ad ("Claim at ..."), its metadata carries the link, and your own curiosity does the delivery. Some campaigns add a pricing trick: the scam token trades against itself on a pool the scammer controls, so your wallet may display the dust as "worth" hundreds of dollars. That number exists to make ignoring it feel expensive.

The bait takes a few forms depending on the chain. Fungible dust — a few thousand units of a token named after a real protocol or a fake voucher. NFT spam — an image whose entire artwork is a URL and an instruction. Memo or metadata spam — transfer notes attached to tiny native-coin deposits, common on chains with cheap memo fields. All three share one property that matters more than anything else in this guide: receiving them changes nothing about your security. Tokens cannot execute code on arrival. No balance sitting in your wallet can move your other assets. The dust is inert until you interact with it or with the address it advertises.

The trap is participation, not possession. Every airdrop drain in the wild requires a step you take: visiting the printed URL, connecting a wallet, and approving something. Refuse that step and the entire kill chain dies in your token list, worth exactly nothing and threatening exactly nothing.

Follow the printed link (don't) and you land on a site built to feel official — cloned branding from a real project, a countdown, an eligibility checker that congratulates every address it's shown. The eligibility theater matters: being told your specific wallet "qualifies for 2,400 tokens" converts a stranger's website into your pending payout, and people protect payouts. The connect-wallet button works normally, because connecting is harmless and builds trust. The harm is queued behind the button labeled Claim, which does not claim anything — it asks your wallet for a signature or transaction whose true effect is written in the fine print your excitement is designed to skip.

Drainer kits are modular, and the request they serve depends on what your wallet holds. The table below is the field guide — the left column is what the site says, the middle is what the wallet prompt really does.

Two properties make these requests nastier than ordinary bad transactions. First, several are gasless signatures — nothing leaves your wallet at signing time, so the theft can execute hours later, disconnected from anything you remember doing. Second, an approval is durable: it survives until revoked, so a single careless click can sit dormant in your account like an unlocked door.

The correct response is deliberately boring. Hide the token using your wallet's spam controls, or simply leave it — it cannot hurt you by existing. Never open the URL in its name or metadata, never Google the token looking for a claim page (search ads are a major drainer distribution channel), and never try to sell or swap the dust: interacting with a scam token's own contract is precisely the engagement its designer wants, and some are built so any interaction reverts, wastes fees, or fires an approval request. Burning is likewise unnecessary — you'd be spending gas to tidy a threat that isn't one. If you did sign something on a claim site, treat it as an active incident: revoke the approval from a trusted revocation tool and move remaining assets to a clean wallet, in that order of urgency.

Legitimate distributions have a shape you can recognize. Eligibility is determined by a snapshot of past activity, announced through the project's long-established channels — never by a token materializing in your wallet with instructions. Genuine claim flows are hosted on the project's primary domain, linked consistently from every official surface, and the claim transaction interacts with a published, verifiable distributor contract; many teams simply send tokens directly with no claim step at all. And no honest airdrop, anywhere, asks for your seed phrase, an upfront "release fee," or an approval over unrelated assets. When an airdrop is real, you generally learn about it from the project. When the "airdrop" is how you learned the project exists, you already have your answer.

One more habit closes the loop: treat claim deadlines as a pressure gauge. Fraudulent flows lean hard on urgency — hours-long countdowns, "unclaimed allocations redistributed tonight" — because reflection is fatal to them. Established projects run claim windows measured in weeks or months precisely so nobody has to rush a signature. The more a flow insists you act immediately, the more certain you can be that waiting costs you nothing and protects everything.

Turn Notes Into a Podcast with Two AI Voices

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 11 • Originally published at murmurtts.com

A notes-to-podcast workflow: reshape six lines of notes into a two-host script, assign two voices in Murmur Projects, generate, and export the episode.

Short answer: To turn notes into a podcast, rewrite the notes as a spoken dialogue with a name in front of every line, then render each name with a different voice. The writing step is yours: the app does not summarize notes or draft a script. What it does is take a finished two-host script, keep each speaker attached to a chosen voice, generate the lines locally on a Mac, and export the episode.

That division of labor matters. Most tools that advertise notes-to-podcast conversion are selling an AI writer plus a cloud voice. If you already know your subject, the writing is the part you should control. The slow, mechanical part is turning a good script into clean audio with consistent voices, and that is the part this workflow removes.

Notes are compressed for your eyes. They skip transitions, assume context, and lean on fragments. A listener has none of that context and cannot reread a sentence. Before any voice generation, convert each note into something a person could say out loud to someone else.

Keep the note file open beside the script while you work. You are not throwing the notes away, you are expanding them. The notes remain the fact check and the outline; the dialogue is the performance.

Here is a complete before-and-after from a fictional short episode about pre-ground coffee. The notes are six lines. The script is twelve turns, alternating between a Host and a Guest.

Notice what the notes do not contain: a greeting, a reason to keep listening, a definition of surface area, or a usable answer for the listener with the grinder question. The rewrite supplies those in the smallest number of turns that still sounds like a conversation.

Host: Welcome back. Today we're talking about pre-ground coffee, and why the bag you bought last month tastes flat. Guest: The short version is surface area. Grinding exposes more of the bean to air, so the aromatics leave faster. Host: So the grinder matters more than the brewer? Guest: For most people, yes. A consistent grind changes how water moves through the bed, and that changes what you taste. Host: Our notes also say grind size beats brew time. Is that fair? Guest: Mostly. If the grind is uneven, no timer will save the cup. Fix the grind first, then adjust time. Host: A listener asked whether a cheap burr grinder is enough. Guest: A burr grinder with a stable setting is a better start than a blade grinder. You do not need the most expensive one to hear a difference. Host: What is one change to try this week? Guest: Grind one dose coarse and one fine, brew both, and taste them side by side. Keep the one you would drink again. Host: That is the whole episode. Try the two-grind test and tell us which cup won. Guest: And use beans you already like, so the only variable is the grind.

Each note became a pair: the Host states or asks, the Guest answers. That is not padding. The second turn forces the script to explain the claim instead of asserting it, which is exactly what a listener needs. Six lines of notes became a dialogue of about 190 words, which is roughly 75 to 90 seconds of speech depending on pace.

A second voice gives the script permission to ask the obvious question, define a term, and signal a new section without saying "section two." It also creates a natural place for the listener's objection. When the Host asks whether a cheap grinder is enough, the listener hears their own question in the show.

The tradeoff is real. Two voices are harder to keep consistent across episodes, and a scripted exchange can sound stiff if every answer is the same length. A solo read is the better choice for dense reference material, a personal essay, or anything where a second character would be theater. If the notes are mostly a list of facts, one narrator is honest. If the notes contain a question you expect listeners to have, a second host earns its place.

Pick the format before you write a single line. Changing format halfway through means rewriting the script and reassigning speakers, which is more work than deciding first. For most notes-to-podcast projects, the two-host explainer is the fastest structure to write and the easiest to follow.

Murmur is a local voice studio for Apple Silicon and Intel Macs running macOS 15 or later. Projects is its multi-speaker workspace, and it is the part that matters here: a project holds a shared cast, one or more scripts, and a timeline. The steps below follow the current app.

Projects is a Pro feature. The free plan covers Kokoro and scripts up to 1,500 characters, which is enough to test a voice but not to produce an episode. The app can start a one-click 3-day Pro trial, and the full license is $49 one-time with a 7-day refund policy. If you only need a single narrator for short clips, the free plan may be all you need.

One honest limitation: the app does not write, summarize, or restructure your notes. If you want the tool to invent the dialogue, this is the wrong workflow. Bring a script you are willing to stand behind. The app's job starts when the writing stops.

Two similar voices are worse than one good voice. The listener loses track of who is speaking and the conversation turns into a blur. Audition both voices on the same sentence before you commit, and listen on a laptop speaker rather than headphones, because that is how a lot of people will hear the show.

If the two voices still sound alike after auditioning, change the model rather than stacking effects on the export. Pitch shifting a finished voice makes it sound processed. A different preset or a different model family is the cleaner fix.

Read the dialogue out loud once, all the way through. This catches most problems before they become audio: a sentence that cannot be said in one breath, a name with two possible pronunciations, a number that reads differently as a figure than as a word. Fix the script, not the take, when the problem is in the writing.

The podcast script example shows a different structure for a solo episode, and the multi-voice podcast workflow covers sponsor reads and section-by-section revision. Both are useful comparisons if your notes do not naturally split into a conversation.

The app exports the timeline mix as WAV or M4A. Use WAV when the episode will be edited further, normalized, or mixed with music. Use M4A when you want a smaller delivery file, and note that timeline markers become chapter markers in M4A. The export sheet can also write separate clip files, a text transcript, and a CSV of marker times.

The transcript is not an optional extra. The W3C's media guidance states that prerecorded audio-only content needs a transcript to meet WCAG Level A, and lists identifying speakers as an optional extra. Apple Podcasts generates transcripts automatically after publication and also accepts a provider-supplied VTT file, which can identify every speaker with each line. Murmur's own transcript export is plain text, so you would convert it to VTT yourself. If you export a plain text transcript from the app, keep it as the source for the published version and check names and numbers by hand.

The WAV, M4A, and MP3 export guide explains which file to keep as a master and which to send to a host. If your notes are study material rather than a show, the study notes to audio workflow is a closer fit than a two-host format.

That checklist takes a few minutes and prevents the most common failure: a polished voice track that says the wrong thing. The Projects workspace keeps the script, cast, and timeline together, so a correction is a block regeneration rather than a rebuild.

Can this workflow turn my notes into a podcast automatically?

No. Murmur does not summarize notes or write a script. You or an assistant you choose produce the two-host dialogue, and the app renders it with the voices you assign. Its value is the multi-speaker project, local generation, and export, not the writing.

How long should a notes-based episode be?

There is no correct length. A short explainer may need three minutes, while a teaching episode may need twenty. Let the notes decide: if a section does not change what the listener knows or does, cut it. Time the finished audio rather than estimating from the word count.

Do I need two different models for two voices?

No. A project uses one model, and each speaker is assigned a voice that model supports. Kokoro offers preset voices, and cloning-capable models can use saved reference profiles. Choose the model first, then confirm it has two voices you can tell apart.

Can I fix one bad line without regenerating the whole episode?

Yes. Generated audio sits on the timeline as clips. Select the clip for the line you want to change, edit the script text if needed, and use Regenerate Block. The rest of the episode stays as it was.

Should I tell listeners the voices are synthetic?

Disclosure rules vary by platform, sponsor, and audience, so check the requirements that apply to your show. A short line in the episode or show notes is usually enough. Never clone a voice without owning it or having written permission.

CosyVoice on Mac: CosyVoice 2 and 3 Setup Guide

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 11 • Originally published at murmurtts.com

Run CosyVoice 2 or 3 on an Apple Silicon Mac: what each version changed, the official install steps, the macOS GPU gaps, and when a shipped app fits better.

Short answer: CosyVoice is an open source text to speech family from researchers at Alibaba Group, built around a language model plus a flow matching decoder, with zero-shot voice cloning from a reference clip. Murmur does not run CosyVoice. On a Mac you run it yourself from the official repository, and the published install path assumes Linux with Python 3.10, so plan on CPU inference or community patches before you get Apple GPU acceleration.

That distinction matters because search results mix three different things: the research papers, the GitHub repository, and the hosted demos. The papers describe model behavior. The repository describes one install recipe. Neither one promises a supported macOS build. This guide sticks to what the project's own README, model cards, papers, and issue tracker say, and it marks the places where Mac users are on their own.

The project now lives under the QwenAudio/CosyVoice repository (the older FunAudioLLM links redirect there) and is licensed Apache-2.0. The architecture splits the job in two: a text to speech language model predicts supervised semantic speech tokens, then a conditional flow matching model turns those tokens into a Mel spectrogram for a given speaker, and a vocoder produces the waveform. The CosyVoice 2 paper describes a gradual semantic decoding procedure and says the design supports both streaming and non-streaming synthesis in one model.

The app ships a different catalog: Kokoro (bundled), Chatterbox Turbo, Chatterbox Multilingual, Qwen3-TTS Base, Voice Design, SparkTTS, Fish Audio S2 Pro, Breeze TTS 2, and OmniVoice. CosyVoice is not among them, and no setting in the app will make it appear. If you want CosyVoice behavior specifically, you need the Python project. If you want a Mac app that handles scripts, speakers, takes, and export without a terminal, compare the shipped models first in the local TTS model guide.

One more piece of small print: the README ends with a disclaimer that the content is for academic purposes and demonstrates technical capabilities. The repository license is still Apache-2.0, and both Hugging Face model cards list apache-2.0, but read the license and the card yourself before you build a commercial product on the weights.

The CosyVoice 3 paper says the language model was scaled from 0.5B to 1.5B parameters and the flow matching model from 100M to 300M, and that this larger capacity improved its multilingual benchmark. The checkpoint the README actually tells you to download is Fun-CosyVoice3-0.5B-2512, which is 0.5B. Both facts can be true at once: the paper reports experiments at two sizes, and the released default is the smaller one. If you see a claim that CosyVoice 3 on your Mac is a 1.5B model, check which weights that person actually loaded.

The version jump also changes the language story. The CosyVoice 2 paper reports training data in Chinese, English, Japanese, and Korean, and its limitations section says the model supports only a limited number of languages. The CosyVoice 3 README lists nine languages (Chinese, English, Japanese, Korean, German, Spanish, French, Italian, Russian) plus 18 or more Chinese dialects and accents, and both the README and the paper describe cross-lingual zero-shot cloning. The paper's data section counts 19 Chinese accents or dialects while the abstract says 18, so treat the dialect count as approximate and check the current card.

Zero-shot here means the model is not fine-tuned on your speaker. You supply a short reference recording and the matching text, and the model imitates the timbre, prosody, and style of that reference. In the CosyVoice 2 model card, the zero-shot call takes the text to synthesize, the prompt text, and the prompt wav path. A separate cross-lingual call takes the target text and the same prompt wav, and an instruct call takes a natural language instruction as a second argument after the text.

cosyvoice = AutoModel(model_dir='pretrained_models/CosyVoice2-0.5B') for i, j in enumerate(cosyvoice.inference_zero_shot('收到好友从远方寄来的生日礼物，那份意外的惊喜与深深的祝福让我心中充满了甜蜜的快乐，笑容如花儿般绽放。', '希望你以后能够做的比我还好呦。', './asset/zero_shot_prompt.wav')): torchaudio.save('zero_shot_{}.wav'.format(i), j['tts_speech'], cosyvoice.sample_rate)

The reference transcript is not decoration. CosyVoice 2's in-context learning mode uses prompt text and prompt speech tokens together, and the card's own examples pass the prompt text explicitly. The CosyVoice 3 card adds an instruction prefix such as "You are a helpful assistant." before the prompt text. If your reference clip is noisy, clipped, or in a different language from its transcript, the clone will reflect that.

Only clone voices you own or have written permission to use. A local model removes the upload step, not the consent requirement. For a broader workflow around reference recordings and takes, see the voice cloning guide.

The CosyVoice 3 README says the system supports text-in and audio-out streaming and "achieves latency as low as 150ms while maintaining high-quality audio output." That is the project's claim about its own streaming path. It is not a macOS benchmark, it does not say which hardware produced the figure, and it does not say how much text must arrive before the first audio chunk. Treat 150 ms as a best-case target to reproduce, not a number you should expect on an M1 laptop running CPU inference.

The CosyVoice 2 paper is more explicit about what the latency depends on. Its first-package latency is modeled as the chunk size M multiplied by the per-token time for the language model, the flow matching model, and the vocoder, summed together. Change the chunk size or the hardware and the number changes. The paper also reports that its streaming mode was nearly lossless compared with offline synthesis in typical tests, with a slight content consistency drop in hard cases. Those are paper results, not a measurement of your machine.

If you care about streaming behavior, measure it. The streaming latency measurement guide covers first-chunk timing and real-time factor on a Mac. A file-based editor is a different product category from a live streaming endpoint, so do not read this section as a claim about any desktop app.

The README's install section is short and Linux-oriented. These are its exact commands, minus the mirror flags the project adds for users in China:

git clone --recursive https://github.com/FunAudioLLM/CosyVoice.git cd CosyVoice git submodule update --init --recursive conda create -n cosyvoice -y python=3.10 conda activate cosyvoice pip install -r requirements.txt

The submodule step is not optional. The FAQ says a missing Matcha-TTS directory produces a ModuleNotFoundError, and that you should run git submodule update --init --recursive if third_party/Matcha-TTS is absent. The requirements file pins torch 2.3.1 and torchaudio 2.3.1, and it installs onnxruntime for darwin, so a macOS CPU install is plausible even though the README never walks through one.

The next step is model download. The README recommends the 3.0 checkpoint for better performance and shows both ModelScope and Hugging Face calls. For Hugging Face, the lines are:

from huggingface_hub import snapshot_download snapshot_download('FunAudioLLM/Fun-CosyVoice3-0.5B-2512', local_dir='pretrained_models/Fun-CosyVoice3-0.5B') snapshot_download('FunAudioLLM/CosyVoice2-0.5B', local_dir='pretrained_models/CosyVoice2-0.5B')

After that the README says to follow example.py, or to start the web UI with python3 webui.py --port 50000 --model_dir pretrained_models/CosyVoice-300M. The README also mentions an optional ttsfrd resource for better text normalization and says that if you skip it, wetext is used by default. Skip it on a Mac. The wheel it installs is named ttsfrd-0.4.2-cp310-cp310-linux_x86_64.whl, and issue #1011 shows that command failing on an M4 Mac mini.

Two more macOS details from the README: the sox troubleshooting commands are apt-get for Ubuntu and yum for CentOS, with no Homebrew line, and the deployment section builds a Docker image and runs it with --runtime=nvidia. The TensorRT-LLM acceleration path and the Docker examples are written for NVIDIA hardware. None of that blocks CPU inference, but none of it is a Mac install guide.

Issue #1011 is the clearest public record of the Mac experience. A user on an M4 Mac mini reports the CPU at 100 percent while the GPU sits idle, then quotes the device line in cosyvoice/cli/model.py: cuda if torch.cuda.is_available(), otherwise cpu. There is no MPS branch. Another commenter says support requires changing the code, and later comments show a patch that checks torch.backends.mps.is_available() before falling back.

Forcing MPS is not automatically a win. One commenter hits NotImplementedError for aten::unfold_backward on the MPS device, with the standard advice to set PYTORCH_ENABLE_MPS_FALLBACK=1 so that unsupported operations fall back to CPU. That operator is a gradient operation, so the thread mixes training and inference reports, and other commenters in it say MPS worked after upgrading to a newer PyTorch. Others report that newer PyTorch and newer macOS are needed, and that results vary by version. In issue #134 (July 2024, before CosyVoice 2 and 3), a project collaborator writes that an earlier MPS pull request was slower than CPU on Mac and that it was maybe not mature yet. These are user reports and maintainer comments, not a supported configuration.

The practical sequence is therefore: get CPU inference working first with the 0.5B checkpoint, confirm that a short line synthesizes and saves, then try an MPS patch on a copy of the repository and compare wall-clock time against CPU. Do not start by patching a fresh clone before you know the baseline works. And do not install torch nightly over the pinned 2.3.1 in the project environment without a separate environment to fall back to.

The honest summary: CosyVoice is worth the setup if you specifically need its multilingual and dialect behavior, or you want to study or modify the model. It is the wrong tool if you want a finished desktop editor, if you need Windows, or if you need a supported Apple GPU path today. Where do I even begin with this thing? I'm so conflicted on the SK700V. It's such a unique problem, simultaneously excellent, and yet surprisingly frustrating to talk about. Let's talk features first, though, before I start to wail on the minor inconveniences that have upset my tech media brain, because the SK700V has one pretty nifty detail straddling its heatpipes, and that's namely, a full-fat display gracefully sitting atop that shiny tower.

Graphics cards, CPU towers, heck, I'd be surprised if we don't see them on DRAM soon. That'll bring the price down, I'm sure of it.

It's not your full-fat OLED display, or anything fancy, but rather a simple digital-clock style system that actively reports on CPU frequency, temp, load, and power draw all on the fly. There's not much in the way of customisation in the software, but to be honest, there doesn't really need to be.

Sudokoo's generally quite an interesting company too. It's relatively new, founded in 2024, and is working in collaboration with a Taiwanese OEM with designs that remind me a lot of DeepCool's.

I'm going to cover the irritating bits now, actually, because I want to get them out of the way, as genuinely there's a lot of good to talk about here.

Unboxing the SK700V is a real experience. The packaging is lush, pink and yellow. You open it up, and inside, in my case, there are two kits, an included Intel mounting bracket kit (which, annoyingly, Sudokoo sell separately), and a severe lack of thermal paste (although bizarrely, you do get some grease cleaner).

I don't understand why these two things are not included by default. I know that's a weird hill to die on, but there's literally zero reason why they shouldn't be included. This isn't some specially designed Noctua NH-L9i where the mounting is built directly into the low-profile frame.

This is a standard run-of-the-mill air-tower (albeit a very slim one), so why that Intel bracket isn't included is beyond me. Intel isn't dead, come on, guys. Oh, and the thermal paste too. Please, we're talking cents here, pennies, sweet naff all, include a tiny tube.

Installing it, though, was perhaps the most seamless experience I've ever had. Get your brackets set up, then take your tower, and slide the fan up and out of its rails (yes, rails). Underneath, you'll see some contact points for the display, and a hole for the included long screw driver on the back side. The front screw mount is easily reachable by any standard model. Then, once you're mounted, just slide the fan back in, clicking it into place, plug in the cables, and you're good to go. No funky, annoying metal clips, no awkward mounts, it's just brilliant. It's also remarkably compact; even with the fan in place, I could easily install memory after, no problem.

The software, though, was a challenge on my machine. For whatever reason, Chrome, Brave, and Edge all flagged it as untrusted, and no matter what I tried with Windows, I could not for the life of me get it to download (this is less a problem with Sudokoo, more Microsoft, being, eh, well, Microsoft). The software's fairly barebones, but it gives you all the system stats you need, and hooks up nicely with the SK700V as well.

A true testament to the design, given it was operating in ambient conditions four to five degrees higher than elsewhere.

The SK700V is, for a first attempt (and I know, yes, it's technically a licensed design), remarkable. At this price point, delivering this kind of performance, it's just brilliant to see. Particularly when the rest of the industry is on such a price hike at the moment, is it perfect?
