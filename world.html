Emirati curator Hoor Al Qasimi to lead Venice Biennale in 2028

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Hoor Al Qasimi, one of the preeminent figures of contemporary art in the Middle East, has been named curator of the Venice Biennale's next art exhibition, the Biennale has announced.

Following this year's edition, which was curated by the late Cameroon-born curator Koyo Kouoh, the appointment of Al-Qasimi, from the United Arab Emirates (UAE), marks the second consecutive edition in which a woman from outside the Western art world has been chosen for the role.

Al Qasimi was born into the royal family of the port city of Sharjah and is the daughter of Sultan bin Muhammad Al Qasimi, the current leader of the emirate of Sharjah.

She has been involved in the Sharjah Biennial since 2003, when she curated the exhibition's sixth edition after completing her undergraduate studies at the Slade School of Fine Art in London. In 2009, she founded the Sharjah Art Foundation, a public art organisation that promotes and funds contemporary art collaborations in the emirate and internationally, including organising the Sharjah Biennial.

She holds board positions for numerous other arts organisations, including the International Biennial Association, Sharjah Museums Authority, Sharjah Architecture Triennial and University of the Arts Sharjah. She has also curated major exhibitions including the Lahore Biennale in 2020, the Aichi Triennale in Japan in 2025, and this year's edition of the Biennale of Sydney.

Pietrangelo Buttafuoco, president of the Venice Biennale, says in a statement that Al Qasimi's work gives “voice to stories and sensibilities shaped by research and the unexpected”.

He adds: “Her work brings together a profound attentiveness to deep-rooted memory and its legacies, an open perspective on the intricate complexities of the present, and a rare ability to listen to places and their people...These are qualities that we feel are particularly close to La Biennale’s vision”.

Buttafuoco, a right-leaning journalist and intellectual who was born and raised in Catania in Sicily, converted to Islam in 2015, adopting the name Giafar al-Siqilli (“Jafar the Sicilian”) in a reference to the Arab-Sicilian general associated with the Fatimid conquest of North Africa.

Since becoming president of the Biennale in 2024, he has sometimes championed art from the Middle East. Under his watch, Qatar opened a national pavilion at the Biennale last year, making it the first permanent addition to the Giardini, one of the Biennale's two main venues, in thirty years.

In a statement, Al Qasimi says that the Biennale “continues to be a critical space to engage with the work of influential artists and curators”, adding that she was “honoured” to be appointed as curator.

Al Qasimi could not immediately be reached for comment.

Chantiers’ Castaing on MSC World Asia Delivery: ‘This Ship Is Exceptional’

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Speaking during the delivery of the MSC World Asia in Saint-Nazaire on Wednesday, Chantiers de l’Atlantique General Manager Laurent Castaing highlighted the vessel’s energy efficiency and celebrated the shipyard’s 25-year partnership with MSC Cruises.

The handover marks the 20th cruise ship built by the French yard for the company, adding a new vessel to the World Class series, which began in 2022 with the MSC World Europa.

“This ship is exceptional,” Castaing said. “Following the wishes of her shipowner and thanks to our great flexibility, major new features for passengers have been added to this excellent World Class platform.”

He also pointed to the sustainability features of the vessel, noting that its greenhouse gas emissions are 50 percent lower than the IMO reference baseline.

“What we are very proud of is the environmental performance of this ship. From a distance, she looks like others, but she is truly exceptional, and we have extensively modified the ship’s machinery,” Castaing noted.

“She is the best in energy efficiency across the entire cruise ship fleet, and by far. Few modes of transport can boast such a 50 percent reduction compared to the benchmarks. We are ahead of our competitors, and we will continue moving toward zero emissions.”

Castaing said the ship was the result of human effort, adding that thousands of people had worked on its construction since 2024.

He thanked MSC Cruises for the partnership with Chantiers de l’Atlantique, noting that the company has now built 20 ships at the French yard over the past 25 years.

“It is exceptional. Our relationship has always been one of trust. We have not weathered any storms in our relations. Sometimes clouds have passed by, but honestly, I have never gone to Geneva with a knot in my stomach,” Castaing said.

“Even if there were occasionally a few last-minute squabbles, our deliveries have always gone smoothly and have always been celebrated with dignity here in Saint-Nazaire, just like today, and we thank you for that,” he said, addressing MSC Cruises Executive Chairman Pierfrancesco Vago.

Castaing said that MSC Cruises has brought more than 100 million hours of work to Chantiers de l’Atlantique over 25 years.

“Building ships is not only about relationships between people, even though that matters enormously. There are also tens of billions of euros in orders, tens of billions of euros necessary for our trade balance,” he added.

Lisa Marei Schmidt appointed to lead Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Lisa Marei Schmidt has worked at other institutions including Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen in Düsseldorf and Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof

Photo David von Becker/Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee

Lisa Marei Schmidt has been appointed as the new director of two major museums in Bern: Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee. The board of trustees of the foundation that oversees both institutions selected Schmidt from 97 applicants following an international search, according to a release. She will lead on artistic direction and also chair the museums' management board.

Schmidt has directed the Brücke-Museum in Berlin, home to the world’s largest collection of works by artists from the German Expressionist group Die Brücke, since 2017. During that time she has co-curated exhibitions exploring complex and sometimes dark aspects of the history of German Expressionism, such as Escape into Art? The Brücke Painters in the Nazi Period (2019) and Whose Expression? The Brücke Artists and Colonialism (2021–22). Under her direction, the museum has also strengthened its work in provenance research, restoration, art education and digitisation. The German Association of Art Critics (AICA) named the Brücke-Museum as its museum of the year for 2023.

Born in 1978, Schmidt studied art history in Marburg, Amsterdam and Berlin, and curating contemporary art at the Royal College of Art in London. Before joining the Brücke-Museum, she worked as a research trainee at Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen and served as a curator at Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof from 2012 to 2017. In 2020, she curated Bonzo's Dream by Swiss artist Vivian Suter, which received AICA's Special Exhibition award.

“To me, museums are places where art and people meet,” Schmidt said in a statement. "They provide space for encounters, new perspectives and dialogue across society." She said she looked forward to getting to know the teams in Bern and building on the distinctive qualities of the two museums, which have shared strategic leadership under the umbrella foundation Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee since 2015.

Schmidt will also inherit a pressing challenge at Kunstmuseum Bern. In June, voters in the canton rejected a CHF15.7m (£14.4m) planning credit, or budget allocation, for a project to renovate the museum's historic building and replace its 1983 extension, with 51.8% voting against it. The museum says its ageing buildings still urgently need repairs.

Schmidt succeeds Nina Zimmer, who led the institutions for a decade and will become director-general of Vienna's Belvedere in January 2027. The foundation’s commercial director Thomas Soraperra will serve as interim director until Schmidt arrives.

Finding Unexpected Kinship With Senior Travelers—And a Joyful New Outlook On Growing Older

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On a grassy field in Don-Aryk, Kyrgyzstan, men on horseback were wrestling over a headless goat carcass. This is kok boru, the country's national sport, sometimes called dead goat polo. The riders violently wrenched the goat from one another's grip, then tore across the field toward a cauldron-size goal. When the game ended, a player invited me to take a victory lap with the goat. I was two weeks into a Smithsonian Journeys trip across five Central Asian countries with a group of mostly retirees. They knew I was genuinely considering his offer, and their reaction was unanimous: Oh, no you won't.

Standing there in the Kyrgyz countryside, being collectively talked out of one of my more impulsive yeses by a busload of surrogate parents, I felt like a teenager being scolded—defiant but also secretly touched that people I had just met cared enough to save me from myself.

On my first trip to Uzbekistan, I joined an Intrepid Travel tour and wondered whether I had booked the wrong departure. Most of the guests were seniors, so I assumed we would have little in common. Then I met Penny, a septuagenarian from Australia who survived a terrorist bombing in Bali that killed three people in her group. Her husband had suffered brain damage and withdrew from the world; Penny kept traveling, even when it meant going alone. We shopped for textiles in Khiva and bonded over meals; by trip's end, whatever distance I'd imagined between us disappeared.

That first trip changed more than my assumptions about whom I might enjoy traveling with. My own parents struggled with severe depression and agoraphobia, each condition feeding the other as their shared world contracted. Older travelers like Penny showed me another way to age: by staying curious, social, active, and engaged with the world.

I saw that same determination on a Lindblad Expeditions–National Geographic cruise through the Peruvian Amazon. Dan and Kathy, both widowed after 30-year marriages, met online, fell in love, and kept a list of trips to take. Another guest had cared for her husband through terminal brain cancer, then received her own cancer diagnosis. Hardship hadn't blunted their zest for adventure; if anything, it had magnified it.

I was on board and turning that contrast over in my mind when I found out my dad died.

The next few days bled together. Through the blur my fellow travelers showed up for me—listening when I wanted to talk and giving me space when I didn't. These folks knew firsthand what it meant to have life cleaved into Before and After. Yet here they were, swimming with piranhas, trekking through the jungle, and plotting where to go next. They were living proof that joy could still exist on the other side of tragedy.

Back in Central Asia my fellow travelers who had vetoed my kok boru ride embodied what I'd come to love about traveling with groups of older people. Linda, an 80-year-old retired psychoanalyst, shared my raunchy humor and skittering attention span during tedious museum tours. I suspected Susan, 83, disapproved when she first asked about my tattoos—until she rolled up her sleeve to reveal Betty Boop on her biceps, acquired at 81. She had survived two comas and a stroke, and still posed for photos in fire-engine red lipstick. A red lip is my signature too. This was my kind of woman.

The whole group was intimidatingly accomplished: pulmonologists, oncologists, former heads of ICUs and surgical departments. Ask for a doctor, and half the bus could raise a hand. They talked passionately about history and architecture. My PhD dad could turn any conversation into a lecture too; I hadn't realized how much I missed that until I was sermonized at dinner. Their adult children came up often: Would they find the right partner? Start a family? Live up to their potential? Beneath all the second-guessing was enormous tenderness.

Traveling with seniors requires patience, of course. Some use canes and walkers, so itineraries are deliberately less demanding. To me, that's a fair trade.

On a Viking river cruise that sailed this past summer from Bucharest, Romania, to Budapest, my husband and I hit it off with Bob and Jeannie, a couple from Long Island. They peppered us with questions about our work, our four-year-old son, and our life in Minnesota. That curiosity touched a nerve. My mother, who has dementia and lives with us, no longer asks about my job, my kid, or anything else. Soon we were seeking one another out, talking for hours and doubling over with laughter. We promised to look them up the next time we were in New York—and meant it. Bob and Jeannie weren't filling in for anyone. They were just friends.

At our farewell Smithsonian dinner in Almaty, the group toasted me for “keeping us young.” I was usually the one volunteering to dance with strangers, climb onto horses, or let an owl perch on my head. If I got parental supervision in return, they got to live vicariously through my harebrained antics.

Their gratitude caught me off guard. I knew what these travelers gave me—perspective, attention, a road map for the years ahead—but hadn't considered what I might be giving them.

Now, when I join a group trip, I genuinely hope I'm the youngest person on the bus. I look forward to seeing whom I'll connect with, hearing stories I'll carry for years, and, if I'm lucky, getting a glimpse of the person I might become.

The Rings Of Power Season 3's Major Balrog Change Explained By Season 3 Director

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Warning: SPOILERS lie ahead for The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3, episode 7, "Forging of the One Ring"!EXCLUSIVE: The Balrog is back with a brand-new bag in The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3.

In J.R.R. Tolkien's source material, the fire-wielding demonic beings were former Maiar transformed into their menacing state by the villainous Morgoth, subsequently hiding in some of Middle-earth's deepest regions, namely Moria and the Misty Mountains. Staying true to the first 1954 Lord of the Rings book, the Balrog was featured in both the Ralph Bakshi and Peter Jackson films, in which Gandalf sacrificed himself to fight it while the rest of the Fellowship escaped.

Now, the Balrog is back for The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3's most-anticipated episode, "Forging of the One Ring." In the new season's seventh episode, Charlie Vickers' Sauron visits the being, residing in the mountains near Moria, in an effort to better understand how to harness the energy and fire of Mount Doom in order to finally concoct the powerful relic.

In honor of the episode's release, ScreenRant's Grant Hermanns interviewed Stefan Schwartz to discuss The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3, episode 7. When asked about the creation of the Balrog for the series and finding the show's own take on the creature, the director acknowledged that it was "tricky trying to imbue the Balrog with emotion and character," especially as they still strove to stay true to the general design from Jackson's movies, itself inspired by John Howe's illustrations.

In order to do so, the Rings of Power production team made a unique change to the character by "bringing in the fire and the color to express mood and emotion," with the Balrog's more tame moments featuring a blue-ish fire while his more intense are covered in traditionally orange flames. Though this required a fair amount of work on the visual effects side of things, Schwartz revealed that there was a surprisingly practical core to the entire scene's production:

Stefan Schwartz: That was a long chat with Jason [Smith], our visual effects person, and JD and Patrick about how those moments would work, and then working with my DOP to bring a light source that was the Balrog, and to change the color of that light source with the emotion. So, we had a big crane with a big lamp that could change different colors, and it moved where the Balrog went, so that Charlie had something to watch. Because obviously, we were on a big green screen set, and didn't have much actual stuff to react against. So, I thought it only fair if Charlie had something to react against.

Another major change made to the Balrog in The Rings of Power season 3 is giving the character a speaking voice, the first time one has ever spoken in any Lord of the Rings medium, played by fan-favorite Simon Pegg. Reflecting on the creation of the Balrog's voice and finding a personality beyond just a hulking foe, Schwartz recalled having "chatted and chatted" with Pegg on set during production of the episode 7 scene, with the pair diving into the character's Middle-earth backstory:

Stefan Schwartz: We chatted about the history of the Balrog as a Maiar, and Sauron as a Maiar, and went a bit deep into that. And then talked about how Sauron turned the conversation around to, in the end, get what he needs.

The introduction of the Balrog into The Rings of Power season 3 is itself a major change in the Lord of the Rings' overall timeline. In Tolkien's original work, it was never directly stated that Sauron and any version of the Balrog, let alone the one residing in Moria, since dubbed Durian's Bane, had interactions with one another.

Moreover, while Sauron's orcs acting as spies in the area are believed to have seen the demon and reported it to the Dark Lord, the absence of Balrogs from the surface of Middle-earth left them unable to properly convey what it was to their master, thus preventing him from interfering. However, given Sauron is also a Maiar, and therefore of equal level to the entity, Sauron would not have been able to control or enlist it for his war.

One of the other notable changes the Balrog's introduction brings to Tolkien's Lord of the Rings lore is that Sauron refers to it as an "old friend," confirming that the pair have a past relationship of some kind. With The Rings of Power frequently utilizing flashbacks to flesh out some of Sauron's backstory and the wider lore of Middle-earth, there's a possibility the now-greenlit season 4 could better explore this.

Middle-earth's timeline covers over 60,000 years, with Frodo's Lord of the Rings story featured at the end - so where does Rings of Power fit?

The other major question surrounding the Balrog's future in The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3 is whether it will become more involved in Sauron's war to conquer the world. Though technically on the same power level as one another, that there is some level of respect between the pair could see the effective forging of the One Ring see Durin's Bane willing to rise up and fight the various forces fighting back against the Dark Lord.

Yet when I asked which medical school he'd attended, he became strangely evasive. When the bill arrived, John meticulously calculated his share down to the penny, despite having spent the evening boasting about his seven-figure income. Maybe he was just frugal. But there was a growing collection of little things that didn't make sense.

Kate thought I was being cynical. At the time, I was an investigative journalist and former sex columnist living in L.A. who had recently enrolled in private investigator school. So, sure, maybe that wasn’t too misplaced. But I trust my instincts and genuinely thought she was in danger—when John left the restaurant, I borrowed my then-husband's Mercedes and followed him. John had told Kate he lived with his sister in Pasadena. He drove somewhere else entirely.

Over the following days, I began digging. I discovered that John wasn't the doctor he claimed to be—he was a nurse anesthetist with a long criminal history, had lost his professional license, and had been accused of stalking women.

I had mixed feelings about telling Kate the truth. I knew that I had to break the bad news, but I was super nervous when I showed up to our happy hour date carrying a printout of John’s mug shot. Fortunately, she believed me and broke things off. And although I suspected at the time that he was a con artist with a serious capacity for violence, I was left feeling helpless.

Years later, I listened in horror as the Dirty John podcast chronicled what happened when John Meehan entered his wife Debra Newell’s life. Their story mirrored his and Kate’s, except that when she tried to break up with him, things took a much darker turn. He eventually attacked her adult daughter, Terra Newell, who bravely fought back and killed him in self-defense. Hearing that, I was relieved that Kate had escaped. But I was also haunted by the knowledge that recognizing a dangerous man hadn't stopped him from finding another victim.

From sitting outside no-tell motels on matrimonial surveillance to piecing together a crime victim’s last day on earth, I’ve seen many situations where people get into dangerous situations—from confronting love rivals to going back for one last suitcase—all for love.

Here’s what detective work has taught me about dating:

Before I became a private investigator, I was a sex and dating columnist for The Independent in London. I spent years documenting my romantic adventures, writing about everything from casual sex to heartbreak. I've always been fascinated by the similarities between dating and detective work.

In both instances, you’re sitting across from a relative stranger. You’re asking questions, and you’re trying to figure out whether they are telling the truth—all the while hoping that, in the dating realm, any skeletons you unearth are metaphorical, and not decomposing in someone’s crawlspace.

But there's one enormous difference. When I'm investigating someone professionally, I'm looking for inconsistencies. When I'm falling for someone, I'm looking for reasons to believe them. And that's where things get dangerous.

It’s been scientifically proven that love causes a dopamine reaction in the brain similar to eating pounds of chocolate or snorting cocaine. Think about it: Would you make any other major life decision—like who to live with, introduce to your children or combine finances with—while high? That’s why I love the idea of a “sexual sober friend,” aka someone who will tell you straight up if you are being totally irrational. Of course, you have to actually be willing to take said friend’s advice–see below!

One of the most useful lessons I learned in PI school was to not to “give people the benefit of the doubt.” Instead, we say, “Trust, but verify.”

When you're investigating a crime, you build a timeline. You figure out what happened, when it happened, and whether the evidence supports the accounts you've been given. I think romantic relationships benefit from a much less obsessive version of the same principle.

Someone tells you they're looking for a serious relationship. Do their actions support that statement? Do they make plans? Follow through? Introduce you to people in their lives? Respect your boundaries? Or do they deliver a spectacular speech about wanting to find their soulmate and then disappear for eleven days because, apparently, their phone fell into the sea? A charming explanation is not the same thing as a credible one.

I once worked on a surveillance case involving a wife whose husband worked a boring day job as a dentist...but she was never allowed to come to his office, and he would disappear for hours in the evening. When he told her that he wanted a divorce and was leaving, she wanted to know where he was going, so she hired me and my partner.

After 18 hours of surveillance, I was able to confirm what six years of couples’ therapy had failed to deliver: confirmation that not only was he cheating on her with his receptionist, which I captured on camera while hiding in a deserted parking lot, but that he was leading a double life as a wannabee-Soprano small-time strip club drug dealer.

The next day, as I played the video evidence of his double life, writing prescriptions for several dancers, smoking pot, and shopping the aisles of Trader Joe’s for munchies, she told me that she had suspected for years that something was off, but she never felt ready to ask him the questions that would shatter her world. That’s happened to me a lot when I was doing matrimonial surveillance: People will tell me about finding incriminating photographs, suspicious messages, or underwear that clearly doesn't belong to them—and then ask me whether I think something is wrong.

Often, they're not really asking me to uncover the truth. They're asking me to give them permission to believe what they've already seen. Which brings me to my next principle:

I believe in intuition, but I don’t believe that it’s supernatural magic. Sometimes it's simply our brains recognizing patterns before we've consciously identified them.

At that first dinner with John Meehan, I couldn't immediately explain why I felt uncomfortable. But as the evening continued, I used my investigative training to spot the inconsistencies: his evasiveness, his elaborate claims, the mismatch between the person he presented and the details of his life. The important part wasn't that I had a bad feeling, it was that I paid attention to the feeling and then looked for facts.

Even after years of studying human behavior, I still sometimes find myself making the same mistakes I warn other people about.

While working as a journalist on a murder investigation in Arkansas, I became attracted to someone I was interviewing, someone who had been good friends with a possible person of interest. I knew perfectly well that personal chemistry could interfere with my judgment but found myself getting involved anyway.

Investigative work can teach you to be skeptical, but skepticism isn't a force field. You can know that someone is wrong for you and still want them. And you can be exceptionally good at uncovering other people's secrets while remaining spectacularly talented at hiding things from yourself.

A lot of people expect me to turn into Nancy Drew with every man I meet. There’s some truth to that; like most women, I do a Google deep dive before meeting someone for the first time.

But after spending years investigating cheating spouses, con artists, and eventually a cold-case murder, I've become convinced that there's a fine line between healthy curiosity and turning your love life into a full-time surveillance operation.

I don't think women should spend their evenings running background checks on every man who buys them a cocktail. Nor should they secretly follow their partners or interpret every unanswered text as evidence of a double life.

I never invade any significant others’ privacy and never would. If I don’t trust them, the relationship isn’t worth it to me. Because tracking people, online or otherwise, is exhausting. And I already have enough of that in my day job!

For years, I thought being a good investigator meant finding answers to every question. Now I think one of the most important skills—both in detective work and in dating—is knowing when you have enough information to make a decision. And when it's time to stop investigating and get on with your life.

How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Study: Brewers can still use up to 50% of smoke-tainted hops without affecting beer taste and aroma.

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on how much tainted hops are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a new paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

All beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University

Next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

By contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

The authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” said Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”

There’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. The findings may not extend to wine production, however, since the fermentation processes are so different, per the authors. And grapes comprise almost all the raw materials in wine, while hops are a smaller fraction of beer brewing ingredients.

The US Air Force Bolted Jet Engines Onto Its Largest-Ever Bomber, But It Still Wasn't Enough To Save It

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker strategic bomber is one of the most ambitious bombers ever built. While the unusual pusher configuration of its six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines is perhaps its most distinctive feature, later variants also carried four General Electric J47 turbojets. These jets were added to improve take-off performance, climb rate, and high-speed dash capability, helping keep the enormous bomber operationally relevant.

This remained the case until newer all-jet bombers, particularly the B-52 Stratofortress, assumed the primary nuclear deterrence role. The United States Air Force procured 384 B-36s, each capable of carrying up to 86,000 lb (39,000 kg) of bombs. Although none remain airworthy today, four complete aircraft have been preserved in museums, where they provide a reminder of the largest piston-engined combat aircraft ever placed into operational service.

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker was the largest bomber built by the US, and it can be thought of as the most important transitional bomber ever built. The US Air Force Museum states that "responding to the U.S. Army Air Forces' requirement for a strategic bomber with intercontinental range, Consolidated Vultee (later Convair) designed the B-36 during World War II. The airplane made its maiden flight in August 1946, and in June 1948 the Strategic Air Command received its first operational B-36."

The museum also explains that "some B-36s served as photographic reconnaissance aircraft, and others were adapted to launch and retrieve specially modified RF-84F/K reconnaissance planes." Although conceived during the Second World War as an ultra-long-range conventional bomber, the B-36 entered service as the United States shifted towards a strategy centred on delivering nuclear weapons across intercontinental distances without relying on overseas bases.

This remains a central mission of the world's current strategic bombers (B-52, B-1B, B-2, B-21, Tu-160, and Tu-95MS). The Peacemaker's origins date to 1941, which is before the US even entered the Second World War. There was a fear in the US that the UK could fall to Germany, and if that happened, the US would lack a forward base for its bombers to raid Germany. As such, the requirements for the bomber included that it could fly intercontinentally from North America to Europe.

Designing and developing the bomber took time, and by the time it entered service in 1948, the Second World War had been over for three years. As things had panned out, the US had been able to use Britain against Germany, and had island-hopped to bring its B-29s within range of Japan.

However, the delays in designing the bomber also allowed engineers to update the design to carry the heavy nuclear bombs that weighed around 8,800 to 11,000 lbs (4,000 to 5,000 kg) and even the extremely heavy hydrogen bombs. The first experimental thermonuclear devices weighed tens of tonnes, while the first operational hydrogen bombs introduced in the early 1950s typically weighed around 30,000–42,000 lb (14,000–19,000 kg).

The Peacemaker was designed with the gigantic bomb bay doors needed to carry these bombs that no other aircraft could. When it entered service, it was able to carry around 86,000 lbs (39,000 kg) of payload, vastly more than any other bomber. With the establishment of the Strategic Air Command in 1946, the B-36 became the first true intercontinental nuclear bomber.

The world's geostrategic picture changed in 1949 as the Soviet Union exploded its first atomic bomb. Then, the US pivoted to requiring a bomber able to strike targets deep inside the USSR while using bases in the continental United States and be able to carry heavy first-generation nuclear bombs. This is something the Boeing B-29 Superfortress couldn't do, but it was very similar to what the B-36 was originally designed for.

Germany developed flying prototypes of longer range bombers that would be able to fly over and bomb New York City.

With the benefit of hindsight, the B-36 was always likely to be overtaken by the rapid pace of jet-powered aviation. It was conceived during the Second World War, but was ultimately built for a very different strategic environment. At first (late 1940s to early 1950s), the B-36 was unmatched.

No fighter could reliably intercept the Peacemaker at its high operating altitude (around 40,000 feet or 12,000 meters, later extending to 45,000 feet or 13,700 meters). This was before the USSR developed an integrated air defense network and a time when early Soviet jet fighters struggled to reach and engage such high-flying targets.

The Peacemaker's enormous range also allowed it to approach the USSR from multiple directions, further complicating interceptions. But the B-36 was becoming a relic of a bygone era. It was designed before jet-powered strategic bombers were practical. By the mid-1950s, new Soviet jet interceptors like the MiG-17 and then MiG-19 made it increasingly vulnerable.

This worsened with the development of increasingly capable radar-guided air defense systems and surface-to-air missile technology. The arrival of the all-jet B-52 Stratofortress further hastened the B-36's retirement by offering greater speed, improved survivability, and comparable intercontinental range. Perhaps somewhat similar, the arrival of the B-21 will inevitably increase pressure to retire the older, expensive, and less survivable B-2 Spirits.

By the time it entered service, the B-52 was in development, but was still years away. The Peacemaker was primarily powered by six enormous 28-cylinder Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines arranged in a distinctive pusher configuration. However, these were too slow in the emerging jet age. In an effort to bridge the gap, the US Air Force invested heavily in modernizing the Peacemaker with four General Electric J47 turbojets.

The J47 turbojet engines were mounted in two twin-engine pods beneath the outwings and earned it the famous slogan "six turning and four burning." They were not intended for continuous cruise as they were incredibly fuel inefficient. Instead, they were used for heavy-weight takeoffs, improved climbing, increased dash speed in hostile territory, and reduced time spent over defended targets.

The USAF looked for other ways to improve the performance of the Peacemaker. It introduced the Featherweight modification programs, which removed defensive armament, ammunition, armor, and other equipment to improve altitude, range, and speed. The B-36 was given improved electronics, while the RB-36 strategic reconnaissance variant was also developed.

The Hercules has many tricks, one of which is external rocket pods, which enable it to get off the snow while operating on the poles.

The Air Force Museum states that "powered by six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 engines, the B-36J cruised at 230 mph [200 knots], but for additional bursts of speed its four General Electric J47s increased the maximum speed to 435 mph [378 knots]." Ultimately, the improvements addressed symptoms, but were unable to fix the underlying problem, which was that the B-36 just didn't belong in the jet age.

Relying on piston engines, the B-36 was too slow, typically cruising at 200 knots (378 knots at maximum speed). The B-47 Stratojet could cruise at 434 knots, and the B-52 at 456 knots. By the mid-1950s, increasingly capable Soviet jet interceptors such as the MiG-17 and MiG-19, together with radar-guided surface-to-air missile systems including the S-25 Berkut and later the S-75 Dvina, steadily eroded the B-36's survivability.

The Peacemaker faced other pressures. For example, the complex push-configuration radial engines frequently overheated, caught fire, and required changing a staggering 336 spark plugs per service. The massive size of the aircraft also demanded immense resources to maintain and operate.

The final B-36 Peacemaker was retired in 1959, just one year before a Soviet S-75 Dvina (SA-2) downed a Lockheed U-2 spy plane at an altitude of around 70,000 feet. The development contributed to the program that would give rise to the SR-71 as well as contributed to the abandoning of the XB-70 Valkyrie bomber.

Today, the Peacemaker is largely forgotten in popular memory. It is overshadowed by the earlier B-29 Superfortress, which entered service too late to make a difference in Germany but in time to play a pivotal role in ending the War in the Pacific, including dropping the nuclear bombs on Japan. The B-36 arrived too late for the Second World War, but it was also ill-prepared for the realities of the Cold War and the jet age.

It was quickly overshadowed by the early Cold War Boeing B-52 Stratofortress that entered service in the mid 1950s and continues in service today. These two successful bombers have had the effect of pushing the Peacemaker out of popular imagination. Unlike the B-29 and B-52, relatively few B-36s were preserved after retirement, with only four surviving in museums. None remain airworthy.

Here’s How To Make Your Holiday EV Road Trip Easier

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It’s a short workweek for most of us here in the United States. In just a day or two, many of us will load up our cars and trek around the country to our loved ones. If you’re reading this, there’s a decent chance you’ll be taking on of those trips in an electric vehicle. EVs have come a long way, so in theory, range anxiety shouldn’t be much of an issue for anyone these days. Still, with so many drivers on the roads, there are bound to be annoyances that come with long EV road trips.

Here are a few tips that can take the edge off a long trip.

DC fast charging times are getting better and better, but charging still takes longer than stopping for gas. Thus, bottlenecks can occur on well-traveled routes with limited DC fast charging stops. It’s just kind of the nature of the beast when most people are stopping for 20-30 minutes. Multiply that by the plethora of drivers also trying to charge up and get home, and well, it’s not hard to see why EV charging queues can balloon, adding time to your drive home.

But you can head some of that off if you prep the night before. For those with home charging, that means simply plugging the car in and ensuring it’s fully charged for the next day’s trip. For those who don’t have access to home charging, using a DC fast charger the night before, when everyone isn’t rushing to leave, could ease some of the frustration of charging up before getting on the roads.

Also, some DC fast charging companies give a discount for charging during off-peak hours. Prepping the night before won’t just save you time, it might save you money.

We may have a set route in mind for our trip, and that could be all fine and dandy, but is it really the best route for our cars? Electric vehicles tend to consume more energy at higher speeds and when traveling up hills. Thus, it could be advantageous to maximize range by picking a flatter route, or one with lower speeds.

In most cases this won’t be necessary, but if you’re pushing the limits of your car and the infrastructure around you, it’s good to know. Most route planning apps—like A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, or even Google Maps—take into account which route is friendlier to your car’s range calculator, and may suggest detours if you get a big range boost from a route that’s nearly as quick.

These route planners will tell you exactly when and where to stop, depending on your car’s efficiency, your destination, your desired state of charge at arrival, and road conditions along the way. They can be adjusted to fit your real-world efficiency data or your need for breaks. Rather than just winging, using a route planner ensures you’re never in an uncomfortable situation without the range or charging ability to get where you need to go.

This goes hand in hand with route planning, but I think it is relevant here. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is typically the single busiest travel day of the year. You’re not the only one on the road who will need charging. Prepping the night before can help with the initial charging wait times at the start of your trip. If you’re on a long journey, chances are you’ll find yourself in a big queue at a strange charger.

When you’re planning your route with charging stops, pay attention to what brands they are and what ports they use. An increasing number of highway stops often have several competing charging stations. A Tesla Supercharging station could have an Ionna Rechargery nearby. Or maybe it’s an EV Go station. Or maybe it’s a charging station by Circle K or Shell. In any case, they tend to cluster together near high-power infrastructure. Rather than fumble with apps and payments at the station, I think it’s best if you research the charging companies on your route and pre-emptively install their apps and set up payment.

Similarly, bring an adapter. NACS is quickly making its way across the country, but not every station has NACS ports, and not every one has CCS. Some of the stations with your desired plug will likely be busy. An adapter that allows your car to access both NACS and CCS ports will save a lot of headaches.

Once again, you’re not the only one trying to get home for the holidays. Let’s keep the queue moving, and try not to charge more than 80%, if possible. Charging speeds slow substantially when EV batteries surpass 80%. It may have only taken 15-20 minutes to get to 80%, but it’ll take another 20 or so to reach 100%, or much longer in some cars. Chances are, there’s someone waiting. Not only is it courteous to unplug and move on, but it’s also probably quicker to charge again (if you need it) closer to your destination.

I know this list has mostly been about minimizing time lost due to charging, but I think it’s important to remember that it’s okay to take a break. Charging stops are inherently longer than refuelling a gas car, but I think that 15-30 minute break is helpful for safe driving. Taking a rest, using the restroom, or getting a snack can take the edge off a trip. Use an EV’s characteristics to your advantage.

I’ve been doing EV road trips since 2021, and it’s amazing to see just how far both vehicle range and charging infrastructure have come. Long trips are possible for most drivers these days, whereas years ago, it was a huge hardship or even impossible. With better apps, better infrastructure, and better cars, it’s only gotten easier. But it still pays to know what you’re doing. Drive safely out there, folks.

United Airlines Remembers Your Bad Flight And May Upgrade You Next Time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

United Airlines appears to be quietly remembering when passengers suffer through a bad trip and then surprising some of them with a complimentary Economy Plus seat on a future flight. It costs United very little when the seat would otherwise remain empty, but it communicates something valuable to the passenger: we remember what happened to you.

A passenger recently shared an interesting message from United Airlines with aviation insider JonNYC.

The traveler had missed a connection about a month earlier and then received the following message before another United flight:

“We know your previous travel experience may have faced some challenges, and we want to make your journey more comfortable.”

United moved the passenger to Economy Plus, offering additional legroom at no charge.

What makes the gesture especially interesting is that the passenger had no MileagePlus elite status. The upcoming flight was lightly booked, which presumably made it easier for United to offer the seat without giving up revenue.

JonNYC described it as United’s technology performing more “dark magic,” and I think that is a fair description. The system appears to have connected an earlier operational disruption with a future reservation, then identified an inexpensive opportunity to improve the next journey.

Reports of similar gestures stretch back at least to 2025, so this does not appear to be entirely new. One United Silver member reported receiving an Economy Plus assignment after an eight-hour delay, even after United had already provided a $75 travel credit.

But United has not publicly announced this as a formal benefit, and we do not know precisely how passengers are selected (not that I would expect otherwise with such a “Surprise and Delight” program, lest it become an entitlement).

With advancing technology, airlines already know when they have inconvenienced you.

They know when your flight was delayed, when you missed a connection, and when you were rebooked. They also know whether you have another reservation and which seats remain available on that flight.

Yet customers are often forced to explain the entire situation again in a complaint, including information the airline already has in its own systems. That creates more work for the passenger after the airline has already disrupted the trip, though I’m sadly not expecting carriers to offer proactive compensation, even when delays are within its control.

United appears to be using its data more intelligently, like with its ConnectionSaver technology, that can help some passengers without inconveniencing others.

I have written before about United introducing technology that saves callers from repeating information when they are transferred from an automated system to a human agent. I also welcomed the addition of an app feature that watches earlier flights for disrupted passengers while preserving their confirmed reservations.

This Economy Plus gesture reflects a similar approach: use the information already available to reduce friction and improve the passenger experience.

An Economy Plus seat is not a first class upgrade. The passenger receives more legroom, but the onboard service remains the same as regular economy class.

Even so, it has value. United charges for these seats, sometimes quite a bit depending upon the route and seat location. Receiving one unexpectedly can make a future flight noticeably more comfortable.

The economics also make sense for United. If the flight is lightly booked and the Economy Plus seat would otherwise remain empty, the marginal cost of assigning it to an inconvenienced passenger is essentially zero.

The value to the passenger can be much greater than the cost to the airline.

I also appreciate that the message explicitly connects the better seat to the previous experience. A random seat reassignment might go unnoticed, while a clearly explained gesture tells the passenger that United remembers what happened and wants another chance to deliver a better trip.

That is smart service recovery…I wonder if the system is smart enough to do this when passengers have to be moved anyway due to weight and balance reasons? (why let a good opportunity go to waste?)

This is not a published benefit, and passengers should not expect a complimentary Economy Plus assignment every time something goes wrong. We do not know what level of disruption qualifies, how much empty space must be available, or whether United considers the passenger’s fare or travel history…and that’s the point, lest it become an expectation.

And of course United must be careful when selecting a seat to avoid a familiar complaint on Delta Air Lines. Moving someone from a regular economy aisle seat to an Economy Plus middle seat will not strike every passenger as an improvement. Ideally, the system should preserve the passenger’s aisle or window preference whenever possible or offer the upgrade rather than automatically moving the traveler.

Furthermore, if a MileagePlus Premier is expecting an entire row and suddenly has a seatmate, I can understand there will be annoyance (though that’s a sort of entitlement that is not helpful either)…in the past I’ve passed on upgrades to domestic first class in order to stretch across multiple seats in economy class.

Still, those limitations do not diminish the appeal of the idea. This is a discretionary gesture, not something passengers can demand, and that element of surprise is part of what makes it effective.

When airlines discuss service recovery, the conversation often centers on travel credits or miles. Those can be appropriate, particularly after a serious operational failure. But compensation need not be limited to those forms.

A better seat, lounge invitation, meal voucher, or another small benefit can communicate recognition when it is offered thoughtfully. The key is identifying something the passenger will appreciate that the airline can provide at a modest cost.

United has become particularly skilled at using technology to address operational problems. ConnectionSaver is the best example, intelligently holding certain flights when connecting passengers can reach the gate without causing a significant delay.

United appears to be quietly offering complimentary Economy Plus seats to some passengers whose previous trips went poorly, including travelers without elite status.

The gesture will not erase a missed connection or make every passenger forget a miserable journey. But it shows that United remembers the disruption and recognizes an opportunity to do something helpful. An empty Economy Plus seat costs United almost nothing. Used this way, it can generate far more goodwill than another automated apology email…it’s a positive step.

6 Underrated Mountain Towns to Visit This Winter

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

You know the big names. Jackson, Wyoming. Stowe, Vermont. Telluride, Colorado. These are the mountain towns that dominate our winter daydreams. The famous ski towns that make us consider taking a sabbatical, buying a van, and living the Aspen Extreme dream for three months in the heart of winter. But there are other winter adventure spots that deserve the spotlight.

I cover mountain towns for Outside, and every year I start plotting out my own winter getaways right about now. Sure, I enjoy hitting up established destinations like Park City as much as the next skier, but the towns on this list get me excited because they fly under the radar. They don’t receive a ton of hype, but they have access to amazing powder stashes, lengthy trail systems, and resort lines that are far shorter than their better-known counterparts’.

A couple of these towns are my longtime favorites, while others are still on my “must-go” list. Here are six often overlooked winter mountain towns that you need to visit once the snow starts to gather.

A legitimate winter town in the South? Yes indeed. Boone is a vibrant college town that grows when Appalachian State’s roughly 20,000 students are in session. It has all of the hallmarks of a fun-loving mountain town, with plenty of dive bars and burrito joints to keep you satiated. But it also sits in North Carolina’s High Country, surrounded by 5,000- and 6,000-foot mountains, which get some of the heaviest snowfalls in the region. There are three ski resorts within 30 minutes of downtown and some of the most consistent cross-country skiing in the South on a series of 6,000-foot peaks near the Tennessee border.

Winter Adventures: You have three downhill resorts to choose from, but Beech Mountain Resort is the highest ski resort in eastern North America, with a summit elevation of 5,506 feet. It also has a bar, the 5506′ Skybar, at the top of the mountain and its own brewery in the base village. As for the terrain, the mountain has 95 acres and just over 800 feet of drop, so you’ll need to do laps to get in your vert. But the terrain park gets better every year, and skiers can usually count on about 80 inches of snow every winter. Full-day adult lift tickets start at $57 on weekdays.

But the best winter adventure near Boone is cross-country skiing Roan Mountain, a high ridge that tops out at 6,285 feet, gets heavy snowfall, and has a variety of terrain to ski, from high-elevation grassy balds to singletrack through rhododendron tunnels. The only catch is you’ll need to bring your own skis, as rentals are scarce in the South. Start at Carver’s Gap and head up the gated Forest Service road, which climbs gradually for about two miles to the Rhododendron Gardens. You’ll get long-range views in both directions and the option to hop onto more challenging trails that branch off the road.

Duluth is a longtime shipping and industrial city at the western tip of Lake Superior that has found new life as an adventure hub, with a world-class mountain bike trail system traversing the hills on the edge of downtown. But the city’s location in northern Minnesota means winter hits the city hard. Duluth gets about 86 inches of lake-effect snow each winter, and that snow sticks around because high temperatures stay below freezing on more than 100 days a year. The constant cold temps are ideal for ice climbing, and the consistent snow and cornucopia of mountain bike trails mean Duluth becomes a fat-biking and cross-country ski mecca during winter.

Need more reasons to visit? How about downhill skiing at nearby Spirit Mountain, which has 175 acres of terrain with 700 feet of vertical drop.

Winter Adventures: Northern Minnesota has a legitimate ski culture, and Spirit Mountain has some of the best blue cruisers in the Midwest, many of which have long-range views of downtown Duluth and Lake Superior. The mountain also has two terrain parks, one for beginners looking to progress and one for shredders looking to send it. There’s a welcoming uphill policy for anyone who wants to earn their turns on touring gear, too. Adult tickets start at $83.

But Duluth is a bike town, and when the snow falls that means it’s time to roll out the fat tires. Many sections of the Duluth Traverse, an epic 40-mile-long route that runs from one side of the city to the other, are groomed for fat bikes in the winter. The machine-built trails in Mission Creek, with their berms and rollers, are particularly fun with a layer of packed snow. Local outfitter Day Tripper offers guided fat-bike tours and rentals.

New England got hammered with snow last winter, with the highest elevations in the mountains getting more than 200 inches of snow, and the tiny town of Jackson was poised to make the most of it. This picture-perfect village, which comes complete with a general store and covered bridge, sits on the edge of White Mountain National Forest with fast access to downhill skiing at Black Mountain and roughly 90 miles of cross-country ski trails scattered throughout the valley.

Jackson doesn’t have the cachet of some other ski towns in New England, but American ski culture got an early boost here when Black Mountain opened in 1935, making it one of the first lift operations in the country. And that culture is still thriving. Case in point: check out Friday Night Lights, a weekly uphill party where skiers put on headlamps and ski to the top of the mountain en masse.

Winter Adventures: The Jackson Ski Touring Foundation grooms and maintains about 150 kilometers of trails, all surrounding a touring center with rentals and lessons. The trails traverse the community throughout three consecutive river valleys over 60 square miles. You’ll get views of Mount Washington from open meadows, tight singletrack through woods, and even the chance to hit trailside restaurants in certain spots. Day passes start at $29.

No trip to Jackson is complete without some laps at Black Mountain, a proudly independent ski resort with 140 acres of glades, narrow groomers, and nonexistent lift lines. You’ll get 1,100 feet of vertical drop, with the steepest terrain at the summit, and a lively pub (the Shovel Handle Pub) at the base of the mountain for après-ski activities. Adult lift tickets start at $49.

The San Juan Mountains are home to some of Colorado’s most iconic mountain towns and most well-regarded ski slopes, but most skiers don’t explore the region beyond Telluride and Silverton. Dig deeper into the mountains of southwest Colorado and you’ll find tiny Pagosa Springs, which sits on the banks of the San Juan River. The hamlet is best known for its geothermal springs, but the mountains surrounding this hot-water hub are even more enticing. Pagosa Springs is 23 miles from Wolf Creek Ski Area, which is known as one of the snowiest mountains in Colorado, pulling down an average of 430 inches of snow every winter.

Want more variety? Summer waterfalls turn into ice-climbing playgrounds when the temperature drops, and the local Nordic club grooms more than 50 kilometers of skate and classic ski trails near town.

Winter Adventures: Forget the champagne huts and concierge service you find at many ski resorts these days. Wolf Creek is a no-frills operation that’s all about the snow and the terrain, with 430 inches of annual snowfall that blanket wide groomers and steep trees. OK, a lot of trees. Wolf Creek has a significant amount of expert terrain, which plays out in a series of steep bowls at the top of the resort and seemingly endless glades all over the mountain. And you won’t have to take out a loan to fund this trip; full-day adult lift tickets at Wolf Creek are right at $110.

The 50 kilometers of cross-country ski trails begin in town, with a mile-long loop inside the town park that’s perfect for beginners. But this is a great chance to dip a toe into fat biking. There’s an 8.5-mile loop that’s groomed for fat bikes when snow allows, winding through a ponderosa pine forest. Pagosa Mountain Sport rents cross-country skis and fat bikes.

Park City, Salt Lake City…these are the ski towns that get the press, but Ogden? Ogden doesn’t typically make the list. But it should. This historic city, which was the first permanent settlement by people of European descent in the state of Utah, sits between the Great Salt Lake and the Wasatch Mountains, just 40 minutes north of Salt Lake City, but seemingly a world apart. Downtown Ogden is anchored by 25th Street, a historic main street with upscale Wild West charm, while three ski resorts known for deep powder are less than 30 minutes away. Powder Mountain anchors the trio, with more than 8,000 acres of terrain, but Snowbasin is continually ranked one of the best ski resorts in the country by Ski magazine and the smaller Nordic Valley has a local-hill vibe complete with night skiing. Bonus: You don’t have to fight Salt Lake City’s notorious canyon traffic to make your turns.

Winter Adventures: You’re here to ski, so get after it at Powder Mountain, the largest ski resort in the Lower 48. Powder has 2,800 acres of lift-served terrain with more than 2,000 feet of vertical drop, but the real gem here is Powder’s sidecountry. Head through the Powder Country or Woody’s World entrance gates from the lift-served side of the mountain and you’ll have 1,200 acres of ungroomed, off-piste action at your ski tips. The best part? A shuttle bus picks you up at the bottom of your line and takes you back to the front of the mountain, so there’s no boot-packing involved. Adult lift tickets start at $99.

You might also want to try your hand at sledding AKA snowmobiling while you’re in Ogden. The Monte Cristo area, east of the Ogden Valley, has some of the best snowmobile terrain in the state, including a 63-mile groomed loop that’s perfect for beginners and has long-range views of the Ogden Valley. Club Rec North offers tours and rentals next to the trailhead. Tours with rentals start at $229.

This former gold-mining post in the North Cascades really leans into its historic roots, with a downtown that’s lined with Old West-style false fronts and wooden boardwalks. And that western heritage is still alive and well. A number of outfitters offer horseback adventures and the annual Ride to Rendezvous, a four-day horse-packing trip complete with a wagon train that rolls into town for Winthrop’s ’49er Days parade. But make no mistake, Winthrop has found new life as a ski town. Well, a skinny-ski town. The Methow Valley surrounding Winthrop has one of the most extensive cross-country trail networks in the country with more than 120 miles of groomed, marked and maintained paths to explore.

Looking for a cozy vibe for the whole family? Take a spin around Winthrop Rink, the town’s outdoor ice-skating rink that serves as the social hub for the season.

Winter Adventures: If you’re spending time in Winthrop, then you’re here to ski the Methow Trails. With more than 120 miles of obsessively groomed paths, there is something for everyone in this valley. Want to ski from lodge to lodge? That’s an option. Want a pretty loop around a lake? No problem. Want to spend the day climbing an obscene amount of vertical? They have you covered. The Methow Trails organization runs clinics and camps all season if you want to improve your kick-and-glide form or maybe try your hand at biathlon. Day passes start at $32.

Loup Loup Ski Bowl is a lift-served mountain with 300 acres that’s operated by a nonprofit. There are 10 runs with 1,240 vertical feet of drop. You’ll find beginner trails and expert trails that drop from the top of the mountain and a very welcoming uphill ski culture if you want to earn your turns. And you’ve got to check out the Ski Bowl’s luge course, where you guide an old-school sled down groomed trails. Adult lift tickets start at $80.

Gun Rights and Guilt by Association

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Some excerpts from the longish opinion today in Shilling v. County of San Diego, decided today by Ninth Circuit Judge Patrick Bumatay, joined by Judges Kenneth Lee and Jennifer Sung:

The San Diego County Sheriff revoked Kenneth Shilling's concealed-carry weapon ("CCW") license over his affiliation with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club…. [We conclude, among other things, that] the district court improperly dismissed his First Amendment [freedom of association] claim….

Kenneth Charles Shilling resides in San Diego, California. He's a member of the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which, according to Shilling, was founded in 1946 by World War II veterans. In his view, it's not like some other motorcycle clubs, which are known for criminal and gang activity. Instead, the Boozefighters focus on charitable work and the "core values of family, work, and community." Shilling's record is clean; he has no criminal history.

In June 2022, when the "good moral character" requirement [for getting a concealed caryr license] still existed [under California law], Shilling applied for and received a CCW license from then-San Diego County Sheriff Anthony Ray. This was short lived. Less than a year and a half later, in December 2023, a different San Diego County Sheriff, Kelly Martinez, used the "good moral character" requirement to revoke Shilling's CCW license. In a letter notifying Shilling of the revocation, Sheriff Martinez explained that the "San Diego Sheriff's Department['s] good moral character definition" excludes a person who:

Has a pattern of or propensity for criminal conduct, as demonstrated by anything in the application or investigation for a license. This includes, but is not limited to, evidence of criminal behavior or a close nexus to a criminal gang. In making this determination, the Sheriff's Department shall use an objective inquiry, considering the facts and circumstances known at the time of the application or through the investigation.

The letter said that Shilling's license was revoked due to his association with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which the Sheriff described as a "criminal street gang." Shilling was not given the opportunity to contest the allegations before his license was revoked, but he could request reconsideration of the decision. The new CCW regime with the "dangerousness" requirement took effect on January 1, 2024, shortly after Shilling's license was revoked….

Here's the court's analysis allowing Shilling's First Amendment freedom of association claim to go forward:

The First Amendment guarantees all Americans the "right to associate with others." … [T]he right protects groups of individuals "in pursuit of a wide variety of political, social, economic, educational, religious and cultural ends." …

The government may infringe the freedom of association in "varied ways," including by "punish[ing] individuals for their affiliations." The government cannot impose "criminal sanctions or deny[ ] rights and privileges solely because of a citizen's association with an unpopular organization." For example, a group of students could not be denied the "benefits" of official campus recognition at a state college merely because of its affiliation with a national organization associated with disruptive and violent campus activity…. [Likewise,] "Civil liability may not be imposed merely because an individual belonged to a group, some members of which committed acts of violence." … If the government seeks to deprive an individual of a benefit, privilege, or right based on association with an alleged criminal organization, "it is necessary to establish that the group itself possessed unlawful goals and that the individual held a specific intent to further those illegal aims." …

Given this understanding of the First Amendment, Shilling easily pleaded a violation of his freedom of association…. [His] allegations sufficiently raise an infringement of Shilling's free-association right because the Sheriff deprived him of a benefit, privilege, or right—the ability to carry a concealed weapon in public—"solely because of [his] association with an unpopular organization." Unless the Sheriff adequately meets her burden of showing the Boozefighters "possessed unlawful goals" and Shilling's "specific intent to further those illegal aims," she cannot "punish [Shilling] for [his] affiliations." This kind of "guilt by association alone, without establishing that an individual's association poses the threat feared by the Government, is an impermissible basis upon which to deny First Amendment rights."

In dismissing this claim, the district court misunderstood the nature of the free-association right. It asserted that Shilling had to show the revocation of his CCW license somehow prevented him from associating with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club or participating in the Club's activities. While preventing an individual from participating in a group's lawful activities may be one way to violate the First Amendment right, it's infringed in "varied ways." And "denying" an individual benefits, rights, or privileges "solely because of [his] association"—as Shilling alleges—is certainly another way….

For the court's analysis of sovereign immunity and of whether Schilling's Second Amendment claim was moot, see the full opinion.

Steelers-Eagles preview: What to expect from Philadelphia’s offense, defense Week 11

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Steelers will travel to Philadelphia — a city where they haven't won since 1965 — on Sunday in a game that many fans have had circled as an Eagles victory since before the season.

However, the 2026 Eagles aren't the same team that made the playoffs every year from 2021-25. They enter Week 11 with a dismal 2-7 record, with all those losses coming in a row against the Bears, Rams, Jaguars, Panthers, Cowboys, Commanders and Giants. Philadelphia hasn't been in the win column since a Sept. 20 victory over the Titans.

Is this Eagles team finally bad enough for the Steelers to break such a lengthy losing streak in The City of Brotherly Love? Or will Philadelphia, fresh off a Week 10 bye, be able to hold off the Steelers at home for yet another season? We'll find out in Week 11.

RB: It's been a rough year for the Eagles offense, now on its third quarterback of the season ahead of Week 11's matchup versus the Steelers. The numbers above speak for themselves: Philadelphia has struggled on both the ground and in the air in 2026, dealing with injuries, the sudden retirement of right tackle Lane Johnson, and poor execution from players on the field. The Eagles have only scored over 20 points four times this season, and have yet to break 30 once.

Before a Week 10 bye, the Eagles faced the New York Giants in Week 9, falling 19-11 in an ugly game that's been all too familiar for the Philly faithful. But one thing did change, with the Eagles trying to spark their Jalen Hurts-less offense by replacing backup Tanner McKee with Joe "Bazooka" Milton at quarterback. He's expected to start again against Pittsburgh on Sunday.

The results were not great against the Giants, with Milton finishing with 169 passing yards and 18/34 completion in Week 9. He didn't throw a single touchdown or interception, and was sacked a whopping seven times. However, the guy with the "Bazooka" nickname does indeed have a powerful arm, with Milton logging an 11.8% deep throw percentage along with eight air yards per attempt (both team highs for the Eagles this season). He's also a much better runner than McKee, leading the Eagles with 64 yards on the ground and a 7.1 average against New York.

Frankly, Milton plays like a still-developing quarterback. He holds onto the ball too long and doesn't seem to go through his reads at NFL speed. However, his arm talent does allow him to rifle balls into open zones when he sees them, and there's always a little bit of worry when playing a passer with his physical abilities — even if everything only clicks a few times a game, he can still make defenses pay. It's worth noting that Pittsburgh hasn't played many highly-mobile quarterbacks this year, and has had some issues with deep coverage this season.

With Milton under center, the Eagles have mixed up their playbook from McKee a bit with a few more rollouts and designed quarterback runs. What hasn't changed, however, is that Philadelphia remains an 11-personnel heavy team that likes short throws outside the numbers to move the ball in the passing game. Making matters worse is that despite the short passing game, Philadelphia ranks dead-last in the league in yards after catch in 2026, with just 738 on the year.

First-round receiver Makai Lemon is a name to watch as a twitchy underneath target, and Milton will get to throw to the now-healthy Devonta Smith for the first time this season after the latter missed the Giants game with injury. That's a big boost to what was a decimated Philly receiving core in Week 9. However, Dontayvion Wicks will still be out for the Eagles against Pittsburgh.

Capping the Eagles' offensive ceiling has been a rough year from the line, which lost All-World right tackle Lane Johnson to retirement in October and has struggled with some regression. Jordan Mailata remains a very good left tackle and Philadelphia now has Landon Dickerson back from injured reserve, but the Eagles still rank seventh-highest in sack rate allowed (9%), third-highest in pressure rate allowed (41.9%), and second-highest in runs stuffed percentage (23.5%) despite facing the seventh-most light boxes in the league.

Having inexperienced signal-callers since Hurts landed on injured reserve certainly hasn't helped the Eagles in pass protection. Philadelphia struggled mightily with interior pressure against the Giants, and gave up a number of unblocked rushers on stunts and twists.

One name to watch is rookie right tackle Markell Bell, a massive human being who has struggled some in pass protection this year, but is a promising run blocker.

Still, on the ground, the Eagles' inside zone-heavy attack is tied for 28th in the NFL in yards per rush (3.7), with just 28 non-Milton yards on the ground against New York. However, star running back Saquon Barkley, who played through injury against the Giants, should be rested up after the Eagles' bye and looking a bit more explosive. In two of Barkley's first three games this season, he averaged 5.5 yards per carry — when healthy, the 29-year-old is still a legitimate offensive weapon.

After a nightmare game against the Bengals last week, the Pittsburgh defense matches up much better against Philadelphia. The key will be stifling the Eagles' run game and pressuring Milton, which the Steelers' front seven is certainly capable of, especially with Derrick Harmon back in play on the interior. Milton averaged 3.12 seconds to throw last week — that should give TJ Watt more time to get home against a rookie right tackle.

Still, with Devonta Smith back in the lineup for the Eagles, along with solid ancillary weapons in Lemon and tight end Dallas Goedert available, a Pittsburgh secondary that got torched last week will still need to bring a better performance to the table. That said, there should be a lot more optimism heading into this week than the last for the Steelers' defense.

RP: Much like the Eagles' offense this year, the defensive side of the ball still possesses a lot of talent, but has performed well below expectations. A unit that was the driving force behind a Super Bowl victory just two seasons ago, the Eagles seemed poised to field a young and imposing defense that would keep them contenders for the rest of the 2020s. Instead, they were sellers at the deadline, shipping away defensive tackle Jordan Davis, whom they'd signed to a three-year, $78 million extension this March.

I spoke with Brandon Gowton of Bleeding Green Nation this week, to try to get a sense of why the defense has underperformed, and he said it's been one of the more baffling developments this season. He cited a lingering injury to superstar defensive tackle Jalen Carter and a general lack of pass rush as the biggest factor. Whether or not Carter is living up to the extension he signed this past summer (four years, $152 million) is seemingly a hot topic among Eagles fans and media, but a Steelers offensive line that just lost Mason McCormick should still be plenty concerned about his presence. Even in a "down" year, Carter's five sacks are only two off the NFL lead for defensive tackles.

As a defense, the Eagles' 16 sacks on the season rank 28th, and their 109 pressures rank 26th. According to Next Gen Stats, the Eagles are below league average in blitz rate (26% vs 32%), and in generating pressure, whether that's with the blitz (41% vs 43%) or without (30% vs 33%).

Carter's 20 pressures this season are tied for second on the Eagles behind only Moro Ojomo (22), an upcoming defensive tackle who allowed the Eagles the flexibility to trade away Davis. Jalyx Hunt, a rotational pass rusher who hasn't played more than 43% of the defensive snaps in a game since Week 4, is tied with Carter. Nolan Smith (17 pressures, 5 sacks), Jonathan Greenard (11 pressures, 1 sack), and Zack Baun (9 pressures, 2 sacks) round out the Eagles' underperforming pass rush.

Baun, the Eagles' top off-ball linebacker, is out for the game, the team announced Friday.

With the lack of pressure, the Eagles' secondary has predictably struggled. Third-year nickel corner Cooper DeJean has taken a step back, allowing four touchdowns this season after allowing just one during his first two seasons, and he has nearly as many missed tackles this season (13) as he had in his career (19) before this season. Meanwhile, Riq Woolen remains a volatile corner who has looked like a shutdown player some weeks and like a weak link in others, including surrendering four touchdowns against zero interceptions so far this season.

If there is one bright spot in Philadelphia's secondary, it's cornerback Quinyon Mitchell, who is still performing at a high level. He's allowed just a 55.6% catch rate and a QB passer rating of 76.5 when targeted. Philadelphia has typically let Mitchell stick to one side of the field, but in their most recent game, the Eagles finally tasked Mitchell with shadowing an opponent's number one receiver. Against the Giants, Mitchell followed Malik Nabers and shut down New York's most explosive playmaker.

As for what coverages we can expect from Philadelphia, the Eagles are a team that has lived in both nickel (68% of defensive snaps) packages and single-high safety looks (54% of snaps) for multiple seasons now. That has most typically shown up as Cover-3 looks in zone coverages (36.5%) and Cover-1 in man looks (15.3%).

On the ground, the Eagles aren't quite as bad as the numbers might indicate. While the Eagles' raw run defense totals look poor, they're mostly in the middle of the pack in most metrics, and are surrendering a high yardage per game total mostly because they have been in negative game scripts for much of the season. Philadelphia allows the seventh-fewest average yards before contact (0.81); however, they also allow the eighth-highest average yards after contact (3.33). As a unit, they've missed 43 tackles on running plays, per PFF, but only DeJean (8) and safety Andrew Mukuba (6) are particularly heinous in this regard. No other defender has more than three missed tackles against the run. Philadelphia ranks 15th in stuffed run percentage (17%), but are tied with the Vikings and Patriots for the 13th-most runs of 10+ yards allowed (27).

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

What Does Trail Magic Look Like Around the World? These 5 Stories Show Us

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The snacks at the hut made Matt's day, despite the mucky conditions (Photo: Matt Jordan)

On the 2024 Highlander UAE, a multi-day 40-mile hike through the Hajar Mountains, I struggled far more than I had expected. The terrain was brutal, with steep descents over sharp, rocky granite tracks along the 6,345-foot Jebel Jais summit. My heavy pack weighed me down, and I could feel every stone beneath my boots. By day three, my two big toes hurt so much that on one challenging descent, I collapsed and couldn’t take another step.

That’s when a local Emirati man came walking up the trail behind me. He saw that I was struggling and stopped to pull out a roll of tape from his bag. Then, he peeled off my very stinky hiking socks without a second thought, taped up my toes, and bandaged my knees. He even carried my pack for several miles, guiding me down the remaining steep sections.

This stranger had no reason to go out of his way to help me, and I know I could not have finished that day without him. Hikers call this kind of experience “trail magic.” The Appalachian Trail Conservancy defines the term as any instance of finding what you need most when you least expect it; experiencing something rare, extraordinary, or inspiring in nature, or encountering unexpected acts of generosity that restore one’s faith in humanity.

Trail magic is often associated with Appalachian Trail history from the early 1980s. However, after my experience in the UAE, I started wondering what it looks like elsewhere. So, here are five stories from other hikers who have experienced trail magic in one form or another around the world.

Matt Jordan, a hiking and backpacking guide with Intrepid Travel, was walking New Zealand’s 1,881-mile Te Araroa Trail southbound in 2023 when he hit the muddy Longwood Forest in Fiordland. After a miserable day of rain and bog, he pushed on toward Turnbull’s Hut, one of the last backcountry huts on that segment. He’d heard that sometimes, free supplies were stored there for hikers passing through. As he approached, he prayed it hadn’t already all been taken by the time he got there.

“Thankfully it hadn’t,” he says. When he opened the store area, he found a cache of goodies.

“A local man who hikes in supplies, hauls out trash, and maintains the hut about twice a month leaves coolers stocked with beer, soda, and candy bars,” Jordan says. Excitedly, he grabbed a drink, then promptly slipped in the mud and fell on his backside. After days of awful weather conditions, though, that didn’t stop him from laughing out loud.

“For me, the gesture was about more than a cold drink. It was a reminder of the generosity of strangers and the sense of community that makes long-distance hikes unforgettable worldwide.”

Meeta Singh Rao, a hiking guide in India, says some of her best trail magic came from shepherds who spend months in the high mountain pastures of the Indian Himalayas. In October 2022, she was leading a trek to Nawali Bugyal in Uttarakhand when the weather turned. Heavy rain began to fall, and strong winds blew. The mules carrying their gear were also lost, and camp was miles away.

The group came across a shepherd with his flock. He heard where they were headed, and offered them shelter in his channi, a mountain hut made of stone, mud, and plastic sheets. “When rainwater leaked through the roof, he walked out into the downpour to rearrange the plastic so we stayed dry,” says Rao.

When Rao offered to pay, the shepherd gently refused, saying they were his guests. “A couple with so little of their own opened their home to us, giving us a feeling of belonging in the mountains,” she adds.

Kaitlyn Bussell has walked four Caminos. But her second, the 62-mile Camino Inglés in September 2023 through Galicia, was particularly challenging. The rain was constant, few hostels and cafés were open in the fall, and she walked long stretches without seeing another pilgrim.

On the second day, wet and ready for a break, she ducked into the first bar she found for a café con leche and to charge her phone. When she went to pay, the young woman behind the counter told her the coffee was free and handed her some water, prayer cards and a small Camino keepsake.

“It was a small thing, but it came at exactly the right moment,” Bussell recalls. “After days of rain, it lifted my spirits and made me realize that this pilgrimage is as much about the people you meet as it is the miles you walk.”

On Japan’s Shikoku 88 Temples Pilgrimage route, Lauren Roerick and her brother were leaving one of the temples when she spotted a sign offering matcha tea ceremonies for pilgrims. They initially walked away unsure whether they would be intruding but returned and decided to try it anyway.

“A Japanese man welcomed us inside where two women in kimonos were preparing and serving matcha under his instruction,” adds Roerick. “To make us feel welcome, he called a friend and had her on speakerphone, translating the whole ceremony from Japanese into English.” Lauren and her brother learned about the tradition, tried making tea themselves, and ate small Japanese sweets.

“It was proof that trail magic isn’t always a bottle of water or a helping hand,” says Roerick. “Sometimes a stranger opens his door, works around a language barrier, and shares a bit of culture with people passing through.”

On a windy hike to Matterhorn Base Camp, Libera Pagano, tour director with EF Adventures, noticed an older woman struggling. The altitude made her dizzy, her legs were shaking, and her friend had walked far ahead. “I offered to sit with the woman while she rested behind a large rock and we started chatting,” says Pagano.

That’s when Pagano learned it was the woman’s 86th birthday, and the hike was a present she’d given herself. They stayed together, and when the woman felt ready, they walked slowly until they caught up with her companion. When the group met back at basecamp, the woman told Pagano that without her help, she would never have made it to the summit.

“I thought nothing of what I had done,” says Pagano. “But for that woman, it was the difference between giving up and fulfilling her birthday wish.”

I never learned the name of the man who bandaged my toes in the Hajar Mountains, and I probably never will. But I think about him whenever I struggle on a trail or see another hiker limping.

All these stories have one thing in common—human compassion. Sometimes that is stopping to help a stranger in need, sharing what little you have, or leaving a cache of supplies for a hiker you’ll probably never meet. These gestures don’t have to be grand or cost much, but they can make an enormous difference and are an easy thing to pay forward so everyone experiences a little magic outdoors.

To what do you attribute the fact that you can still tour, write and compose at 85 years old?

I really think it’s because of my passion for learning. Also, the thrill of sharing and connecting with the audience that’s listening. UNCUT is not your first solo piano album?

I have others. But it had been a long time since I made a solo piano album, and this one was truly improvised. I just sat down to play whatever came to mind. But yes, I’ve done a solo album before. Was there ever a moment when you didn’t want to be a pianist?

No. Always, always, my dream was to learn how to play the piano. Inspired by my father, but also because of myself. I wanted to be a pianist.

If you didn’t play the piano, what instrument would you play?

I think I would play the double bass or the cello. The cello because it sings so beautifully. To me, it’s the most expressive instrument.

What do you feel most comfortable with: jazz, classical music or Latin jazz?

I swear, listen carefully, I swear. It’s just sitting at the piano without a plan, without premeditation, and starting to play whatever comes to mind in the moment. That’s the thing that gives me the most joy.

What has jazz taught you that classical music or Cuban music hasn’t?

They’re different things. Cuban music is very rich in rhythms. Years ago, it was extremely rich in rhythm, but it was somewhat limited harmonically. The rhythm of Cuban and Afro-Cuban music was rich, but the harmony of the tumbaos [repeating piano patterns] was quite simple. It was beautiful but a bit limited. Jazz, on the other hand, offered a much richer harmonic field and a much broader harmonic landscape. As I started to mature, what I did was mix the two: adding jazz-influenced harmonies to Cuban music and mambo.

That’s something else. They are such, such different worlds. When you’re alone, you’re free from everything. You just have to start playing whatever comes to mind. When you’re in a trio, you’re accompanied by a bassist and a drummer, and you have to think differently. You have to think about how to create a teamwork dynamic, where we’re speaking the same language and giving space to the other musicians. At this point in my life, it comes automatically because I’ve done it so many times. But it’s not the same. It’s the same as playing with a big band. In a big band, the piano is a supporting instrument. You have to add details exactly where they won’t interfere with the band. I learned that from my father and from the orchestras I played in back in Cuba. Every world is different. But sitting there alone, I’m on cloud nine.

So I’m going to include that.

When you’re not inspired, where do you go?

Sometimes the piano and I kind of fight. Because sometimes I say, I’m over-practicing, and I don’t want to play it. And it says to me, “Fine, don’t play me.” And sometimes I’ll go three days like that. But when I sit down again, it makes me pay for it – without mercy. One finger over here, another finger over there.

What do you think when people call you a genius?

I’m not a genius at all. I’m just a person who loves music and was raised with a sense of respect and always learning something new. And if that results in someone thinking that it’s genius, I love it.

In a life full of memorable moments, what’s the one that’s most engraved in your memory?

Let me tell you. We were in Cuba in 1978, and leaving Cuba was practically impossible for a Cuban jazz group. Our dream was to meet the great jazz artists, but to meet them personally… And we were lucky in 1978 to get a contract with Columbia, and our debut in the United States was on June 25, 1978, at Carnegie Hall. It was the first time a Cuban jazz group played at a jazz festival in the United States. And two of the jazz pianists I was obsessed with performed there: Bill Evans and McCoy Tyner, as well as a lady who was an expert in blues, Mary Lou Williams. That was a moment I will never forget. It had been 18 years since I had seen my father because he lived in Sweden, and I couldn’t go to the United States. But my father flew from Sweden to New York, and I knew he was in the audience. And after that concert, we hugged for an hour and cried and kissed. That was the greatest moment of my life. I was 19 years old when my father left. And the emotion of knowing he flew from Sweden, and I was playing but didn’t know where he was sitting, was something incredible. The dream of my life.

I’m not going to lie. I buy a lot of information. I buy a lot of music books, of course. But the truth is, I spend all my money on clothes. I love it. Louis Vuitton, Gucci, Robert Graham. I have a collection of shirts.

Who, alive or dead, would you like to share a bottle of wine with?

With Bill Evans. He often brings me to tears. He was such an emotional, sincere pianist. Wow. I was lucky that day in Carnegie Hall, I met Bill Evans, and when they told me, “This man wants to meet you,” and he reached out his hand, I hugged him crying and told him that I listened to him all the time. He was and still is my favorite pianist.

When British author Ian McEwan, one of my favorite storytellers, thinned out his home library of duplicates and extras to make room for new stock, he took boxes of books into the park next door and gave them away to passersby. To his surprise, the only ones accepting these gifts were women. He came away from this experience with the realization that if females ever give up on reading, this remarkable invention of human technology will surely die with them.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

I’ve often discussed in my books on the topic of boys and reading, as well as in speeches and lectures at conferences, that for those of us with sophisticated literacy skills, we hope the same for the guys within our care. This is because we desire boys, and indeed all youth, to experience the joy and benefits of the type of highly developed literacy abilities that we value and that have in very real ways contributed to our personal enrichment and professional accomplishments. However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

Research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts.

These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.

Moving from Finland, where he has since 2025 been director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA), he said he wants to bring an outsider’s perspective and a focus on how the modern city connects with the world.

“The Stedelijk museum’s strength is in bringing large and Modernist exhibitions to the Netherlands,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “People go to the Rijksmuseum for Rembrandt and to the city [Stedelijk] museum to understand how Amsterdam has developed, to understand Modernism through the lens of art and design.”

The Stedelijk Museum enjoyed success last year with an Anselm Kiefer show—organised with the neighbouring Van Gogh Museum—and an exhibition of work by the Dutch photographer Erwin Olaf, increasing annual ticket revenue compared to 2024 by almost a quarter, to €10.8 million. This success stands out against a backdrop of Dutch museums reporting financial pressure and a fall in visits compared to pre-pandemic levels. The Rijksmuseum’s most recent annual report describes internal discussions that took place about the fact visitor numbers fell 4% in 2025 to 2.3 million, while the Rembrandt House Museum recently warned that rising tourist taxes will damage cultural tourism.

But Gruijthuijsen said he is more interested in variety than visitor records. “I’m not a visitor numbers director,” he says. “It is quality rather than quantity: what we need economically, and the rest is a plus. From one of the first exhibitions to establish Van Gogh’s work in 1905 to experimental shows of the 1960s, the Stedelijk Museum created an international reputation for contemporary art, which Gruijthuijsen wants to make relevant today. “The DNA of the city museum is a modern day and global perspective—trying to find connections,” he says.

Ayumu Imazu Talks ‘musubi’ & The Belief that Music ‘Brings People Together’: Billboard Japan Monthly Interview

He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song & what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

Singer/songwriter Ayumu Imazu spoke to Billboard Japan for its Monthly Feature interview series spotlighting artists and releases on its radar.

Imazu’s latest single “musubi,” released digitally on Oct. 2, has become the biggest hit of his career. “musubi” is a love song portraying the bonds between people through the motif of the “red string of fate.” A demo posted to TikTok in late August, when only a single verse and chorus existed, spread widely and led to an “emergency release.” The track swept the charts across streaming services immediately after it dropped and earned the ​26-year-old artist his first-ever No. 1 on Billboard Japan’s Streaming Songs chart (dated Oct. 14, 2026), marking the biggest breakthrough of his career to date.

After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. There is so much to discover: the last light of day illuminating the mist rising from Iguazú Falls, the silhouettes of trees and rocks, the birds circling above, the sunlight streaming through an opening in the rock at the center, and the subtle waterfall on the left,” says jury member Ilse Koks.

Another American photographer, Matt Pérez, won the Underwater category with a striking photo of a turtle beneath a collection of newt eggs.

“Pérez’s photograph immediately drew us in. As a jury, we wanted to linger over it, exploring the details and wondering what we were seeing,” remarks Guillot. “Newt egg masses decorate the submerged branches like baubles on a Christmas tree, framing the scene and guiding our eyes towards the pond turtle with its piercing yellow eyes.”

Guillot adds that the scene looks “otherworldly,” which is a very apt description.

Chinese photographer Xuzhong Liu’s abstract drone shot of a winding landscape ticks all the right boxes. It has interesting patterns, striking colors, and a very pleasing composition.

Liu captured the image in a wetland area in China and says the scene strongly reminded him of peach blossoms he had previously photographed—hence the title, “Peach Blossoms in the Snow.”

“This photograph has everything that can make a drone shot interesting and fascinating. First and foremost, there are beautiful shapes and subtle colours, but there is also a sense of alienation. What am I actually looking at, and what is the scale of the landscape in the photograph?” asks the chair of the judging panel, Theo Bosboom.

Czech photographer Petr Bambousek’s winning photo depicts a sobering scene of a Malayan sun bear resting inside a garbage incinerator.

“The winning image stood out for its striking depiction of the impact human activity has on local wildlife,” explains jury member Lana Tannir. “The sleeping Malayan sun bear inside a garbage incinerator creates a powerful visual contrast, showing how habitat loss and human waste increasingly force wild animals into close contact with human environments.”

Tannir says Bambousek’s photo is “unexpected and unsettling.”

Hungarian photographer László Tóth won the Black and White category for his striking, high-contrast, abstract photo.

“This photo really stood out to us. At first, we were not quite sure what we were looking at. It sparked some wonderful discussions among the jury and made us look even closer,” says Ilse Koks.

As for what the photo actually depicts, it’s a thin layer of snow on top of a frozen lake, shaped by the wind.

This photo might be familiar to PetaPixel readers. Norwegian photographer Audun Rikardsen recently won an award for a similar underwater portrait of a polar bear peering down through a hole in the ice.

“Just when we thought we had seen about every kind of polar bear image there is, along comes this shot taken from inside a hole in the ice looking up at the bear,” says Steffen Foerster. “Once you realize what is happening, you start thinking about how this shot could possibly have been taken. Then it dawns on you that it seems almost impossible, no matter how.”

This special category, “De Lage Landen” (which translates to “The Low Countries”), celebrates photos captured in the low-lying region of northwestern Europe, including the Netherlands, where Nature Photographer of the Year is based. This year’s winning photo is by Hungarian photographer Enikő Kálovics.

“The winning image stood out for its ability to transform a familiar landscape into something dreamlike and unexpected,” says Lana Tannir. “The combination of the tiny lacewings, the autumn forest, and the purplish night sky creates a delicate, atmospheric interpretation of the nature of ‘De Lage Landen.'”

American photographer Parham Pourahmad, who was also recently celebrated in the 2027 North American Nature Photography Association (NANPA) Showcase, won this year’s Youth category with a lovely monochromatic photo of a coyote beneath a large tree.

“In ‘Tree of Life’, Pourahmad captured a striking scene that could comfortably hold its own in the adult categories,” says Guillot. “Black and white was an excellent choice, defining the coyote’s silhouette and revealing the majestic oak’s intricate network of branches. Even within this wider scene, the coyote’s downward gaze is clear. Pourahmad explained that it was searching for rodent prey, a small detail that brings the photograph to life.”

Pourahmad has also been named Youth Photographer of the Year.

The Fred Hazelhoff Award celebrates a portfolio of nature photos, rather than a single image. This year’s portfolio winner is German photographer Simone Baumeister.

“In the portfolio category, the bar is set considerably higher for participating photographers than in the individual categories. Whilst a single fantastic photograph is sufficient there, in the portfolio category you need a whole series of outstanding images that not only complement one another but also fit together well in terms of style and theme. Simone Baumeister has succeeded brilliantly in this with her portfolio on eagle owls in the city,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom.

Baumeister has been fascinated by owls for as long as she has been interested in nature photography. The eagle owl’s story is a positive one, in contrast to the plight of many species across the globe. The species, once on the brink of extinction in Germany, has made an incredible recovery. Its resurgence is thanks largely to major conservation efforts and extensive reintroduction programs.

“It was perched in twilight on a church, right below the street lamps, moving cars, and a string of people passing by, with music blaring from a pub right on the church square. Not a soul took notice of the impressive bird with a wingspan of 1.7 meters flying up the steeple, sitting enthroned right within everybody’s view,” the photographer says of the first time she saw an eagle owl in a city.

Ever since then, she has been consistently excited by eagle owls and keen to photograph them in many different places. Observing and photographing them in various cities inevitably morphed into a dedicated project.

However, in addition to these new Capcom-made Robot Masters, Dual Override is slated to bring back a long-dormant tradition of the Mega Man series: fan-designed Robot Masters. Not long after the game’s initial reveal, Capcom launched a character design contest in which fans were encouraged to design their own original Robot Master and submit it for a chance to be included in the game.

Capcom showed off a new Mega Man: Dual Override demo at Tokyo Game Show this year, proving the veteran franchise has still got it.

Many months later, Capcom has officially announced the winner of this contest and revealed the fanmade Robot Master who will be joining the rest of the new Wily Numbers. Strap on your scouring pads and turn your spray nozzles to “ON,” because our latest menace is Cleanser Man.

As director Shinya Ikuta and producer Hiroyuki Minamitani announced in a video revealed during its TwitchCon 2026 panel and posted to the official Mega Man YouTube channel, Cleanser Man was selected out of around 10,000 entrants into the contest from around the world, which then had to be meticulously narrowed down to 20 proper participants, followed by just 6 finalists. These 6 finalists included Sweeper Woman, Recycle Man, Juggle Man, Valve Man, Cactus Man, and of course, Cleanser Man.

“In the end, we chose Cleanser Man because of the unique way the concept was brought together and the originality of the color design,” Ikuta explained.

These 6 finalists included Sweeper Woman, Recycle Man, Juggle Man, Valve Man, Cactus Man, and of course, Cleanser Man.

According to an official character summary sent by Capcom to Game Informer, Cleanser Man is a cleaning robot with an obsessive love of all things sparkling and pristine who will go to absurd lengths to scrub out any perceived imperfections, even if he ends up breaking things in the process. His stage will be set in a trash processing plant that’s flooding the world with garbage so people better appreciate his dedication to cleanliness.

The Tokyo Game Show 2026 Presentation revealed new details regarding both the second protagonist and the game's core mechanics.

Cleanser Man comes to us from a Brazilian artist who goes by “miya” on their X, formerly Twitter account. Upon the reveal that their design was chosen, they posted a screencap of Cleanser Man’s official render side-by-side with their concept art, adding the caption, “The attention to detail almost made me cry. It truly means a lot.”

Not only was the character-design contest a fun way to get people hyped up for Mega Man: Dual Override, not to mention showcase some really cool character-design work, it’s also an important revival of a long-forgotten Mega Man tradition.

Following the release of the original Mega Man game and prior to the release of Mega Man 2, Capcom held a similar contest for its Japanese players to decide the designs of that game’s eight Robot Master. This contest format would be repeated for every subsequent mainline Mega Man game up to and including Mega Man 8, resulting in many of the most notoriously-difficult Robot Masters, before being discontinued from Mega Man & Bass through Mega Man 11. While there was still some fan design encouragement, it was mostly relegated to spin-off series like Battle Network and Star Force.

While it’s only for a single Robot Master rather than a full set of eight, Cleanser Man is still the first fanmade Robot Master to make it into a mainline Mega Man title since 1996, with the added accolade that he’s the first Robot Master to come from a designer in South America. Starbucks has been very busy lately. They launched their holiday menu on Nov. 5, quickly followed by a fresh lineup of viral Bearista cups.

That's obviously very exciting, but it's not the main draw for seasoned coffee lovers and cup collectors. We're all thrilled about the Peppermint Mocha, but Red Cup Day reigns supreme. This is arguably the biggest day on the coffee lovers' calendar. Starbucks hands out a free, limited-edition red reusable cup with every qualifying handcrafted holiday beverage order on this special day. And you guessed it: fans and collectors go wild.

Starbucks has officially confirmed the date for Red Cup Day 2026: You can score a free reusable holiday cup on Nov. 17.

The 2026 reusable red cup is made with 95% recycled materials and features an illustration of a coffee tree branch with coffee cherries tied with a red bow. The cup is encircled by a green and white grosgrain ribbon motif that also features on paper holiday cups, Christmas Blend coffees, and holiday decor in coffeehouses this season.

Getting your hands on the free collectible cup is pretty straightforward, but we should note that supplies run out notoriously fast.

To get your free cup, buy any handcrafted holiday or seasonal beverage (hot, iced, or blended) in any size.
