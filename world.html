Matteo Thun designs Torre Velasca interiors using "building itself as the starting point"

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Alberto Strada (unless stated otherwise)

Italian architect Matteo Thun looked to the original materials and colours of Milan’s iconic Torre Velasca when creating its new interiors, which feature pink hues inspired by the tower’s dusky facade.

Thun has designed 72 flats in the upper floors of the tower, ranging from suites to duplex penthouses. He approached the interior design of the skyscraper, built in the 1950s by the architecture studio BBPR, with a focus on reinterpretation.

“We allowed the masters and the building itself to speak, imagining a way of living within it that would carry the building a little further into the future, adding a new chapter to its history,” Thun told Dezeen.

“We did not seek contrast: our design was informed directly by the materials and colours of Torre Velasca, and we chose not to intervene where we felt the architecture had already achieved a sense of completeness.”

His studio preserved original details, including doors, window frames, loggias and tiles in the bathrooms and kitchens. These were then juxtaposed with modern furniture and evergreen design classics.

“The interiors bring together new furniture designed by the studio and design references spanning the 1950s to the 1980s, including iconic pieces developed in new colourways in collaboration with historic Milanese companies,” Thun said.

“With a deep appreciation for craftsmanship and a strong attention to detail, these designs share an ability to evolve without losing their identity.”

The colour palette, too, was inspired by the original hues of the Torre Velasca.

In the living rooms, walls are covered in custom-made velvet wall coverings by textile company Dedar in a pale pink colour that resembles the pink plaster on Torre Velasca’s facade.

“The building itself was our starting point,” Thun said.

“The pink plaster, developed and patented specifically for the tower, together with the blue and green floor tiles found both inside and in the loggias, formed the basis of our colour palette,” he continued.

“We developed different gradations of these tones, opting for darker shades of green and blue to define the entrance areas and bedrooms. “

As well as the apartments, Thun also designed the interior of the reception, lobby and the corridors on the ground floor.

And while this was an interior project, Thun says it ended up becoming “a lesson in architecture” for his studio.

“Working with a building as distinctive as Torre Velasca challenged us to think and work first and foremost as architects,” Thun said.

“The challenge was not to reinvent the building, but to find ways to make its original character feel relevant today, while remaining true to BBPR’s vision,” he continued.

“In this sense, the project became a process of discovery, with the building itself serving as our greatest teacher. It was, in many ways, a lesson in architecture for us.”

Thun has previously designed the interior of the boutique hotel The Julius Prague and “hotel-like” rooms for a German hospital.

36 Years Later, Hulk's Greatest Design Still Has No Equal

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Almost 40 years after its Marvel debut, the greatest Hulk design of all time has yet to be bested, despite major competition both before and after. Here's why the design in question deserves its crown, and which other contenders come close.

Inspired by horror icons Frankenstein and Mr. Hyde, the Hulk was created by Stan Lee and Jack Kirby. Despite being one of the most iconic characters of all time, Hulk was originally very different - a grey-skinned jerk who spoke in sophisticated sentences and only came out at night.

Over the years, Hulk became the monosyllabic, emerald-skinned monster that Marvel fans know, though it took several of the most legendary creators in comic history to perfect Bruce Banner's other half.

The best Hulk design of all time is a hotly contested topic among fans of the Jade Giant, but we're handing the gold medal to Dale Keown, who drew The Incredible Hulk from 1990-1992 alongside acclaimed writer Peter David, with the two re-teaming for the non-canon Incredible Hulk: The End in 2002, which sees the character contend with a nuclear post-apocalypse.

Keown's Hulk is a barrel-chested behemoth with fists bigger than his head. His brow isn't just pronounced, it combines with a smaller, flatter nose and gaping mouth to create a hyper-expressive, highly distinct silhouette. The green skin is bright and luminous with a tonsure-like head of hair that looks like it didn't grow at the same rate as the rest of Bruce Banner's body.

So, what makes Keown our pick for the Hulk GOAT? It all comes down to one thing: Hulk's humanity. The best Hulk stories of all time understand that whatever Bruce Banner might think, the Hulk isn't a simple, singular expression of his rage. Hulk and Bruce are equally complex aspects of the same person - unique but deeply connected - and Keown communicates that better than any other artist.

There are a LOT of artists who have nailed Hulk's monstrous intensity and world-shattering rage. However, Keown's art is uniquely capable of showing Hulk laughing, glowering, grinning, wincing and roaring without losing the core of the character. Many of Hulk's earliest artists nail the sense of an impossible monster, but not one with a legitimate inner life.

The ability to depict different aspects of Hulk's personality was vital working alongside Peter David, who further splintered Bruce's psyche into the grey Joe Fixit, genius Professor Hulk and the brutal Maestro - the villain Bruce may one day become. These concepts have become the bedrock of Hulk's best modern stories - they were all good ideas, but it was Keown who ensured they were all 'Hulk.'

While we're holding Dale Keown up as creating the best Hulk design of all time, it's a close thing, given the wealth of talent who have worked on the character. And while you may not agree with our call, rest assured that there are many, many Hulk artists we still consider greats.

Hulk co-creator Jack Kirby drew Hulk with unbelievable menace, giving the character his heavy brow and tombstone teeth. Under Kirby, Hulk was a pure creature of the id - teeth bared and fists balled. Likewise, Herb Trimpe's Hulk is a howling monstrosity, reducing concrete to powder under his apish mitts and possessing a hunched, embattled physicality.

John Romita Sr.'s Hulk nails the concept of a monster that is still recognizably Bruce Banner, while Sal Buscema turned the character into a canvas for raw emotion. Despite a famously short run, it's worth noting that John Byrne accomplished the same sense of humanity as Keown, conveying a Hulk who is more than just rage.

In modern comics, Hulk has attracted some of the best artistic talent in comics. Adam Kubert's Hulk is an explosive mass of muscle that can't be contained by page or panel, Lee Weeks returns him to his Frankenstein-ian roots as a tragic homunculus, and John Romita Jr. captured a Hulk destroyed by grief and wrath, his humanity hanging on by a thread.

Bryan Hitch reinvented Hulk for the 2000s-era Ultimate Universe, turning him into a near-parodic wrecking ball of hate, lust and frustration. Daniel Acuña's work on Avengers: Twilight realized Bruce Banner's worst fears - the Hulk as an apocalyptic atrocity.

And while allegations that Joe Bennett hid antisemitic imagery in the background of Immortal Hulk can't be forgotten, he undeniably collaborated on the single greatest Hulk story of all time with writer Al Ewing, juxtaposing intense body horror and flying spittle with quiet, existential despair and righteous anger, bringing back Hulk's simian brow and bared teeth without abandoning the truth of a frightened child lashing out at an often deserving world.

While discussing Hulk's best comic designs, it would be silly to ignore his live-action appearances. Lou Ferrigno's snarling Hulk changed the franchise forever, acting as a near-unparalleled cultural touchstone. Ang Lee's Hulk embraced the imagery of a confused, superpowered toddler - dull-eyed and slathered in dirt - surpassing the pure rage of 2008's Incredible Hulk, at least in design.

However, the obvious winner is the MCU's Hulk, and for the same reasons as Dale Keown's art. Designed around the likeness of Bruce Banner actor Mark Ruffalo (who performs the Hulk's scenes using motion-capture technology), the most modern depiction of Hulk rages, but he also smirks, mourns, comprehends and fears.

This approach doesn't just make Hulk more interesting, it lets fans in on the vital lie that great Hulk stories deconstruct - that the Green Goliath is just a rage monster who lives inside Bruce Banner, rather than a vital aspect of his soul that's just as complicated and valid as the timid scientist.

Hulk's strength and size are essential to the character, but ultimately he also needs to be capable of emotional depth on par with his human side. The greatest piece of Hulk art of all time may or may not be by Dale Keown, but his design for the character encompassed everything the Hulk needs to be to tell the range of stories the character is capable of supporting.

Prime Video Has The #1 And #2 Fantasy Shows On TV Right Now

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It's a good time to be a fantasy fan with a streaming subscription. On Disney+, Percy Jackson and the Olympians is about to launch into its third season after banishing the memory of its big screen predecessor. Over at Netflix, the red brand is still working its way through The Witcher. Prime Video boasts the big-budget adaptation of Middle-earth's Second Age that is The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power. And for those who prefer a little more grit in their sandals, HBO's Westeros spinoffs are rapidly proving that Game of Thrones' poorly-received final season was just an unfortunate blip.

There is, then, a lot of fantasy TV around these days, with different flavors of the genre on offer to suit everyone's personal taste. And with all the major streamers getting in on the act, it would be almost ridiculous for the best two fantasy shows on TV right now to hail from the same platform. As it happens, the two best fantasy shows on TV right now not only hail from the same platform, they hail from the same franchise.

Even before forging its way onto streaming, the Critical Role franchise enjoyed significant success, popularizing the now-undisputed notion that viewers aplenty will watch a tabletop Dungeons and Dragons campaign broken up into online episodes. As well as spearheading a whole new format of fantasy storytelling, Critical Role became a brand unto itself by successfully introducing new campaigns. That ability to swap characters and storylines without losing momentum proved the group itself, and their endless creativity, was the real attraction.

From an early stage, anyone following Critical Role's journey could see the immense potential for a TV adaptation, and once Amazon joined the party as a partner, it became highly unlikely that The Legend of Vox Machina would roll streaming's equivalent of a natural one. But even the most passionate supporters of Critical Role's early endeavors couldn't have predicted just how deftly the franchise would transition to TV.

With its razor-sharp humor, handcrafted world-building, and tried-and-tested characters, The Legend of Vox Machina delivered that rarest of things: an adaptation to please existing fans while also enticing those who wouldn't recognize Matt Mercer if he was standing behind them in a convention toilet queue. Vox Machina's animated adventures are the pinnacle of fantasy television, beating every rival series by a comfortable distance.

Indeed, the only fantasy series coming close to The Legend of Vox Machina is its own Prime Video sibling series, The Mighty Nein, which offers an ingeniously magical twist on the same formula. Between them, the two shows are Dungeons and Dragons' equivalent of Breaking Bad and Better Call Saul in the sense that it's almost impossible to decide which is better. We only know they're both brilliant.

That dual success grants Prime Video the #1 and #2 spots on any list of the best fantasy shows around right now.

The prevalence of successful fantasy shows on TV can only mean one thing: we're getting more of them. From Harry Potter to Artemis Fowl, Brandon Sanderson to Stephen King, Fourth Wing to Ravenloft, streaming stands on the precipice of a massive fantasy explosion.

And yet none of them are obvious candidates to knock The Legend of Vox Machina and The Mighty Nein off the top spots.

The likes of Artemis Fowl and The Dark Tower are notoriously tricky to adapt, while expectations from book fans could hamper shows based on Fourth Wing, Stormlight Archive, and other highly popular works. Ravenloft's Dungeons and Dragons roots immediately set lofty expectations thanks to Amazon's Critical Role shows, and HBO's Harry Potter faces a near-impossible challenge to win over its many critics.

That's not to say that one of streaming's upcoming fantasy projects can't blossom into brilliance and overtake both The Legend of Vox Machina and The Mighty Nein. But, on paper at least, there isn't a project in the pipeline that'll have Scanlan waking up at night nervously grabbing his lute.

Add This Spicy Ingredient To Ground Beef For A Burger That's 10x Juicier

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If you're looking for a burger patty that not only has a bit of kick, but also plenty of juice, you need 'nduja, a legendary Italian sausage. It is not only extremely flavorful, but also quite high in fat and moisture, making it a perfect addition no matter how you like to cook your burgers.

'Nduja is technically a type of salami, except it's far more decadent and incorporates a hefty dose of Calabrian chili peppers for next-level taste. Its high fat-to-lean meat ratio makes it perfect for introducing more moisture, even in small amounts. Even if you only have exceptionally lean meat on hand, one part 'nduja to four parts beef keeps your patties tender and juicy, and even helps develop a better crust.

The idea is similar to many meatball recipes from around the world that incorporate both pork and beef. Whereas beef provides body and a hearty texture, pork can provide extra fat, flavor, and moisture, working together to cook up something truly delicious. The peppers also create a slightly savory and spicy flavor profile that, while immediately noticeable, isn't so overwhelming that you might need to change up your preferred toppings. Each bite will drip with flavorful juices that mix with your condiments for the most moisture-rich burger possible.

Incorporating anything into a burger is a bit of a tightrope walk. You want to disperse your ingredients evenly but not work the muscle fibers so much that your patties end up dense or tough. 'Nduja's texture makes this quite easy, but you'll want to be careful about what type of meat you add it to.

Since it has a spreadable, pâté-like texture, working 'nduja directly into the meat is quite easy. Whether you mix it in thoroughly or squeeze pinches into already formed patties for pockets of flavor, you get plenty of fatty juices. Still, if you're worried about overworking your patties or want to add the salty meat at the very last minute, 'nduja is typically safe to eat right out of the package. Treat it like cheese and add a dollop after flipping your patties, letting it melt over the whole thing for a juicy entree.

If you want a custom burger blend, you have to be mindful of the proper fat-to-meat ratio. 'Nduja's exceptional fat content means it's best to add it to already lean blends so you don't end up with anything too greasy. The leaner your beef, the more 'nduja you can incorporate, so it's a balance between how much of its flavor you want included. Something that's at least 85% lean is ideal, letting you incorporate enough to taste without adding so much that you can't appreciate the beef's natural flavor.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

That search concluded late Tuesday night when searchers found the second hiker dead. Officials with the B.C. Conservation Officer Service (BCCOS) believe both were attacked by a grizzly bear, and efforts are ongoing to locate and trap any bears in the area for assessment.

Authorities and family members have since identified the pair as 48-year-old Jennifer Balcarras and her daughter, 20-year-old Selah Reed.

“It is not yet clear whether more than one bear was involved,” BCCOS Investigator Jeff Tyre told Backpacker. “Any captured bear will be assessed. It’s unclear how long the current search will continue. A spokesperson for BCCOS said investigators were unavailable for comment due to heightened field activity.

“Our thoughts are with the family of the victims during this tragic time,” the agency said. “We are in contact with the family to offer any support we can as we continue to investigate this incident.”

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required.”

During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using.

This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today.

In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity.

Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L).

Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure.

As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack.

As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work.

Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources.

This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment.

Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote.

The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time.

Syfy Cancelled Its Ambitious Sci-Fi Series — Now It’s Prime Video’s Greatest Space Opera

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After just three seasons, Syfy decided to pull the plug on an ambitious sci-fi series. Adapted from a bestselling book series, the show in question consistently landed in critics' books and even acquired a cult following. Yet, its run was cut short after three seasons. Fortunately, this is when Prime Video stepped in and renewed the series before it lasted for three more seasons.

Adapted from James S.A. Corey's acclaimed book series, The Expanse was first developed by Mark Fergus and Hawk Ostby for the Syfy Network. However, in 2018, Syfy prematurely ended the show's run in 2018. Sci-fi fans were devastated as The Expanse was easily one of the greatest additions to the genre in the 21st century.

Even some of the best sci-fi series, no matter how much potential they initially show, do not return after being canceled. Perhaps, in a parallel world, The Expanse, too, saw the same fate. Fate, however, had different plans for the series. After extensive campaigns from viewers, Amazon picked up the show and continued it for three more seasons, which cemented its "masterpiece" status.

Even in its first three seasons, The Expanse was undeniably an incredible sci-fi series. This explains why many relentlessly campaigned to bring it back. However, it was still short of becoming the masterpiece it eventually turned into. Everything from its portrayal of Newtonian physics to geopolitical conflicts between Earth, Mars, and the Belt were compelling in the first three seasons.

However, the show initially struggled with pacing issues. Especially in season 1, The Expanse felt inconsistent when it balanced Joe Miller's detective noir arc on Ceres with overarching political tension surrounding the Rocinante crew's escape. Syfy's network budget also limited the visual scope of the series, although its VFX team still did an incredible job.

Even main characters in The Expanse, like Holden, Amos, Naomi, and Alex, were yet to break free from their early archetypes in the show's initial chapters. The Expanse season 3 also had to race through Book 2 (Caliban's War) and significantly compress Book 3 (Abaddon's Gate) before ending its run. All in all, the sci-fi series was from its true potential after three installments.

The Expanse started putting its brilliance on full display after it returned for three more seasons on Prime Video. If it wasn't for its revival, it seems possible that The Expanse would have become another forgotten sci-fi series that was canceled a little too soon.

Landing on Prime Video after its revival not only allowed The Expanse to slightly extend its episode runtimes but also become more realistic. With no broadcast censors holding the show back from exploring the grit and violence of its world, The Expanse gradually became a more accurate and relatable representation of how space colonization works.

Going into season 5, The Expanse also finally got to split the Rocinante crew across different locations. This, in turn, helped the series explore and test the individual strengths and journey of its main roster. Even story beats surrounding the Ring Gate and the Marco Inaros and the Free Navy appeared much later, allowing the series to dive deeper into its portrayal of revolutionary stress and economic extinction.

All in all, if Prime Video had not given The Expanse a second chance, its legacy would have been far less long-lasting than it is today. While it is unfortunate that even Prime Video canceled the show before it could cover the complete length of its source material, it is hard not to be grateful that Syfy's cancellation did not mark the end of its run.

The Expanse is a sci-fi series set in the distant future where humanity has spread out across the solar system, but the alliances between the three most potent governing bodies have reached a state of cold war. A mixed-up crew finds themselves at the center of a dangerous intergalactic conspiracy that threatens to bring war to the colonized worlds.

Winter Container Inspiration From New Jersey

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Hi GPOD! Today we’re returning to New Jersey to see more from Ellen Goldfarb and Tracy Buttrick. After sharing their beautiful fall containers with us, Ellen is back with some winter inspiration using colorful branches, evergreens, berries, pinecones, and more.

This beautiful antique lead planter in Ridgewood, New Jersey, always calls for a generous amount of greenery and nice height. We try to change what we do from year to year. This time we used curly willow, artificial white berry picks, magnolia leaves, red winterberry (Ilex verticillata, Zones 3–9), and frosted pinecones. Silver bell eucalyptus, silver brunia stems, noble fir (Abies procera, Zones 5–6), and incense cedar (Calocedrus decurrens, Zones 5–8) fill out the arrangement.

This is the same antique planter and house as in the previous photo, but from another winter. That year we used a combination of yellow twig dogwood (Cornus sericea ‘Flaviramea’, Zones 3–7) and red twig dogwood (Cornus sericea cvs., Zones 2–7) for lots of winter color. We also used artificial berry picks in pink and white and natural brown pine cones.

We like to use real berries, but sometimes the birds eat them before the season ends. Artificial berries give the client guaranteed color for longer. For greenery, we used desert juniper (Juniperus osteosperma, Zones 3–9), balsam fir (Abies balsamea, Zones 3–6), and incense cedar.

This slim planter sits on a small sliver of a step at an entry in Ho-Ho-Kus, New Jersey. It is simple but one of my favorites, with stunning red twig dogwood stems. We buy these from a farm that also grows and sells the more common darker-red stems.

Beneath the red is a froth of desert juniper. Dried lotus pods (Nelumbo cvs., Zones 4–10) and clusters of silver brunia punctuate the greenery. The finishing touch is a frilly skirt of bright chartreuse incense cedar.

This one sounds like an outfit, doesn’t it?

One of a pair of pots on the front porch of a Mission-style house in Teaneck, New Jersey, these had plenty of space for tall branches. We liked the combination of golden curly willow (Salix matsudana ‘Tortuosa’, Zones 4–8) and redtwig dogwood against the creamy tan stucco.

We tucked in shiny magnolia leaves with taller silver bell eucalyptus, then added natural brown pinecones and small clumps of silver brunia. A generous, draping swath of incense cedar finishes the bottom of the arrangement.

This is a window box in Englewood, New Jersey. A classic house calls for a classic planting. We used red winterberries, magnolia leaves, frosted pinecones, and silver brunia clusters. Some noble fir and our favorite evergreen, incense cedar, finish the arrangement.

This is a close-up detail of a planter in Franklin Lakes, New Jersey. Dark red twig dogwood stems are mixed with curly willow and branches of multiflora rose hips. We love using the rose hips, but they are sometimes hard to find.

This pot also had artificial white berry picks and Eastern white pine (Pinus strobus, Zones 3–8). Silver brunia on longer stems, noble fir, and incense cedar complete the arrangement.

Thank you for sharing more of your beautiful container designs with us, Ellen and Tracy! I love seeing how many different looks can come from a relatively small palette of winter materials. Changing the color and shape of the branches, berries, cones, and evergreens gives each container its own personality.

‘Painful’ – Alexis Mac Allister doesn’t hold back about ‘unpleasant situation’ at Liverpool

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Alexis Mac Allister has spoken out about his ‘painful, unpleasant situation’ at Liverpool in terms of not being offered a new contract.

The Argentine caused a stir in September when he said that he was ‘very sad‘ not to have been offered a new deal by the club, having seen fellow 2023 signings Ryan Gravenberch and Dominik Szoboszlai both pen contract extensions earlier this year.

The Telegraph’s Dominic King reported last month that Anfield chiefs aren’t in ‘any rush’ to begin talks over a new deal for the Reds’ number 10 (who’s contracted to June 2028), despite his resurgence in form since the start of this season.

Appearing on the Los Edul show in his homeland, Mac Allister spoke candidly about Liverpool’s decision not to offer him new terms over the past few months.

The 27-year-old said: “We’re in a situation where I have a year and a half left on my contract. Last season I was told that the club’s idea was not to renew it, at least for now, and obviously that was a painful, unpleasant situation.”

When asked if the situation was making him despondent, he replied: “Not that much, because at the end of the day, I still had – and still have – time left at this club, which is what I want most – to stay here.

“The reality is that the manager has now changed, the sporting director has changed, and new investors are coming into the club, so I think I’m now in a position where anything can happen. We’ll see what happens in the future. I’ve always said that I’m very happy here, that I really enjoy it, and we’ll see.”

Mac Allister added: “Look at Szoboszlai and Gravenberch. They’re two players who were in the same contractual situation as me, and when I was told that, they renewed their contracts.

“It was more a position taken by the club, and generally that’s how things are done. At the end of the day, as a club you don’t want to lose a lot of money, and you don’t want players leaving on free transfers either.”
