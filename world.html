Performance artist Derek DelGaudio and director Frank Oz are reteaming for “Unknown Caller,” a new stage production that debuts next spring at New York City’s Public Theater. The two had collaborated on DelGaudio’s unconventional one-man show “In & Of Itself,” also directed by Oz, which explored themes of identity and illusion.

Veiled in secrecy, “Unknown Caller” is described as a “new encounter with mystery that asks what we can believe in a counterfeit world” and promises to “push the boundaries of storytelling and live performance.” It will world premiere at the Public’s LuEster Hall on March 30. Billam-Smith 'devastated' but grateful after loss

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Disappointment, but some important perspective.

Those were the emotions felt by Chris Billam-Smith when reflecting on the third defeat of his professional career against Cheavon Clarke earlier this month.

The former cruiserweight world champion was dropped to the canvas and stopped for the first time in the pro ranks as Clarke repeated the outcome from when the pair met in their amateur days.

Although the loss is a stumbling block to Billam-Smith's ambitions, he felt it was important to put things into perspective.

On Tuesday, it was announced Irish boxer Lee Reeves had died aged 31, three months after he suffered serious injuries in a bout in Dublin.

Billam-Smith is also close friends with former gym-mate Raven Chapman, who is recovering after suffering a brain bleed over the summer in a sparring session.

"We had news recently of fighters ending up unwell and losing their lives," an emotional Billam-Smith told BBC Sport.

"I get to come home to my kids and other people haven't had that luxury so in the grand scheme of things, coming home healthy is most important.

"There's plenty of perspective to be taken and I've had a lot of that around me, so when I look at life as a whole, it's good.

"I think the ref got it (the stoppage) wrong in the moment but I'm not angry at him for that because his job is fighter safety in a brutal sport. Boxing wise I'm devastated and you never like to lose let alone get stopped."

Billam-Smith stopped as Clarke repeats amateur win

Billam-Smith won the WBO cruiserweight title in 2023 in front of 15,000 supporters at his beloved AFC Bournemouth's Vitality Stadium.

The following summer, he defended his title at Selhurst Park as he outclassed Richard Riakporhe at the home of Crystal Palace.

After losing a WBA and WBO unification fight against Gilberto Ramirez in late 2024, he then beat Brandon Glanton at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium before signing a deal with Zuffa Boxing and stopping Ryan Rozicki back in his hometown.

Two weekends ago at the Copper Box Arena, Billam-Smith was slow to start against Clarke before the Londoner caught the Bournemouth man with a heavy right hand that sent him to the floor in the seventh round.

The 36-year-old was then sent to the floor for a second time before the referee stepped in and called a halt to the contest.

Although he now has three defeats to his name alongside 22 wins, Billam-Smith believes he still has more to give in the sport.

"The fight against Chev isn't the ideal way (to end his career) and I wouldn't want to go out like that," he added.

"It (retirement) has probably crossed my mind, but I haven't spoken to the team about anything like that and there haven't been any suggestions.

"We're still quite soon after the fight and I think it's dangerous to make a decision either way when it's still so raw.

"I haven't properly thought about it and at the end of the day it will be my decision when I retire but people close to me, their opinions matter too."

Tulum Is Losing Airlines Fast: Why The Hyped New Airport Is Failing

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In late 2023, we saw the opening of the Felipe Carrillo Puerto International Airport (TQO), in Tulum, Mexico. For those not familiar, Tulum is located on the eastern coast of the Yucatan Peninsula, and for years, it was a growing tourist destination.

While it could previously be accessed via Cancun International Airport (CUN), the hope was that by opening a new international airport, visitor numbers would expand massively. Unfortunately what has actually happened is exactly the opposite, as airline after airline has pulled out. What exactly is going on here? I think it makes for an interesting story, and thanks to TravelinWilly for flagging this.

In 2023, we saw airline after airline announce flights to Tulum. Among US carriers, we saw American, Delta, JetBlue, and United, all launch flights to Tulum, while Spirit announced flights, but never launched them.

That was only a small part of the overall international service to the airport, as we also saw flights to Tulum on Air Canada, Air Transat, Avianca, Copa, Discover, and WestJet, along with flights on domestic carriers like Aeromexico, Mexicana de Aviacion, Viva, and Volaris.

Less than three years after the airport opened, where do things stand? When it comes to international year-round service, we’ve now gone from service to 12 airports, to service to four airports, which is an absolutely massive reduction:

Just to put this into numbers, in the first half of 2026, the airport saw a 33.2% reduction in passengers. June 2026 was the worst month to date, with a 44.8% drop, as there were just 47,600 passengers, compared to 86,200 passengers the year prior.

It goes without saying that for a newly built airport, this is rather catastrophic, and not what you hope to see.

In some ways, it feels like the whole concept behind Tulum Airport was sort of unnecessary. That’s also easily explained by the fact that the airport was well over a decade in the making, so while I can see how it might’ve seemed like a good idea at some point, tourism trends do evolve over time.

A few years ago Tulum Airport opened to some fanfare, as a good number of airlines rushed to add service there. Unfortunately the excitement didn’t last long, as the airport has gone from a dozen year-round international routes, to just four.

This reduction in demand particularly evident from the United States, as soon there will just be nonstop service on American and Delta, and even that will be with reduced frequencies and routes.

Tulum Airport is facing endless challenges, not the least of which is a decline in visitors to Mexico from the United States, combined with increasing competition for those fewer visitors. Mexico just isn’t a value destination in the way that it used to be in places like Cancun, Tulum, Los Cabos, etc., and that’s understandably impacting demand.

After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. Cozy Bed to Selden Pass — John Muir Trail Day 15

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After last night’s fabulous dinner, I expected to sleep like a baby.  And I did.  For a while.

Then a very disturbing event occurred, relating to my left wrist injury.  I woke up in the middle of the night and my thumb, index finger, third finger and fourth finger were completely and totally numb on my left hand, and the pinky was partially numb.

I hoped (fervently!) that this only meant I had slept on my injured wrist in a bad way.  The alternative would seem to be nerve damage of some kind.  Of the kind that says, “Get off the trail right away and see a doctor!!”

Gradually I managed to get feeling back.  As it slowly returned, I was cautiously relieved.  But I recognized that if the extreme numbness came back, then it was not a random misfortune, but something I really needed to worry about.  Aside from possible long-term damage, hiking with no feeling in my left hand was out of the question.

The other change overnight was temperature.  When I turned in, it was hot, even stuffy in my little cabin home, so I went to sleep with just my shirt on, with bare feet and bare legs.  There was no top sheet on the bed, just a sheet on the mattress and a blanket.  So, I slept between the sheet and the blanket.

Over the course of the night, it started to get ever chillier.  As the night went on, I added my little knit cap; then I added socks.  As it cooled further, I got out my sleeping bag liner and hopped in.  Finally, I got my puffy jacket and wrapped it around my legs and eventually that seemed to do the trick.  I was extremely resistant to pulling out my sleeping bag, I did not want to do that if I could help it.  Somehow that would have made the overnight feel less like a true break from the trail, I guess.

When I got up in the morning, my fingers all still had sensation, they were moving.  My wrist was still injured, but my fear of nerve damage abated.

I went up to breakfast and oh what a spread!  This is a place that serves hikers. They had scrapple, bacon, pancakes, eggs, fruit, yogurt, and coffee, of course.  The same crew from the night before all sat around and we stuffed our faces, understandably, as hungry hikers are prone to do.

Smiles showed me a picture of the inside of her cabin from the night before and it was hilarious!

With a great fear of SPIDERS, Smiles had set up and slept in her tent INSIDE her MTR cabin!

This picture gives a good sense of how she obtained her trail name!

So that’s what retired New York oncologists look like!

Lauralyn was there too, and she said, ”You know, you said you were going to recite that poem, how about it?”

Lauralyn encouraging me to recite The Raven

I warned them all it would take more than ten minutes and they said, “That’s okay, go ahead.”

As everyone was getting served, and kitchen staff were moving through the room, I recited The Raven.  They said they really enjoyed it, and I think they did.  With the distractions in the room, it was probably not my best performance, but I did all right.

It was time to get going, so I got my resupply bucket that was stashed in the kitchen under lock and key for bear protection.  Shlepping it to my cabin it felt very heavy.  Then I opened it and there was so much stuff I didn’t need.  I began sorting – everything I wanted went on the bed for packing, everything I did not went back in the bucket.

Smiles dropped by and told me, “I extended, I’m gonna stay here another day.”

“Okay, fine,” I replied, adding, “you know, I’m so tempted to go up to the dining area and fill one of my water bottles with their lemonade!”

She said, “Come on, I’m going that way anyway, let’s go.”

Together we went up.  Maybe my plan was perfectly acceptable, but it helped to have a partner in crime.  I filled up on lemonade, and it proved so delicious on the trail, I enjoyed it so much.

While near the dining room I used the flush toilet, the only one on their whole property.  It didn’t seem like a big deal, but I wanted to get my money’s worth.  (It was $375 a night, remember?)

Unfortunately, I never went in the hot spring, and the people who believe in those things were just raving about it.  The day before, when I might have done it, they had girls cleaning it with toothbrushes and I just didn’t feel like it any other time.

Back in the cabin, I finished packing, surprised that almost all my food fit in the bear canister.  It seemed I was getting a bit more efficient at that task.  My charger block almost got fully charged, finally, so that was good news too.

Before leaving Muir Trail Ranch, I dropped off my plastic five-gallon  bucket full of not-needed items (mostly food) at the reception location.   Just at that moment a German couple arrived and said, “Oh we forgot to send food, we have no food.”

I replied, “Well this is your lucky day, there’s a ton of food in here.  It’s good, expensive food too.”  It was, because a lot of it was the pricey freeze-dried stuff.  Somehow, I had badly miscalculated.  Whatever the reason, that couple made out like bandits.  The adage, “the trail provides” was certainly true for them that day!

I was facing a long uphill, over 3,000 feet to cross Selden Pass, and the very first stretch was one of the steepest.  After a break, when you first start walking, especially uphill, and when your pack is heavy from resupply, your legs can feel like lead.  Mine felt that way, for sure, but the good news was, it didn’t last long.

Pretty soon I was going up at a good pace, but I was stuck behind a horse being ridden by next-door-cabin Jay, who was behind a horse ridden by his guide.  Oddly, my pace was pretty close to that of the horses, but I didn’t want to get in front and hold them up.  At the same time, walking behind I was breathing the dust they were kicking up, so I kept having to drop back to get clear of that.

Finally, there was some separation between us.  The ascent included a lot of long switchbacks, so that the “crow fly” distance to MTR was not that great.  I could even still see it, just far, far beneath me now.  My blood was really pumping from all the uphill!

I was pleased at how I handled it.  True, it was at a lower elevation, but I thought at last I might be getting my trail legs.  Soon I crossed over some forests and meadows and was out of sight of the resort.  I spotted some wildflowers by the trail.

Perhaps it was the season, but I did not see the profusion of wildflowers on the JMT I have seen on other trails

In one of the meadows I was puzzled.  There was a path straight across, but someone had blocked it off.  As I skirted the glade, following the detour, I came across the team building the trail on more sustainable ground, to preserve the fragile meadow.   These folks do such an important job, making the trail better for all of us, and getting paid rather small salaries for doing it.  As always I stopped and thanked them profusely.  I wanted them to know that they and their work were deeply appreciated.

I came to the twin Sallie Keyes lakes.  As I arrived, I crossed some steppingstones over the exit creek and saw a beautiful snake swimming in the water; a garter snake, I think.  I even got its picture.

Right after this shot I watched the snake enter the water and swim away

Feeling hungry, I was tempted to have lunch there.  But then I looked at the map, and saw that the last lake before Selden Pass was called Heart Lake, because it’s shaped like a heart.

I thought, “That seems very appropriate. I will go to Heart Lake and have my lunch.”  At Muir Trail Ranch breakfasts they also lay out the fixings for making and packing a lunch.  I had prepared a sandwich and packed it into my Nalgene soaking container.  I also brought a fresh apple.

When I got to Heart Lake, I took off my shoes and socks, dunked my bare feet into the lake, and ate half of my sandwich.  Under the dry surface of the moss I was sitting on, the lower portion was very wet, meaning my shorts were quickly soaked.

The moss is where I sat and the water is where I swam

As I realized how wet my seat was, I also realized the thought of a swim was very attractive.  So, I took off my clothes, put on my water shoes, and walked into Heart Lake.  I plunged my whole body in, swam out my usual 10 yards, then swam back.  It refreshed me so much.  And yes, I was skinny dipping, except for my water shoes.

But nobody came by while I was swimming.  Afterwards I dried off a little bit and put my damp shorts back on.  Then I ate the rest of my sandwich and my apple and finished off the MTR lemonade.  It was so good.  I’d been drinking it all day, climbing and climbing.  Now it was gone.

A little hard to see, but those are the two upper lobes of the “heart” that names Heart Lake

Energized by food and swim, I continued on up to Selden Pass.  It was a little weird, because no one else was there.  Usually when you get to a pass, you are not alone.  It seems people get to a pass, and they often stay there for a while.  The are resting, or eating, or just enjoying the view and celebrating their accomplishment on top of the world.  Sam and I crossed Forrester Pass alone, but that day the weather was challenging, so nobody stayed up there.

On Selden Pass I was alone, and it was nice to be there by myself.  Then I started down, realizing it was now around 3.30 p.m., which meant my progress was faster than I expected.  That morning I envisioned arriving at closer to 5:00 p.m.  It seemed I was in better shape than I thought.

View north from Selden Pass, the lake is Marie Lake

Descending I was trying to balance going pretty fast, while also being careful to avoid another accident.  As I  proceeded down, I met a couple of women and, in the course of the discussion, one of them revealed their hike was a birthday trip.

So, I asked the birthday girl her name (Sydney) and I sang “Happy Birthday dear Sydney” to her.  That’s just what I do.

Further down I met a woman, an American of Korean origin, who was just finishing a (very late!) lunch at a stream.  She told me her trail name was Ninja Queen because she is allergic to UV light and therefore wears a ninja’s face mask and bright reflector sunglasses.

Just then another couple came going southbound and she too was Korean and was even carrying a Korean flag!   She said some Korean guys had a bunch of extra flags and offered  her one.  She felt patriotic and said, “Okay, sure!”

The guy she was with had the trail name Eminem.  It seemed they had met on trail and were just friends.  We took a selfie of the four of us meeting on the trail.

Papa George, Flag Girl, Eminem, and Ninja Queen hiking the High Sierra!

Such a random coincidence, right?  I said she should take “Flag Girl” as her trail name.  But it didn’t seem to really appeal to her.

I proceeded down and down and down, trying to make up as much distance as possible, so that tomorrow’s trip to VVR, Vermilion Valley Resort, is a relatively short one.  I did stop to refill my water at a stream crossing, and Ninja Queen passed me while I was there.  We exchanged greetings and found we both intended to overnight at VVR on the following day.

Finally, I got to the tent site that I was targeting.  To my disappointment, it appeared packed full.  I walked up to a tent to ask if there were any spots available nearby, and it was Sally!  The news about spaces was not hopeful, but I enjoyed stopping and chatting with her briefly.  That was very nice, especially when she said, “Yeah, all day I was asking, where is George? Where do you think George is?”

When I admitted I left MTR at 10:00 a.m. she went, “You left at 10 o’clock???”  Only then did I feel a little abashed at my late start.

That campsite was full, then the next campsite was full too.  The sun was getting lower and finally dropped behind the ridge.  It was still light enough to see, but it was quickly darkening.  Finally, the third place was empty!  I found a spot that seemed adequate in the fading light and set up camp.  I soaked and then ate an al pastor rice dish that was actually good, and I hid my bear cannister.  It was too dark by then to think about digging a cathole.

My faithful Big Agnes tent in the campsite near the river

Then I settled in for another night in the woods.

Defamation Counterclaim Arising out Reagan National Helicopter Crash Case Can Go Forward

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

[A.] From Ellis v. Wallace, decided today by Chief Magistrate Judge Scott Varholak (D. Colo.):

On January 29, 2025, a Black Hawk Helicopter and American Airlines Flight 5342 collided mid-air near Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport. Hours after the crash, several posts and articles appeared claiming Plaintiff was the helicopter pilot in the midair collision ("the rumor"), including:

Defendant made his first X post addressing the rumor on January 31, 2025, at 1:00 a.m. Eastern Time. And Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this matter on April 9, 2025, alleging that Defendant's posts were defamatory. Plaintiff then proceeded to appear as a guest on several podcasts. On July 18, 2025, she was interviewed on the Kill Tony podcast. During the interview, the hosts asked Plaintiff about the collision rumor and her pending litigation. Plaintiff explained that Defendant was the "[f]irst one to tie my likeness to the rumor. We don't know if he started the rumor, but he had millions of followers. He did the most what we can determine as damage."

Ten days later, Defendant filed the Special Motion to Dismiss Plaintiff's Complaint, attaching copies of X posts and articles linking Plaintiff to the crash hours before Defendant commented on the rumor. Plaintiff then appeared on a second podcast, the RVA Magazine podcast, on September 30, 2025. Plaintiff was again asked about the rumor and her pending litigation. In response, she stated:

[W]ithin 24 hours of the crash, I was finding out that I was being named as as the pilot as, like, the unnamed female pilot that was the supposed DEI hire that Trump, President Trump had alluded to at a press conference that "we know it's DEI because we just know." And so that kind of sent the online world into a frenzy of like, let's figure out who this pilot is. And, from what we can tell, [Defendant] picked up on that and used a picture of me and the [Michael] Smerconish article and, um, kind of put it all together. And with his following, he had, I think, two and half million followers or something like that, we believe he did the most damage. Like it would not have had the legs it had if he hadn't posted about it.

Finally, on January 12, 2026, Kim Congdon interviewed Plaintiff on The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast. Congdon asked Plaintiff if she was suing the individual that started the rumor. Plaintiff responded:

I don't, yeah, I don't know if he started it, but he definitely, like, he pushed it. He's the first one to tie my likeness to it. Yeah. His name's Matt Wallace and, uh, he, uh, millions of followers on Twitter, and he pushed it pretty hard and we believe he did the most damage and so I'm suing him in federal court."

On April 6, 2026, Defendant filed the Counterclaim alleging that these podcast statements were defamatory.

Defendant alleges that Plaintiff made repeated defamatory statements that: (1) Defendant "was the first one to post [Plaintiff's] likeness in connection with the helicopter crash," and (2) that Plaintiff believes Defendant's "statements caused the most damage." {Plaintiff alleges that there is a material difference between claiming that Defendant was the first to name Plaintiff in relation to the tragedy, and that Defendant was the first one to post Plaintiff's likeness in connection with the collision. The distinction is unclear to the Court. Accordingly, the Court assesses Plaintiff's "likeness" statements as conveying Defendant was the first to connect Plaintiff to the collision.}

[B.] The court rejected Plaintiff's arguments that Plaintiff's statements were protected by the "litigation privilege." That privilege bars defamation lawsuits based on statements in court filings and in-court statements, but doesn't apply to statements about the lawsuit made outside the litigation.

(Recall that, though in a typical case it would be a defendant who's arguing the statements were privileged, opinion, true, etc., and plaintiff would be responding, here the roles are flipped because the defendant is counterclaiming against plaintiff based on plaintiff's statements.)

[C.] The court also rejected Plaintiff's arguments that the statements were nonactionable opinion: It concluded that both statements were "susceptible of being proved true or false," and were framed as factual assertions rather than using "loose or hyperbolic language that provided the impression that her statements should not be taken seriously."

[D.] The court concluded that Defendant had introduced sufficient evidence that the statements were false:

First, with respect to Plaintiff's "likeness" statements, Plaintiff repeatedly claimed that Defendant was "the first one to tie [her] likeness to the rumor." However, Defendant provided several pieces of evidence showing that other X users linked Plaintiff to the rumor before Defendant….

Second, Defendant has shown a reasonable likelihood of proving by clear and convincing evidence that Plaintiff's "most damage" statements were false. Defendant declares that "the rumor was spread primarily by much larger voices than [Defendant's,] such as Ann Coulter's, Alex Jones's, and others." He claims that these "prominent individuals and entities" had a "far greater reach than anything [he] posted" about Plaintiff. If true, this declaration could provide clear and convincing evidence that Defendant's "most damage" statements were false. At the very least, it creates a question for the jury since Plaintiff has offered competing evidence. …

[E.] The court also concluded that Defendant had introduced sufficient evidence that Plaintiff knew the "likeness"/"first one to tie" statements were probably false (which is required by Colorado law in such cases):

First, Plaintiff's statements on the RVA Magazine podcast and The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast occurred after Defendant filed his Special Motion to Dismiss and Plaintiff filed her response. In his Special Motion to Dismiss, Defendant included evidence of other X users linking Plaintiff to the rumor before Defendant's first post. And Plaintiff's podcast statements referred to the ongoing litigation. Thus, it is plausible to infer that Plaintiff had a high degree of awareness of the probable falsity of her statements.

Second, Plaintiff's statements reveal inconsistencies in her account of the underlying events. During her interview on the Kill Tony podcast and The Kim Congdon Takeover podcast, Plaintiff repeatedly stated that she did not know whether Defendant started the rumor despite claiming he was "the first one to tie [her] likeness to it." Plaintiff repeated these conflicting statements in her declaration. was the first to blame [her] for this horrific crash because [she] clearly knew that information was incorrect.")] Consequently, such inconsistencies weigh in favor of finding that Defendant has shown a reasonable likelihood of proving actual malice by clear and convincing evidence….

But the court concluded there wasn't enough evidence of knowing or reckless falsehood as to the "most damage" statement:

Defendant declares that at the time Plaintiff made her public statements, she knew "prominent individuals and entities" had discussed her connection with the crash and that those individuals had a "far greater reach than anything [Defendant] posted." However, such evidence does not support an inference that Plaintiff entertained serious doubts as to the truth of the statements or was highly aware of their probable falsity. Further, Defendant provides no other evidence to support his actual malice claim here.

On the other hand, the mere fact that the actor stated that the podcaster did the most harm—even knowing the New York Times had a broader audience—does not necessarily equate to actual malice; the actor could easily believe (even if wrongfully) that the podcaster caused the most harm because the podcaster gave credibility to the false rumor and thus led others to run with the story. Here, Defendant has only presented evidence that those with broader audiences likewise posted the false story. But, as the hypothetical shows, this does not necessarily mean that Plaintiff's statement that Defendant caused the most damage was made with actual malice.}

[F.] Finally, the court concludes that Defendant had sufficiently alleged damages, as required by Colorado law:

Specifically, he alleged that Plaintiff's statements have caused: (1) "significant stress and strain in [his] family relations," and (2) stress from his alleged tarnished reputation. Although Plaintiff claims that that Defendant asserted these damages "in conclusory fashion," the Court finds the presented evidence sufficient to satisfy Defendant's burden at this stage.

The court thus denies Plaintiff's motion to dismiss as to the "likeness"/"first one to tie" statements, which means Defendant's counterclaim can go forward (though of course it doesn't mean the Defendant's counterclaim will ultimately prevail).

The Vault will eventually open to public eyes, and it’ll be worth a visit once it does.

For decades, Ford’s internal policy was to not keep examples of cars it’s made. Once their useful lifespan was up, they were to be sold, donated, or trashed. That being the case, there’s never been an official museum of cars past and present operated by the Blue Oval here in the U.S. Until now, that is. Say hi to the Ford Vault.

In the same sense that Porsche has the Porsche Museum and Mercedes-Benz has the Mercedes-Benz Museum, the Ford Vault is a place to admire the long history of Ford and Lincoln. It’s not open to the general public yet – Ford intends to ultimately open its doors to anyone – but we got a sneak preview of what to expect when Ford’s finished with this large hall of vehicles.

As of now, there are 125 cars and trucks inside this warehouse-like building in Romulus, Michigan. Situated about 20 or so miles from Ford World Headquarters in Dearborn, this only exists because of Jim Farley’s blessing. Thanks to orders from the top to change Ford’s vehicle retention policy, a collection of historical vehicles finally became possible. It’s taken about three years of buying special vehicles and unearthing some that were squirreled away, but Ford finally has an array of cars worth showing off. A small fraction of them (about 15 at this stage) are on loan from folks happy to show their cars off in Ford’s space. It’s a practice that Ford says it intends to continue, giving new visitors something fresh to see each time they return.

We got a chance to walk through the Vault this week. Read on for some highlights of what you can expect should you find yourself in the Ford Vault one day.

This Mustang doesn’t look like much from the outside, but it houses one of the most ambitious skunkworks engine projects from Ford that never made it past the prototype phase. Back when Ford was developing the 2005 Ford GT, it was searching for the right powertrain for the job. Hilariously, it fit in the engine bay of the Mustang. This prototype in particular has been kept off the books for decades since to ensure it lived on, and now it’s proudly displayed in the Vault.

Speaking of Ford GTs, the Vault is home to a huge number of them. One of the particularly special ones is this concept that debuted at the 2002 Detroit Auto Show. It’s close, but not identical to the 2005 GT that came out a few years later.

The Ka being present here in the Vault foreshadows that it won’t just be American-sold Fords and Lincolns that will make their way into the collection. It’s also a charming aside from the barrage of Ford Mustangs and GTs on display here.

There’s no guarantee these pieces of history will be there when Ford decides to open up to the public, but it sure is a great way to kick this collection off with a bang.

Aer Lingus Pilots Wanted A Safer Place To Land After Bomb Threat, Now Face Criminal Investigation

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Aer Lingus pilots responding to a bomb threat were directed to land at a French military airfield. According to their unions, they lacked the charts and operational information needed to land safely and requested another airport. They ultimately landed at the base, but then faced questioning and now a criminal investigation over their initial response. I find that troubling, particularly if their supposed failure to cooperate was an attempt to protect the people onboard.

As One Mile At A Time highlights, the handling of a recent Aer Lingus diversion has prompted a pointed warning from European pilot unions.

The incident occurred on November 5, 2026, aboard Aer Lingus flight EI402 from Dublin (DUB) to Rome (FCO). Following a bomb threat, the Airbus A320 diverted to Évreux-Fauville Air Base in Normandy under a French fighter escort. No explosives were found, and the aircraft eventually returned to Dublin.

But the dispute over how it reached that airfield deserves a closer look.

In a November 10 joint statement, the European Cockpit Association, Irish Air Line Pilots’ Association, and French pilot union SNPL said the crew did not have the necessary aeronautical charts and operational information for the military airfield. According to the unions, the pilots requested a more suitable alternative, but that request was refused.

The organizations also expressed concern that the pilots subsequently faced possible criminal prosecution over decisions they say were intended to protect those onboard. ECA Vice-President Captain Paul Reuter put it succinctly:

“The pilots must not find themselves treated as a threat by the very authorities whose assistance they require.”

That strikes me as a very reasonable starting point.

French authorities described the initial exchange differently. The prime minister’s office said fighter aircraft were dispatched because the pilot had not cooperated as expected.

Évreux public prosecutor Rémi Coutin told The Irish Times that investigators were examining whether the pilot initially refused the landing instruction and whether that conduct could constitute a criminal offense. Both pilots were questioned and permitted to leave France. The prosecutor said on November 6 that the investigation remained ongoing.

Aer Lingus has defended its crew, saying they “acted impeccably and were thoroughly professional.”

So we have competing descriptions of the same exchange. An investigation has been opened, but no criminal charges have been filed. I would be interested in what the cockpit recordings and communications with authorities actually reveal.

It is understandable why authorities might want an aircraft carrying a suspected explosive brought to a military installation rather than a busy passenger airport. There may have been very good reasons to choose Évreux.

But those reasons need to account for whether the crew can safely get the aircraft onto the ground there.

The objection described by the unions concerns missing operational information. That is a substantial concern, even if the runway itself can easily accommodate an A320. Pilots still need to understand the approach and assess the conditions for their particular aircraft (I realized this myself when trying to land at Madeira Airport in the United Airlines 737 flight simulator).

A fighter escort cannot make those calculations for them.

I would not expect a captain to continue toward Rome simply because diverting was inconvenient. But requesting an alternative airport because you lack information needed for a safe landing sounds like precisely the sort of judgment I want the person flying my aircraft to exercise!

If the authorities had a compelling reason why no alternative was acceptable, that should have been explained to the pilots and more precise guidance given for landing at the diversion airport. I have trouble seeing how the prospect of prosecution helps anyone land more safely.

I recently wrote about European pilots reporting fatigue and fearing the consequences of speaking up. This is a different situation, but it raises a similar concern: what happens when exercising professional judgment appears personally riskier than going along?

Of course pilots are accountable for their decisions. Their position does not place them above the law, and authorities should examine a disputed response during a security emergency. But there needs to be room for a captain to say that an instruction creates a safety problem. Otherwise, we tell pilots they bear responsibility for everyone onboard while discouraging them from exercising the judgment that responsibility requires.

I also would not dismiss the original threat simply because the search found nothing. I made the same point after the recent Austrian Airlines diversion under a MiG-29 escort. Crews and authorities must respond to the information available at the time and hindsight does not make a precaution foolish.

But taking a bomb threat seriously should include taking the pilots’ landing concerns seriously too.

I cannot resolve this dispute without knowing what was actually communicated between the aircraft and French authorities. Reviewing those communications is appropriate and I hope they are released soon.

Nevertheless, if the union account is accurate, I am firmly sympathetic to the pilots. Asking for an airport where they could conduct a properly informed landing was a responsible response to an already difficult situation.

The bomb threat required a security response. The concern now is whether that response gave enough weight to the safety judgment of the people actually flying the airplane. Those pilots deserve a substantive answer…not prosecution.

The Legend of Korra has aged well, but it was far less universally acclaimed. Seven Havens should take as much inspiration from Aang and Team Avatar as possible. Britain’s coastal floods have a surprising connection to distant skies

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and Arctic help explain where extreme storm surges strike Britain’s coast and northwestern Europe.

Storm surges look like local disasters, but which stretch of Britain’s coast floods can often be traced to air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Those far-off patterns help decide whether northern or southern coasts take the high water.

A surge is hard to forecast, because wind, low air pressure, and the shape of the coast all play a part at once.

Understanding why surges hit certain places, such as East Anglia in eastern England, could give coastal towns earlier flood warnings.

Lisanne Blok of the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the British Antarctic Survey.

Her team studied more than 70 years of sea levels around northwestern Europe.

A computer model rebuilt those levels day by day from 1950 onward, using past weather data rather than tide gauge readings.

The team first removed the tides and the normal seasonal rise and fall of the sea. Then they used machine learning to sort the coastline into regions that tend to get extreme surges on the same days.

They ended up with six regions across the seas of northwestern Europe, plus eight smaller ones inside the North Sea.

“If we can identify conditions emerging in, for example, the Atlantic that might result in a storm surge in East Anglia, then we can pinpoint parts of the coast that should prepare for flooding,” said Blok.

Big, repeating pressure patterns over the ocean are what scientists call climate modes.

“Computer models used to forecast risk of coastal flooding don’t always account for large-scale climate modes, instead focusing on localized weather variables,” she said. “But what our findings tell us is that climate modes are really key.”

A storm surge builds when strong winds and low air pressure push a bulge of seawater toward land.

Sometimes it rises high enough to spill over flood defenses. If that happens at high tide, with big waves on top, the sea can reach extreme heights and flood the coast.

Over January 31 and February 1, 1953, a deep low-pressure system moved down the North Sea and pushed a huge mass of water along Britain’s east coast.

It arrived with a spring tide, the extra-high tide that comes around a new or full moon, and sea levels rose about 10 feet (3 meters) above predicted levels.

Floods swept through parts of Britain, the Netherlands, and Belgium.

Another surge struck Britain’s east coast on December 5 and 6, 2013, with water levels in some places higher than during the 1953 flood, but modern defenses prevented a disaster on that scale.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel shape, tightest between East Anglia and the Netherlands, pushes water toward land. Its shallow floor, roughly 30 to 490 feet (10 to 150 meters) deep, makes the surge taller.

On the worst 1% of days in the rebuilt records, surges added at least 22 inches (57 centimeters) to the North Sea’s water level, more than in any other region.

“The storm surges are very dynamic and complex in this region,” said Blok. “There is also heightened risk, because lots of people live along the low-lying coastal regions bordering the North Sea.”

Next, the team compared each region’s surges with those big pressure patterns.

“We can link regional storm surge extremes to very distinct climate patterns,” Blok said. “I didn’t expect such clear relationships, especially across the large distances involved.”

The strongest links were to pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere, including the North Atlantic Oscillation, a seesaw in air pressure between the regions around Iceland and the Azores.

When Iceland’s pressure is low compared with the Azores, the jet stream, a fast wind high up, moves north, and surges become more likely on northern and central coasts.

“As the storms shift to the north, they also deliver high seas to more northerly coasts,” said Blok.

When pressure over Iceland is unusually high instead, the pressure difference with the Azores shrinks.

The jet stream slows and bends south. Storms then take southern routes, bringing high seas to southern coasts.

Storms behind extreme surges off southwest England and west of Ireland tended to start near Newfoundland, off eastern Canada, thousands of miles away.

Storms behind surges farther north, in the seas toward Norway, more often began off Greenland or in the central North Atlantic.

Those southwestern surges were also more common in autumn, while the other regions saw most of theirs in winter.

Ali Mashayek, an associate professor in Cambridge’s Department of Earth Sciences, was the study’s senior author.

“Conditions in the Arctic have a big impact on UK weather,” he said. “That’s something we’ve known for a while, although we haven’t been able to link it to storm surge distribution before.”

Mashayek said Arctic warming will weaken the pressure difference that powers the jet stream, and “that means it is likely we will see a shift in UK storm patterns, including where storm surges occur, in future.”

But he added that “this is something we are still trying to understand.”

It’s time to view local weather extremes in a global context, Mashayek said. “We now have more evidence that the extreme weather in our backyard may be driven by what happens in very distant parts of the world.”

“This speaks to the broader issue that climate change and the extremes it induces are a truly global problem that need global solutions.”

The surge records left out waves and long-term sea level rise. The team also didn’t check whether the links change from season to season.

Next, the researchers want to run the same analysis on future climate projections, to see whether the surge regions shift as the planet warms.

The full study was published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

Volcanic eruptions leave a lingering chill in the ocean

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Major volcanic eruptions may briefly strengthen ocean cold spells, but their cooling effects vary widely across different regions.

Pinatubo’s eruption was over long before the cooling was. The 1991 eruption cooled the lower atmosphere worldwide by as much as 0.9°F (0.5°C), with effects lasting about two years.

At sea, that chill had an uneven reach. A spell of unusually cold water could grow stronger in one region without doing so in another.

A new study follows those cold spells through six big eruptions. Each eruption preceded an increase in their strength overall, a short-lived reversal of the weakening seen across 175 years.

Marine cold spells are runs of days when the sea surface is unusually cold. An icy sea isn’t necessarily having one; it may just be as cold as it usually is.

The study used the coldest 10% of temperatures for a given place and time of year as its cutoff. The water had to stay that cold for five days or longer, rather than just dip for a day or two.

Jan Kempert, at South Westphalia University of Applied Sciences, worked with Raven Quilestino-Olario, at the University of Cologne’s Institute of Geology and Mineralogy, on the research. Both institutions are in Germany.

“We noticed that while the cooling effects of major volcanic eruptions have been widely studied, relatively little attention has been given to how these volcanic winters affect extreme cold events in the ocean,” Quilestino-Olario said.

Their source was COBE-SST3, a record from the Japan Meteorological Agency. It gives daily estimates of ocean surface temperatures from 1850 to 2024, based mostly on readings taken at sea.

For each cold spell they found, the pair added up how far below normal the water was, day by day. That total became the spell’s score.

The score thus counted the length of the spell as well as the cold. A longer stretch or a deeper chill would push it up.

In 79% of the ocean map squares, however, the scores went down over those 175 years.

Cold spells grew less frequent in about the same share of squares, extending a decline that satellites had already recorded for 1982 to 2020.

The eruption list began with Krakatau in 1883 and Santa Maria in 1902. It continued with Novarupta in 1912, Agung in 1963, El Chichón in 1982, and finally Pinatubo in 1991.

The stratosphere, high above the ground, is where sulfur-rich particles from large eruptions can linger. They cut down the sunlight reaching the surface, sometimes leaving months or years of cooling.

The comparison put cold spells from the 30 years before an eruption on one side and a short period after it on the other.

The later run always started the next year and lasted five years for Krakatau, two for Pinatubo, and one for each of the other four.

There was more than a volcano to reckon with in some of those years. El Chichón, in particular, had some inconvenient company.

The eruption came in 1982. Across 1982 and 1983, the Pacific also experienced one of only six “super” El Niño events since 1850.

Both events could leave a mark on the temperature record, so the researchers had to allow for the overlap.

Kempert and Quilestino-Olario removed the influence of El Niño and La Niña, along with the long-term weakening of cold spells.

After those adjustments, marine cold spells were stronger than expected following all six eruptions.

“Our study shows that although cold extremes in the ocean have generally become less frequent and less intense over the past 175 years, major volcanic eruptions may temporarily interrupt this long-term pattern,” Kempert said.

They also tried choosing the years without looking at eruption dates at all. In 10,000 random draws, each containing six years, they checked for an increase in cold-spell strength.

Just about 2.4% of the draws produced an increase as large as the one after the eruptions. In other words, most random choices of years didn’t produce anything of that size.

The world average was one thing, though. The maps were another.

From 1884 to 1888, about 80% of the ocean squares had stronger cold spells than expected. That was the period following Krakatau; for Pinatubo, the share came to 70%.

For Santa Maria and Agung it was roughly half. El Chichón and Novarupta came in below half, a reminder that an increase in the world average needn’t appear in most places.

Five eruptions had their biggest response in the middle latitudes, the bands between 30 and 60 degrees north or south of the equator. Novarupta broke that pattern.

The only high-latitude eruption in the six, Novarupta also had the weakest effect on the global average. The authors were cautious about that result.

None of these eruptions occurred in an otherwise unchanging ocean. Ocean circulation was still shifting water around, the seasons were changing, and natural swings in climate were still going on.

According to the authors, volcanic cooling and those ongoing changes together could account for the patchy map. Changes in circulation can even bring warmer winters to parts of the far north after an eruption.

Some parts of the ocean have very few nineteenth-century observations. The record is less reliable in places with few historical readings, and in places where sea ice was present.

For Pinatubo, the team ran the analysis again with a satellite-era record from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

The two records agreed in 79% of the map squares about whether cold spells were stronger or weaker than expected.

Another choice affected the answer: the years used to stand for normal temperatures. Comparing each eruption with its previous 30 years gave stronger cold spells after all six.

When the researchers used the same modern reference period for every eruption, fewer eruptions showed an increase.

The count was five with 1961 to 1990 as the comparison, and four with 1981 to 2010.

How much extra cold can be pinned on the volcanoes themselves remains uncertain. The records describe the aftermath, but can’t separate out every cause.

Additional tests removed a North Atlantic climate pattern as well as El Niño.

Several other natural cycles remained, partly because their consistent monthly records don’t reach as far back as 1850.

There’s a further difficulty with El Niño: an eruption may affect it. Remove El Niño from the calculation and some of the volcanic effect may go with it.

The authors call for further checks using other temperature records, measurements beneath the surface, and climate models.

Their own next project will look at marine heatwaves, periods of unusually warm ocean water.

“We hope to use the same long-term ocean temperature dataset to explore how marine heatwaves have changed over the past 175 years, this time placing greater emphasis on the uncertainties associated with historical observations,” Quilestino-Olario said.

The heatwave work could help establish whether eruptions change unusually hot conditions at sea as well as cold ones.

The full study was published in the journal Ocean-Land-Atmosphere Research.

Record renewable energy growth is driving climate progress in some countries, but Germany and many major emitters are falling behind.

Germany has slipped down the climate action ranking for the third consecutive year, with the former green leader now being outperformed by Brazil, Columbia and Romania.

In its worst performance since 2019, the country fell to place 25 and received a "medium" rating overall on the yearly Climate Change Performance Index (CCPI), which assesses efforts to curb global warming in 64 countries plus the European Union.

Still, "a number of countries have been making good progress for years," said Jan Burck, CCPI lead author in a statement. Denmark is leading the way across all four categories assessed — energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, renewable energy and national climate policy. A total of 15 states, including Lithuania, Chile, and Morocco, received an overall "high" rating, which is two more than last year.

The European Union also moved up a spot, while Hungary jumped 21 places after the defeat of former prime minister Viktor Orban's government, which had repeatedly vetoed EU climate plans, dismissing them as a "utopian fantasy."

The top three positions on the CCPI remained vacant again this year, as no country has yet done enough to meet global climate goals aimed at preventing worsening weather extremes and climate damage.

"We are seeing a striking contrast," said Burck. "Progress on climate action is often slow and in some countries, such as the United States, things are even moving backwards. But technologically and economically there is enormous momentum."

That momentum is apparent in record solar energy growth. Last year 75% of global electricity demand growth was met by the green energy source, according to energy think tank Ember's latest global electricity review. Investment in clean energy has also outpaced that in fossil fuels for multiple years.

"Governments have to act now to turn the momentum into sustained emissions reductions," said CCPI co-author Niklas Höhne of the NewClimate Institute, which compiles the report with environmental NGO Germanwatch.

Burck attributed Germany's worst ever performance in the category of national climate policy to current policies being pursued by Germany's economy minister Katherina Reiche.

"Measures that primarily aim to increase the use of natural gas, threaten to undermine progress in the energy transition and risk significantly worsening the conditions for renewable energy and electrification for many years to come," said Burck.

A European Commission report published on November 9 on the sidelines of the UN COP31 climate summit in Turkey said Germany was one of nine EU countries set to miss 2030 emissions reductions targets.

Germany is not alone in its disappointing ranking. The other G20 countries — the world's leading industrialized and emerging economies, responsible for around 80% of global CO2 emissions — also perform poorly.

The G20's oil- and gas-producing countries occupy some of the lowest positions on the climate action table. Iran, the United States, Russia, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Canada all languish at the bottom of the league, with an overall rating of "very low."

Several oil-producing countries, including Kazakhstan, Saudi Arabia, Uzbekistan and the United Arab Emirates, are also performing better than expected in the renewables category and "are significantly expanding their previously limited" capacity, said the report.

The Peaceful Second Amendment Revolution

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

How the right was restored from its nadir half a century ago

This speech was delivered during Wyoming Law Week at the University of Wyoming College of Law, October 13, 2026.

Ladies and gentlemen, cowboys and cowgirls, good evening.

The title of my presentation, "The Peaceful Second Amendment Revolution," is about a revolution in the original meaning of that word: a return to the original starting point, as when the earth completes a revolution around the sun.

The American Revolution began in that conservative sense. The Americans wanted the British to respect all their pre-existing rights, including the written right to arms from the 1689 English Bill of Rights.

Law Week is an appropriate time to reflect on the rule of law and the revolutionary role of lawyers, then and now.

Writing in 1789, after victory in the American Revolution, physician David Ramsay explained one reason for the Revolution: "The study of law was common and fashion­able." David Ramsay, 1 A History of the American Revolution 29 (Lester H. Cohen ed., Liberty Fund, 1990) (1789). According to Ramsay:

"No order of men has, in all ages, been more favourable to liberty, than lawyers. When they are not won over to the service of government, they are formidable adversaries to it. Professionally taught the rights of human nature, they keenly and quickly perceive every attack made upon them. While others judge of bad principles by the actual grievances they occasion, lawyers discover them at a distance, and trace future mischiefs from gilded innovations."

‍Ramsay pointed out that half of the First Continental Congress, in 1774, were lawyers.

"Bred in the habits of public speaking, they made a distinguished figure in the meetings of the people, and were particularly able to explain to them the tendency of the late acts of parlia­ment. Exerting their abilities and influence in the cause of their country, they were rewarded with its confidence."

We ought always feel gratitude to those lawyers, and other Americans, who defended and advanced natural rights during the American Revolution. Tonight is the story of how one of the rights which those lawyers and others had guaranteed in our Constitution was nearly obliterated during the last third of the twentieth century. And how the peaceful revolution that has restored the Second Amendment was the cooperative work of millions of Americans, especially lawyers, and among them, especially legal scholars.

Although the story does involve the recovery of the original meaning of Second Amendment, the story is also about living constitutionalism; the most important reason the right to keep and bear arms survived against the attempts to destroy it was that the right lives and thrives in the hearts and minds of the American people.

The U.S. Supreme Court decisions of District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008 and McDonald v. Chicago in 2010 were the opposite of cutting edge. Those decisions were lagging indicators. They were the long-term consequence of elections, of a generation of historical scholarship, and of the refusal of millions of ordinary people to accept that a right they had always exercised and cherished could be defined out of existence by unreasoned pronouncements.

During the nineteenth century, virtually all commentators and courts treated the right to keep and bear arms as a broad individual right, including for people not in the militia.

The most influential legal treatise of the early republic, St. George Tucker's American edition of Blackstone, described the Amendment as securing the rights of self-defense and resistance to tyranny.

Next came William Rawle's 1825 A View of the Constitution of the United States of America, which said that if either Congress or a state government, in "blind pursuit of inor­dinate power" should "attempt" "to disarm the people," "this amendment may be appealed to as a restraint on both."

The great Supreme Court Justice and prolific legal scholar Joseph Story wrote in 1840 that

"The right of the citizens to keep and bear arms has justly been considered, as the palladium of the liberties of a republic; since it offers a strong moral check against the usurpation and arbitrary power of rulers; and it will generally, even if these are successful in the first instance, enable the people to resist and triumph over them."

The preeminent constitutional writer of the latter 19th century was Michigan Supreme Court Chief Justice and Michigan Law School Dean Thomas Cooley. In 1880 he explained that "The right is general." "[I]f the right were limited to those enrolled," in the militia, "the purpose of this guaranty might be defeated altogether by the action or neglect to act of the government it was meant to hold in check."

Congress agreed. When the former slave states attempted to keep the freedmen in de facto servitude by enacting Black Codes that forbade the freedmen to possess arms, or required them to obtain a license, the Reconstruction Congress in 1866 overrode President Andrew Johnson's veto and passed the Second Freedmen's Bureau Bill, guaranteeing the "consti­tutional right to bear arms" as among the "immunities and rights" of Americans. The Civil Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment were passed with a similar and oft-expressed intent.

The theory that the right to arms was actually not a right made its first substantial appearance in a 1905 Kansas Supreme Court decision, Salina v. Blaksley. The case involved a municipal ordinance against carrying a firearm while intoxicated; the ordinance was easy to uphold based on precedents from other states. But the Kansas court aimed to use the case to destroy the right. Section 4 of the 1859 Kansas Bill of Rights declared "that the people have the right to bear arms for their defense and security." Yet the Kansas court claimed that this provision, in the state constitution Bill of Rights, merely affirmed the inherent power to state government to conduct military activities. The Kansas court also claimed that the federal Second Amendment had the same, empty meaning.

The Kansas court could not cite a single authority for its radical propositions. For example, a Massachusetts case had said that the state constitution right to arms is not violated by requiring a permit for large armed parades. The Kansas court pretended that the Massachusetts court had declared that there is no right to arms.

Based on how the U.S. Supreme Court had misinterpreted the Fourteenth Amendment in The Slaughterhouse Cases and U.S. v. Cruikshank in the 1870s, the Second Amendment, like most of the rest of the Bill of Rights, was not considered applicable to the States in the latter 19th and 20th centuries. State courts varied in how strictly they enforced their state constitution right to arms.

In 1934, Congress passed the first major federal gun control law, the National Firearms Act, which required a hefty $200 tax and registration for a small subset of arms, including machine guns and short-barreled shotguns. The Supreme Court said in U.S. v. Miller that the short-barreled shotgun owned by a bootlegger had not been shown to be the sort of arm useful to a militia, and it upheld the National Firearms Act on that narrow ground. Notably, the Court was very interested in Miller's weapon, and did not even mention that Miller while bootlegging was not actively serving in the Arkansas militia.

The Supreme Court's decision was consistent with the most restrictive line of state constitution cases from the 19th century, all of which came from States of the Confederacy: every individual citizen had the right to arms, but only for the types of arms used in "civilized warfare." For example, rifles, swords, or large handguns, but not pocket pistols.

The First Circuit, in the 1942 Cases v. United States, said it would not follow Miller any further than Miller's particular holding about the short-barreled shotgun. According to Cases, the Miller rule, if generalized, would protect far too many weapons.

That same year, the Third Circuit in U.S. v. Tot asserted that the Second Amendment "was not adopted with individual rights in mind," and further asserted that the Supreme Court had said so in Miller.

Congress did not think so. Shortly before U.S. entry in World War II, the U.S. Congress passed the Property Requisition Act, to allow the executive branch to seize, with full compensation, property needed for the national defense. Except for firearms. The federal government could not seize or register firearms possessed "by any individual for his personal protection or sport." The government could not "impair or infringe in any manner the right of any individual to keep and bear arms." The committee report explained that right to arms protections were "[i]n view of the fact that certain totalitarian and dictatorial nations are now engaged in the willful and wholesale destruction of personal rights and liberties."

But things changed drastically starting in the late 1960s. First, by then the original public meaning of much of the Bill of Rights and of the Fourteenth Amendment had been long forgotten. Many original source documents were not available in any practical sense to federal or state court libraries. For example, David Young' 1997 book The Origin of Second Amendment would compile hundreds of original documents about the right to arms from 1787 to 1792; as of the 1960s or 1970s, most of these documents existed in archives, and were waiting to be rediscovered by scholars and published in modern anthologies. But not yet.

Beginning in the mid-1960s, surging violent crime rates and race riots all over America terrified the public. In 1963, President Kennedy was assassinated by a communist using a mail-order rifle; then in April 1968 Martin Luther King was assassinated by a racist using a rifle; and then in June Senator Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated by an anti-Jewish Jordanian immigrant using a cheap imported handgun.

Connecticut Senator Thomas Dodd was Chairman of the Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency and in the early 1960s he had been criticizing violence on television and cinema. But under pressure from Hollywood and from advertisers, Dodd had shifted his focus to guns, and in 1968, after the second Kennedy assassination, he was able to pass the Gun Control Act of 1968. As amended, it is still the major federal gun control law.

The Act banned mail-order gun sales, outlawed the import of inexpensive pistols, and imposed many other controls. But it did not fulfill the objectives of President Lyndon Johnson and other advocates for national gun licensing or national gun registration.

Most notably, from the viewpoint of the federal courts, the Act greatly expanded the categories of persons who were forbidden by federal law to possess a firearm or ammunition. Prosecutorial resources for such crimes were also greatly augmented.

Soon, the federal courts were hearing many cases from convicted felons, whose desperate public defenders were arguing that prohibiting a convicted violent felon from possessing a firearm violated the Second Amendment.

In 1973, the Office of Legal Counsel under President Nixon, who personally opposed all firearms ownership by citizens, wrote a formal opinion that there is no personal constitutional right under the Second Amendment to own or use a gun.

The federal judges appointed by Presidents Johnson and Nixon tended to be hostile to gun ownership. So rather than simply upholding bans on gun possession by convicted violent felons, many federal courts denied that there was an individual Second Amendment right. Instead, the right was said to a "collective" right that no individual could exercise. This is similar to "collective property" in communist dictatorships, where property supposedly belongs to all the people, but in fact only to the government.

Or the right was said to a "States' right," although nobody who said so could articulate what this right was. Or why the authors of the U.S. Constitution, who repeatedly used the word "State" or "States" to identify states' rights, had momentarily forgotten when they were writing the Second Amendment, and had written "the People" when they actually meant "the States."

The judicial opinions were typically terse and contemptuous, often consisting of string cites of post-1968 federal cases, all of which traced back to Tot or Cases. The opinions dared not quote the Supreme Court's words in Miller that in the Second Amendment's militia, "ordinarily when called for service these men were expected to appear bearing arms supplied by them­selves and of the kind in common use at the time."

The purpose of the nullificatory lower court opinions was not to describe what the Second Amendment was. Rather, the effort was to declare by ipse dixit that there was no individual right. The effort reached its zenith of absurdity in a 1975 report of the American Bar Association asserting, "It is doubtful that the founding fathers had any intent in mind with regard to [the] meaning of this amendment." Ben R. Miller, The Legal Basis for Firearms Controls, 100 Ann. Rep. A.B.A. 1050, 1052 (1975).

So according to the ABA, the Second Amendment was essentially similar to the Beatles song "I am the Walrus," for which John Lennon wrote nonsense lyrics to get even with people who overanalyzed Beatles songs.

Man, you should have seen them kicking Edgar Allan Poe.

In 1974, two national groups were formed in the District of Columbia to ban handguns, although they later expanded their objectives to include long guns. The National Coalition to Ban Handguns is now known as the Coalition to Stop Gun Violence. The other was the National Council to Control Handguns, which after several name changes is today known as Brady.

The leader of the latter group explained the strategy to The New Yorker in 1976: "The first problem is to slow down the increasing number of handguns being produced and sold in this country. The second problem is to get handguns registered." Finally, make "totally illegal" "the possession of all handguns and all handgun ammunition," except for the government and a few exceptions, such as licensed collectors.

Shortly after Congress granted the District of Columbia home rule, the District Council in 1975 took the initiative and banned handguns and forbade residents from keeping any firearm loaded and assembled for self-defense inside the home.

In 1976, Massachusetts put a handgun confiscation measure on the ballot. Although early polling was favorable, the measure lost, 69% to 31%. It even lost in Boston. Although the Boston police chief was a fervent advocate, the rest of law enforcement, including police unions and police chiefs, was almost unanimously opposed. They pointed out the ban would not affect criminals but would disarm law-abiding citizens, including off-duty police.

After the landslide defeat, an official with the League of Women Voters (which had vigorously supported the ban) said, "I think a lot of voters have that idea that this was designed to get guns away from the criminals. That's not the real purpose."

In the opinion of the gun confiscation movement, the American people are not capable of keeping firearms in their home without eventually harming themselves.

Morton Grove, Illinois, banned handguns in 1981, and during the next several years, Chicago and five more nearby suburbs followed suit. But the reaction across the rest of the country was a wave of state preemption laws. Legislatures decided that cities would not be allowed to outlaw firearms the state itself permitted. Within a few years, the possibility of municipal gun bans had been eliminated in most states.

Prohibitionists tried next in California in 1982, with a ballot initiative for a "handgun freeze." Unlike the Massachusetts measure, the California freeze would allow current owners to keep their handguns.

The California initiative was defeated by a vote of 63% to 37%. Opposition to the freeze brought so many additional voters to the polls that they even carried the Republican to a 1% victory for Governor.

In 1993-94, President Clinton was enthusiastically supporting gun control, and so the confiscation lobbies tried ballot initiatives in three left-leaning cities in Wisconsin, one of the few states without preemption. After confiscation was defeated in Milwaukee, Kenosha, and even in Madison, the backlash led to the enactment of Wisconsin preemption, and then to a state constitution right to arms adopted with 73% voter support.

Nationally, President Clinton worked tirelessly on gun control, passing a major anti-crime bill September 1994 that included a 10-year ban on the sale or manufacture of many semiautomatic firearms and on standard magazines over 10 rounds.

In November 1994, Democrats lost control of the U.S. House for the first time since 1953. President Clinton said, "The NRA is the reason the Republicans control the House." A Conversation with President Clinton, Cleveland Plain Dealer, Jan. 14, 1995, at 11B. Clinton also blamed the NRA for the 1993 defeat of New Jersey Governor James Florio, who had signed a similar ban.

At the state level, the 1994 elections led to tremendous changes in state gun laws. State after state enacted standardized, objective Shall Issue licensing for handgun carry permits; new or enhanced preemption laws to eliminate local gun control; instant checks to replace state-level waiting periods for handgun purchases; range protection bills to prevent noise nuisance suits against shooting ranges; and other right to arms measures.

In 1999, the murders of 12 students and one teacher at Columbine High School south of Denver reinvigorated the gun control movement. Indeed, in many states and in Congress, the immediate aftermath of a mass shooting is the only opportunity for antigun activists to win legislative victories. A few weeks after Columbine, Vice President Al Gore cast the tie-breaking vote in the U.S. Senate for an amendment to a juvenile crime bill that would have given the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives the administrative power to shut down any or all gun shows in the United States.

The 2000 presidential election promised to be the great showdown on gun control. Like the election of 1800 for the First Amendment (when the Sedition Act was being used to silence Republican opposition to the Federalists), the 2000 election would decide the fate of the Second Amendment.

In response to a letter from a concerned citizen, Clinton's Solicitor General Seth Waxman articulated the DOJ's position that "the Second Amendment does not extend an individual right to keep and bear arms." Quoting the citizen's letter, Waxman concurred that the government believes that it "could 'take guns away from the public,' and 'restrict ownership of rifles, pistols and shotguns from all people.'" The NRA put Waxman's "take guns" quote on billboards in swing states.

President Clinton later wrote that the NRA had been the reason that his Vice-President Al Gore lost five swing states in the Southeast, and hence the election.

The Justice Department under President George W. Bush withdrew the old Nixon administration opinion. In its place came an Office of Legal Counsel opinion over 100 pages, affirming the individual right.

Bush won re-election by a close margin in 2004, contrasting his support of gun rights with Massachusetts Senator John Kerry, an ardent gun control advocate. That election would made the difference in the 5-4 decision in District of Columbia v. Heller, decided at the end of President Bush's second term in 2008. President Bush had nominated John Roberts and Samuel Alito; whereas the Democratic shortlist included former Solicitors General Seth Waxman and Walter Dellinger and D.C. Circuit Judges David Tatel and Merrick Garland, all of whom defended handgun bans.

State Constitution ballot measures are the highest possible means by which the people can express their views on what ought to be a right. Since 1978, 17 states by vote of the people have added or strengthened state constitution right-to-arms provisions. (In 2026, Arkansas.) Not a single proposed amendment to fortify the right has ever been defeated at the ballot box.

Starting in the 1970s, pollsters began asking Americans if they believed they had a constitutional right to arms, and large majorities always said yes.

But not the large majority of the scholarly and legal elites, starting in the 1960s. To many of them, the notion that an ordinary American had a right to own a firearm and use it for self-defense was unbearable. The idea could not even be considered.

Those who did dare consider it tended to be practicing lawyers, whose professional livelihood did not depend on the approval of law professors. Starting in the 1970s, Virginia attorney Stephen Halbrook did more than anyone else to found the field of firearms law. His career record in U.S. Supreme Court merits cases is five wins and zero loses, including Printz v. United States in 1997, one of the most important modern federalism decisions, and NRA v. Oak Park, the companion case to McDonald v. Chicago in 2010, making he Second Amendment enforceable against the States via the Fourteenth Amendment.

Author of the Thomson West treatise on Firearms Law, Halbrook has been a prolific author of law journal articles and books, especially on the history of the right to arms.

He has taken full advantage of the fact that, because every law school has at least one law review, law has many more scholarly journals than do most other disciplines, so there is much more room for publication of articles that do not conform to the conventional wisdom of the moment.

Next to Halbrook, the lawyer who did the most in the long term for the restoration of the Second Amendment was the late Don Kates. As a Yale Law Student, Kates had volunteered for the 1964 Freedom Summer, to provide support for rural Black Mississippians to register to vote. In late June, three civil rights workers were murdered.

Kates adopted the longstanding practices of the Black and White civil rights workers who lived in the South fulltime: carrying firearms for protection against the Ku Klux Klan and other violent white supremacists who were tacitly supported by local law enforcement. After graduation, Kates went to work for California Rural Legal Assistance, and was soon named poverty lawyer of the year by the National Legal Aid and Defender Association.

Kates began to specialize in firearms law, and he too became a prolific scholar. In 1983, the Michigan Law Review, including me as a student citechecker, published Kates' article "Handgun Prohibition and the Original Meaning of the Second Amendment."

The Kates article in the Michigan Law Review was the first article about the Second Amendment  that had appeared in what was then a top-5 law review since the early 20th century. Professor William Van Alstyne, who was an eminent constitutional law textbook author and very liberal, later recounted that "this pipsqueak Kates," who was not a law professor, had convinced many of the leading constitutional law professors that the Second Amendment really was an individual right.

But none dared express that idea in print. That is why Sanford Levinson, also a very eminent and very liberal constitutional law professor, titled his 1989 essay in the Yale Law Journal "The Embarrassing Second Amendment." He wrote:

"I cannot help but suspect that the best explanation for the absence of the Second Amendment from the legal consciousness of the elite bar, including that component found in the legal academy, is derived from a mixture of sheer opposition to the idea of private ownership of guns and the perhaps subconscious fear that altogether plausible, perhaps even 'winning,' interpretations of the Second Amendment would present real hurdles to those of us supporting prohibitory regulation."

Reassured by Levinson's grant of respectability and permission, many law scholars began to explore the unknown-to-them legal history of the Second Amendment. The large majority found the Second Amendment to guarantee a normal individual right. Other explorers  agreed that the Second Amendment was meant to be an individual right, but argued that the right no longer existed because conditions had changed; for example, the American people today are no longer virtuous and unified, as they supposedly were in 1791.

Besides Halbrook and Kates, there were many other lawyers and professors who played a role, including Arizona attorney David Hardy since 1974.

Federal courts often move slowly. It took until 2001 for a modern federal circuit panel, the Fifth Circuit in United States v. Emerson, to hold that the Second Amendment is an individual right.

The next Circuit to come around was the D.C. Circuit in 2007, whose decision striking the D.C. handgun ban would be affirmed by the Supreme Court the next year in Heller.

By the time Heller was being briefed in December 2007, even the gun ban groups had given up on the "collective right" theory that had once been hegemonic in elite circles. Only one amicus brief in support of the D.C. ban argued for the collective right theory at length, and it came from President Clinton's former Attorney General Janet Reno and some other former officials.

The Supreme Court in Heller ruled unanimously that the Second Amendment was an individual right. Justice Stevens' dissent expressly rejected the collective right. Instead, he described the Second Amendment as a narrow individual right that could only be asserted by individuals who were upholding a state's interest in its militia. That particular theory had been invented in 1989 by gun control attorney Dennis Henigan, who had studied the issue in enough depth to realize that the negation of an individual Second Amendment right was untenable.

The problem for Henigan and other supporters of the DC handgun ban was that the text of the Second Amendment guaranteed "the right of the People," and not "the right only of people actively serving in the militia." Since the 1990s, scholars had been calling the normal individual right for the general public "the standard model." The standard model was standard because it had been the standard model throughout the first century of the Second Amendment.

The standard model never forbade all gun control. In constitutional litigation over the Second Amendment or other liberties safeguarded by the Bill of Rights, the standard result is that the civil rights advocates and opponents each win sometimes and lose sometimes.

That is where the Second Amendment is today; as in the Early Republic and thereafter, there will always be disputes about what it means for the right to be "infringed." Many gun controls are not even challenged, and when there are challenges, the government usually wins. But not always, because the Second Amendment is today recognized as a meaningful and enforceable constitutional right that safeguards  natural right.

In the United States of America, the Rule of Law includes the government obeying the Constitution; the freedom of speech, including academic speech; free elections; and the leading role of liberty-minded lawyers in public life. All of these combined the rescue a fundamental right from a nadir half a century ago. The revolutionary restoration of the right to arms was accomplished peacefully, thanks to and because of the Rule of Law. This week, let us celebrate the Rule of Law, and let each of us honor it with our conduct every day of the year.

Nobody Said The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Was This Funny

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Though I’ve never played The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time until now, I’ve heard people sing its praises for years. Mostly, this revolved around its impossibly high score of 99 on Metacritic, its influential open world and combat design, and its memorable dungeons. But none of those ardent fans bothered to tell me that this classic game has some genuinely hilarious moments. Because the game was originally released 28 years ago, a spoiler-free experience is pretty much off the table for me. But the goofs and gags? Those are all 100% fresh.

I love a zingy insult or quip as much as anybody else, but some of the best video game humor leans into the unique qualities of the medium. These gags go beyond writing to incorporate at least some element of interaction — or a conspicuous lack thereof. An early example of this in Ocarina of Time comes when you show a message in a bottle to King Zora, which reveals his daughter is currently stranded inside the massive fish Jabu-Jabu. The grateful king moves aside to allow you access to the entrance of the fish’s belly — very, very slowly. No button-mashing through this one. The Iron Age palisade likely blocked an invading army. But who built it—and who were they afraid of?

Archaeologists sifting through portions of Denmark’s western peninsula ahead of a highway expansion keep uncovering remnants of the area’s past, often in small snapshots: a grave here, an axe there, or maybe sometimes even the foundation of an ancient home. They certainly weren’t expecting to find the remains of battle lines in West Jutland, indicating a conflict they didn’t know ever transpired.

“The find came as a complete surprise to us,” Michael Alrø Jensen, curator at Museum Vest, said in a translated statement. “It points to previously unknown conflicts in West Jutland dating back to the earliest part of the Iron Age.”

The find showed up via a 2,500-year-old line of pits, which experts believe fighters created during the Iron Age to block hostile incursions along an ancient West Jutland thoroughfare. The discovery came as archaeologists from the museum examined fields along Route 11, between Varde and Korskro, before the Danish Road Directorate began a highway expansion.

Experts dubbed the battle lines, which were found beneath a field near Schaefergården, a “post-hole alignment,” likely from around 500 B.C.E. The lines feature a long series of closely spaced holes in which one end of pointed wooden posts were buried to form a defensive palisade.

“Our best theory is that there was a fear of an invading army arriving from the north via the ‘Drivvej’ route, and that attempts were therefore made to control and block the passage,” Jensen said.

More evidence may still lie buried at the site. Experts note that the current Route 11 alignment between Varde and Korskro matches the ancient “Drivvej,” or Drove Road, that was the main transportation line through West Jutland for centuries. The modern road project adds an additional lane to the two-lane highway and shows how closely the ancient road—whose origin date remains unknown—matches the modern setup.

Still, the find raises questions. Archaeologists and historians don’t know who developed the defensive structure—or why. The team hopes that additional work in the area may offer fresh clues about the ancient mystery.

Attempts to pinpoint answers to a 2,500-year-old question got a bit muddy as the excavations revealed artifacts from a range of eras in spots that hadn’t been searched previously. Archaeologists now have evidence of human activity along the road from the first Stone Age farmers, who buried their dead in small mounds through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and into the Viking Age (including the discovery of a 4,500-year-old battle axe buried alongside a Stone Age warrior).

“What we are most excited about is that we have found traces from so many different periods within a relatively small area,” Jensen said. “We have suddenly gained a wealth of new pieces that provide much greater insight into how people lived and moved about in this region over several thousand years.”

Every new discovery provides nuance to the historical record, whether the remains of a house, farm, or grave. Museum Vest hopes to learn more by excavating additional burial mounds. The various discoveries in the region together form a narrative of humans’ transformation of the land, first into farming plots and then into villages. “Once our work is complete, we hope to be able to offer the local community a more cohesive narrative about the area they live in,” Jensen said.

After years of third-party Android trackers from companies like Samsung, Motorola, and Pebblebee, Google’s Pixel Tag is now available for purchase. Android phones with a UWB chip can display the exact distance and direction to the Pixel Tag in the Find Hub app once you’re close to it, while Bluetooth Channel Sounding is limited to just distance.

The Pixel Tag is compatible with smartphones running Android 9 or later, but you’ll need devices that specifically include a UWB chip or support Bluetooth 6.0 to take advantage of Find Hub’s precise locating features.

United Airlines wants Washington Dulles to move beyond its mobile lounges. But when I asked Andrew Nocella whether he actually likes them, his response suggested that retirement may not mean disappearance. Could one become a bar or café? During a press gaggle at Washington Dulles yesterday, I asked United Airlines Executive Vice President and Chief Commercial Officer Andrew Nocella a simple question: does he like the people movers? (it was in the context of their future place at an evolving IAD).

“I love it,” he responded, before explaining:

“The people movers, of course, is part of my history. I can remember, back in the day, coming out to the aircraft through the people mover, so it’s part of my history. But I do understand the need to evolve and change and innovate. And the people movers need to be innovated out. There are better ways to do it. But it will be an important part of the history of Dulles Airport and I can assure you that People Moves, while they will be retired, they won’t be forgotten.”

These vehicles are technically known as mobile lounges, though people movers is a fitting description too and I’ve called them “moon buggies” for years. Whatever you call them, I found that last assurance intriguing, even if we should be careful not to read too much into it.

Nocella did not announce a preservation project or describe a future use. But promising they will not be forgotten invites the question: how might Dulles preserve something so closely associated with the airport while finally moving beyond the need to use it?

My favorite possibility is turning one into a bar or café.

Imagine a retired mobile lounge parked in a suitable location, with its distinctive shape preserved and the interior converted into somewhere to have a drink while watching aircraft. Keep some of the original seating,  add photographs explaining its history, and suddenly an airport frustration becomes an attraction.

I would happily stop for a cappuccino. I suspect plenty of aviation enthusiasts would make a point of visiting too.

There would obviously be practical questions about access, utilities, and where to put it…maybe the wheels would have to be removed. A vehicle designed to transport passengers is not automatically suitable for operating a restaurant. But as a concept, it strikes me as a fun way to preserve a piece of Dulles without requiring anyone to ride it to immigration.

Nostalgia becomes much easier to appreciate when you can choose whether to participate!

Think how cool it would be to take one of these and put it inside the new Polaris Lounge in Concourse E that will be coming in the years ahead.

Another possibility is that the mobile lounges disappear from most journeys but continue in a narrower role.

I wonder, for example, whether there might be a useful way to run passengers between the D and E gates during the airport’s lengthy transition. Could a mobile lounge provide a convenient connection while the rest of the concourse development catches up? It woudl certainly save a lot of walking.

That is a question, not something Nocella proposed and I do realize that keeping an aging fleet operating for a limited purpose could undermine the savings and simplicity that retirement is supposed to deliver.

Still, “retired” can mean different things. Retired from routine passenger service would not necessarily preclude an occasional special ride or some other carefully limited use.

Of course, “won’t be forgotten” may mean something much less elaborate.

A preserved vehicle, a display in the terminal, or a design reference inside a future lounge could honor the mobile lounges without retaining their transportation function.

I would prefer preserving at least one actual vehicle rather than relying exclusively on photographs. These machines are unusual enough that future travelers should have an opportunity to see what generations of Dulles passengers experienced. I still love them and will always love them.

Nocella’s answer was affectionate, but clear about the need for change. He did not offer a retirement date or reveal what remembering the mobile lounges will look like.

But I do hope that assurance becomes something tangible. A mobile lounge café or bar would get my vote, preferably inside the new Polaris Lounge. A useful transitional shuttle or occasional heritage ride could be interesting too and much easier to maintain than a vintage aircraft.

For years, passengers have boarded these vehicles because they had to. It would be wonderful if Dulles eventually gave us a reason to step inside one because we wanted to.

The heroes of the DC Universe are naturally more powerful than those of the Marvel Universe, regardless of how hard the Avengers try to keep up with the Justice League. The Justice League and the Avengers are both the ultimate gathering of their respective universes' greatest superheroes, assembled to save the world and the cosmos no matter what it takes. Both superhero teams have also expanded way beyond their original lineups to include characters whose abilities reach extraordinary levels, to the point that they occasionally become threats themselves.

The enormous popularity of the Marvel Cinematic Universe has introduced millions to some of Marvel Comics' most extraordinary power feats, although the movies have occasionally simplified or substantially altered the original stories. Thanos' infamous Snap from The Infinity Gauntlet became a pop culture staple thanks to Avengers: Infinity War, and Scarlet Witch is now famous for her reality-altering abilities thanks to WandaVision and Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness, which have echoed her actions from House of M. Thor has also become synonymous with extraordinary physical strength and divine weaponry, though the God of Thunder has accumulated even bigger feats of power in the Marvel Comics multiverse.

However, Marvel Comics' characters are inherently a few notches below DC's characters in terms of power, all thanks to the very nature of both realities.

In JLA/Avengers #2, when Thor unleashes Mjolnir against Superman, the Man of Steel catches the hammer, leading the God of Thunder to ask how it can be possible. Superman responds that "the dials go up to eleven" in his world and delivers a punch that knocks Thor unconscious. Although the Avengers of the time have Scarlet Witch, Vision, Quasar, Ms Marvel, Jack of Hearts, Hercules, and Thor in their ranks, the Justice League easily overshadow Marvel's mightiest heroes with Superman and Wonder Woman alone, not to mention Martian Manhunter, the Flash, Captain Atom, Aquaman, Green Lantern, and Zatanna.

The DC multiverse also possesses an elaborate hierarchy of increasingly abstract realms, including the Sphere of the Gods, the Monitor Sphere, the Source Wall between realities, and the Overvoid, with entities that exist beyond ordinary space-time and perceive entire universes as comparatively insignificant concepts. Marvel certainly has its own equivalents, including the Living Tribunal, Eternity, the Beyonders, and the One-Above-All, but DC particularly emphasizes the idea of successive planes of existence that transcend everything beneath them. DC takes the concept even further with Darkseid, whose true form exists on a scale so immense that his fall into reality threatens every single timeline across the multiverse.

Individual DC characters have reached absurdly elevated levels. Superman's Prime One Million and King Omega forms make him virtually invincible, and his Cosmic Armor fusion with Ultraman makes him the physical embodiment of superhero storytelling. The Flash has achieved similar feats, like running so fast he singlehandedly reboots the DC multiverse in Crisis on Infinite Earths, or being so fast he's basically everywhere at once in Kingdom Come and Ryan North and Gavin Guidry's 2026 The Flash run. Marvel has certainly produced comparable cosmic spectacles, particularly through the Beyonder, God Emperor Doom, and the Maker's manipulation of the multiverse, but the scale and effort of their actions is noticeably different.

While competition inspires the DC and Marvel Universe to get creative with power levels, focusing too much on them introduces the risk of pushing away what makes each character compelling. For instance, the Flash often claims the title of the fastest speedster in fiction, but in doing so, he also renders every villain and threat inoffensive.
