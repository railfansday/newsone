Germany slips in climate protection rankings as other countries make gains

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Record renewable energy growth is driving climate progress in some countries, but Germany and many major emitters are falling behind.

Germany has slipped down the climate action ranking for the third consecutive year, with the former green leader now being outperformed by Brazil, Columbia and Romania.

In its worst performance since 2019, the country fell to place 25 and received a "medium" rating overall on the yearly Climate Change Performance Index (CCPI), which assesses efforts to curb global warming in 64 countries plus the European Union.

Still, "a number of countries have been making good progress for years," said Jan Burck, CCPI lead author in a statement. Denmark is leading the way across all four categories assessed — energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, renewable energy and national climate policy. A total of 15 states, including Lithuania, Chile, and Morocco, received an overall "high" rating, which is two more than last year.

The European Union also moved up a spot, while Hungary jumped 21 places after the defeat of former prime minister Viktor Orban's government, which had repeatedly vetoed EU climate plans, dismissing them as a "utopian fantasy."

The top three positions on the CCPI remained vacant again this year, as no country has yet done enough to meet global climate goals aimed at preventing worsening weather extremes and climate damage.

"We are seeing a striking contrast," said Burck. "Progress on climate action is often slow and in some countries, such as the United States, things are even moving backwards. But technologically and economically there is enormous momentum."

That momentum is apparent in record solar energy growth. Last year 75% of global electricity demand growth was met by the green energy source, according to energy think tank Ember's latest global electricity review. Investment in clean energy has also outpaced that in fossil fuels for multiple years.

The rapid expansion of renewable technology, as well as the Iran War and a reduction in deforestation are behind a projected slight fall in emissions for the first time since the COVID-19 pandemic, according to the CCPI.

"Governments have to act now to turn the momentum into sustained emissions reductions," said CCPI co-author Niklas Höhne of the NewClimate Institute, which compiles the report with environmental NGO Germanwatch.

Burck attributed Germany's worst ever performance in the category of national climate policy to current policies being pursued by Germany's economy minister Katherina Reiche.

"Measures that primarily aim to increase the use of natural gas, threaten to undermine progress in the energy transition and risk significantly worsening the conditions for renewable energy and electrification for many years to come," said Burck.

A European Commission report published on November 9 on the sidelines of the UN COP31 climate summit in Turkey said Germany was one of nine EU countries set to miss 2030 emissions reductions targets.

Germany is not alone in its disappointing ranking. The other G20 countries — the world's leading industrialized and emerging economies, responsible for around 80% of global CO2 emissions — also perform poorly.

The only exception among them is the United Kingdom, which ranks seventh and received a high rating, performing well in greenhouse gas emissions, energy use and climate policy. However, study authors rated it low for renewable energy expansion.

The G20's oil- and gas-producing countries occupy some of the lowest positions on the climate action table. Iran, the United States, Russia, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Canada all languish at the bottom of the league, with an overall rating of "very low."

The hosts of this year's COP31, also received a "low" overall score. Australia, a major coal exporter with some of the world's highest per-capita emissions, ranks 54th, while Turkey, which is also heavily dependent on fossil fuels, comes in at 53rd.

China is the world's largest emitter of greenhouse gases and ranks 56th in the CCPI, receiving an overall rating of "very low." Its main weaknesses include its now high per-capita emissions and substantial energy consumption.

However, among the G20, China leads the way in renewable energy expansion. The country, which is five years ahead of schedule in reaching its goal of adding 1,200 GW of wind and solar capacity to the grid by 2030, also produces over 80% of the world's solar panels.

Several oil-producing countries, including Kazakhstan, Saudi Arabia, Uzbekistan and the United Arab Emirates, are also performing better than expected in the renewables category and "are significantly expanding their previously limited" capacity, said the report.

The authors said these countries have significant potential to further expand renewables.

"In the Arab states in particular, very high per-capita emissions and an above-average level of electrification coincide with a relatively low existing share of renewable energy and enormous solar potential," said co-author Natalie Pelekh in a statement. "These are, in fact, ideal conditions for more ambitious climate action."

When England head coach Thomas Tuchel decided to pick Leeds United goalkeeper James Trafford to face World Cup holders Spain at Wembley in the last international break, it sparked the first stirrings about preparations for life without long-time first choice Jordan Pickford.

Tuchel made it clear Pickford was still England's number one pick, but the simple fact he decided to alternate keepers - playing Trafford in the 3-2 loss to Spain then victory against Czech Republic, before Everton's 32-year-old returned for the Wembley win against the Czechs and the 7-0 rout in Croatia - created intrigue and debate.

England meet Croatia at Wembley on Thursday before facing the biggest test of their Uefa Nations League campaign when the play Spain in Madrid on Sunday.

Tuchel maintains strong faith in Pickford, an outstanding performer for England, but Trafford has at least had the chance to stake his claim.

Tuchel has suggested he may use a rotation system for the pair once more in the upcoming internationals, which will only increase scrutiny on England's goalkeeping position.

Pickford's record is outstanding since he made his debut in a 0-0 draw against Germany at Wembley in 2017.

In his time as first choice, England have once more emerged as major players on global stage, reaching the World Cup semi-finals in 2018 and 2026, as well as successive Euros finals at the delayed 2020 tournament and Euro 2024.

Measured by Opta stats, Pickford has made only two errors leading to goals in 93 appearances, with 46 clean sheets and a 74% save ratio.

Robinson told BBC Sport: "It obviously remains to be seen what Thomas Tuchel does in these two England games, but I could see exactly what he was doing by giving James Trafford game time in the last round of matches.

The Peaceful Second Amendment Revolution

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

How the right was restored from its nadir half a century ago

This speech was delivered during Wyoming Law Week at the University of Wyoming College of Law, October 13, 2026.

Ladies and gentlemen, cowboys and cowgirls, good evening.

The title of my presentation, "The Peaceful Second Amendment Revolution," is about a revolution in the original meaning of that word: a return to the original starting point, as when the earth completes a revolution around the sun.

The American Revolution began in that conservative sense. The Americans wanted the British to respect all their pre-existing rights, including the written right to arms from the 1689 English Bill of Rights.

Law Week is an appropriate time to reflect on the rule of law and the revolutionary role of lawyers, then and now.

Writing in 1789, after victory in the American Revolution, physician David Ramsay explained one reason for the Revolution: "The study of law was common and fashion­able." David Ramsay, 1 A History of the American Revolution 29 (Lester H. Cohen ed., Liberty Fund, 1990) (1789). According to Ramsay:

"No order of men has, in all ages, been more favourable to liberty, than lawyers. When they are not won over to the service of government, they are formidable adversaries to it. Professionally taught the rights of human nature, they keenly and quickly perceive every attack made upon them. While others judge of bad principles by the actual grievances they occasion, lawyers discover them at a distance, and trace future mischiefs from gilded innovations."

‍Ramsay pointed out that half of the First Continental Congress, in 1774, were lawyers.

"Bred in the habits of public speaking, they made a distinguished figure in the meetings of the people, and were particularly able to explain to them the tendency of the late acts of parlia­ment. Exerting their abilities and influence in the cause of their country, they were rewarded with its confidence."

We ought always feel gratitude to those lawyers, and other Americans, who defended and advanced natural rights during the American Revolution. Tonight is the story of how one of the rights which those lawyers and others had guaranteed in our Constitution was nearly obliterated during the last third of the twentieth century. And how the peaceful revolution that has restored the Second Amendment was the cooperative work of millions of Americans, especially lawyers, and among them, especially legal scholars.

Although the story does involve the recovery of the original meaning of Second Amendment, the story is also about living constitutionalism; the most important reason the right to keep and bear arms survived against the attempts to destroy it was that the right lives and thrives in the hearts and minds of the American people.

The U.S. Supreme Court decisions of District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008 and McDonald v. Chicago in 2010 were the opposite of cutting edge. Those decisions were lagging indicators. They were the long-term consequence of elections, of a generation of historical scholarship, and of the refusal of millions of ordinary people to accept that a right they had always exercised and cherished could be defined out of existence by unreasoned pronouncements.

During the nineteenth century, virtually all commentators and courts treated the right to keep and bear arms as a broad individual right, including for people not in the militia.

The most influential legal treatise of the early republic, St. George Tucker's American edition of Blackstone, described the Amendment as securing the rights of self-defense and resistance to tyranny.

Next came William Rawle's 1825 A View of the Constitution of the United States of America, which said that if either Congress or a state government, in "blind pursuit of inor­dinate power" should "attempt" "to disarm the people," "this amendment may be appealed to as a restraint on both."

The great Supreme Court Justice and prolific legal scholar Joseph Story wrote in 1840 that

"The right of the citizens to keep and bear arms has justly been considered, as the palladium of the liberties of a republic; since it offers a strong moral check against the usurpation and arbitrary power of rulers; and it will generally, even if these are successful in the first instance, enable the people to resist and triumph over them."

The preeminent constitutional writer of the latter 19th century was Michigan Supreme Court Chief Justice and Michigan Law School Dean Thomas Cooley. In 1880 he explained that "The right is general." "[I]f the right were limited to those enrolled," in the militia, "the purpose of this guaranty might be defeated altogether by the action or neglect to act of the government it was meant to hold in check."

Congress agreed. When the former slave states attempted to keep the freedmen in de facto servitude by enacting Black Codes that forbade the freedmen to possess arms, or required them to obtain a license, the Reconstruction Congress in 1866 overrode President Andrew Johnson's veto and passed the Second Freedmen's Bureau Bill, guaranteeing the "consti­tutional right to bear arms" as among the "immunities and rights" of Americans. The Civil Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment were passed with a similar and oft-expressed intent.

The theory that the right to arms was actually not a right made its first substantial appearance in a 1905 Kansas Supreme Court decision, Salina v. Blaksley. The case involved a municipal ordinance against carrying a firearm while intoxicated; the ordinance was easy to uphold based on precedents from other states. But the Kansas court aimed to use the case to destroy the right. Section 4 of the 1859 Kansas Bill of Rights declared "that the people have the right to bear arms for their defense and security." Yet the Kansas court claimed that this provision, in the state constitution Bill of Rights, merely affirmed the inherent power to state government to conduct military activities. The Kansas court also claimed that the federal Second Amendment had the same, empty meaning.

The Kansas court could not cite a single authority for its radical propositions. For example, a Massachusetts case had said that the state constitution right to arms is not violated by requiring a permit for large armed parades. The Kansas court pretended that the Massachusetts court had declared that there is no right to arms.

Based on how the U.S. Supreme Court had misinterpreted the Fourteenth Amendment in The Slaughterhouse Cases and U.S. v. Cruikshank in the 1870s, the Second Amendment, like most of the rest of the Bill of Rights, was not considered applicable to the States in the latter 19th and 20th centuries. State courts varied in how strictly they enforced their state constitution right to arms.

In 1934, Congress passed the first major federal gun control law, the National Firearms Act, which required a hefty $200 tax and registration for a small subset of arms, including machine guns and short-barreled shotguns. The Supreme Court said in U.S. v. Miller that the short-barreled shotgun owned by a bootlegger had not been shown to be the sort of arm useful to a militia, and it upheld the National Firearms Act on that narrow ground. Notably, the Court was very interested in Miller's weapon, and did not even mention that Miller while bootlegging was not actively serving in the Arkansas militia.

The Supreme Court's decision was consistent with the most restrictive line of state constitution cases from the 19th century, all of which came from States of the Confederacy: every individual citizen had the right to arms, but only for the types of arms used in "civilized warfare." For example, rifles, swords, or large handguns, but not pocket pistols.

The First Circuit, in the 1942 Cases v. United States, said it would not follow Miller any further than Miller's particular holding about the short-barreled shotgun. According to Cases, the Miller rule, if generalized, would protect far too many weapons.

That same year, the Third Circuit in U.S. v. Tot asserted that the Second Amendment "was not adopted with individual rights in mind," and further asserted that the Supreme Court had said so in Miller.

Congress did not think so. Shortly before U.S. entry in World War II, the U.S. Congress passed the Property Requisition Act, to allow the executive branch to seize, with full compensation, property needed for the national defense. Except for firearms. The federal government could not seize or register firearms possessed "by any individual for his personal protection or sport." The government could not "impair or infringe in any manner the right of any individual to keep and bear arms." The committee report explained that right to arms protections were "[i]n view of the fact that certain totalitarian and dictatorial nations are now engaged in the willful and wholesale destruction of personal rights and liberties."

But things changed drastically starting in the late 1960s. First, by then the original public meaning of much of the Bill of Rights and of the Fourteenth Amendment had been long forgotten. Many original source documents were not available in any practical sense to federal or state court libraries. For example, David Young' 1997 book The Origin of Second Amendment would compile hundreds of original documents about the right to arms from 1787 to 1792; as of the 1960s or 1970s, most of these documents existed in archives, and were waiting to be rediscovered by scholars and published in modern anthologies. But not yet.

Beginning in the mid-1960s, surging violent crime rates and race riots all over America terrified the public. In 1963, President Kennedy was assassinated by a communist using a mail-order rifle; then in April 1968 Martin Luther King was assassinated by a racist using a rifle; and then in June Senator Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated by an anti-Jewish Jordanian immigrant using a cheap imported handgun.

Connecticut Senator Thomas Dodd was Chairman of the Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency and in the early 1960s he had been criticizing violence on television and cinema. But under pressure from Hollywood and from advertisers, Dodd had shifted his focus to guns, and in 1968, after the second Kennedy assassination, he was able to pass the Gun Control Act of 1968. As amended, it is still the major federal gun control law.

The Act banned mail-order gun sales, outlawed the import of inexpensive pistols, and imposed many other controls. But it did not fulfill the objectives of President Lyndon Johnson and other advocates for national gun licensing or national gun registration.

Most notably, from the viewpoint of the federal courts, the Act greatly expanded the categories of persons who were forbidden by federal law to possess a firearm or ammunition. Prosecutorial resources for such crimes were also greatly augmented.

Soon, the federal courts were hearing many cases from convicted felons, whose desperate public defenders were arguing that prohibiting a convicted violent felon from possessing a firearm violated the Second Amendment.

In 1973, the Office of Legal Counsel under President Nixon, who personally opposed all firearms ownership by citizens, wrote a formal opinion that there is no personal constitutional right under the Second Amendment to own or use a gun.

The federal judges appointed by Presidents Johnson and Nixon tended to be hostile to gun ownership. So rather than simply upholding bans on gun possession by convicted violent felons, many federal courts denied that there was an individual Second Amendment right. Instead, the right was said to a "collective" right that no individual could exercise. This is similar to "collective property" in communist dictatorships, where property supposedly belongs to all the people, but in fact only to the government.

Or the right was said to a "States' right," although nobody who said so could articulate what this right was. Or why the authors of the U.S. Constitution, who repeatedly used the word "State" or "States" to identify states' rights, had momentarily forgotten when they were writing the Second Amendment, and had written "the People" when they actually meant "the States."

The judicial opinions were typically terse and contemptuous, often consisting of string cites of post-1968 federal cases, all of which traced back to Tot or Cases. The opinions dared not quote the Supreme Court's words in Miller that in the Second Amendment's militia, "ordinarily when called for service these men were expected to appear bearing arms supplied by them­selves and of the kind in common use at the time."

The purpose of the nullificatory lower court opinions was not to describe what the Second Amendment was. Rather, the effort was to declare by ipse dixit that there was no individual right. The effort reached its zenith of absurdity in a 1975 report of the American Bar Association asserting, "It is doubtful that the founding fathers had any intent in mind with regard to [the] meaning of this amendment." Ben R. Miller, The Legal Basis for Firearms Controls, 100 Ann. Rep. A.B.A. 1050, 1052 (1975).

So according to the ABA, the Second Amendment was essentially similar to the Beatles song "I am the Walrus," for which John Lennon wrote nonsense lyrics to get even with people who overanalyzed Beatles songs.

Man, you should have seen them kicking Edgar Allan Poe.

In 1974, two national groups were formed in the District of Columbia to ban handguns, although they later expanded their objectives to include long guns. The National Coalition to Ban Handguns is now known as the Coalition to Stop Gun Violence. The other was the National Council to Control Handguns, which after several name changes is today known as Brady.

The leader of the latter group explained the strategy to The New Yorker in 1976: "The first problem is to slow down the increasing number of handguns being produced and sold in this country. The second problem is to get handguns registered." Finally, make "totally illegal" "the possession of all handguns and all handgun ammunition," except for the government and a few exceptions, such as licensed collectors.

Shortly after Congress granted the District of Columbia home rule, the District Council in 1975 took the initiative and banned handguns and forbade residents from keeping any firearm loaded and assembled for self-defense inside the home.

In 1976, Massachusetts put a handgun confiscation measure on the ballot. Although early polling was favorable, the measure lost, 69% to 31%. It even lost in Boston. Although the Boston police chief was a fervent advocate, the rest of law enforcement, including police unions and police chiefs, was almost unanimously opposed. They pointed out the ban would not affect criminals but would disarm law-abiding citizens, including off-duty police.

After the landslide defeat, an official with the League of Women Voters (which had vigorously supported the ban) said, "I think a lot of voters have that idea that this was designed to get guns away from the criminals. That's not the real purpose."

In the opinion of the gun confiscation movement, the American people are not capable of keeping firearms in their home without eventually harming themselves.

Morton Grove, Illinois, banned handguns in 1981, and during the next several years, Chicago and five more nearby suburbs followed suit. But the reaction across the rest of the country was a wave of state preemption laws. Legislatures decided that cities would not be allowed to outlaw firearms the state itself permitted. Within a few years, the possibility of municipal gun bans had been eliminated in most states.

Prohibitionists tried next in California in 1982, with a ballot initiative for a "handgun freeze." Unlike the Massachusetts measure, the California freeze would allow current owners to keep their handguns.

The California initiative was defeated by a vote of 63% to 37%. Opposition to the freeze brought so many additional voters to the polls that they even carried the Republican to a 1% victory for Governor.

In 1993-94, President Clinton was enthusiastically supporting gun control, and so the confiscation lobbies tried ballot initiatives in three left-leaning cities in Wisconsin, one of the few states without preemption. After confiscation was defeated in Milwaukee, Kenosha, and even in Madison, the backlash led to the enactment of Wisconsin preemption, and then to a state constitution right to arms adopted with 73% voter support.

Nationally, President Clinton worked tirelessly on gun control, passing a major anti-crime bill September 1994 that included a 10-year ban on the sale or manufacture of many semiautomatic firearms and on standard magazines over 10 rounds.

In November 1994, Democrats lost control of the U.S. House for the first time since 1953. President Clinton said, "The NRA is the reason the Republicans control the House." A Conversation with President Clinton, Cleveland Plain Dealer, Jan. 14, 1995, at 11B. Clinton also blamed the NRA for the 1993 defeat of New Jersey Governor James Florio, who had signed a similar ban.

At the state level, the 1994 elections led to tremendous changes in state gun laws. State after state enacted standardized, objective Shall Issue licensing for handgun carry permits; new or enhanced preemption laws to eliminate local gun control; instant checks to replace state-level waiting periods for handgun purchases; range protection bills to prevent noise nuisance suits against shooting ranges; and other right to arms measures.

In 1999, the murders of 12 students and one teacher at Columbine High School south of Denver reinvigorated the gun control movement. Indeed, in many states and in Congress, the immediate aftermath of a mass shooting is the only opportunity for antigun activists to win legislative victories. A few weeks after Columbine, Vice President Al Gore cast the tie-breaking vote in the U.S. Senate for an amendment to a juvenile crime bill that would have given the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives the administrative power to shut down any or all gun shows in the United States.

The 2000 presidential election promised to be the great showdown on gun control. Like the election of 1800 for the First Amendment (when the Sedition Act was being used to silence Republican opposition to the Federalists), the 2000 election would decide the fate of the Second Amendment.

In response to a letter from a concerned citizen, Clinton's Solicitor General Seth Waxman articulated the DOJ's position that "the Second Amendment does not extend an individual right to keep and bear arms." Quoting the citizen's letter, Waxman concurred that the government believes that it "could 'take guns away from the public,' and 'restrict ownership of rifles, pistols and shotguns from all people.'" The NRA put Waxman's "take guns" quote on billboards in swing states.

President Clinton later wrote that the NRA had been the reason that his Vice-President Al Gore lost five swing states in the Southeast, and hence the election.

The Justice Department under President George W. Bush withdrew the old Nixon administration opinion. In its place came an Office of Legal Counsel opinion over 100 pages, affirming the individual right.

Bush won re-election by a close margin in 2004, contrasting his support of gun rights with Massachusetts Senator John Kerry, an ardent gun control advocate. That election would made the difference in the 5-4 decision in District of Columbia v. Heller, decided at the end of President Bush's second term in 2008. President Bush had nominated John Roberts and Samuel Alito; whereas the Democratic shortlist included former Solicitors General Seth Waxman and Walter Dellinger and D.C. Circuit Judges David Tatel and Merrick Garland, all of whom defended handgun bans.

State Constitution ballot measures are the highest possible means by which the people can express their views on what ought to be a right. Since 1978, 17 states by vote of the people have added or strengthened state constitution right-to-arms provisions. (In 2026, Arkansas.) Not a single proposed amendment to fortify the right has ever been defeated at the ballot box.

Starting in the 1970s, pollsters began asking Americans if they believed they had a constitutional right to arms, and large majorities always said yes.

But not the large majority of the scholarly and legal elites, starting in the 1960s. To many of them, the notion that an ordinary American had a right to own a firearm and use it for self-defense was unbearable. The idea could not even be considered.

Those who did dare consider it tended to be practicing lawyers, whose professional livelihood did not depend on the approval of law professors. Starting in the 1970s, Virginia attorney Stephen Halbrook did more than anyone else to found the field of firearms law. His career record in U.S. Supreme Court merits cases is five wins and zero loses, including Printz v. United States in 1997, one of the most important modern federalism decisions, and NRA v. Oak Park, the companion case to McDonald v. Chicago in 2010, making he Second Amendment enforceable against the States via the Fourteenth Amendment.

Author of the Thomson West treatise on Firearms Law, Halbrook has been a prolific author of law journal articles and books, especially on the history of the right to arms.

He has taken full advantage of the fact that, because every law school has at least one law review, law has many more scholarly journals than do most other disciplines, so there is much more room for publication of articles that do not conform to the conventional wisdom of the moment.

Next to Halbrook, the lawyer who did the most in the long term for the restoration of the Second Amendment was the late Don Kates. As a Yale Law Student, Kates had volunteered for the 1964 Freedom Summer, to provide support for rural Black Mississippians to register to vote. In late June, three civil rights workers were murdered.

Kates adopted the longstanding practices of the Black and White civil rights workers who lived in the South fulltime: carrying firearms for protection against the Ku Klux Klan and other violent white supremacists who were tacitly supported by local law enforcement. After graduation, Kates went to work for California Rural Legal Assistance, and was soon named poverty lawyer of the year by the National Legal Aid and Defender Association.

Kates began to specialize in firearms law, and he too became a prolific scholar. In 1983, the Michigan Law Review, including me as a student citechecker, published Kates' article "Handgun Prohibition and the Original Meaning of the Second Amendment."

The Kates article in the Michigan Law Review was the first article about the Second Amendment  that had appeared in what was then a top-5 law review since the early 20th century. Professor William Van Alstyne, who was an eminent constitutional law textbook author and very liberal, later recounted that "this pipsqueak Kates," who was not a law professor, had convinced many of the leading constitutional law professors that the Second Amendment really was an individual right.

But none dared express that idea in print. That is why Sanford Levinson, also a very eminent and very liberal constitutional law professor, titled his 1989 essay in the Yale Law Journal "The Embarrassing Second Amendment." He wrote:

"I cannot help but suspect that the best explanation for the absence of the Second Amendment from the legal consciousness of the elite bar, including that component found in the legal academy, is derived from a mixture of sheer opposition to the idea of private ownership of guns and the perhaps subconscious fear that altogether plausible, perhaps even 'winning,' interpretations of the Second Amendment would present real hurdles to those of us supporting prohibitory regulation."

Reassured by Levinson's grant of respectability and permission, many law scholars began to explore the unknown-to-them legal history of the Second Amendment. The large majority found the Second Amendment to guarantee a normal individual right. Other explorers  agreed that the Second Amendment was meant to be an individual right, but argued that the right no longer existed because conditions had changed; for example, the American people today are no longer virtuous and unified, as they supposedly were in 1791.

Besides Halbrook and Kates, there were many other lawyers and professors who played a role, including Arizona attorney David Hardy since 1974.

Federal courts often move slowly. It took until 2001 for a modern federal circuit panel, the Fifth Circuit in United States v. Emerson, to hold that the Second Amendment is an individual right.

The next Circuit to come around was the D.C. Circuit in 2007, whose decision striking the D.C. handgun ban would be affirmed by the Supreme Court the next year in Heller.

By the time Heller was being briefed in December 2007, even the gun ban groups had given up on the "collective right" theory that had once been hegemonic in elite circles. Only one amicus brief in support of the D.C. ban argued for the collective right theory at length, and it came from President Clinton's former Attorney General Janet Reno and some other former officials.

The Supreme Court in Heller ruled unanimously that the Second Amendment was an individual right. Justice Stevens' dissent expressly rejected the collective right. Instead, he described the Second Amendment as a narrow individual right that could only be asserted by individuals who were upholding a state's interest in its militia. That particular theory had been invented in 1989 by gun control attorney Dennis Henigan, who had studied the issue in enough depth to realize that the negation of an individual Second Amendment right was untenable.

The problem for Henigan and other supporters of the DC handgun ban was that the text of the Second Amendment guaranteed "the right of the People," and not "the right only of people actively serving in the militia." Since the 1990s, scholars had been calling the normal individual right for the general public "the standard model." The standard model was standard because it had been the standard model throughout the first century of the Second Amendment.

The standard model never forbade all gun control. In constitutional litigation over the Second Amendment or other liberties safeguarded by the Bill of Rights, the standard result is that the civil rights advocates and opponents each win sometimes and lose sometimes.

That is where the Second Amendment is today; as in the Early Republic and thereafter, there will always be disputes about what it means for the right to be "infringed." Many gun controls are not even challenged, and when there are challenges, the government usually wins. But not always, because the Second Amendment is today recognized as a meaningful and enforceable constitutional right that safeguards  natural right.

In the United States of America, the Rule of Law includes the government obeying the Constitution; the freedom of speech, including academic speech; free elections; and the leading role of liberty-minded lawyers in public life. All of these combined the rescue a fundamental right from a nadir half a century ago. The revolutionary restoration of the right to arms was accomplished peacefully, thanks to and because of the Rule of Law. This week, let us celebrate the Rule of Law, and let each of us honor it with our conduct every day of the year.

Nobody Said The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Was This Funny

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Though I’ve never played The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time until now, I’ve heard people sing its praises for years. Mostly, this revolved around its impossibly high score of 99 on Metacritic, its influential open world and combat design, and its memorable dungeons. But none of those ardent fans bothered to tell me that this classic game has some genuinely hilarious moments. Because the game was originally released 28 years ago, a spoiler-free experience is pretty much off the table for me. But the goofs and gags? Those are all 100% fresh.

I love a zingy insult or quip as much as anybody else, but some of the best video game humor leans into the unique qualities of the medium. These gags go beyond writing to incorporate at least some element of interaction — or a conspicuous lack thereof. An early example of this in Ocarina of Time comes when you show a message in a bottle to King Zora, which reveals his daughter is currently stranded inside the massive fish Jabu-Jabu. The grateful king moves aside to allow you access to the entrance of the fish’s belly — very, very slowly. No button-mashing through this one. Just like you would for a real-life monarch (or older person), you must patiently wait for the scooting to be done. With each incremental booty-shuffle, he makes a little noise that sounds like “moo–ee.”

Once you venture inside the giant fish, Princess Ruto must be carried everywhere by Link, making it impossible for you to use your sword or shield while holding her on your shoulders and moving from room to room. Thankfully, you can use Ruto as a literal dead weight to press down switches to open doors. Better still, you can toss her at enemies like a rock, and she won’t take any damage. (She will complain about it, though.)

Later in the game, you‘re tasked with rescuing nine Gorons from the Fire Temple. Some of them will give you a small morsel of information about how to proceed through the dungeon. Archaeologists Found 2,500-Year-Old Battle Lines From a Conflict No One Knew Ever Happened

The Iron Age palisade likely blocked an invading army. But who built it—and who were they afraid of?

Archaeologists sifting through portions of Denmark’s western peninsula ahead of a highway expansion keep uncovering remnants of the area’s past, often in small snapshots: a grave here, an axe there, or maybe sometimes even the foundation of an ancient home. They certainly weren’t expecting to find the remains of battle lines in West Jutland, indicating a conflict they didn’t know ever transpired.

“The find came as a complete surprise to us,” Michael Alrø Jensen, curator at Museum Vest, said in a translated statement. “It points to previously unknown conflicts in West Jutland dating back to the earliest part of the Iron Age.”

The find showed up via a 2,500-year-old line of pits, which experts believe fighters created during the Iron Age to block hostile incursions along an ancient West Jutland thoroughfare. The discovery came as archaeologists from the museum examined fields along Route 11, between Varde and Korskro, before the Danish Road Directorate began a highway expansion.

Experts dubbed the battle lines, which were found beneath a field near Schaefergården, a “post-hole alignment,” likely from around 500 B.C.E. The lines feature a long series of closely spaced holes in which one end of pointed wooden posts were buried to form a defensive palisade.

“Our best theory is that there was a fear of an invading army arriving from the north via the ‘Drivvej’ route, and that attempts were therefore made to control and block the passage,” Jensen said.

More evidence may still lie buried at the site. Experts note that the current Route 11 alignment between Varde and Korskro matches the ancient “Drivvej,” or Drove Road, that was the main transportation line through West Jutland for centuries. The modern road project adds an additional lane to the two-lane highway and shows how closely the ancient road—whose origin date remains unknown—matches the modern setup.

Still, the find raises questions. Archaeologists and historians don’t know who developed the defensive structure—or why. The team hopes that additional work in the area may offer fresh clues about the ancient mystery.

Attempts to pinpoint answers to a 2,500-year-old question got a bit muddy as the excavations revealed artifacts from a range of eras in spots that hadn’t been searched previously. Archaeologists now have evidence of human activity along the road from the first Stone Age farmers, who buried their dead in small mounds through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and into the Viking Age (including the discovery of a 4,500-year-old battle axe buried alongside a Stone Age warrior).

“What we are most excited about is that we have found traces from so many different periods within a relatively small area,” Jensen said. “We have suddenly gained a wealth of new pieces that provide much greater insight into how people lived and moved about in this region over several thousand years.”

Every new discovery provides nuance to the historical record, whether the remains of a house, farm, or grave. Museum Vest hopes to learn more by excavating additional burial mounds. The various discoveries in the region together form a narrative of humans’ transformation of the land, first into farming plots and then into villages. “Once our work is complete, we hope to be able to offer the local community a more cohesive narrative about the area they live in,” Jensen said.

Crystals reveal what pushed seven very different volcanoes to erupt

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Crystals from seven Andean volcanoes share one chemical fingerprint, tying each volcano eruption trigger to a pulse of hot magma from below.

A volcano that erupts once and goes quiet forever seems like a different animal from one that has blown up again and again.

A study of seven Andean volcanoes suggests that, despite their different histories, their eruptions shared a trigger.

The last crystals to grow before each eruption carry the same chemical fingerprint, and it points to a pulse of hotter magma pushing up from below.

Daniela Parra-Encalada, a doctoral student in the School of the Environment at the University of Queensland, read that fingerprint out of plagioclase.

That’s the common white mineral that makes up most of the crystals in volcanic rock.

Parra-Encalada worked with senior author Teresa Ubide and colleagues from Australia, the Universidad de Chile, Spain and Argentina.

Together they collected lava and ash from seven volcanoes along 143 miles (230 kilometers) of the Central Andes.

The work rests on 28 rock samples. Whether the same signal could ever help forecast an eruption is a question the team has only begun to test.

“Plagioclase grows in magma only a few kilometers below the surface, reacting to changes in temperature, water content and magma composition,” Parra-Encalada said.

One of those reactions is a swap of calcium for sodium, so each crystal keeps a record of the melt it grew from.

Two kinds of crystal turned up in every rock the team cut open. Large ones, up to about a twelfth of an inch (2 millimeters) long, grew slowly while the magma was stored underground.

Tiny ones, shorter than about a hundredth of an inch (300 micrometers), grew during the final rise to the surface.

The tiny crystals are the only ones that formed entirely from the magma that erupted.

Across all seven volcanoes, they held nearly the same calcium-to-sodium balance, and the outer rims of the large crystals matched them.

The team named that narrow band the plagioclase eruptibility field. The volcanoes couldn’t have been much more different.

Lascar, the most active volcano in the region, has neighbors that erupted only once, some as long as 3 million years ago.

Some lie on the main volcanic arc and others far behind it, where the crust is half as thick.

In an interview with Earth.com, Parra-Encalada was asked what surprised her most about the match.

“What surprised us most was discovering that this similarity was related to the textures and shapes of plagioclase crystals,” she said.

The cores of the large crystals told a different story. About half of all the plagioclase in these rocks didn’t grow from the erupted magma at all.

Its chemistry points to a silica-rich melt that cooled and crystallized for a long time a few miles down. Along the way it soaked up material from the surrounding crust.

Volcanologists call that kind of sluggish, crystal-packed body a mush.

In the Andean rocks, pieces of it arrived as clusters of interlocking crystals and as chunks of fully solidified rock, pulled loose and carried up when the eruption came.

The trapped crystals share their chemistry with crystals in the region’s ancient super-eruption deposits, which Parra-Encalada told Earth.com was one of the two findings that excited her most.

The same stalled magma systems may also matter for how copper and gold collect underground, she said.

The team is now comparing Andean crystals with those from large ore deposits.

To work out what the fingerprint means, the researchers ran computer simulations of magma cooling and crystallizing at different depths.

The trapped crystals formed at about 1,380°F to 1,560°F (750°C to 850°C), within 7 miles (11 kilometers) of the surface.

The eruptibility-field crystals formed hotter, at roughly 1,650°F to 1,790°F (900°C to 975°C). Their magma had just been reheated by a fresh injection from deeper down.

The fresh injection also carried more dissolved water. As the mixture rose, the water began bubbling out and building pressure in the shallow magma chamber.

Strontium isotopes told the same story: crystal cores carried the signature of melt that had digested old crust, while the rims matched the fresh carrier magma.

The team’s reading is that a volcano on this arc needs a shove from below to erupt, and the shove is what the newest crystals record.

Parra-Encalada then pulled plagioclase data from a public geochemistry database covering 26 volcanic arcs.

The average crystal composition worldwide fell inside the Andean eruptibility field. So did the averages for the Cascades and for Kamchatka.

“And this was not just in the Andes!” she said.

In the Aleutians and in Indonesia, the crust changes from continental to oceanic. There too, the crystal rims fell in the field while the cores scattered.

The result also speaks to an old puzzle. Lavas with intermediate silica levels commonly erupt along these arcs, yet the silica-rich and silica-poor magmas that mix to produce them rarely reach the surface on their own.

The crystals suggest the crust works as a filter: the thick, silica-rich mush and the dense, iron-rich mush both stay trapped, and only the reheated blend in between gets out.

Two of the samples came from an ancient deposit beneath Lascar. It was left by one of the caldera-forming explosive eruptions that buried the region about 4 million years ago.

Those crystals showed almost none of the reheating signature. In a chemical map of an entire rock slice, 97% of the crystals had grown steadily without disturbance.

So the shove that sets off an ordinary Andean eruption may not be what sets off the giant ones.

The authors suggest those huge silica-rich reservoirs can destabilize and empty on their own once enough magma has piled in.

Using crystal chemistry to identify volcanoes more likely to erupt remains an untested idea.

The idea, Parra-Encalada said, is that a volcano whose large crystals also fall near the eruptibility field may hold magma already close to erupting conditions.

Some crystal interiors at Lascar do. Lascar last erupted in the year 2022, two weeks after the team finished fieldwork there.

“If we observe a similar pattern in other polygenetic volcanoes, where most plagioclase crystals have compositions within the eruptibility field, it could indicate a volcanic system that deserves closer attention,” Parra-Encalada noted.

Polygenetic means a volcano that erupts more than once.

Nothing in this study links the fingerprint to how often a volcano erupts or how soon, and the team’s next paper takes up that question.

The samples also come from one slice of one continental arc. The global comparison relies on other researchers’ data, most of it without the crystal-by-crystal sorting this study depended on.

The team is expanding its work to Tonga to test whether the pattern also holds in an oceanic setting.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.

On a gray winter day in Paris three years ago, art historian Maria Cristina Terzaghi walked into the back room of a Parisian gallery and came face-to-face with a painting that had been missing for nearly two centuries. The picture was covered in grime, but she recognized it right away: Young Man with a Carafe of Roses, a work believed to have been painted by Caravaggio in the 1590s.

Terzaghi, a professor at Roma Tre University and one of the world’s leading Caravaggio scholars, has now offered her most detailed account yet of the discovery. Writing in La Repubblica on Sunday, she explained why she believes the painting is an original by the Italian Baroque master, rather than one of the copies that have intrigued art historians for decades. The painting shows a curly-haired young man in a white shirt leaning against a table covered with fruit, an artichoke, and a glass carafe filled with roses. His mouth hangs just slightly open. One eyebrow is raised, like he’s about to say something. Look closely at the carafe, and there’s another surprise: reflected in the glass are a window and the figure of another young man, possibly Caravaggio himself.

The composition has fascinated scholars for nearly a century. Four old copies are known to exist, including one at the High Museum of Art in Atlanta, but the original had apparently vanished. According to Terzaghi, the newly identified canvas is that missing painting, made around 1596 or 1597, when Caravaggio was in his twenties and beginning to establish his reputation in Rome.

Her research found that the painting appeared in a 1607 inventory of works belonging to Giuseppe Cesari, known as Cavalier d’Arpino, whose workshop employed the young Caravaggio. Cesari eventually ran into trouble with papal authorities after being accused of illegally possessing weapons and orchestrating a street brawl that “disfigured a painter” named Pomarancio. He surrendered his art collection to escape prosecution, and the works eventually passed to Cardinal Scipione Borghese, nephew of Pope Paul V.

The painting was recorded in the Borghese collection in 1693, and Terzaghi believes it remained with the family until at least 1831, when restrictions that had previously prevented its sale had passed.

By the early twentieth century, the painting had made its way into the holdings a Paris bookseller. Following his death, his widow put the work up for auction in 1909―cataloged simply as a work by an anonymous Italian painter. An ancestor of the current owners bought it, and it has remained in the family ever since.

Terzaghi believes the painting is closely connected to Caravaggio’s Boy Bitten by a Lizard, another early work showing a young man alongside an elaborate still life. Scholars have previously suggested that the two compositions might belong to a series representing the five senses, with the lizard representing touch and the roses representing smell.

For Terzaghi, the painting’s quality is the best evidence of its authenticity. She points to the young man’s expressive face, the shimmering white fabric of his shirt, and the remarkable handling of light passing through the water-filled carafe. The floral arrangement also bears a striking resemblance to a description of Caravaggio’s work by his contemporary and rival Giovanni Baglione, who recalled the artist’s extraordinary treatment of water, glass, and flowers covered in dew.

The discovery isn’t entirely new to the art historical world. In January 2025, Spain’s Ars Magazine reported that a French family believed it owned the missing Caravaggio. Terzaghi also alluded to the painting in the catalog for the Caravaggio 2025 exhibition in Rome. Her account in La Repubblica, however, offers a much fuller explanation of the attribution and the provenance research behind it.

There are still questions, particularly about how the painting traveled from Italy to France. And as with almost any newly attributed Caravaggio, other specialists will need to assess the evidence before the work can gain wider acceptance.

Papi has yet to publish the evidence supporting his claim.

Supreme Court weighs effort to shield federal law enforcement as ICE complaints rise

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court is seen on Oct. 1 in Washington, D.C. J. Scott Applewhite/AP hide caption

Five years ago, a gang riot broke out inside a federal prison in Hawaii.

Kekai Watanabe was beaten with a makeshift weapon known as a "lock in a sock."

When he complained about serious back pain, a nurse told him to "stop being a cry baby."

Seven months later, doctors diagnosed Watanabe with a fractured tail bone, and he sued the nurse for violating his Eighth Amendment right against cruel and unusual punishment.

On Monday, the Supreme Court will hear his case, which could determine whether federal law enforcement officers can be held to account when they violate constitutional rights. The claim by Watanabe, now 40, echoes far beyond the walls of prison — and into American streets where immigration officers have been accused of beating and running over migrants.

"What's at stake here is whether, basically, we as a country are going to hold federal law enforcement officers accountable when they violate clearly established constitutional rights," said Georgetown law professor Steve Vladeck, who filed a "friend of the court" brief in the case. "I mean, it really is, I think, that stark."

It's already difficult for these claims to proceed, because the court requires people to show the facts in their cases are identical to precedent. In the federal prisons, the landmark case dates to 1980. The prisoner in that lawsuit died from untreated asthma. The nurse's lawyers say Watanabe, the inmate from Hawaii, survived the gang attack in 2021, so his case is different enough that it should be dismissed.

Mary Hampton Mason worked at the Justice Department for nearly 35 years, in an office that evaluated these kinds of legal claims. Mason said rogue officers are rare, and problems are typically systemic.

"It's a result of agency culture, it's a result of training, it's a result of all sorts of things, but these failures are really institutional," Mason said.

The Supreme Court created a right to sue federal officers for wrongdoing in a 1971 case known as Bivens. But Mason said that precedent has been hollowed out for years, and the court should finally overrule it because it gives plaintiffs false hope.

"In fact in the last 20 years, I'm only aware of one Bivens case that has resulted in a judgment for the plaintiffs that … didn't get reversed on appeal, and that's in thousands and thousands and thousands of cases filed," she said.

But supporters of the prisoner say that number doesn't account for settlements or how these lawsuits can deter other bad behavior. If individual federal officers and agents can be on the hook financially, the argument goes, they could think twice before breaking the law.

"That's why damages are so important," said Gregory Cui, an attorney at the MacArthur Justice Center, a civil rights legal organization. "Because they're a way to try to fix things, to give a person the means to pay for medical care or rebuild their life."

In a "friend of the court" brief, Cui reported that the grievance process for people inside federal prison almost never works for inmates, who succeed just 1% of the time in changing things such as access to medical care or better conditions behind bars.

Cui and other legal experts said the Supreme Court agreed to hear this case at a time when abuses by federal immigration agents and others are in the spotlight. Now, they said, is the wrong time for the court to eliminate one of the few avenues of accountability for those errant officers.

"A world with no Bivens is a world in which federal law enforcement officers would enjoy absolute immunity, where no matter how egregious their conduct is, no one can hold them liable," said Vladeck, of Georgetown Law Center. "As alarming a prospect as that may have been five years ago, it should be an especially terrifying one today, and it ought to be one that even this Supreme Court is worried about endorsing."

Both sides agree Congress has the power to weigh in and give people whose rights have been violated by federal officers a clear way to sue. But so far lawmakers have not advanced legislation to hold those people to account.

37 Years Later, One Calvin And Hobbes Comic About Love Still Has No Equal

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Bill Watterson’s Calvin and Hobbes is far more than just laughs and giggles. The beloved strip has earned a special place in readers’ hearts for its surprisingly profound observations about life, particularly considering they come from the perspective of a mischievous six-year-old boy. So, it should come as no surprise that Calvin and Hobbes is also home to one of the greatest quotes and insights about love in comic strip history.

Love comes in many forms, but two of the most common are romantic and platonic. While both can be equally powerful, they are fundamentally different, meaning that quotes about love typically tend to speak to one or the other. However, Calvin and Hobbes' best quote on the subject is so universally applicable that it perfectly captures both, making it an obvious standout.

When it comes to grief, Calvin and Hobbes delivered one of the best quotes in comic strip history. But be warned: you’ll likely need a box of tissues.

The quote comes from a strip Bill Watterson originally published on April 23, 1989, and remains one of the best, if not the very best, quotes about love in comic strip history: “Things are never quite as scary when you’ve got a best friend.” While we'll explore the context behind this quote and its deeper meaning shortly, what's particularly special about it is how broadly it applies to the different kinds of love we experience. After all, a best friend can be a romantic partner, a sibling, a parent, or virtually anyone who makes us feel loved and less alone, including imaginary tigers.

This strip is one of Bill Watterson’s full-color Sunday spreads and begins with Calvin struggling to fall asleep. As he lies in bed, he finds himself frightened by the darkness of the night. At the height of his fear, however, Calvin looks over and sees Hobbes sleeping peacefully beside him. The sight of his best friend immediately comforts him, shifting his attention from his fears to the affection he feels for his beloved tiger.

While the strip features a “3-14” date marking, the comics archive GoComics.com lists its original publication date as April 23, 1989.

After petting Hobbes and settling back into bed, Calvin delivers the now-iconic line: “Things are never quite as scary when you’ve got a best friend,” before drifting off to sleep. Simple on the surface, the quote conveys a profound truth about love: having someone who cares about you can make even life's most frightening and difficult moments a little easier to face.

At its heart, this quote is about partnership and companionship, regardless of the form they take. Yes, Calvin specifically refers to a "best friend," but that description can apply to virtually anyone, whether it's a bond formed through family or one built by choice. That's what makes this quote so universal when it comes to love. It speaks to anyone who has someone whose presence alone makes them feel safer, more secure, and less alone in the world.

The quote also subtly answers the question of what love really is. In this case, love is the person who shows up and stays by your side. It's not necessarily about grand gestures, extravagant gifts, or even having someone who can fix all your problems. Rather, love is having someone willing to weather life's storms alongside you. That companionship and support can fundamentally change how we experience the challenges life throws our way.

In short, love changes how we experience the world. When we're alone, fear, uncertainty, and hardship can feel overwhelming. However, when we have someone we love by our side, those same experiences can feel far less intimidating. In that sense, we're strengthened by the mere presence of someone who cares about us.

This is truly one of Bill Watterson's greatest strips, capturing one of the strongest heartbeats at the core of Calvin and Hobbes: the love between a six-year-old boy and his best friend. This companionship is at the very heart of what made the strip so special and has kept it alive in fans' hearts, even after all these years. 10 Greatest Opening Lines In Fantasy TV History, Ranked

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Fantasy television has a unique challenge when introducing audiences to a new series. Unlike shows grounded in reality, fantasy stories must establish unfamiliar worlds, explain their rules, and convince viewers to invest in characters whose experiences might be entirely impossible. While some shows spend episodes establishing their premises, others accomplish something remarkable with just a few words.

The best opening lines in fantasy television do more than introduce a story. They establish atmosphere, foreshadow conflict, and reveal themes that eventually define the series. Whether delivered through an ominous conversation or unforgettable narration, these introductions can become as recognizable as the shows themselves.

Some beloved fantasy series have especially memorable introductions because their opening words gain significance as their stories progress. What initially seems like a simple statement can eventually reflect everything the characters experience, making those moments more meaningful during subsequent rewatches.

From supernatural procedurals to adventures involving magic and mythology, fantasy television has produced outstanding opening moments. However, the best examples capture the spirit of their shows while giving audiences something worth remembering long after the final episode.

NBC's Grimm establishes its relationship with traditional fairy tales through a deceptively simple exchange between detectives Hank Griffin and Nick Burkhardt: "Oh, you're a happily ever after guy." Hank's observation about Nick's romantic outlook initially feels like harmless banter, but it's much more meaningful once the show's supernatural premise emerges.

Grimm takes stories traditionally associated with happy endings and transforms them into something darker. Nick discovers that fairy tale monsters are real, and his responsibility as a Grimm frequently jeopardizes his relationships and personal happiness.

The opening line introduces the contrast defining the series. Nick might believe in happy endings, but his new reality makes it much harder to experience one.

Although not technically the first spoken line of The Magicians, "It's happening," from the premiere's opening conversation between Eliza and Dean Henry Fogg makes an immediate impression. Viewers have virtually no context for understanding what Eliza means, but the urgency surrounding their exchange suggests something dangerous is approaching.

The conversation establishes that powerful individuals already understand the supernatural threats awaiting the central characters, even before those characters discover their magical abilities. It also foreshadows the Beast's terrifying arrival.

Unlike fantasy stories that initially present magic as an exciting escape from ordinary life, The Magicians immediately suggests that the supernatural world carries considerable danger. Its mysterious warning establishes those stakes before audiences understand the larger conflict.

After an intense opening battle against a monstrous kikimora, Geralt of Rivia delivers a surprisingly understated observation in The Witcher's premiere: "Today isn't your day, is it?" His first words establish the dry humor and emotional restraint that make Henry Cavill's portrayal of the monster hunter so entertaining.

Rather than celebrating his victory or expressing relief at surviving, Geralt treats the encounter like another inconvenience in his difficult profession. The contrast between the fight's brutality and his casual response makes the moment memorable.

This introduction establishes that Geralt isn't a conventional fantasy hero. He approaches extraordinary circumstances with remarkable practicality, and his humor frequently emerges from his apparent indifference.

Netflix's The Sandman introduces its supernatural mythology through narration from Dream, also known as Morpheus. His opening words, "We begin in the waking world..." challenge the assumption that the physical world represents the entirety of human existence, introducing audiences to a realm most people experience only while sleeping.

The narration establishes the essential distinction between the waking world and the Dreaming while helping viewers understand Morpheus' enormous responsibility.

What makes this introduction effective is its philosophical approach to fantasy storytelling. Rather than simply presenting an unfamiliar supernatural environment, The Sandman encourages audiences to reconsider something they experience every night, transforming the ordinary act of dreaming into something extraordinary.

Outlander begins with Claire Randall making an observation that initially resembles the introduction to a missing-person mystery: "People disappear all the time." However, her words take on a double meaning once audiences discover that her disappearance involves traveling through time.

Claire's narration presents disappearing as an ordinary occurrence before revealing the extraordinary circumstances of her experience. Her unexpected journey from the 20th century to 18th-century Scotland fundamentally changes the life she expected to lead.

The statement becomes increasingly meaningful as Claire navigates relationships separated by centuries. Throughout Outlander, disappearing isn't always about leaving a particular location. Sometimes, it means losing access to an entire life that can never be recovered.

HBO's Game of Thrones opens with members of the Night's Watch investigating an unsettling discovery beyond the Wall. When Will says, "We should start back. They're all dead," suggesting they abandon their mission after discovering dead wildlings, the audience is introduced to the dangers lurking beyond the Seven Kingdoms.

The fantasy umbrella covers a wide range of TV shows, but only some have stood out as the very best, further influencing the genre as a whole.

The statement establishes the supernatural threat that eventually overshadows the political conflicts dominating much of the series. Viewers quickly discover that, beyond the Wall, death doesn't necessarily mean the end.

While Game of Thrones became famous for shocking betrayals and struggles for the Iron Throne, its opening words emphasize another danger entirely. The White Walkers represent an existential threat that makes humanity's political rivalries seem considerably less important.

The opening narration of Buffy the Vampire Slayer establishes one of television's most recognizable supernatural mythologies. Before audiences meet Buffy Summers, they learn that a single young woman is selected to protect humanity from vampires, demons, and other dangerous creatures. "In every generation, there is a Chosen One."

The introduction establishes the enormous responsibility placed on Buffy's shoulders. However, its significance grows as the series explores the consequences of being chosen for a destiny she never asked for.

Throughout Buffy the Vampire Slayer, the expectations surrounding the Slayer frequently conflict with Buffy's desire for an ordinary life. The show's eventual decision to challenge the idea that only one girl should carry this responsibility makes its original introduction even more meaningful.

Prime Video's The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power opens with Galadriel delivering a line that captures an important theme in J.R.R. Tolkien's mythology: "Nothing is evil in the beginning." Rather than introducing an established conflict, her narration encourages audiences to consider how darkness emerges within a world that wasn't always corrupted.

The opening is especially appropriate for a prequel exploring events thousands of years before The Lord of the Rings. Audiences already understand the devastating consequences of Sauron's influence, making the origins of evil particularly significant.

Galadriel's words also emphasize how admirable intentions can become corrupted. It's a fitting introduction to a story where the line between good intentions and dangerous decisions becomes increasingly blurry.

Few fantasy series have an opening narration as recognizable as BBC's Merlin. Delivered by the Great Dragon, Kilgharrah, the introduction establishes the legendary world of Camelot while emphasizing that the kingdom's destiny rests on someone's unexpected shoulders. "In a land of myth and a time of magic..."

The narration transforms the familiar legend of Merlin into a story about a young man discovering his abilities. Rather than presenting the wise wizard traditionally associated with Arthurian mythology, the series follows someone whose extraordinary destiny remains largely unrealized.

As Merlin's relationship with Arthur Pendragon develops, the opening narration becomes more meaningful. It reminds audiences that ordinary decisions can have enormous consequences, particularly when someone is responsible for protecting a future that hasn't happened yet.

Avatar: The Last Airbender delivers arguably the most effective opening narration in fantasy television history. Through Katara's introduction, audiences learn about the four nations, the Avatar's responsibility to maintain balance, and the devastating war transforming their world. "Water. Earth. Fire. Air. My grandmother used to tell me stories about the old days..."

This opening is impressive because it efficiently establishes a complicated mythology without overwhelming viewers. Within moments, audiences understand the central conflict, the missing Avatar's importance, and why his return represents humanity's greatest hope for peace.

However, the narration's greatest strength comes from Katara herself. Rather than presenting an impersonal historical account, Avatar: The Last Airbender establishes its mythology through someone personally affected by the war.

Her belief that the Avatar can save the world becomes the fantasy series' emotional foundation. Even after witnessing Aang's complete journey, those opening words retain their significance, reminding viewers that one person's hope made everything that followed possible.

The Aircraft Replacing The Chinook

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As it stands, there is technically no official replacement for the Boeing CH-47 yet under US Army development. Significant progress on the Sikorsky UH-60 Black Hawk’s successor has taken place in recent years, however. That is the Bell V-280 Valor, a tiltrotor under development that has key differences from its older cousin, Bell-Boeing’s V-22 Osprey.

The lack of a direct successor for the heavy-duty whirlybird has led to some speculation that the Army may consolidate its fleet, with the backbone model being the Valor (officially designated MV-25). Concerns about the CH-47F’s survivability in contested airspace and vast domains like the Pacific lend credence to the possibility of a total phase-out.

The Chinook’s unrivaled heavy-lift capability and outstanding track record make it a favorite with the special forces communities of the US Department of Defense. The Army’s Chinook fleet numbers more than 470 aircraft and is expected to serve as the primary heavy-lift rotorcraft for many more years to come.

Since debuting in 1961, the Chinook has never been toppled as the apex of tandem-rotor helicopters. The soviets experimented with tandem-rotor designs, but favored different configurations for heavy lift. Even the US Navy and Marines’ CH-46 has been retired for a decade. The Chinook is still being modernized, as the Block II variant offers a gross weight increase of 54,000 pounds, upgraded fuel systems, and rotor blades.

Honeywell has developed the new T55-714C, with 20% more power and 8% better fuel efficiency than previous powerplants. Vertical Mag quoted General John Murray in 2021, saying that no replacement had been planned yet, and the Chinook remains a critical asset for Army Special Forces, among other missions:

“The Future Attack Reconnaissance Aircraft and the Future Long Range Assault Aircraft are the two plans, programs we have working right now. There is nothing working for a replacement for the CH-47.”

The first CH-47F Block II Chinooks reached the Army in 2024, and the first of a scheduled 465 helicopters was delivered in July of that year, according to World of Aviation. Some compare the CH-47 to the equally enduring B-52, for being so good at what it does that there simply is no replacement. Perhaps it will soldier on into the distant future, but let's explore the latest challenger and if it has what it takes to fill the Chinooks’ place on the flightline.

The Army’s Future Vertical Lift program sought to develop the Future Long Range Assault Attack, or FLRAA. The winning platform was Bell Textron’s V-280 Valor tiltrotor. The new aircraft will initially supplement the UH-60 Black Hawk fleet but will likely replace the aging Sikorsky completely over time.

Although the V-280 is cut from the same cloth as the Bell-Boeing Osprey, engineers have implemented key differences from the start. Aside from a smaller overall footprint, the V-280 uses a V-tail configuration rather than the V-22’s twin vertical fin tail. While the V-22’s nacelles, which house both the engine and gearbox, fully rotate, only the V-280’s props and gearbox move while the engines remain fixed during transition between flight modes.

The V-280 also features a different clutch to the V-22’s problematic design, as it operates more like a conventional helicopter. The Osprey’s transmission has been linked to fatal hard-clutch engagements on more than one occasion and requires somewhere in the ballpark of 45 days to replace, according to Military.com.

The Bell V-280 Valor is a next-generation tiltrotor aircraft developed specifically by Bell-Textron for the US Army's FLRAA program. The V-280's defining feature is its ability to transition between helicopter-like vertical flight and airplane-like forward flight by tilting its rotors and drive shafts. It offers significant improvements in speed, range, and maneuverability over the UH-60.

This allows vertical takeoff and landing like a helicopter while providing speed and range comparable to a fixed-wing aircraft. Flying as fast as 300 knots, the Valor is almost twice as fast as the Chinook, which tops out at 170 knots. The Valor also surpasses the Chinook in range by a long shot.

Under combat load, the CH-47 can only go about 400 NM, while the V-280 can fly 800 NM on a single fill-up without any aerial refueling. The Valor can perform fast-rope sling-loading like the UH-60 or CH-47, but armament has yet to be publicly revealed. A key area where it falls short of the CH-47 is, inevitably, raw payload.

The V-280, or MV-25 as it recently became designated, has under half the sling-load capacity at 10,000 lbs compared to the Chinook’s 26,000 lbs. The Valor can’t compete with the Chinook in weight or volume capacity, which is the crux of the issue when it comes to replacing the venerable CH-47.

Like its Bell-Boeing V-22 Osprey predecessor, Valor’s tiltrotor design is a radical departure from conventional rotorcraft. The MV-25 is equipped with two Rolls-Royce AE 1107F engines, which are of the same powerplant family as used in the C-130 Hercules, Global Hawk drone, and V-22 Osprey.

The V-280 gearbox design has been refined from the V-22's design into a simpler, lighter, and more easily maintainable design. This new system eliminates the need for a mid-wing gearbox, which cuts the total number of gearboxes from five to four, versus the Osprey.

The design allows for a straight wing design that reduces complexity and eliminates a number of failure points. The V-280's stationary engines result in weight savings, thanks to fewer parts, and the lower empty weight also allows for better performance and fuel efficiency.

Its simplified mechanical system also improves reliability and maintenance, reducing the notoriously long downtime seen with the Osprey as well as operational costs overall. The Valor is superior to the Chinook in the area of efficiency, and likely around the same level of complexity. Still, the question of operation costs and maintenance intensity remains unclear.

The MV-25 has the potential to revolutionize US military operations, particularly in the Pacific. There, its speed, range, and survivability are greatly superior to the CH-47 in long-range air assault missions. Its ability to take off and land vertically like a helicopter and transition to airplane-like flight profiles allows for rapid deployment and maneuverability that the Chinook can’t match in the vast, island-dotted Pacific.

This would be particularly important in any potential conflict with China. Here, long distances and limited infrastructure are major challenges for any conventional rotorcraft. The Valor greatly exceeds the capabilities of the Black Hawk in this scenario. It will enable the Army to project power across greater distances in the Pacific, like the South China Sea, more quickly and efficiently.

The MV-25 can operate from austere locations like its predecessors, and its long range can overcome logistical challenges in the vast and dispersed island chains of the Pacific. Improved survivability features make it more difficult for adversaries to target. The primary strength is speed, but it also features radar cross-section reduction and infrared suppression to minimize its detectability.

In potential conflicts with near-peer adversaries with advanced air defenses, the Valor will significantly improve the survivability of air assault operations. That also allows for more dispersed and decentralized operations over extended distances. That adaptability and flexibility make it a well-suited platform for the Pacific theater.

The US Army is undergoing a significant transformation in its aviation branch as a whole. The future will prioritize unmanned systems and more long-range crewed platforms. The Valor is one of the top priorities, as it will replace the Black Hawk, which is the current backbone of Army Aviation. The Army is also investing in long-range precision munitions for its aircraft to match its tactics to faster platforms.

The Army is rapidly working toward affordable, reliable, and lethal uncrewed aerial systems in the field. Deploying drone swarms or executing pinpoint strikes from units integrated with drones at the brigade level will evolve the Army’s strategy to the next level. The 'Replicator' initiative, headed by the Defense Innovation Unit, aims to field thousands of low-cost, autonomous systems.

The new devices would be attritable drones that counter adversary threats as well as provide man-portable intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance with strike capability. In a future of drones at the tip of the spear and tiltrotor workhorses as the backbone of the vertical lift fleet, it's hard to say if the CH-47 will still have a place.

Its raw payload-carrying power has no equal in current or planned platforms, which may see it live on in a way the B-52 has endured. On the other hand, it may simply be unneeded, as tiltrotor technology improves and other aircraft like the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency Liberty Lifter are introduced.

Trail Camera Photos of the First Coyotes to Use the World’s Largest Wildlife Crossing

The world’s largest wildlife crossing, the $114 million Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing, hasn’t officially “opened,” but that hasn’t stopped the animals from using it above the extremely busy Highway 101 in Agoura Hills, California.

On October 28, a coyote became the first large mammal — a lizard was the first across the line, technically — to use the wildlife bridge. Yesterday, a second coyote went on it.

They were both caught on recently installed remote wildlife camera traps, an incredible tool for scientists and award-winning wildlife photographers alike.

As SFGate reports, the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing, which has been in the works since the National Wildlife Federation (NWF) and Santa Monica Mountains Fund began raising money for it in 2014, isn’t slated to have its ribbon-cutting ceremony until early December. Construction is ongoing, so it isn’t even technically a crossing yet.

Wildlife doesn’t wait. The project, which includes two bridges crossing the 10-lane Ventura Freeway, includes 13 acres of habitat restoration. Birds and bugs have already enjoyed the growing reclaimed habitat above the busy highway, while a western fence lizard was the first to go on the bridge’s surface itself.

The two coyotes were the first large mammals spotted on the structure itself, but a mountain lion and her two cubs, a mule deer, and bobcats have all been spied walking next to the wildlife crossing.

“To everyone who wondered how wildlife would know where to go without signs… apparently, they’ve figured it out,” writes the Facebook account for P22 Mountain Lion of Hollywood, an influential mountain lion that was tragically euthanized in 2022. Matteo Thun designs Torre Velasca interiors using "building itself as the starting point"

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Alberto Strada (unless stated otherwise)

Italian architect Matteo Thun looked to the original materials and colours of Milan’s iconic Torre Velasca when creating its new interiors, which feature pink hues inspired by the tower’s dusky facade.

Thun has designed 72 flats in the upper floors of the tower, ranging from suites to duplex penthouses. He approached the interior design of the skyscraper, built in the 1950s by the architecture studio BBPR, with a focus on reinterpretation.

“We allowed the masters and the building itself to speak, imagining a way of living within it that would carry the building a little further into the future, adding a new chapter to its history,” Thun told Dezeen.

“We did not seek contrast: our design was informed directly by the materials and colours of Torre Velasca, and we chose not to intervene where we felt the architecture had already achieved a sense of completeness.”

His studio preserved original details, including doors, window frames, loggias and tiles in the bathrooms and kitchens. These were then juxtaposed with modern furniture and evergreen design classics.

“The interiors bring together new furniture designed by the studio and design references spanning the 1950s to the 1980s, including iconic pieces developed in new colourways in collaboration with historic Milanese companies,” Thun said.

“With a deep appreciation for craftsmanship and a strong attention to detail, these designs share an ability to evolve without losing their identity.”

The colour palette, too, was inspired by the original hues of the Torre Velasca.

In the living rooms, walls are covered in custom-made velvet wall coverings by textile company Dedar in a pale pink colour that resembles the pink plaster on Torre Velasca’s facade.

“The building itself was our starting point,” Thun said.

“The pink plaster, developed and patented specifically for the tower, together with the blue and green floor tiles found both inside and in the loggias, formed the basis of our colour palette,” he continued.

“We developed different gradations of these tones, opting for darker shades of green and blue to define the entrance areas and bedrooms. “

As well as the apartments, Thun also designed the interior of the reception, lobby and the corridors on the ground floor.

And while this was an interior project, Thun says it ended up becoming “a lesson in architecture” for his studio.

“Working with a building as distinctive as Torre Velasca challenged us to think and work first and foremost as architects,” Thun said.

“The challenge was not to reinvent the building, but to find ways to make its original character feel relevant today, while remaining true to BBPR’s vision,” he continued.

“In this sense, the project became a process of discovery, with the building itself serving as our greatest teacher. It was, in many ways, a lesson in architecture for us.”

Thun has previously designed the interior of the boutique hotel The Julius Prague and “hotel-like” rooms for a German hospital.

36 Years Later, Hulk's Greatest Design Still Has No Equal

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Almost 40 years after its Marvel debut, the greatest Hulk design of all time has yet to be bested, despite major competition both before and after. Here's why the design in question deserves its crown, and which other contenders come close.

Inspired by horror icons Frankenstein and Mr. Hyde, the Hulk was created by Stan Lee and Jack Kirby. Despite being one of the most iconic characters of all time, Hulk was originally very different - a grey-skinned jerk who spoke in sophisticated sentences and only came out at night.

Over the years, Hulk became the monosyllabic, emerald-skinned monster that Marvel fans know, though it took several of the most legendary creators in comic history to perfect Bruce Banner's other half.

The best Hulk design of all time is a hotly contested topic among fans of the Jade Giant, but we're handing the gold medal to Dale Keown, who drew The Incredible Hulk from 1990-1992 alongside acclaimed writer Peter David, with the two re-teaming for the non-canon Incredible Hulk: The End in 2002, which sees the character contend with a nuclear post-apocalypse.

Keown's Hulk is a barrel-chested behemoth with fists bigger than his head. His brow isn't just pronounced, it combines with a smaller, flatter nose and gaping mouth to create a hyper-expressive, highly distinct silhouette. The green skin is bright and luminous with a tonsure-like head of hair that looks like it didn't grow at the same rate as the rest of Bruce Banner's body.

So, what makes Keown our pick for the Hulk GOAT? It all comes down to one thing: Hulk's humanity. The best Hulk stories of all time understand that whatever Bruce Banner might think, the Hulk isn't a simple, singular expression of his rage. Hulk and Bruce are equally complex aspects of the same person - unique but deeply connected - and Keown communicates that better than any other artist.

There are a LOT of artists who have nailed Hulk's monstrous intensity and world-shattering rage. However, Keown's art is uniquely capable of showing Hulk laughing, glowering, grinning, wincing and roaring without losing the core of the character. Many of Hulk's earliest artists nail the sense of an impossible monster, but not one with a legitimate inner life.

The ability to depict different aspects of Hulk's personality was vital working alongside Peter David, who further splintered Bruce's psyche into the grey Joe Fixit, genius Professor Hulk and the brutal Maestro - the villain Bruce may one day become. These concepts have become the bedrock of Hulk's best modern stories - they were all good ideas, but it was Keown who ensured they were all 'Hulk.'

While we're holding Dale Keown up as creating the best Hulk design of all time, it's a close thing, given the wealth of talent who have worked on the character. And while you may not agree with our call, rest assured that there are many, many Hulk artists we still consider greats.

Hulk co-creator Jack Kirby drew Hulk with unbelievable menace, giving the character his heavy brow and tombstone teeth. Under Kirby, Hulk was a pure creature of the id - teeth bared and fists balled. Likewise, Herb Trimpe's Hulk is a howling monstrosity, reducing concrete to powder under his apish mitts and possessing a hunched, embattled physicality.

John Romita Sr.'s Hulk nails the concept of a monster that is still recognizably Bruce Banner, while Sal Buscema turned the character into a canvas for raw emotion. Despite a famously short run, it's worth noting that John Byrne accomplished the same sense of humanity as Keown, conveying a Hulk who is more than just rage.

In modern comics, Hulk has attracted some of the best artistic talent in comics. Adam Kubert's Hulk is an explosive mass of muscle that can't be contained by page or panel, Lee Weeks returns him to his Frankenstein-ian roots as a tragic homunculus, and John Romita Jr. captured a Hulk destroyed by grief and wrath, his humanity hanging on by a thread.

Bryan Hitch reinvented Hulk for the 2000s-era Ultimate Universe, turning him into a near-parodic wrecking ball of hate, lust and frustration. Daniel Acuña's work on Avengers: Twilight realized Bruce Banner's worst fears - the Hulk as an apocalyptic atrocity.

And while allegations that Joe Bennett hid antisemitic imagery in the background of Immortal Hulk can't be forgotten, he undeniably collaborated on the single greatest Hulk story of all time with writer Al Ewing, juxtaposing intense body horror and flying spittle with quiet, existential despair and righteous anger, bringing back Hulk's simian brow and bared teeth without abandoning the truth of a frightened child lashing out at an often deserving world.

While discussing Hulk's best comic designs, it would be silly to ignore his live-action appearances. Lou Ferrigno's snarling Hulk changed the franchise forever, acting as a near-unparalleled cultural touchstone. Ang Lee's Hulk embraced the imagery of a confused, superpowered toddler - dull-eyed and slathered in dirt - surpassing the pure rage of 2008's Incredible Hulk, at least in design.

However, the obvious winner is the MCU's Hulk, and for the same reasons as Dale Keown's art. Designed around the likeness of Bruce Banner actor Mark Ruffalo (who performs the Hulk's scenes using motion-capture technology), the most modern depiction of Hulk rages, but he also smirks, mourns, comprehends and fears.

This approach doesn't just make Hulk more interesting, it lets fans in on the vital lie that great Hulk stories deconstruct - that the Green Goliath is just a rage monster who lives inside Bruce Banner, rather than a vital aspect of his soul that's just as complicated and valid as the timid scientist.

Hulk's strength and size are essential to the character, but ultimately he also needs to be capable of emotional depth on par with his human side. The greatest piece of Hulk art of all time may or may not be by Dale Keown, but his design for the character encompassed everything the Hulk needs to be to tell the range of stories the character is capable of supporting.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required.”

During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using.

This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today.

In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity.

Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L).

Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure.

As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack.

As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work.

Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources.

This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment.

Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote.

The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time.

Alexis Mac Allister has spoken out about his ‘painful, unpleasant situation’ at Liverpool in terms of not being offered a new contract.

The Argentine caused a stir in September when he said that he was ‘very sad‘ not to have been offered a new deal by the club, having seen fellow 2023 signings Ryan Gravenberch and Dominik Szoboszlai both pen contract extensions earlier this year.

The Telegraph’s Dominic King reported last month that Anfield chiefs aren’t in ‘any rush’ to begin talks over a new deal for the Reds’ number 10 (who’s contracted to June 2028), despite his resurgence in form since the start of this season.

Appearing on the Los Edul show in his homeland, Mac Allister spoke candidly about Liverpool’s decision not to offer him new terms over the past few months.

The 27-year-old said: “We’re in a situation where I have a year and a half left on my contract. Last season I was told that the club’s idea was not to renew it, at least for now, and obviously that was a painful, unpleasant situation.”

When asked if the situation was making him despondent, he replied: “Not that much, because at the end of the day, I still had – and still have – time left at this club, which is what I want most – to stay here.

“The reality is that the manager has now changed, the sporting director has changed, and new investors are coming into the club, so I think I’m now in a position where anything can happen. We’ll see what happens in the future. I’ve always said that I’m very happy here, that I really enjoy it, and we’ll see.”

Mac Allister added: “Look at Szoboszlai and Gravenberch. They’re two players who were in the same contractual situation as me, and when I was told that, they renewed their contracts.

“It was more a position taken by the club, and generally that’s how things are done. At the end of the day, as a club you don’t want to lose a lot of money, and you don’t want players leaving on free transfers either.”

That the Argentine has spoken out publicly about his contract situation once more gives a strong hint that privately he’s seething at not being offered a new deal in the same way that Szoboszlai and Gravenberch were.

However, the ex-Brighton man has been excellent in the first two months of this campaign, almost as though he’s motivated by a burning desire to earn a new deal at Anfield and show that the hierarchy have been mistaken in their reluctance to tie him down for the long-term.

The 2022 World Cup winner is currently on £180,000 per week at Liverpool, a figure exceeded by 10 of his teammates (Capology), and it could be the case that he’s seeking a higher amount than what the club are prepared to offer him.

Mac Allister could hardly have made his desire for a new contract any clearer. We don’t know exactly why his situation is taking longer to resolve than those of Szoboszlai and Gravenberch, but if he keeps performing on the pitch, then surely it’s just a matter of when and not if he’s offered an extension by the Anfield hierarchy.

What Has Happened to Vozinha and His Family Since the World Cup

📅 Sep 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Vozinha became one of the breakout stars of the 2026 World Cup, but the Cape Verde goalkeeper has admitted he would rather have his old life back.

The 40-year-old was one of the more unexpected stars after making seven saves in a 0-0 draw with eventual champions Spain in Cape Verde's first-ever World Cup match, and went on to help the island nation reach the knockout stage.

His Instagram following jumped from 56,000 to more than a million by the end of the Spain game, after Brazilian broadcaster CazéTV encouraged its audience to follow him, yet it has proven to be a difficult time.

Speaking to The Observer, Vozinha explained how dramatically his life has changed.

"I've lost my peace and tranquillity," he said. "I no longer have any privacy, or at least much less than I used to. Now I go out, I go to a restaurant, and there's always someone who wants to take a photo with me or asks me for an autograph. Or, worse, someone is filming me."

Cristiano Ronaldo gained over 11 million social media followers during the World Cup... but that's not even close to number one.

He added: "To be honest, if I were offered the life I have today or the life I had before, I'd choose the one I had before."

The attention has spread to his family. The Observer reports that supporters turn up at his mother's home in Cape Verde every day, asking for photos or a chat. "I'm not there, so I can't help them," Vozinha said. "They have to learn to live with the situation and set boundaries."

On the pitch, things have not been straightforward either. Vozinha was a free agent at the time of his fame, and opted to leave Portuguese second-tier side Chaves to join Chilean giants Colo-Colo after the tournament, but his deal only runs until the end of 2026.

According to Chilean outlets La Tercera and Emol, the contract extends automatically for another year only if he plays at least half of the club's available minutes. If he falls short, Colo-Colo can let him leave at no cost. He has kept clean sheets, but has faced criticism over his handling.

His return to international duty also went badly. Vozinha was at fault for Mali's third goal in a 3-1 defeat in 2027 Africa Cup of Nations qualifying, coming off his line poorly and leaving the ball loose for Dorgeles Nene to score.

Spanish newspaper AS described the result as a "golpe de realidad", a reality check, for Cape Verde. As Frank Sinatra sang, that's life.

The two players were seen in conversation after the match.

Just weeks after missing out on the Yashin Award at the 2026 Ballon d'Or ceremony, Vozinha proved he's still got plenty to give to the sport. In November, the veteran helped Colo Colo to the Chilean title.

After sealing the trophy, the Cape Verde international dedicated the triumph to his family and country. He said: "Today is a day for celebrating; we'll talk about the future later. This title is for my entire family and for Cape Verde."

The Best Ski Resorts in the US and Canada: 2026 Readers’ Choice Awards

📅 Dec 05, 2014  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

For our 39th annual Reader’s Choice Awards, voters weighed in on their favorite ski resorts in North America—including the US and Canada. Big Sky’s decade-long transformation has paid off, landing the Montana resort in the top spot this year. But Colorado reigns as the top US ski destination, with 10 resorts making our top 30. Meanwhile, six East Coast resorts were recognized, including Mont-Tremblant in Quebec, Canada. And in the western US, new enhancements at Alyeska Resort helped get Alaska back on the list.

From Taos to Telluride and Stowe to SilverStar, these ski resorts were picked by our readers as the best in North America for their trails, lodges, lifts, and après-ski scenes. Whether you’re a powder hound or après ski devotee, these ski resorts ticked all your boxes.

View the 2025 Readers' Choice Awards winners for best ski resorts as a list with scores for the United States here and Canada here.

Aspen-Snowmass’s four mountains are each wildly unique, and Buttermilk might be the most playful. More like one giant terrain park, it’s home to seven different parks for everyone from beginners to pros. Its 22-foot Zaugg-cut Superpipe is the stage for star-studded sporting competitions including X Games Aspen and The Snow League. But Buttermilk is also the mountain for easygoing beginner terrain and awesomely intense trails for uphillers—those showoffs practicing the cardio-busting trend of trekking up a mountain on skis.

Where to stay: Just a free, five-minute shuttle ride away in downtown Aspen at your pick of great hotels, including the Little Nell, Limelight Aspen, St. Regis Aspen, and Hotel Jerome.

Stats: 8 lifts and 44 trails. One-day lift tickets average from $189; on the Ikon Pass and Mountain Collective.

The longest continually operating ski resort in Colorado, Winter Park is a Rocky Mountain playground known for its varied terrain and consistent snowfall (roughly 350 inches a year). It’s also one of Colorado’s most accessible mountains. Located just 67 miles west of Denver, it’s an easy drive from the airport, or take the Amtrak Winter Park Express ski train and get dropped off just steps from the lifts. This season, the resort is more than doubling snowmaking on a key base trail, again improving upon its recent $40 million snowmaking system upgrade. Families continue to find tons of value. Back this season, kids can nab a free ski or snowboard rental with a three-day adult gear rental package and every kid 14 and younger who signs up for a Winter Park Ski and Ride School lesson gets a free lift ticket for the day. The Gondola is free to everyone after 4 p.m. every Thursday through Saturday from mid-December through the end of March. That means you can wrap up the day at Mountaintop Après at Sunspot Lodge whether you skied or not.

Where to stay: Zephyr Mountain Lodge has studios, 1, 2, and 3-bedroom condos with full kitchens. In town, Gravity Haus Winter Park offers co-working spaces as well as gear demos and rentals. A-Frame Club is an Instagram-sensation with its 31 A-Frame cabins and historic saloon.

Stats: 23 lifts, 171 designated trails and 1,212 acres of off-piste terrain. One-day lift tickets from $108; on the Ikon Pass.

Where to stay: The Park Hyatt Beaver Creek Resort and Spa is fresh off a $20 million renovation. Other favorites include The Westin Riverfront Resort & Spa Avon, Vail Valley, The Osprey, a RockResort, and the Ritz-Carlton, Bachelor Gulch.

Stats: 24 lifts for 167 trails. One-day lift tickets from $116; on the Epic Pass.

Skiing in Alaska is a different experience than anywhere in the lower 48. Located just 40 miles southeast of Anchorage, Alyeska’s terrain is uniquely Alaskan, boasting everything from high alpine to the steep faces the 49th state is known for. Experts can push their limits, but with over 55 percent intermediate terrain, tree skiing, wide open groomed runs, and a lower mountain venue perfect for beginners, Alyeska caters to all ability levels. Aside from resort skiing, Alyeska serves as a backcountry basecamp offering guided ski touring, cat skiing, or heli skiing with top-notch outfits like SMG Alaska. And when it comes to après-ski, a trip to the mountaintop Bore Tide Bar for an off-menu Fizz cocktail is an Alyeska ritual.

Where to stay: The 299-room Alyeska Resort has eight dining venues, including stellar sushi spot Sakura and fine dining restaurant, Seven Glaciers, with its panoramic views. The eighth-floor Black Diamond Club feels like a hotel-within-a-hotel thanks to perks like a private lounge that serves complimentary breakfast and après bites. The resort’s 50,000-square-foot, indoor-outdoor Nordic spa is the perfect place to spend a down day.

Stats: 7 lifts for 76 runs, with an additional 1,400 vertical feet accessible via hike-to terrain when conditions allow. One-day lift tickets from $77; on the Ikon Pass.

Tamarack’s primarily east-facing slopes receive an average of 300 inches of snowfall annually. Readers love the diversity of terrain, which welcomes everyone from beginners and families to experts and powder chasers. The resort’s 2,000 mountain acres and 2,800 feet of vertical deliver everything from open bowls, steeps and signature cornice drops to high-speed corduroy groomers, natural tree-skiing glades and three terrain parks. At 6,700 feet, the new Mid-Mountain Lodge crowns the top of Tamarack Express with panoramic dining and event space, while a vibrant alpine-style village anchors the base with lodging, dining, and après. And Tamarack's Nordic trail system winds through open meadows and forested terrain for classic and skate skiing, snowshoeing, and fat biking.

Where to stay: Tamarack Resort has accommodations ranging from studios to four-bedroom penthouses plus amenities such as a pool, hot tubs and gear valet. The Lodge at Osprey Meadows is steps from the lifts and has studios and two- and three-bedroom condos.

Stats: 7 lifts for 59 runs. One-day lift tickets from $99; Ikon Pass Bonus Mountain

Founded by the late Robert Redford in 1969, Sundance Mountain Resort is a beloved escape tucked into Utah’s Wasatch Mountains at the base of the 12,000-foot Mount Timpanogos in Provo Canyon. Steep bowls give experienced skiers a thrill, while a third of the runs are green and beginner-friendly. Just an hour from Salt Lake City (hello, easy airport access!), the 5,000 acres of protected wilderness that surround the resort will help you find solitude. This season’s debut of Electric Horseman, a high-speed quad, marks the first phase of Sundance’s back mountain expansion, adding 60-plus acres of terrain and bringing the resort to 605 skiable acres. At the heart of the terrain expansion is Storyteller, a new signature ridgeline run carved from the terrain formerly known as the Far East Ridgeline. An additional 100 acres of terrain are planned for the 2027-2028 winter season. Off the slopes, guests can explore the resort’s renowned Art Studio & Gallery (think silversmithing and pottery classes), or unwind at the Spa and The Springs.

Where to stay: The Inn at Sundance Mountain Resort, which opened in January 2026, introduced ski-in/ski-out hotel accommodations to the resort for the first time, with 63 thoughtfully designed rooms in the heart of the village.

Stats: 10 lifts for 72 trails. One-day pass is $169.

Spanning six peaks and over 3,000 acres in the Yampa Valley, Steamboat is revered for its tree skiing, trademarked Champagne Powder snow, and family-friendly amenities. But many folks keep coming back for its deep Western roots and a down-home vibe that extends from the mountain to the town. A multiyear, multimillion-dollar redevelopment has reimagined Steamboat. The base area now features Wild Blue, a high speed ten-person gondola that takes skiers and riders from the base to the top of Sunshine Peak in 13 minutes, plus Skeeter's ice rink, and the Range Food & Drink Hall. Beginners can hone their skills mid-mountain at Greenhorn Ranch with natural feature-based instruction, while experts can test their mettle on 655 acres of challenging terrain in Mahogany Ridge and Fish Creek Canyon. One of the most accessible resorts in the Rockies, Steamboat is served by 18 major U.S. cities including Portland, Austin, and Houston.

Where to stay: The Steamboat Grand is a favorite for its massive heated pool and hot tubs. Ski-in/ski-out Sheraton Steamboat Resort Villas is conveniently located next to Christie Peak Express. Gravity Haus is an affordable, slopeside boutique stay. The Amble opened this summer and has 42 residences and resort amenities.

Stats: 23 lifts for 184g trails. One-day lift tickets from $131; on the Ikon Pass.

Of the four mountains that make up Aspen Snowmass, the ultimate rite of passage is here at Aspen Highlands’ massive Highland Bowl. Here’s how to do it: Hike some 45 minutes to the top of the bowl, soak in rewarding 360-degree views that include the twin-peaked Maroon Bells and own the drop into almost 2,500 feet of steep bowl skiing. Reward yourself at mid-mountain with the Merry-Go-Round’s signature Boomerang Burger, topped with onion rings, pastrami bacon and grilled cheese buns.

Where to stay: The Little Nell for five-star service and a great spa or its more casual sister property, Limelight Aspen. That Gant for apartment-style residences with full kitchens.

Stats: 5 lifts for 144 trails. One-day lift tickets from $189; on the Ikon Pass and Mountain Collective pass.

With nearly 5,000 acres of diverse terrain, Heavenly offers the best of both California and Nevada skiing and riding including wide-open groomers, glades, and 1,600-foot mogul runs. Sparkling Lake Tahoe views, an average of 300 days of sunshine a year, and high-energy events and après make Heavenly a dream getaway for your party crew that is always looking for the next adventure. The unique Skyline Trail is a must-do run, taking in majestic mountain landscapes, flat valleys, and lake panoramas while crossing from California to Nevada. Plus, the Heavenly Village is located in the heart of South Lake Tahoe, surrounded by snow-based activities and restaurants, and fun events throughout the winter season. If the views of Lake Tahoe inspire you on the slopes, a trip to the shore is just minutes away.

Where to stay: Basecamp Tahoe South is just a four-minute walk to the gondola and offers a variety of rooms from bunks to luxe tents. Zalanta Resort at the Village is steps away from Heavenly Village and residence-style rooms include gourmet kitchens and private balconies. Lakefront Edgewood Tahoe Resort has a partnership with Ski Butlers to have gear delivered directly to your room.

Stats: 27 lifts for 111 trails. One-day lift ticket from $115; on the Epic Pass.

A winter haven for East Coasters, this Poconos resort’s snow-making capacity could cover the George Washington Bridge with one foot of snow in 24 hours. The mountain’s 900-foot vertical drop is great training before venturing to bigger mountains in the West. More than half of the mountain, however, is covered in beginner-friendly greens. Families love the amenities, including one of the country’s largest snow-tubing park, an indoor water park, and a plethora of dining options including ski-in/ski-out Trail’s End Pub & Grille, known for its creative burgers and craft beer, and Alpine Outpost, a new grab-and-go spot along the Coolmoor teaching trail.

Where to stay: Camelback Lodge, the only ski-in/ski out resort in the Poconos, recently refurbished 453 guest rooms that come in 16 different configurations including bunk rooms and a suite that can sleep up to 14 guests.

Stats: 14 lifts for 45 trails. One-day lift tickets from $78; on the Ikon Pass.

Hard-core skiers who live for hike-to terrain and super steeps have always been fans of Taos Ski Valley. But a revitalized base area with an ice-skating rink, improvements to beginner and intermediate terrain, and $3 million in glading and expanding snowmaking capacity have made this New Mexico mountain destination way more family friendly. Taos Ski Valley leans into sustainability, equity, and inclusivity as the first ski resort in the world to have been B-Corp certified, and one of a few to be Certified CarbonNeutral®. The challenging terrain of Kachina Peak and the moguls of Al’s Run are the big draw for experts. This season ups the resort’s luxury appeal with the introduction of Taos by Belvedere, a fully hosted, all-inclusive mountain experience that features slopeside penthouse accommodations, a private chef, privately guided skiing, restorative wellness therapies, and insider access to local experiences. For dining, the Bavarian is an institution known for its brats and German beers (they also host a sleigh ride dinner during the winter), and newcomer Corner Office gives the state its first natural wine bar. Down days are far from dull thanks to surrounding cultural attractions including the Taos Pueblo UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Where to stay: Located adjacent to Lift 1, The Blake offers a variety of accommodation options that include hotel rooms, suites, penthouses, and condos; decor blends rustic European architecture with one of North America’s finest collections of Southwestern art, including original works by Georgia O’Keeffe.

Stats: 13 lifts for 110 trails. One-day lift tickets from $75; on the Ikon Pass and Mountain Collective pass.

The largest of the SkiBig3 resorts in UNESCO-designated Banff National Park earns its keep with 4,200 acres spanning four different mountain faces and a solid mix of beginner, intermediate, and expert runs from every chair. Here’s what else makes it a dream for all walks of skiers: steeps backed by an awe-inspiring wilderness, bowls dusted in famously dry powder, four terrain parks for beginners to experts, and long cruisers—one of which winds on for five miles. The ‘West Bowl’ is a freeride playground with some 480 wilderness acres to explore. And the recent addition of the Juniper Express Quad provides an ideal learning zone for beginner and intermediate skiers and riders looking to advance their skills. This season, Lake Louise is enhancing its recently opened Richardson’s Ridge terrain with new groomed runs, glades, and improved snowmaking, alongside a major Top of the World chair upgrade designed to increase capacity and reliability.

Where to stay: Located lakeside in Banff National Park, Fairmont Chateau Lake Louise looks like a fairytale castle and its new Basin Glacier Waters Thermal Wellness Spa will have you wishing for a down day to spend soaking in its healing waters. A member of Relais & Châteaux, Post Hotel & Spa has a saltwater pool and is known for its extensive wine cellar and talented culinary team.

Stats: 13 lifts for 169 runs and back bowls. One-day SkiBig3 lift tickets from $172; on the Ikon Pass and Mountain Collective pass.

Nestled on the northern edge of British Columbia’s North Okanagan wine region, SilverStar is one of Canada’s best-kept secrets, with 3,065 skiable acres that receive 275-plus inches of annual snowfall. Guests can skip the rental car and take one of the regular shuttles from Kelowna International Airport, just 55 minutes away. The resort has exceptionally groomed terrain for all abilities and families appreciate that kids age 5 and under receive free lift tickets all season. Alpine skiing is just one of many winter activities on offer. You’ll also find 65 miles of groomed cross-country ski trails, 9 miles of fat-tire bike trails, 4 snow tubing lanes, and a natural skating pond. The dining experiences, such as the Ravenwood snowshoe raclette dinner and the Paradise Camp snow cat dinner tour, are not-to-be-missed. And La Stella, a new Italian spot with wood-fired pizzas debuts this season.

Where to stay: SilverStar’s mid-mountain location means most accommodations are ski-in/ski-out. Snowbird Lodge, Silver Creek Lodge, and Lord Aberdeen have prime locations in the heart of the village, steps from the Summit Express Gondola. Firelight Lodge offers convenient access to the Nordic trail heads.

Stats: 10 lifts for 132 runs. One-day lift ticket from $119; on the Ikon Pass.

Originally known as Little Gap Ski Area when it opened in 1977, the resort changed its name in 1989 to better reflect the grandeur of our mountain. With a vertical drop of 1,082 feet, Blue Mountain boasts the highest elevation in Pennsylvania. Whether you’re a beginner or expert, you’ll find the perfect challenge on the mountain’s 171 acres. You can conquer 15 green, 6 blue, 16 black diamond, and 3 double black diamond trails across the region’s most varied terrain. Home to the East Coast’s largest snowmaking system, the mountain delivers some of the most consistent conditions all season long in the mid-Atlantic region. For lunch with gorgeous views, pop into Slopeside Pub & Grill for wood-fired pizzas and hearty sammys like chicken cheesesteaks. For a quick, grab-and-go bite, swing by the new spud truck, serving up loaded baked potatoes.

Where to stay: Blue Mountain-area Airbnb rentals are the way to go here.

Stats: 15 lifts for 40 trails; One-day lift ticket from $48; on the Ikon Pass.
