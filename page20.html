West Ham tracking Sheffield United star alongside six Premier League clubs

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Sheffield United midfielder Sydie Peck has been among the standout performers for Chris Wilder’s side this term. The SUFC ace is no stranger to transfer interest, and his displays mean rival clubs are regularly keeping tabs on his situation.

A new report from TEAMtalk states Championship rivals and summer admirers West Ham are among those monitoring Peck ahead of a potential January swoop. Also interested in the 22-year-old are six Premier League clubs: Newcastle United, Sunderland, Brighton & Hove Albion, Crystal Palace, Brentford and Bournemouth.

It is added that the Blades are ‘aware’ of the interest in Peck and hope to keep him, but there is an ‘increasing expectation’ that exit opportunities arise in January. The England youth international is currently under contract until 2028, and separate reports have said that a new deal is being eyed.

Peck’s impressive displays have seen his stock surge. He’s a regular in the England U21s setup and his hat-trick against Preston North End showed he has potential to add goals to his game from midfield too.

It means that belief in his ability to step up to the Premier League is growing, and Sheffield United’s uphill battle to break into the Championship promotion fight may well speed up the process. However, Peck himself is in no rush just yet.

Asked about his future after summer transfer speculation, the SUFC star expressed a desire to repay the faith shown in him by Wilder and the first-team and academy coaching staff at Bramall Lane. Speaking to The Star in August, Peck said:

“I joined when I was 16 and the gaffer’s given me every debut since. From Premier League to Championship and in the cup. The staff as well, Mickey Collins. I’ve got a lot of stuff to pay back. They’ve helped me become the player I am today.”

Those comments certainly don’t show a burning desire to move on to a higher level. His loyalty would indicate he has little intention of leaving as early as January too, despite his progress and the growing interest.

Amid this new report and fresh claims from TEAMtalk, Peck’s own words would suggest he is unlikely to exit as early as January.

Peck’s comments aren’t the only reason West Ham could have a tough time persuading him to join. The Hammers spent big on a new midfielder in Arne Engels in the summer, and with all of Soungoutou Magassa, Edson Alvarez, Mohamadou Kante, Tomas Soucek, Divine Mukasa and Lewis Orford on the books, there is not a burning need for another player in this position.

Peck will want to go somewhere he is a regular starter, just like he is at Sheffield United, and that may not be the case with the Hammers.

Furthermore, given his commitment to SUFC, it would be a surprise to see him leave mid-season for a club at the same level. If he is to be prised away from the Blades, it seems more likely that Peck heads for the Premier League.

Bitcoin’s rate-cut hopes face a $5 diesel problem in 2027

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cheaper crude may not bring the fuel-price relief Bitcoin needs: damaged refineries are keeping the 2027 diesel forecast above $5 a gallon.

The US Energy Information Administration raised its forecast for next year’s retail diesel price by 12%, reducing the fuel-cost relief it previously expected. That leaves less room for falling fuel costs to cool inflation and bring the lower interest rates that could help Bitcoin, even as the agency projects cheaper crude in 2027.

The EIA’s November 10 outlook puts the 2027 US diesel average at $5.03 a gallon, up from $4.49 in its October 6 forecast. That is an upward revision to the same annual forecast, rather than a 12% increase in observed pump prices.

Diesel is still projected to fall from a forecast $5.25 average in 2026. EIA also expects Brent crude to average $86 a barrel in 2027, down from a projected $97 this year. Inputs were finalized November 5, so the outlook does not specifically account for subsequent market events.

Cheaper crude does not guarantee a matching decline in finished-fuel costs. EIA assumes petroleum-product supply constraints will continue through the end of 2027, supporting wider crack spreads: the gap between refined-product prices and crude costs.

The agency attributes elevated refining margins to damaged refineries in the Middle East and Russia, which have reduced product exports. It expects disruptions to be concentrated early next year and constraints to ease thereafter, while warning that unplanned outages and deferred maintenance could disrupt refinery operations.

US refinery crude inputs are forecast at 16.5 million barrels a day in 2027, against 16.7 million this year, despite similar utilization. Running the available refineries hard therefore does not necessarily mean processing more crude.

Persistent fuel costs can reach prices beyond direct energy components. In her November 10 speech, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman warned that higher production and transportation costs could pass into consumer prices and keep core inflation elevated. She favors looking through temporary energy inflation when possible, while watching for broader price and expectations effects.

For Bitcoin, the connection runs through monetary policy and investors’ willingness to take risk. A 2023 IMF working paper found that US monetary tightening depresses a common crypto-price factor through a risk-taking channel, similar to equities. If fuel costs prolong underlying inflation and delay monetary easing, that channel could constrain Bitcoin risk appetite.

There are reasons that risk could fade. Ex-Serie A flop Jardel opens up on drug abuse: ‘I nearly died’

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Former Ancona striker Mario Jardel opens up on his alcohol and drug abuse: ‘I nearly died; a friend of mine saved my life.’

The former Brazilian striker played for Ancona in Serie A in the second half of the 2003-04 campaign. He joined the Italian club from Bolton in the January transfer window.

“I only have bad memories. In six months, I didn’t get paid, and I played just three matches,” the ex-striker told La Gazzetta dello Sport about his brief spell in Ancona.

“The squad was packed full of players, poorly run, and went bankrupt at the end of the year. I bonded with Sonetti and Galeone, much less so with the fans.

“I was 31, I was overweight, and someone called me ‘Lardel’. Probably because when they unveiled me against Perugia I saluted the wrong end: we had the same colours. Some fans took offence after that gesture,” the former striker added.

“Jorge Mendes. He wasn’t my agent, but a close friend,” Jardel replied.

“He had contacts in Italy, mentioned my name to some agents and somehow they brought me to Ancona. I was looking for a club where I could play regularly and recover after the nightmare of missing out on the 2002 World Cup, but mentally I never recovered.”

Between 2001 and 2003, Jardel had scored 67 goals in 62 appearances with Sporting CP, still not enough to earn a call-up to the Brazil national team for the 2002 World Cup, which the South Americans won.

“I fell into depression and started drinking excessively,” Jardel admitted.

“Playing in a World Cup was the dream of my life. My first wife left me, and there were issues regarding the custody of our children. I felt that everything was slipping away from me, but the worst was yet to come.”

But the ex-Serie A flop hadn’t hit rock bottom yet.

“In the summer of 2003, I tried cocaine. I was on holiday, and I knew I could take it during that period because there was no doping control,” he said.

“I liked it; it made me feel alive and not depressed. Once, I stayed awake for an entire week. Then I had an overdose; I nearly died, and a friend who was with me saved my life. That was where I hit rock bottom.

“I spent months speaking with psychiatrists,” he continued.

“I came out of it for good when I met my second wife, Sandra: she made me understand the meaning of life, and today I’m happy.”

The former has returned to Brazil and is an investor.

“In Fortaleza, the property market is very popular, and it’s a good way to earn money. In my spare time, I enjoy the sun on the beach, although I miss Italy,” he concluded.

Merchandising For Bots: The Critical Questions Retail Leaders Aren't Yet Asking

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zohar Gilad is cofounder and CEO of Fast Simon.

In a recent survey, 24% of shoppers said they would be comfortable letting an AI agent shop for them. While AI-driven traffic to online stores is still a small share of visits, it’s growing quickly.

These numbers will undoubtedly increase. So what happens when AI becomes the intermediary between a brand’s catalog and the shopper?

Because the technology is developing so rapidly, anyone claiming to have a definitive answer or playbook on merchandising for AI is suspect. The more useful exercise is to ask harder questions about what happens to product discovery when an AI system starts deciding which products a shopper sees.

Here are four issues that AI-savvy retailers should be thinking about now.

Traditional SEO taught marketers to compete for visibility inside a ranking system: Identify search intent, optimize pages, earn authority and improve your position.

Now, a new system has emerged: AI (or generative) engine optimization (AEO/GEO).

Unlike keywords, AI prompts are essentially infinite and personalized, and countless different prompts can be clustered around the exact same consumer intent. Consider these requests:

• Find me a breathable, floral dress for under $200.

• Give me a romantic, cottage-core dress appropriate for a guest at a summer wedding.

• I’m going to an outdoor wedding next month, and it’s going to be 85 degrees; what dress will look elevated but won’t show sweat?

Each prompt could lead AI to recommend the same product or three different ones. With open-ended prompts, there’s little predictability.

The answer isn’t to optimize for every possibility, but to make the underlying product information understandable to AI. Then it can connect a product to the many different ways a shopper might describe it (fabric, weight, seasonality, occasion, etc.).

That creates a new merchandising question: How do you structure your catalog to map to these infinite systems? And when the system deciding what gets surfaced is contextual and constantly changing, how do you reliably earn product visibility?

AI agents are well-suited for the shopping that consumers view as bothersome chores: groceries, household essentials and even the frustrating process of finding a holiday gift for Aunt Mathilda.

Conversely, shopping for high-consideration items, like a designer handbag, involves a desire to dream, be inspired and browse. Many shoppers actively enjoy this process and wouldn’t want to hand it off to an AI agent.

That prompts the question: Which parts of the shopping experience would a consumer actually want to delegate?

AI’s impact will likely be most immediate in the “chores” space. Merchandisers selling commodities should consider what happens when AI can compare products on price or availability without exposing the consumer to the brand experience.

Which parts of your catalog become commodities once an AI agent strips away the marketing and compares products side by side? Even for commoditized items, how will descriptions, origin countries and imagery influence the agent’s final selection?

The more important question might be what happens on the consumer’s end. If discovery is part of your value proposition, what does an AI agent remove from the brand experience?

Traditional search lets consumers scroll through and click on numerous options. But as AI search models try to catch up to human discovery habits, they distill endless results down to three to five recommendations.

When presented with AI’s narrowed choices, humans default to convenience, accepting what AI shares. If ChatGPT presents three options for the best running shoes, the shopper is unlikely to look elsewhere for a fourth option.

So how does a brand secure its place in the coveted golden set of results when AI is significantly limiting consumer choice?

In traditional search, a retailer competes for page-one visibility and relies on the shopper to browse. In AI search, the retailer may need to earn consideration before the shopper ever sees the product.

That could create a new competitive real estate, a small shelf of products that AI deemed worth showing. And if AI systems are increasingly determining which products matter, how will this narrowing of options alter market competition?

Agentic commerce is making it easier for AI systems to access structured product information, compare products and eventually complete transactions on a shopper’s behalf.

That creates an uncomfortable question for merchandisers: If AI controls the interface, what happens to the careful curation they’ve built into the catalog?

We’re already moving in this direction. For example, Shopify’s model context protocol (MCP) server displays a brand’s entire catalog to AI agents, leaving all the product discovery power in the hands of AI platforms.

In this instance, a retailer’s products may be more accessible to AI, but accessibility isn’t the same as merchandising.

Merchandising is about deciding which products to emphasize, how to group them and what story to tell around them. An AI agent can potentially bypass much of that curation and make its own decisions about what to show the shopper.

To exert more influence over the shopping process, brands may need to deploy their own AI to interface with the shopper’s AI.

But what will that look like? Will merchants use their own AI to negotiate with or even manipulate the consumer’s shopping agent? How can retailers use technology to restore their product discovery power?

The transition toward merchandising for bots is imminent, but a one-size-fits-all approach will fail.

The first question for retailers is what they’re willing to give up when AI becomes the intermediary between their catalog and the customer.

As AI shopper agents threaten to remove some of the merchandiser’s control, retailers should resist the urge to immediately rewrite their strategies based on unproven theories. The brands poised for success are those that are actively interrogating their catalog’s purpose, data structure and defensive posture against AI intermediaries.

Scientists Ranked 20 Protein-Rich Foods on Health, Affordability, and Sustainability. One Surprising Food Came Out on Top

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tofu took the crown in a showdown of 20 protein-rich foods judged on health, affordability, availability, and their toll on the planet.

Protein-rich foods can look very different when cost, access, health, and environmental impact all enter the picture. In a study published in Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems, Dr. Chris Macdonald developed a framework that combines these four considerations to compare commonly consumed protein sources.

Among the 20 foods assessed, tofu took the top spot, while cheddar cheese finished last. The results followed a clear pattern: plant-based foods occupied the entire highest-scoring fifth, and animal-based products made up the lowest-scoring fifth.

Tofu’s lead came from strong results across both parts of the assessment. It earned a perfect 16 points for environmental performance, placing in the best-performing fifth for greenhouse gas emissions, water pollution, land use, and freshwater use. It also scored 20 out of 24 points for health and accessibility, including full marks for providing all nine essential amino acids.

Together, these measures explain why the rankings go beyond protein content alone. They weigh the environmental costs of producing protein alongside nutritional factors and practical considerations such as price and availability.

The paper argues that ratings focused on one feature can leave consumers with an incomplete picture. A product’s performance in one area does not explain its trade-offs elsewhere. Macdonald presents the framework as a way to help people understand those competing considerations and the food systems their purchases support.

As the paper states, “Against deliberate corporate obfuscation and marketing myths, the goal of a holistic score is not to create a new label, but to foster a new literacy, one that empowers us to see the reality of the systems we are funding.

Ultimately, beyond the spreadsheets and scores, lies a fundamental reckoning: our protein choice is a referendum on the compassion we are willing to show to animals, to the planet, and to future generations.”

The research builds on Macdonald’s work at the Better Protein Institute, which examines how much protein people need for optimal health, how to shift consumer behavior effectively, and where investment could support future protein sources. By questioning common assumptions and starting from first principles, that work addresses some of the most frequently Googled questions about dietary protein.

A Look At The Salaries Of US Navy Carrier Air Wing Commanders In 2026

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A US Navy Carrier Air Wing Commander (CAG) in 2026 oversees roughly 2,400 personnel, dozens of aircraft, and one of the most complex operational organizations in modern military aviation, but how much does that responsibility actually pay? Following the 3.8% military pay raise implemented under the FY26 NDAA, a Navy Captain (O-6) serving as a CAG can earn well over $200,000 annually once housing allowances, Aviation Incentive Pay, and deployment-related compensation are included.

At a time when commercial airlines continue recruiting experienced military aviators with salaries that can exceed $500,000 per year, CAG compensation has become part of a much larger conversation about retention, naval aviation readiness, and the future of carrier air wings built around the Boeing F/A-18E/F Super Hornet Block III and Lockheed MartinF-35C Lightning II.

Taking into account 2026 DFAS pay tables, Military-Ranks.org compensation data, Glassdoor salary submissions, Navy personnel policies, and official US Navy organizational information, this article breaks down the major factors shaping a Carrier Air Wing Commander’s earnings in 2026. The analysis examines several key areas: base pay and allowances, aviation incentive compensation, how CAG salaries compare with other senior naval aviators and airline pilots, and the operational realities that justify one of the Navy’s most demanding leadership roles.

A Carrier Air Wing Commander holds the rank of Navy Captain, pay grade O-6. According to the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS) 2026 pay tables, O-6 base pay starts at $8,067.90 per month for officers with fewer than two years of service at that grade, but a CAG, by definition, is not a junior captain. Most officers who earn a carrier air wing command have accumulated over 20 years of service, placing them firmly at the upper end of the pay scale.

At 20+ years, O-6 monthly base pay reaches $12,638.40 per month, and at maximum longevity steps it climbs to $14,282.40 per month — though federal law caps O-6 base pay at $15,000.00 per month. That translates to an annual base salary of roughly $151,660 to $171,388 for a typical CAG, before a single dollar of allowances or special pay is added.

The 2026 figure already reflects a 3.8% pay raise that took effect on January 1, 2026, after President Donald Trumpsigned the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2026 (FY26 NDAA) into law on December 18, 2025. That raise, as noted by NavyCS, was mandated by U.S.C. Title 37 and applies uniformly across all active military branches.

For historical perspective, that same O-6 pay grade in 2020 topped out near $12,638 per month; The accumulated raises of the last six years have added well over $1,600 per month to a senior captain's baseline earnings. That upward trajectory has been consistent, partly driven by Congress responding to retention pressures as the commercial airline industry entered one of its largest hiring cycles in history.

Base pay tells only part of the story. Military compensation is deliberately structured as a package, with non-taxable allowances and incentive pay that can add tens of thousands of dollars annually. For a Carrier Air Wing Commander, those components typically include several distinct streams. According to Military-Ranks.org, when the most common allowances are included, total annual compensation for an O-6 Navy Captain in 2026 ranges from $131,113 to as much as $205,687.

The largest single add-on is the Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH), a non-taxable, location-dependent payment designed to offset the cost of off-base housing. The average BAH for an O-6 with dependents sits at $3,075.37 per month ($36,904 per year), though at high-cost duty stations like Naval Air Station North Island in San Diego, CA, or the Washington, D.C. area, that figure rises substantially above the national average. Alongside BAH, every commissioned officer also receives a Basic Allowance for Subsistence (BAS) to offset food costs: according to The Military Wallet, the 2026 officer BAS rate is $328.48 per month ($3,941 per year), non-taxable, and uniform regardless of rank or assignment.

Aviation Incentive Pay (AvIP) is a further pillar of aviator compensation: as documented in the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS), AvIP maxes out at $1,000 per month ($12,000 per year) for aviators with 14 or more years of aviation service, a threshold any Carrier Air Wing Commander has long cleared. Finally, when a CAG's air wing deploys to an active combat zone or operates in an area subject to hostile fire, an additional hazardous duty payment applies: that rate stands at up to $250 per month ($3,000 per year).

Cutting across all of these allowances is a tax advantage that civilian compensation packages simply cannot replicate: BAH and BAS are entirely exempt from federal income tax, meaningfully increasing real take-home value compared to a nominally equivalent civilian salary. As already explained by Simple Flying, compensation for senior naval aviators rises sharply at the command and flag-officer levels, reflecting both operational responsibility and years of specialized aviation service.

To understand why a CAG earns what they earn, the compensation must be weighed against the sheer scope of the role. The "CAG" title dates to World War II, when carrier aviation was organized into "Air Groups" rather than the modern "Air Wings." The formal designation changed in 1963, but the acronym stayed as a small, enduring tribute to the role's lineage.

In 2026, the CAG commands a carrier air wing that typically embeds several squadrons aboard a Nimitz- or Gerald R. Ford-class carrier, operating strike fighter jets, electronic warfare aircraft, early warning platforms, helicopters, and logistics aircraft in an integrated fashion. As reported by LegalClarity.org, "a CAG is a Navy Captain (pay grade O-6) who oversees roughly 2,400 personnel and dozens of aircraft spanning multiple squadron types, making it one of the most demanding leadership positions in naval aviation."

That means a CAG commands more personnel than many Army colonels and manages an air arm more complex than the entire air forces of several NATO nations. As we recently noted, the Super Hornet remains the backbone of current carrier air wings, with the Block III upgrade program ensuring it will remain central to CAG operations well into the 2040s, which also means today's CAG must be conversant in cutting-edge avionics, networked warfare, and the integration of fifth-generation aircraft like the F-35C Lightning II alongside legacy platforms.

According to Glassdoor's March 2026 data, based on 117 salary submissions from active and former O-6 Navy Captains, the estimated average total salary is $203,144 per year, with a range from roughly $159,193 (25th percentile) to $263,446 (75th percentile). These numbers, while self-reported and not official, align well with the computed figures derived from the DFAS pay tables plus standard allowances, and indicate that real-world total compensation for a CAG falls comfortably in the $200,000+ range.

Placing the CAG's salary in context requires a look at both one rank below and one rank above. An O-5 Commander, the rank that typically commands an individual fighter or strike squadron aboard the carrier, earns a base pay of roughly $9,929 per month ($119,148 per year) at the 10-year mark, rising to a maximum of $12,280 per month.

That makes the O-6 CAG's base pay approximately 15–20% higher in dollar terms, but the gap in responsibility is far wider: a CAG runs nine squadrons and 2,400 people, whereas an O-5 squadron commander runs a single unit of roughly 200 to 300 personnel. Above the CAG sits the Rear Admiral (O-7 and O-8) tier, where base pay is no longer capped at $15,000 per month but instead climbs toward the Level II Executive Schedule cap of $18,808.20 per month.

A Rear Admiral Lower Half (O-7) with a carrier strike group command earns between $12,803.70 and $18,458.10 per month in base pay alone, or roughly $153,600 to $221,500 annually, meaningfully above the CAG's ceiling. The trade-off is that the path from CAG to flag officer is intensely competitive: a very small fraction of O-6s earn their first star.

The commercial aviation comparison is, as always, illustrative. According to our previous analysis of USAF pilot salaries, senior airline captains flying major widebody international routes can earn over $550,000 annually in 2026, roughly three times the total compensation of a Navy O-6 with full allowances. The financial gap is simply not closable from a base pay perspective, which is precisely why Aviation Retention Bonuses (AvB) and quality-of-life factors remain critical levers in keeping experienced naval aviators in uniform through the CAG pipeline.

Every salary figure cited in this article comes with an important context that can shift the real value of compensation significantly. The BAH figure, for example, is highly location-sensitive: an O-6 stationed at Naval Air Station Lemoore (in California's Central Valley) receives a different BAH rate than one based at NAS Oceana in Virginia Beach, VA, or attached to a forward-deployed unit in Japan.

Navy personnel assigned to overseas bases like NAF Atsugi or MCAS Iwakuni receive Overseas Housing Allowance (OHA) instead of BAH, calculated differently and potentially higher. On some overseas assignments, additional Cost of Living Allowances (COLA) can add several hundred dollars per month on top of that.

Deployment also changes the compensation calculus. When a carrier strike group deploys to a combat zone, as multiple carrier strike groups did across the Red Sea, the eastern Mediterranean, and the Indo-Pacific in recent years, service members may qualify for hostile fire pay ($225/month), tax exclusion on base pay earned in a combat zone (a benefit that, for an O-6, can represent a tax saving of $30,000 to $40,000 per year), and in some cases imminent danger pay.

Readers should also note that Aviation Bonus (AvB) figures are not included in the totals above. AvB contracts are negotiated at the end of a service obligation and are not universally offered or accepted at the CAG career point. While some O-6 aviators do receive retention bonuses, these are managed at the Navy Bureau of Personnel (BUPERS) level and vary by community need, timing, and individual agreements. A CAG who is within two to three years of potential retirement may or may not be eligible for or interested in a new AvB contract.

A Carrier Air Wing Commander in 2026 earns a base salary of approximately $151,660 to $171,388 per year. When standard allowances, Aviation Incentive Pay, and broader military benefits are included, total compensation typically rises to between $200,000 and $225,000 annually, with the possibility of climbing even higher during combat deployments.

These are numbers that would make any senior manager in the civilian industry pause, but they also reflect the extraordinary scale of the role itself. A CAG effectively commands the equivalent of a mid-sized air force from the flight deck of a 100,000-ton nuclear-powered aircraft carrier, overseeing roughly 2,400 personnel and dozens of aircraft while operating in some of the world’s most demanding and strategically sensitive environments.

The 3.8% military pay raise implemented on January 1, 2026, represents another attempt by Congress and the Pentagon to narrow the compensation gap between military aviation and the civilian airline industry. Even so, the difference remains significant. Senior airline captains flying the largest commercial widebody aircraft still routinely earn two to three times more than a CAG, a reality that continues to influence Navy retention efforts for highly experienced strike aviators. At the same time, the job itself is becoming increasingly complex.

Ongoing Block III Super Hornet upgrades, the continued integration of the F-35C, and the Navy’s evolving carrier air wing structure are making the CAG position more technically demanding than ever, even as commercial airlines continue competing aggressively for the same pool of elite aviators.
