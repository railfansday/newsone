Medicare doesn’t pay for long-term care, so in your 50s, find out what care costs where you live while your health still qualifies you for coverage. Then choose how you’d pay by what you’ve saved: your own money if you’re wealthy, Medicaid if your assets are modest, and long-term care insurance if you’re in between.

Almost 70% of people turning 65 will need long-term care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and other daily tasks, according to the Administration for Community Living. One in five will need it for more than five years.

Your age and health set what insurance costs and whether you can buy it. Most individual long-term care policies require a health review, so a diagnosis in your 60s can raise your price or rule you out.

The American Association for Long-Term Care Insurance’s 2026 price index shows a couple, both 55, paying about $5,000 a year combined, on average. That buys each of them a policy whose care benefits start at $165,000 and grow 3% a year. At 65, the same coverage averages about $7,000 a year.

Start by looking up typical prices in your area with CareScout’s cost of care calculator. Nationally, CareScout’s 2025 survey puts an in-home caregiver 44 hours a week at $80,080 a year, assisted living at $74,400, and a private nursing home room at $129,575.

Talk with your family, too, about who would give care and whether you’d stay in your own home or move to a facility. Ask your parents the same questions about their own care.

Get quotes for both kinds from an independent agent who sells several insurers’ policies. Identical coverage for an Illinois couple, both 60, ranged from $4,591 to $7,173 a year across five insurers in the same price index.

Newborn stars are hiding in the red threads of a neighboring galaxy

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Webb peers into N159, where dusty filaments shelter some of the youngest stars in a neighboring galaxy.

Today’s Image of the Day takes us inside a cloud where stars are being born in a neighboring galaxy.

In this view from the James Webb Space Telescope, pale blue gas fills broad cavities, orange pillars jut from their edges, and dark red threads wind across the middle.

Some of the youngest stars in the scene are still tucked inside those threads.

The cloud, known as N159, lies about 161,000 light-years away in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

Webb’s image spans about 230 light-years. Across that distance, some stars are still gathering the material they need to grow, while others already shine fiercely enough to change the shape of the cloud around them.

Start with the pale blue areas. They trace hydrogen gas exposed to intense ultraviolet light from massive stars.

That radiation can knock electrons loose from hydrogen atoms. As the electrons rejoin hydrogen nuclei, the gas gives off light of its own.

A bright patch can therefore reveal where massive stars are affecting their surroundings, even if the glow itself comes from the gas.

Those stars also send streams of particles into the cloud. Over time, these stellar winds can help hollow out cavities and sculpt the denser material along their edges.

Look closely at where the blue meets the orange. Small pillars extend into the open spaces, marking places where pockets of dense gas and dust have persisted as material around them has been cleared away.

The bright knot on the left is the Papillon Nebula, an unusually compact pocket of glowing hydrogen that the Hubble Space Telescope first resolved in 1999.

In Webb’s wider view, it takes up only a small part of the frame. Its intense glow draws the eye, but the quieter areas around it hold some of the best clues to where stars are forming now.

One of the best places to zoom in is just above the blue cavity on the right. Thin red strands cross this part of the frame.

They trace molecular hydrogen, a form of the gas in which two hydrogen atoms are bound together.

Deep inside dense strands like these, gas and dust can shield developing stars from the radiation and winds of their more powerful neighbors.

Webb’s Near-Infrared Camera records light at wavelengths beyond what our eyes can see.

Some of that light gets through dust that blocks visible starlight, revealing points of light buried in the cloud.

Image processors assign visible colors to observations made through different filters.

Here, those colors distinguish the red threads of molecular gas from the orange glow of carbon-based molecules and the pale blue of ionized hydrogen.

Astronomer Nolan Habel and colleagues compared the brightness of more than 300,000 points of light across ten infrared filters.

Some gave off more infrared light than expected from a star alone. That extra light can come from surrounding dust, which absorbs energy from the star and radiates it back into space.

Many of the sources the researchers identified as possible young stars lined up with dusty strands and clouds of molecular gas.

In a preprint describing the observations, the team reported that the eastern part of N159 contains more developed groups of stars, while the western part shows signs of more recent star formation.

Some groups may be less than half a million years old. That difference suggests that star formation unfolded in stages across the cloud.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has a smaller proportion of elements heavier than helium than the Milky Way does.

The early universe was also poorer in those elements, which built up over generations of stars.

Studying a region like N159 gives astronomers a chance to see how stars form with a mix of ingredients closer in some respects to what was available long ago.

The image also captures a change that is easy to miss at first glance. In one part of the cloud, stars have already opened clearings around themselves.

In another, dense gas still shelters stars that are just getting started. Given time, much of that gas will disperse, leaving the stars exposed.

For now, the thin red strands mark places where some of the newest stars in this corner of a neighboring galaxy are still taking shape.

How I Had to Learn to Stop Saying “Yes” When I Meant “No”

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern. Truly growing beyond alcohol often requires us to develop new skills and tools to navigate the challenges we once relied on alcohol to handle. For the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop, learning to say no is one of those skills.

Fortunately, like most skills, saying no is something we can learn through practice. Personally, I've found that having a few ready-to-use ways to say no makes a world of difference. That's why I've put together 7 of my favorite scripts for saying no with grace.
