The world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange by volume, Binance is witnessing a rapid surge in its futures trading activity as the broader crypto market continues to face increased selling pressure.

While the market is still struggling with volatility and rapidly declining investor interest, the latest on-chain data from the crypto analytics platform CryptoQuant shows that futures traders have become extremely active on Binance.

The data shows that both Bitcoin and Ethereum have seen their perpetual futures trading volume on Binance surge to a combined high of $39.8 billion as of November 20.

Notably, this marks the highest level the metric has reached since September 21, indicating that traders are actively adjusting their positions to hedge against further losses as volatility continues to intensify.

Nonetheless, Bitcoin saw the highest increase in perpetual futures volume as it surged to $24.75 billion on the same day.

The data further showed that the volume was slightly higher than the $24.57 billion recorded on September 21, when Bitcoin was trading above $87,000.

Ethereum, on the other hand, followed with a notable increase in the same metric, recording $15.05 billion in perpetual futures volume on the same day.

However, Ethereum did not entirely mimic Bitcoin in the trend as this was still below the $17.27 billion recorded during the September 21 trading period.

Further data shows that the high futures trading activity recorded on Binance on November 20 has coincided with BlackRock’s record Bitcoin ETF outflow.

The data shows that BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) recorded $511 million in net outflows on that day, marking a sharp reversal from the positive inflows seen in September.

While this shows that investors are also pulling their funds from Bitcoin ETFs, it marked the highest single-day outflow BlackRock has ever recorded.

Wusongkou Forum Unveils River-Sea Cruise Plan and Consumption Index

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The 2026 Wusongkou Forum took place on Nov. 8 at the Shanghai International Cruise Tourism Resort in Baoshan. It brought together regulators, port authorities, cruise operators and industry experts to discuss what organizers called “three-cruise synergy,” which is the coordinated development of ocean cruise ships, river cruise vessels and yachts.

Hosted by Shanghai Wusongkou Culture & Tourism Investment (Group) Co., the event included the launch of a “Three-Cruise Synergy · A New Voyage from River to Sea” program. Four initiatives were announced.

The first is a “Cruise Plus” brand renewal plan. It targets four consumer segments: seniors, weddings and romance travel, sporting events and younger travelers. The goal is to turn passenger volume into more onboard and shoreside spending.

The second is a river-sea linkage project that uses Wusongkou’s location at the mouth of the Yangtze to connect inland river cities with ocean itineraries. Its flagship product is the Yangtze No. 3, which has begun sailing between Chongqing and Shanghai from the Wusongkou International Cruise Terminal. It is the first river-sea cruise service to cover the full length of the Yangtze.

Third, China Huaxia International Cruise Co. released October 2026 data for its China Cruise Consumption Vitality Index. The company described it as the country’s first regular data tool for the cruise market.

The fourth initiative links the cruise terminal with Shanghai Baoshan railway station, which is due to open next year as the meeting point of the Shanghai–Chongqing–Chengdu high-speed line and the Shanghai–Nantong railway. Plans include combined rail and cruise ticketing, a direct shuttle, baggage transfer from car door to cabin door and shared VIP benefits.

On forum day, the Adora Magic City and the Adora Flora City, China’s first two domestically built large cruise ships, were berthed together at Wusongkou for the first time.

Hailey Bieber’s It-Jacket Wardrobe Is Becoming Untenable

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hailey Bieber must have some serious wardrobe storage. The Rhode founder has long been a devoted collector of excellent outerwear—oversized leather bombers, vintage furs, sculptural shearling, and the occasional extravagant Conner Ives robe—but her latest run of looks in New York has taken things to another level. At this point, we’re struggling to keep up with her ever-expanding collection of It-jackets.

The last few days have seen Bieber leave just about everybody else out in the cold. On November 17, she stepped out a Phoebe Philo shaggy Dancer jacket: cropped, voluminous, in mottled brown and gray shearling. She paired it with her trusty light-wash Levi’s Ribcage jeans, vintage black Manolo Blahnik sandals, and amber-tinted Gucci sunglasses. The jacket, now sold out, was the undisputed star of the outfit. It’s a tried-and-true Bieber styling trick and very much a formula to follow this season.

The following day, she dipped into Carolyn Bessette-Kennedy’s wardrobe playbook for an appearance at the New York Times DealBook Summit. Bieber wore a black Yohji Yamamoto jacket from spring 1998, paired with a white pencil skirt and black pumps. The same design was famously worn by Bessette-Kennedyin 1998. It was a major archival find.

But she wasn’t finished. Later, Bieber emerged in an ivory satin opera coat by New York-based Oriori Maison, framed by a dramatic swathe of voluminous gray-white fur, worn with black trousers, dark sunglasses, and her trusty Inez pumps from Reformation.

The designer behind the coat, Xiaoxi Zhang, tells Vogue that Bieber’s stylist contacted her directly to arrange the pull. For Zhang, whose designs explore Chinese heritage through a contemporary wardrobe, the moment is particularly meaningful. “I’ve always loved my cultural heritage, but I’ve never really worn traditional Chinese clothing,” Zhang shared. “Before making my first Chinese-inspired jacket, my intention was always to find a way to bring elements of Chinese culture into everyday fashion—to make heritage feel natural, modern, and wearable.”

Zhang made her first jacket in 2024, initially just for herself, rather than as a business venture. Discovering vintage quilts helped crystallize her approach. “It was the beginning of seeing my culture through my own perspective, rather than simply recreating the traditional,” she says. Her luxurious satin-and-fur creation has now found its way onto one of fashion’s most closely watched dressers. Her one-of-one pieces using sumptuous archival textiles are dropped via Instagram, if you want to emulate this excellent look.

By November 19, Bieber was back in black leather, arriving at Rigor Hill Market in a glossy, longline leather coat, relaxed trousers, and black shoes worn with white socks.

Of course, Bieber’s expansive love of leather outerwear is nothing new. Earlier this fall, she was spotted in a fringed leather blazer, while her collection of oversized bombers and sharply tailored jackets has long been the foundation of her off-duty wardrobe. What’s changed is the sheer variety: from minimalist archival tailoring to flamboyant, fur-trimmed opera coats, Bieber is evidently having fun with her outerwear, heralding a post-quiet luxury era that keeps things cozy.

And she’s certainly not alone. As Vogue recently observed, the personality coat is having a major moment, with Jennifer Lawrence embracing Colleen Allen’s chartreuse velvet housecoat, and other fashion favorites turning to extravagant silhouettes and trimmings.

If there’s one thing we’ve learned from Hailey Bieber’s fall wardrobe, it’s that you can never have too many great jackets. Though, at this rate, she may need another home to house them all.

Indian auteur Girish Kasaravalli looked back on his career in Kannada-language cinema at the 57th International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa, in a masterclass titled “The Moral Universe of Parallel Cinema: 50 Years of Girish Kasaravalli.”

The 75-year-old spoke about the moral contradictions of society and the relationship between cinematic form and social content. He also argued that even entertainment is a political act. The session was moderated by Variety‘s Asia editor Naman Ramachandran. Across films such as “Ghatashraddha” (1977), “Tabarana Kathe” (1986), “Thaayi Saheba” (1997), “Dweepa” (2002) and “Gulabi Talkies” (2008), Kasaravalli has kept returning to one question: how societies exclude individuals and communities, and how people who are themselves victims of oppressive structures can also become complicit in perpetuating them.

Asked whether the 1977 Kannada classic “Ghatashraddha,” which examines religious orthodoxy and social exclusion, still spoke to contemporary India, Kasaravalli said: “All my films talk about othering. I always talk about how society looks at some people from a different lens, and in that process they make them ‘other.'” He described this as “the biggest cruelty an individual or a group of people can impart on another person.”

Asked whether he considered himself a political filmmaker, Kasaravalli said: “Whoever makes something, whether he makes mainstream cinema or art cinema, he is making a political statement, either in support of the dominant ideology or opposing the dominant ideology. He cannot avoid it.” There is nothing called only entertainment, he added, and even entertainment is a political form.

On his characters, he said: “I don’t want any of my characters to become a kind of a mouthpiece to a certain ideology.” Drawing on Jean-Luc Godard, he compared understanding a character to buying a house: one must examine it from the outside and then from within before making a judgment. Similarly, a filmmaker must inhabit a character and then step back to assess it. For Kasaravalli, characters are too complex for a filmmaker to easily take sides.

On the evolution of his cinematic language, Kasaravalli described the visual construction of “Ghatashraddha,” as “ritualistic cinema.” “The style is not imposed on it; it comes from within the content itself,” he said. Since “Ghatashraddha” was about a ritual, he felt its cinematic form should reflect that structure.

Kasaravalli said he could not write a script until he had clarity about its images. “For me, the film is not merely making statements; it’s an attempt by the filmmaker to impart an experience.” He described writing detailed instructions for his crew, down to which hand a character uses to lift a glass, because even that changes the composition of a shot.

This attention to the relationship between character and environment shaped “Gulabi Talkies.” Kasaravalli told cinematographer S. Ramachandra that the coastal community had to be as important as the protagonist. He wanted the images to feel like “actuality coverage,” not a predetermined visual construction. Rejecting conventional scenic shots of the sea and rocks, he told his cinematographer: “Film should not have any pretty image.” They chose 16mm, allowing the grain to contribute to the film’s sense of actuality.

The discussion of visual aesthetics brought him to Cuban filmmaker Julio García Espinosa’s idea of “imperfect cinema,” which, for Kasaravalli, challenged the assumption that Western aesthetics should define cinematic perfection. “I don’t want to take your aesthetic sensibility as my sensibility,” he said, quoting Espinosa. Kasaravalli said each country and each region has its own aesthetic concerns. “We should make films according to that condition; we should never imitate the West. Why should I be perfect? I should be loyal to my own milieu.”

Asked whether parallel cinema belonged to a particular period, Kasaravalli disagreed, pointing to notable films being made across the country today. The larger problem, he said, was reaching audiences. Of streaming platforms, he said: “We all thought it is going to be a solution for all of us. But OTT [streaming] is exactly doing what the mainstream distribution network was doing earlier.”

In the audience Q&A, he addressed open endings and whether a filmmaker should provide answers. “A filmmaker who says that he knows answers for all the questions in life is telling a lie,” he said. “He is also as confused as you are. He only throws up options so that you can start thinking. There is nothing called closure… life is always open.”

As the session concluded, IFFI festival director Ashutosh Gowariker described Kasaravalli’s work as “textbooks,” saying he had returned to “Ghatashraddha” while developing a new film. He also singled out the filmmaker’s close-ups. “You let your actors simply be. You make the audience think and anticipate what the character is going through,” Gowariker said.

Marion Cotillard, who serves as the jury president at this year’s Marrakech Film Festival, says she prefers watching films with “as little information as possible.”

“I don’t know anything about the movies we’re gonna see, I haven’t seen a single image,” the Oscar-winning French actress said at the festival’s jury press conference on Saturday morning. “That’s how I love to see movies, I don’t want to have any information.” Related Stories Peter Alsted’s Animal Conservation Doc ‘Dr. Hedgehog’ Boarded by Autlook Filmsales Ahead of IDFA Forum (EXCLUSIVE) What Happens When Documentary Makers Sell Their Footage to AI Aggregators?

“I want to be like a virgin,” she quipped.

Ironically, she said she doesn’t think her role on the jury is to “judge movies.” “I consider myself as part of an audience: I’m here to be touched, to be taken by stories.”

Cotillard was also asked about “Inception,” in which she starred alongside Leonardo DiCaprio. Does she understand Christopher Nolan’s famously complex film? “I don’t know…I thought I had,” she said to laughter. “Maybe I have. And maybe I haven’t.”

The nine-member jury includes “Sex Education” and “Barbie” star Emma Mackey; “Phantom Thread” standout Vicky Krieps; Danish actor Claes Bang, who featured in Ruben Östlund’s Palme d’Or winner “The Square”; French actor-turned-director director Gilles Lellouche, known for “Beating Hearts”; “The Voice of Hind Rajab” helmer Kaouther Ben Hania; “All of Us Strangers” filmmaker Andrew Haigh; Franco-Senegalese helmer Ramata-Toulaye Sy; and Moroccan director-producer Saïd Hamich Benlarbi.

The jury will award the festival’s Étoile d’Or to one of the 14 films in competition, which consists of first and second features.

Cotillard said her only expectation she has in helping to select this year’s competition winner is “to be shaken, to be moved.”

“When I can watch a movie and I can laugh and smile and be questioned at the same time,” she continued. “When there’s a story that on the paper I wouldn’t be very interested [in] and the filmmaker who tells the story will open a new vision for me.”

She summed it up simply: “How do you judge art anyway?”

Mackey agrees with Cotillard that her role on the jury is to “receive the films as a spectator. “We’re here to learn, its very pure somehow,” the “Sex Education” star said. “I don’t pretend that I know everything and I’m here to learn and to absorb these films as as a cinema lover.”

“It’s an honor and I’m happy to spend a week watching two films a day, that’s amazing,” she added.

Jury members were also asked about the relatively strong female representation at Marrakech and its broader implications for gender equality in the industry. This year, women hold a majority of seats on the nine-member jury while six of the 14 films in competition were directed by women.

Krieps drew on her role as an Irish maid in Ryan Murphy’s “Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story” to reflect on the implications of this type of female representation on-screen. “I think women have had the role of oppressed and silent,” she said. “Often we had to hide, we had to disappear because otherwise we weren’t safe.”

“I think the role I play in ‘Monster’ is someone who becomes very visible in a time when people were supposed to be invisible,” she continued, adding that her character’s wild red hair and contemporary costuming could “help people see differently in the future.”

Sy, meanwhile, offered a more succinct response, suggesting that true progress will come when female representation is no longer a point of discussion.

“The day when we stop counting how many women there are in juries, in jobs, as directors, then it will be normal,” the Sengalese filmmaker said to enthusiastic applause. “We have to stop asking the question and counting how many there are.”

On Friday night, the festival opened with Jesse Eisenberg’s “The Debut,” a musical comedy starring Julianne Moore as a shy New Jersey housewife who auditions for a regional production. Though Eisenberg and Moore weren’t in attendance, the opening ceremony was still a starry affair with guests including Tilda Swinton and “Knives Out” director Rian Johnson, who is giving a talk at the festival on Sunday.

Mackey is next playing the White Witch in Greta Gerwig’s “Narnia: The Magician’s Nephew,” and told Variety on the red carpet that she was excited to meet Swinton — who played the role in the original film trilogy — for the first time at Marrakech.

“It’s thrilling, my absolute dream person,” she said, adding: “She’s the queen. I wouldn’t even know how to breathe around her, I don’t think.”

The Marrakech Film Festival runs through Nov. 28.

Brazilian Court Orders Arrest of the Buenavista Over Corazul Dispute

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A Brazilian court ordered the arrest of Corazul’s the Buenavista at the Port of Santos after the cruise line’s exclusive Brazilian distributor claimed it was owed about $270,000 (R$1.48 million), according to a court decision dated Nov. 13.

Judge Frederico dos Santos Messias of the São Paulo State Court of Justice’s specialized maritime court granted the request from R11 Travel Viagens e Turismo against GLCL Global Cruiseline Ltd., the Cyprus-registered company that operates as Corazul.

The judge issued the order without first hearing from Corazul, the decision showed.

The order barred the Buenavista from leaving Brazilian waters. The court said the ship was anchored off Santos for repairs.

The judge set security at about $323,000 (R$1.78 million) and said the ship would be released if Corazul deposited that amount or posted a bank guarantee.

According to the decision, R11 said Corazul owed about $1.27 million (R$6.96 million) under a four-year exclusive distribution agreement signed in March. That figure included about $780,000 (EUR 675,000) in unpaid quarterly marketing contributions. After subtracting about $1 million (R$5.48 million) in passenger funds it held, R11 put the net balance at about $270,000.

The filing, as summarized by the court, described a series of disruptions including the cancellation of the Buenavista’s European season in June; the cancellation of its Oct. 23 inaugural Brazilian cruise nine days before departure and complaints about onboard service on the Oct. 30 sailing. The company has since suspended operations and R11 has stopped selling cruises on the ship.

R11 said Corazul demanded $400,000 on Nov. 4 before it would operate the Nov. 6 departure. R11 refused.

The court noted that only two of the 11 sailings scheduled between Oct. 23 and Nov. 27 operated. Corazul also suspended operations until Nov. 30 and moved the end of its Brazil season up to March 5, 2027. The judge wrote that Corazul’s own communications showed a “serious liquidity collapse.”

The dispute is headed to arbitration in São Paulo under AMCHAM, the American Chamber of Commerce for Brazil. The decision stated that R11 must file within 30 days or the order lapses. Corazul has five days after being served to contest it.
