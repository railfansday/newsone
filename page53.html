TL;DR: NEAR AI has published a security incident report involving an SGLang vulnerability in legacy deployments. The company says it revoked exposed credentials and addressed the issue, while acknowledging that some requests could have been observed. Its findings distinguish confirmed exposure from data it has no evidence was accessed.

A security disclosure from NEAR AI highlights a familiar danger in the expanding AI-agent industry: sophisticated applications can inherit serious problems from the infrastructure they run on.

In a November 9 incident report, the organization described an SGLang remote-code-execution vulnerability affecting legacy deployment infrastructure. The investigation identified roughly 100 user details and 18 agents in the affected context, with two Cloud API keys exposed and subsequently revoked.

The company said there was no evidence that stored Private Chat content had been compromised. It also cautioned that some requests made on November 8 could potentially have been observed. Those are not contradictory statements: investigators may find no evidence of one category of access while being unable to rule out another.

AI applications are assembled from multiple components: model servers, request routers, authentication systems, logging and the user-facing product. SGLang is part of the infrastructure used to serve language models. A flaw at that level can create opportunities for attackers before a user ever interacts with an onchain wallet or signs a transaction.

Remote-code-execution issues can be particularly serious because they may allow an attacker to run unauthorized instructions in the affected environment. The scope of what follows depends on the privileges, network access and data available to that environment.

For readers, this means the incident should not be conflated with a vulnerability in the NEAR blockchain consensus rules or a general compromise of all NEAR wallets. The report concerns particular AI-serving infrastructure and its potential exposures.

According to the published account, the team investigated affected deployments, revoked the two exposed API keys, remediated the vulnerability and notified impacted parties. Those are necessary containment steps, though the effectiveness of remediation depends on whether other pathways remain open and whether credentials are rotated wherever they were used.

The report’s numerical counts also need careful interpretation. Approximately 100 user details and 18 agents do not mean 100 users lost funds or that every associated request was read. Exposure, confirmed access and financial loss are separate findings that should not be collapsed into one sensational claim.

Crypto firms are integrating AI into development, support, trading and operational workflows. An AI tool may eventually handle private business information even when it cannot directly sign onchain transactions. That raises the value of conventional safeguards: isolating services, limiting credentials, monitoring access and maintaining incident-response plans.

The broader institutional push toward blockchain infrastructure, including the NYSE’s tokenized-securities plans, depends on more than smart-contract security. Offchain services must also be robust. A well-audited ledger cannot compensate for a compromised application server.

Likewise, rising institutional attention to crypto assets, which NewsBTC tracks through developments such as Ether ETF flows, makes operational controls increasingly consequential. These links are contextual rather than a claim that those products were involved in this incident.

Affected users should rely on NEAR AI’s direct notifications and published guidance. Where they have used credentials in related services, reviewing access permissions and rotating keys is prudent. Users should not assume that ordinary NEAR wallet keys have been exposed simply because the company reported an infrastructure flaw.

The most important follow-up is a clear technical postmortem explaining affected versions, root cause and validation of the fixes. For companies building AI agents around finance, the episode is a reminder that infrastructure security and candid disclosure are both part of earning trust.

Ocarina Of Time Remake Sells 5.2 Million Copies In 4 Days

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It feels a little like everyone has been playing nothing but The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time for the last few days, and a first look at its sales figures might explain why it feels that way. 5.2 million copies sold in its first four days, putting it way up there in the Zelda sales rankings before the end of its first week.

In a statement shared by Nintendo (thanks, Wario64), the studio confirmed the Ocarina of Time Remake's massive launch sales, noting that 810,000 of those sales were in Japan. It also confirms that this is the fastest-selling Switch 2 exclusive so far, outpacing Donkey Kong Bananza, Pokemon Pokopia, and even Mario Kart World, which launched with the console.

And came bundled along with it for a lot of people.

You might see this news and say to yourself, "Well, of course a Zelda game has sold this well this fast," but Zelda games selling this well is still a fairly new phenomenon. So new that the Ocarina remake selling 5.2 million copies makes it the eighth best-selling game in the series after just four days, right above Phantom Hourglass, which sold 4.76 million, and right behind the Nintendo 3DS version of Ocarina, which sold 6.44 million copies.

Some of the bigger Zelda games the Ocarina of Time remake has managed to outsell in less than a week include A Link to the Past (4.61 million), The Wind Waker (4.43 million), A Link Between Worlds (4.26 million), and Skyward Sword (4.15 million). Now all that remains to be seen is just how high up the Zelda rankings the new Ocarina can climb.

Another 1.31 million copies, which it will likely hit within the next few days, and it will have outsold the original The Legend of Zelda on the NES. Hard to believe considering its status among video games as a whole, but the remake is already within touching distance of the original Ocarina of Time, which sold 7.6 million copies. The Nintendo 64's limited sales are largely to blame for that. The N64 finished its run having sold 32 million units. The Switch 2 will likely move past that during this coming holiday season since more than 26 million of them have been sold already.

That means around one in five Switch 2 owners bought Ocarina of Time within four days of its release.

Twilight Princess is the third best-selling Zelda game having sold 8.93 million copies across both its GameCube and Wii versions. It's whether the Ocarina remake can sell enough to breathe the same rarified air as this new era's Zelda games, Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom, and based on its launch numbers, it almost certainly can.

Breath of the Wild was the game that took the series to new heights and set a new standard for Zelda, launching with the Switch and selling almost 35 million copies. Tears of the Kingdom comfortably became the second best-selling Zelda game at 21.73 million copies. Ocarina might be big enough to hit those numbers too, especially as more Switch 2s are sold and along with the likes of Mario Kart World and Pokopia, it just becomes one of the games everyone feels like they have to buy when they get the console.

DW Science explainer on the default mode network: what your brain does when you aren't focusing on anything, and why it's important.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Doing nothing might be the ultimate brain hack. When you're bored, the brain goes into default mode network. It's not only healthy for you, but it may also shape the very essence of your identity.

You know when you lock your phone and the screen goes black? It seems like it's not doing anything. But it's not doing nothing. It's syncing your messages, updating apps, and running other background processes.

Imagine you're sitting on a bus, staring out of a window, taking a shower or walking, and you get that feeling of doing nothing and letting your mind drift?

Well, your mind, the brain, is doing a lot when it feels like you're doing nothing.

It's going over your memories, shaping your sense of who you are, imagining possible futures, helping you understand other people, and connecting ideas.

That's your brain's default mode network (DMN).

Scientists conducted brain-imaging experiments in the 1990s and early 2000s to find out which regions of the brain were active during demanding tasks, like solving a math problem or memory exercise.

But to measure that, they needed a so-called baseline. They needed to know the level of brain activity at rest and during demanding tasks.

So, between the tasks, they asked participants to relax, close their eyes, stare at a fixed point on a wall, or try not to think of anything at all.

And they measured the participants' brain activity both when they were solving a task and doing nothing. That's how scientists noticed the brain was active in up- and downtime.

They noticed that when participants started to focus on a new task, after having done nothing, some brain areas lit up and other areas dimmed down. That suggests those areas that dimmed down must have been active while the participant was apparently — physically and mentally — at rest.

This realization eventually led to the discovery of the default mode network or DMN.

The DMN is crucial because it helps your brain make sense of your life and experiences.

It helps you connect and revisit memories, reflect on who you are, imagine what might happen in future scenarios, understand other people, build meaning from experience, and connect ideas in new ways.

It turns all your memories, experiences, knowledge, emotions and social information into your internal story. Think of that story as a constantly evolving, updated model of who you are and how you engage with the world around you.

But it's important to understand the DMN involves more than one area of the brain. It is a group of brain regions that light up when you're not focused on an external task.

There's another player in your brain that acts as a monitoring system. You can think of it as constantly asking "Is there anything that needs my attention?"

Imagine you're daydreaming while driving, and a car suddenly swerves into your lane.

In a fraction of a second, your daydream disappears and your attention snaps back to the road. That player is called the salience network (SN).

The salience network helps you notice important signals, like a sound, a mistake, pain, an emotion, a threat, or something happening in your body, and helps shift the brain toward attention and control systems when action is needed.

The DMN, SN and attention/control systems constantly interact.

And the DMN doesn't just turn off completely with every hard task. Parts of it can also contribute when tasks involve memory, stories, meaning, or other people.

Smartphones have filled many of the small empty moments of our days — waiting for the bus, standing in line, eating alone, lying on the couch. We are constantly looking at our devices.

So, it seems reasonable to ask: are we crowding out those moments of boredom where DMN kicks in?

Maybe, but, so far, there's no strong evidence that scrolling directly affects mental health by reducing DMN time.

What we do know is that problematic smartphone use can increase stress, anxiety, task-switching, and disrupt sleep.

And sleep loss and stress also have clearer evidence for altering DMN-related brain connectivity.

When your mind wanders, it might produce a great new idea.

But it might also replay an embarrassing thing you did five years ago. Again. And again.

In depression, the self-reflection part of the DMN can get caught in repetitive negative loops that are hard to stop — what's known as rumination.

Scientists have also found that Alzheimer's disease targets key DMN areas, particularly those responsible for your autobiographical memory and remembering other aspects of the past. Which may help explain why memory problems are so central to the disease.

Protect your sleep and take stress seriously. Sleep loss and stress have direct evidence for disrupting DMN-related connectivity.

Take real quiet breaks after mentally demanding tasks, like studying. It can even help you memorize facts for your exam.

Try to reframe boredom as this essential, energy-intensive active work in your brain that builds your internal narrative and sense of self, consolidates memories, creates connections and sparks creativity.

Try waiting in line without checking your phone. Look out the window next time you take the bus or take a walk without listening to anything.

You can also try scheduling DMN time. It may be the closest thing to a real brain hack.
