She thanked the teams behind Claude Code, Cowork and Tag, describing the products as the sum of thousands of ideas from colleagues. Wu’s March 19, 2026, account of her product-management work showed how she reached that role. Before that, she worked as a product engineer at Scale AI and Dagster, then as a venture capitalist, continuing to write code that automated company-announcement monitoring and identified open-source projects gaining momentum.

When Claude Code became available internally in fall 2024, she used it to build Streamlit applications that analyzed large-scale user feedback, run evaluations to assess potential benchmarks and create reinforcement-learning environments to better understand training. Wu reported that these projects took hundreds of hours of prompting with Sonnet 3.5 (new), without her writing any of the code by hand.

She also tested successive models with the same exercise: adding a table tool to Excalidraw. The attempts began with Sonnet 3.5 (new) in October 2024, when Claude still failed. Wu reported that Opus 4 occasionally succeeded in June 2025 and that Opus 4.6 later completed such feature requests reliably enough for live demonstrations before thousands of professional developers.

Her account connected that progress to changes in the team’s working methods. Designers shipped code, engineers made product decisions and product managers built prototypes and evaluations, while short self-directed experiments and demos displaced longer documentation-first cycles. Claude Code on Desktop, the AskUserQuestion tool and todo lists were among the features that emerged from those experiments.

Wu’s farewell contrasted Claude Code’s early reliance on manually approved permission prompts and uses such as answering questions and writing tests with the current default she described: trusting Claude’s judgment to auto-approve, conduct large migrations and build new products. Anthropic’s auto-mode announcement, published August 7, 2026, and updated October 22, 2026, documents the product mechanics behind that shift.

The initial announcement set August 14, 2026, for new sessions on Pro, Max and Team plans to run in auto mode. The October update said the default would roll out during the following week for new sessions in most Claude apps on those plans. Instead of asking users to approve each action, auto mode routes tool calls through a classifier intended to block irreversible or destructive actions and actions aimed outside the user’s environment, while allowing safe actions to proceed.

When the classifier blocks an action, Claude can seek a safer route or ask for approval. Three consecutive blocks, or 20 across a session, return Claude Code to manual approvals. Anthropic cautions that the classification systems do not eliminate risk and recommends that users review Claude’s actions themselves for high-stakes changes to production infrastructure.

For Claude Code users, the practical change is a redistribution of oversight: routine actions can move forward with fewer interruptions, while high-stakes production changes still call for direct human review. That balance between autonomous execution and meaningful supervision remains central to the product Wu leaves behind.

Medicare beneficiaries who remain in their current stand-alone Part D drug plans could face sharply higher premiums in 2027 if they don’t switch to lower-cost plans during open enrollment, according to a KFF analysis.

The average monthly premium for stand-alone prescription drug plans, or PDPs, is projected to rise 50%, from $34 in 2026 to $51 in 2027, if current enrollees remain in the same plans, according to the analysis from KFF, an independent source for health policy research, polling and news.

The increase would widen the gap between stand-alone drug plans and Medicare Advantage plans that include prescription drug coverage. KFF projects that drug coverage through Medicare Advantage plans that offer Part D coverage, or MA-PDs, will average $9 a month in 2027, compared with $51 for PDPs. The average PDP premium would rise by $17, compared with an increase of less than $2 for MA-PDs.

For Medicare Part D prescription drug coverage, the average beneficiary has 28 Medicare Advantage prescription drug plans (MA-PDs) and 9 stand-alone prescription drug plans (PDPs) to choose from, with varying costs associated with these different options. KFF analysts examined premiums and cost-sharing requirements for Part D coverage in 2027 and how these costs have changed over time, based on Medicare plan landscape and benefits data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).

The $51 figure assumes current PDP enrollees remain in their existing plans. The actual average premium could be lower as beneficiaries switch to less expensive plans during open enrollment and new beneficiaries select coverage.

CMS projected a much smaller increase in the national average Part D premium, from $35 to $36. KFF noted that CMS, which based its estimate on September 2026 enrollment, did not explain its calculation methodology.

Among PDP enrollees who do not receive low-income subsidies, 76%, or 10.4 million people, would pay higher premiums if they keep their current plans. About 2.4 million would see their monthly premiums increase by more than $50.

That's a sharp increase from 2026, when only 38,000 enrollees faced monthly premium increases exceeding $50, according to KFF. The Trump administration’s decision to discontinue the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration after 2026 ended the additional premium subsidies that were provided to PDP sponsors and the cap on the maximum allowable monthly PDP premium increase, which was set at $50 for 2026, according to KFF analysts.

Another 4.1 million beneficiaries without low-income subsidies are enrolled in zero-premium PDPs this year, but no zero-premium PDPs will be available in 2027.

“With some plans increasing premiums substantially, the stakes are higher this year for people with Medicare stand-alone drug coverage if they sit on the sidelines during open enrollment,” Juliette Cubanski, a vice president and director of the Program on Medicare Policy at KFF, said in a statement. “There are relatively low-premium PDP options, but people facing large premium increases need to take action if they want to avoid paying significantly more.”

According to KFF's analysis, if PDP enrollees who are not receiving Part D Low-Income Subsidies (LIS) keep their current plan in 2027, more than half (55%), or 7.5 million people, will pay premiums of less than $20 per month, but nearly 30%, or 3.9 million people, will pay at least $100 per month.

The mortgage operating model after AI

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI could move mortgage origination from role-based handoffs to a persistent, evidence-backed borrower profile. Lenders and agencies could apply different risk models to the same underlying data while AI agents manage conditions and document work with human oversight.   AI Summary

In my previous two articles, I explored why the mortgage industry’s biggest AI challenge is structural and how the traditional brokerage operating model creates complexity as businesses grow. That brings us to a more fundamental question: If we could redesign mortgage origination around what AI now makes possible, what would we build?

Today, the process is organized around a series of narrowly defined roles and handoffs, with loan officers, assistants, processors, underwriters and others each responsible for moving the file to the next stage. Every handoff creates another opportunity for information to get lost and responsibility to be pushed downstream.

I believe the mortgage operating model of the future will be organized around a persistent, provable understanding of the borrower and the lifecycle of the loan, rather than the people, systems and handoffs that define the process today.

At its core, a mortgage involves building an evidence-based profile of an individual and applying a risk model to it. Today, that profile is repeatedly reconstructed or revalidated by originators, lenders, underwriters and other participants because each has limited visibility into, or confidence in, the work that came before.

Imagine instead a persistent, evidence-backed borrower profile that brings together information from pay stubs, tax returns, credit reports and other sources, with each data point retaining a verifiable connection to its original evidence. That includes where the information came from and how it was calculated, interpreted or reconciled with other information.

The result would be a more complete and transparent understanding of the borrower that persists throughout the loan lifecycle. Rather than reconstructing that information each time another party needs to assess the loan, participants could trace the underlying evidence, understand how previous conclusions were reached and build on the work already performed, while still conducting their own necessary evaluations and verifications.

To be sure, understanding the borrower is only part of the equation. Every lender, agency and mortgage product has its own requirements and risk criteria. Today, those requirements are translated into guidelines, matrices and documents that must be interpreted and applied by people across multiple stages of the origination process.

With a common, verifiable borrower profile, different risk models could be applied to the same underlying information. A system could evaluate how Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac or a particular lender would assess the borrower, identify missing evidence and determine what needs to happen next.

Specialized AI agents could then coordinate and execute much of that work, from collecting documents and preparing calculations to addressing conditions and identifying exceptions that require human review.

Consider how often a missing document or unresolved discrepancy surfaces late in the process, forcing a loan officer or processor to revisit work that should have been completed weeks earlier. With a more complete understanding of the borrower and the requirements of the loan, those agents could identify many of those issues earlier, coordinate the necessary follow-up and maintain an auditable record of the work performed.

The implications extend beyond making individual tasks faster to changing how information is maintained and how work gets done, creating an opportunity to rethink the organization itself.

When technology maintains the evidence, context and requirements throughout the loan lifecycle, people no longer need to serve as the connective tissue between every task and system. Specialized AI agents can increasingly handle work traditionally distributed across different roles, while experienced mortgage professionals oversee execution, validate results and exercise judgment.

Consider the processor, whose role could evolve from coordinating documents, conditions and handoffs to overseeing the quality and performance of the loan process. Specialized AI agents could collect documents, prepare calculations and track conditions, while the processor sets standards, validates results, resolves exceptions and continuously improves the workflow.

This shift would also change how mortgage organizations define and reward expertise. Rather than measuring success by the number of people someone manages or the tasks they complete, organizations could focus on accurate files, predictable closings, borrower experience and ultimately, a lower cost to originate. By reducing manual handoffs and repetitive work, experienced professionals could oversee more loans without a corresponding increase in staffing.

The result would be a mortgage organization structured around the lifecycle of the loan rather than inherited job descriptions. Instead of scaling by adding more people to manage each stage of origination, companies could build teams around broader accountability for the loan from application to funding, with AI supporting the work required along the way.

Origination is the logical place to prove this model because it is where the borrower’s evidence is first assembled and evaluated. But once a provable, evidence-based profile exists, its usefulness could extend well beyond funding. Other participants across the mortgage value chain, including investors and capital markets firms, could eventually apply their own risk models to the same underlying information, allowing for more granular risk assessment without repeatedly reconstructing the loan.

My prediction is that AI will reshape not only mortgage organizations, but also how they work together. Even as the technology supporting the industry becomes more sophisticated, the underlying operating model could become fundamentally simpler. With evidence and context following the loan, each participant could build on the work already performed while focusing its expertise on the decisions and responsibilities that matter most.

Low-Calorie Diet and Supervised Exercise May Reverse Early Onset Type 2 Diabetes

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

The RESET for Remission trial, presented at the 2026 European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting and published in The Lancet Regional Health Americas on October 1, found that more than half of adults with early onset type 2 diabetes entered remission after six months on a low calorie diet and supervised exercise program.

Early onset type 2 diabetes is when the condition is diagnosed before age 45.

Those with early onset type 2 diabetes may be at a higher risk of complications like long-term damage to the capillaries (microvascular complications), adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and earlier death.

Typical treatment for early onset type 2 diabetes is often a combination of lifestyle changes, like diet and exercise, and medications.

However, this new study suggests that a supervised low calorie diet and exercise program may help some people with early onset type 2 diabetes enter remission.

The RESET for Remission trial included 96 adults from England and Canada who were between the ages of 18 and 45, had early onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, and were not on insulin treatment.

The participants had an average age of 38 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 35.2 kg/m2. They were randomized into a control group that received usual care alone and the intervention group, which ate a low calorie diet and participated in supervised exercise programs.

During the first 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group followed an 800–900-calorie-per-day diet and completed twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance exercise sessions, along with one independent exercise session per week.

For weeks 13–24, they transitioned to a weight-maintenance diet and independent exercise.

Both groups were supervised by physicians, and the intervention group was also closely supervised by licensed dietitians.

The low calorie diet consisted of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrate, and 20% fat. Participants were also encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids per day and given access to a fiber-based laxative as needed.

In the first two weeks, all meals were replaced with specially formulated nutritional products (Optifast). Then, in weeks 3–12, the participants maintained the same calorie intake but had more flexibility in their food choices.

This second phase of the diet included one conventional meal per day, with daily intake targets that included protein, fruit, and vegetables. Typically, this meant 60 to 90 grams of protein from all sources combined, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

At the end of 24 weeks, 54% of the intervention group entered remission of type 2 diabetes, compared with only 4% in the control group. Researchers defined remission as an HbA1c level below 6.5% without glucose-lowering medication for at least 12 weeks.

“This study proves what most physicians have advocated for quite some time. Clearly, diet and exercise can work to improve or even resolve diabetes,” David Cutler, MD, board certified family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

Cutler also added that this study provides a clear example of how difficult it can be to enter diabetes remission with diet and exercise alone. He stated that the small number of participants in the study also makes him wonder if these findings can be applied to all populations.

Another limitation of this study, as Cutler pointed out, was that the strict dietary limits imposed on study participants would be very difficult for the average adult to adhere to.

The study also followed participants for only six months, so it’s unclear how many would remain in remission over the longer term.

“It is worth trying diet and exercise first because they can work to achieve meaningful weight loss and remission of diabetes,” he said.

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) notes that losing 5%–7% of your body weight can help reduce your risk of type 2 diabetes.

GLP-1s May Help Lower Diabetes, Heart Risks Better Than Standard Prevention

GLP-1 drugs are more effective in helping prevent type 2 diabetes than the commonly prescribed medication metformin, according to a new study.

Researchers said GLP-1s such as Ozempic and Mounjaro were more likely to reduce blood sugar to healthy levels in people with prediabetes and obesity.

They added that people prescribed GLP-1 medications had a lower risk of progressing from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

The researchers noted that GLP-1 users also had a lower risk of death from any cause as well as fewer heart attacks, strokes, and heart failure events compared with metformin users.

“The great news with prediabetes is that it’s still possible to reverse the progression of the disease,” said Vinicius de Sousa Barzon Serra, MD, a study author and research fellow in cardiovascular medicine at the Mayo Clinic in Scottsdale, Arizona, in a press release. “This research may add to the case for considering GLP-1 medications earlier for certain patients, rather than waiting until they develop type 2 diabetes.”

Serra noted that metformin still remains an effective treatment for people with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

“This is less about replacing one medication with another and more about discussing additional options with specific patients,” he said.

The researchers presented their findings at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

David Cutler, MD, a family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the findings were “quite impressive.”

“It elevates the comfort of prescribing GLP-1 drugs as a front-line treatment,” he told Healthline.

Robert Eckel, MD, a former president of the American Heart Association who was likewise not involved in the study, agrees the research provides more options for patients and prescribers.

“GLP-1s provide another option for prevention, perhaps as an add-on therapy. However, costs and access to these medications are important factors,” said Eckel, an endocrinologist at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, in the press release.

For their study, the researchers analyzed the medical records of 256,000 adults in the United States with prediabetes and obesity. About 76% of the subjects were women, and the average age of the participants was 52.

About half of those studied were taking GLP-1 drugs with the active ingredient semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide, or dulaglutide.

The other half had been prescribed metformin, a medication used to help manage blood sugar levels in adults and some children with type 2 diabetes by decreasing glucose production in the liver, reducing glucose absorption from food, and improving the body’s insulin sensitivity.

The researchers reported that the people in the study who were taking GLP-1 drugs had a 1.5 times higher chance of reversing high blood sugar to healthy levels compared to people who were using metformin.

The researchers also noted that GLP-1 medication users had a 31% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes and a 41% lower risk of death from any cause.

They added that GLP-1 medications were associated with 15% fewer heart attacks, 20% fewer strokes, 25% fewer cases of heart failure, and 8% fewer cases of atrial fibrillation (AFib), a common type of irregular heartbeat.

The researchers cautioned that their study is observational and, therefore, cannot prove a cause-and-effect relationship. They also noted they relied on prescription records, so the frequency and dosage at which patients took their medication was not reflected.

In addition, the GLP-1 drugs were grouped together, so individual differences between medications were not considered.

Eckel said in the news release that the benefits for people taking GLP-1 drugs were probably mainly due to weight loss. Eckel noted that the research results open up a wide range of possibilities.

“This is an important finding because the GLP-1 receptor agonists are commonly used today and this is a population that in the transition from prediabetes to diabetes are at increasing risk for cardiovascular disease,” Eckel said in a taped interview alongside the study findings.

Cutler pointed out that several GLP-1 drugs also target blood sugar levels and insulin production. They could be effective in people with prediabetes who don’t have weight management issues.

Cutler noted that these people, however, might be reluctant to take GLP-1 drugs because they may not be experiencing symptoms of their prediabetes but could develop side effects with the medications.

“They would not be getting any immediate benefits,” Cutler said. He added that metformin, which was first used clinically in 1958, remains an effective alternative. “It’s been proven safe, effective, and it’s very cheap,” Cutler said.

It’s estimated that 40 million people in the United States have diabetes, with more than 90% of those cases being type 2 diabetes, a chronic condition characterized by high blood glucose levels that results from the body’s ineffective response to insulin or insufficient insulin production.

It’s also estimated that 115 million adults in the United States have prediabetes, a condition in which a person has higher-than-normal blood sugar levels but not high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes.

Around 5–10% of people with prediabetes develop diabetes every year. More broadly, up to 70% of people with prediabetes eventually develop diabetes.

Medication is sometimes prescribed for people with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes. Lifestyle changes can help reverse type 2 diabetes and prediabetes, as well as prevent the progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

Cutler said the lifestyle changes needed to prevent type 2 diabetes are pretty clear.

“The most important thing for people with prediabetes to know is they should lose weight, reduce their carbs, exercise daily and, if that doesn’t work, then consider medication,” he said.

The health system is trying to close a $50 million budget hole by the end of March, several media outlets reported.

Hennepin Healthcare also is confronting a projected $100 million loss in uncompensated care and a $1.7 billion reduction in Medicaid revenue over the next 10 years, MPR reported.

Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center shared plans to cuts 265 roles—128 via planned retirements and vacant position closures and the remaining 137 through layoffs and a reduction in hours. The layoffs will take effect by March 8, according to a WARN filing.

The 427-bed nonprofit, which employs about 4,000 people, cited federal and California reimbursement cuts for its decision as well as a budget shortfall of $40 million. The plan for job reductions and other measures was put together following a “thorough evaluation of operational requirements, organizational structure and redundancies in positions,” a spokesperson told press.

The layoffs will take effect by March 8, according to a WARN filing. Those affected will receive severance and other support.

Pomona Valley said the reductions affect management as well as clinical and nonclinical roles but that patient care would not be affected. A labor union representing some of the hospital’s nurses told membership it plans to enforce protections outlined in its collective bargaining agreement, including an option for laid-off nurses to apply for and be given other open positions for which they are qualified.

Blue Cross of Idaho said it will be laying off fewer than 90 workers, a move the group said will allow it “to remain a strong, Idaho-based company” and better serve its nearly 600,000 members.

The insurer did not give details on specific roles affected to a timeline for the cuts. It had already reduced its head count by 135 early last year when the state health department passed it over for a dual-eligible contract.

L.A. Care Health Plan will lay off 225 employees, about 3% of its workforce, on or before March 13.

The country’s largest publicly operated health plan said in a statement the decision stemmed from federal and state funding cuts, and that its restructure will strengthen critical capabilities and introduce new roles to help do so. It also said it will provide transition support and resources to affected workers.

Trinity Health said it will be laying off about a tenth of its revenue cycle management team, instead outsourcing the work to a vendor in a bid to reduce costs.

The 92-hospital nonprofit system did not disclose how many workers are affected but said the reductions affect workers in 15 states. The organization also did not share whether those losing their positions would receive a compensation package.

Alameda Health System will lay off about 250 employees across multiple locations.

A regulatory filing outlining most of these lists March 9 as the terminations’ date of effect. The California organization said its cuts will span several areas, and it is also offering voluntary resignations and retirements with financial incentives.

The reductions stem from impending funding cuts.

North Star Health Alliance announced layoffs affecting around 120 of its workers.

The New York system told press its cuts will span clinical, nonclinical and management employees and be split roughly equally across its two main campuses. Those affected did not receive severance payments outside of what was obligated under their employment contracts.

The cuts come in the wake of a payment dispute between North Star and New York’s Department of Health related to the restructuring of its hospital into a critical access hospital and a co-located inpatient acute psychiatric hospital.

Vibra Healthcare will be laying off 310 employees when it closes Specialty Hospital of Portland, the state’s only long-term acute care facility, due to financial challenges.

The decision was announced in December but will take effect on or before Feb. 1, per a WARN filing. Leadership, nonclinical workers and clinical positions such as nurses and therapists who worked at the 73-bed facility are affected.
