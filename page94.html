Former UK Prime Minister Boris Johnson said Monday that London should lead on blockchain development and asset tokenization, eight months after he likened bitcoin Bitcoin (BTC) and other cryptocurrencies to a Ponzi scheme.

During a Solana Breakpoint panel titled "Crypto Is Key to the UK's Economic Future," moderated by The Block's Gareth Jenkinson, Johnson was asked why he had called bitcoin a Ponzi scheme. He acknowledged he was "not a great crypto buff," but recalled trying to help a man he met at church in Oxfordshire who had been "absolutely ripped off."

In his March Daily Mail column, Johnson wrote that the man initially handed over £500 ($660) to someone who promised to double his money by investing it in bitcoin. The man kept paying fees in the hope of getting his money back and ended up paying about £20,000 ($26,380), Johnson wrote.

"I want to know who's in charge," Johnson said of buying crypto, adding that he wouldn't know where to turn if his money was stolen. "Nobody knows who's in charge, apart from [Satoshi Nakamoto]," Johnson said. "And I don't know where to find him."

Nevertheless, Johnson described himself as "totally, totally in favor" of blockchain innovation and tokenizing "assets of all kinds," saying that London should be at the forefront of that work.

"Please don't think that my Luddite anxiety about crypto in any way means that I wish to slow down the development of blockchain or the lead that London should rightly have in that sort of technology," he said.

Fellow panelist Lord Ross Kempsell said he shared Johnson's concerns about the risks, but saw room for the UK crypto industry to grow under regulation.

Kempsell said he was primarily interested in institutional adoption of blockchain and that traditional financial firms in the City of London were increasingly using networks such as Solana as regulation advances.

The Financial Conduct Authority began taking applications for authorization under the UK's new crypto regime in September, and its initial application window closes Feb. 28, 2027. Death is everywhere, yet for much of my life I have encountered it only in glimpses. As a scholar with the Centre for Death and Society at the University of Bath, I regularly encounter different perspectives on death and dying. My own experiences, though, have felt brief and distant.

In my 20s, I visited my grandma in hospital. She had suffered a stroke that would prove fatal. I remember gasping quietly as I entered the room. Something about her contorted face, laboured breathing, and pained expression shocked me.

I felt relieved when I was told visiting hours were over. I left the ward feeling sick to my stomach.

At funerals over the years, I watched a succession of coffins disappear behind plush curtains: Grandma, Grandpa, Nana, Uncle Chris, Aunty Lil, Aunty Rachael, Aunty Mary, and Uncle Mick.

Looking back, these were glimpses of death and dying: processes that felt detached, as though they somehow existed outside my life.

It was much the same with Dad. I got the call from a hospital consultant around 11 p.m.

“I’m sorry, but your dad is now unconscious,” he told me.

“I’d suggest coming to visit him as soon as you can. There’s nothing more we can do but keep him comfortable.”

This was it. Dad was dying. I drove through the night.

The curtain was drawn around his bed when the nurse guided me to his cubicle. I was terrified of what I’d see. Again, I wanted to run away when I first saw him lying there, occasionally flinching, his face slightly contorted, struggling for breath, no longer fully alive.

I asked for a little time alone with him, and the nurse told me she thought he could still hear me. “Leslie! Leslie…” she said loudly, gently shaking his shoulder as though she was trying to wake him from a deep sleep. “There's someone here to see you, Leslie. That's nice, isn't it?”

I had things I wanted to tell Dad. I wanted him to know that I loved him, and that wherever he was going next, I hoped he would find peace.

I also felt a need to touch him. At first, I touched his arm, like a child prodding something mysterious they have found in a rock pool. As my reticence evaporated, I placed the palm of my hand gently on his skin, closed my eyes, and spoke.

“I love you, Dad. And Em, and Mum, and Joe—they told me to tell you they love you too.”

He died five days later. At his funeral, I watched his coffin disappear behind a pair of curtains.

Recently, I examined an excellent Ph.D. thesis on home death care in the United Kingdom. It explored the experiences of a small group of people who chose to care for a loved one’s body at home before the funeral.

For various personal reasons, they chose to care for a loved one’s body at home after death—sometimes after the person had died elsewhere. They became, in the words used in the research, “custodians of the dead.”

The practical commitment could be considerable. Depending on the circumstances, caring for the body could involve washing or dressing it, repositioning it, preparing a room, and deciding who could visit. If the body remained at home for several days, arrangements also had to be made to keep it cool. Families described living alongside a body that cooled, stiffened, and gradually changed.

The experience also gave people time and space to confront things they might otherwise not have been able to. They began to make sense of a loved one’s transition from living person to dead body, taking a closer look at death itself.

Keeping the deceased at home for an extended period also allowed people to say goodbye at a slower, more organic pace. One woman described talking to her mother as she cleaned the living room, commenting on the birds, the sky, and the trees, just as she had when her mother was alive.

Home death care will not suit every person or family. The thesis focused on people who chose it, and we also need to hear from those who found the experience difficult or regretted it.

As I listened, I thought about Dad and the short time I spent with him as he lay dying in hospital. My experience sits within a broader shift.

The Lancet Commission on the Value of Death describes how, over generations, dying has moved from family and community settings toward health systems, where it is often treated primarily as a medical or physiological event. In England in 2023, hospital was the most common place of death: 42.8 percent of people died there. Another 28.4 percent died at home and 21 percent in care homes.

The separation can continue after death. Research on home death care in England describes how the body is commonly moved to a mortuary or funeral home, where its care passes to professionals. I began to wonder what this separation from the body might mean for the people closest to someone who has died.

What might it have been like if I’d had the chance to be with Dad in the way the people in the thesis had been with their loved ones? Might I have moved through my initial shock and experienced something beyond it? Would our goodbye have felt different? And might I have begun to relate to death and dying in a way I can’t quite fathom now, when the physical experience of death feels so far removed from my life?

Webb reveals a stellar nursery with secrets even infrared cannot reach

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Why does Sagittarius B2 produce so many stars? Webb’s infrared observations offer a closer look at an unusually active region near the Milky Way’s center.

Today’s Image of the Day reveals a crowded stretch of the Milky Way where bright stars and orange clouds surround patches of almost complete darkness.

Those dark areas may look like gaps in the scene, but they contain some of its most intriguing material: dense clouds concealing the birth of stars.

The James Webb Space Telescope captured this view of Sagittarius B2 with its Near-Infrared Camera, or NIRCam.

Released on September 24, 2025, the image offers a detailed look at a vast stellar nursery near our galaxy’s center.

Its brilliant points of light are only part of the story. Understanding what remains hidden could help astronomers explain why this particular cloud produces stars so much more actively than its surroundings.

Sagittarius B2 lies a few hundred light-years from Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the Milky Way’s heart.

This central neighborhood contains crowded star fields, thick clouds and complicated magnetic fields.

Yet an abundance of gas does not automatically translate into abundant star formation.

According to NASA’s release, Sagittarius B2 contains about 10 percent of the gas in the galactic center but accounts for roughly half of its star formation.

That imbalance makes it a valuable place to investigate what allows some clouds to become especially productive stellar nurseries.

Astronomers Adam Ginsburg and Nazar Budaiev of the University of Florida led the observing program.

The team used Webb to examine how massive stars emerge within this unusually dense environment.

Stars begin inside cold molecular clouds, where gas gathers into denser pockets.

As a clump gains material, its gravity strengthens. Eventually, gravity can pull it inward, beginning a collapse that heats the material and produces a developing star called a protostar.

At first, the object draws much of its energy from that collapse. Later, if its core becomes sufficiently hot and compressed, hydrogen nuclei begin combining to form helium.

This process, nuclear fusion, supplies the energy that allows a star to shine through the longest stage of its life.

The cloud surrounding a developing star can make that transition difficult to observe. Astronomers must examine stellar birth through the very material that makes it possible.

A bright, mature star may announce itself across space, while a much younger object remains buried in its original supply of gas and dust.

Webb’s instruments reveal different parts of this environment.

In the NIRCam image, stars crowd the field, interrupted by luminous clouds and dark structures.

Near-infrared light can pass through some of the obscuring dust, bringing objects into view that would be harder to distinguish in visible light.

A companion image from Webb’s Mid-Infrared Instrument, or MIRI, changes the emphasis.

Warm dust becomes much more prominent, while far fewer stars remain visible. Only the brightest stars emit strongly enough at these longer wavelengths to stand out through the clouds.

Together, the views show why one image cannot provide a complete inventory of a stellar nursery.

Changing the wavelengths changes which objects and structures dominate the scene. The same cloud can appear rich in stars in one view and rich in glowing dust in another.

Even Webb’s sensitivity has limits in Sagittarius B2.

The team’s observations used 14 filters covering wavelengths from 1.5 to 25 micrometers.

They revealed a layered cloud containing both exposed massive stars and deeply obscured populations.

The researchers also identified candidate regions of gas energized by massive stars that earlier radio observations had missed.

Elsewhere, MIRI detected radiation escaping from the embedded cluster Sagittarius B2 North along cavities associated with outflows.

These openings provide routes for light to emerge from an otherwise heavily concealed environment.

But many young stellar objects previously detected with the ALMA radio telescope remained invisible to Webb.

Their absence from the infrared images illustrates how effectively the surrounding material can conceal ongoing star formation.

The result gives today’s image a second way to be read. Its bright stars reveal what has emerged into view.

Its dark clouds mark places where the next part of the story is still unfolding beyond Webb’s sight.

Your smart home hub placement is more important than you'd think

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Whether it fits your home's aesthetic or not, that hub may need to be front and center.

A robust smart home system makes it easy to control the many devices keeping your household in the 21st century, but there are many ways that system can fail. Like any gadget, smart home devices are prone to issues that have no clear cause and can be frustrating to diagnose. Among those potential causes is something you may never guess: placement. As the center of your setup, capable of receiving signals from all directions, you'd think you can place your smart home hub anywhere, but it's not as simple as that.

Many hubs feature controls for all of your connected devices, so it makes sense that you'd keep them in a central spot. But if you control your devices from separate apps or just don't need to make manual adjustments often, it can be tempting to hide the hub out of sight. But since it needs to send and receive signals from all your home's devices, it's important to ensure they aren't blocked or being impeded.

While not all smart home devices run on Wi-Fi, it's helpful to treat the hub the way you would a Wi-Fi router. The most important part of placing a smart home hub like Anker's MindBase is making sure signals aren't being physically obstructed. Wi-Fi and other forms of wireless communication can be blocked by walls, furniture and doors, so try to keep as clear a path as possible between the hub and the devices communicating with it. Because you're likely controlling devices throughout your home, some will inevitably have a weaker connection than others, but placing the hub in a central place will help minimize problems. If your hub has visible antennas, try to also point them toward your smart home devices.

Hubs can also experience interference from other communication devices, like Wi-Fi routers. It's impossible to completely eliminate this kind of interference, especially if you're living somewhere with a lot of Wi-Fi traffic, like an apartment building. You should place your hub close enough to a router so that it's still receiving a strong signal, but they should never be right next to each other to avoid interference. Modern routers have settings that allow them to change to less crowded frequencies, and turning that on or setting your router and hub to different bands can help cut down on congestion as well. Other electronics like phones and microwaves can also interfere with smart home hubs, and the only solution here is to keep them away from each other.

If you're limited on placement options, you can create a linked network for Zigbee or ZWave connections to travel to the hub. Keep in mind that the signal they use has a fairly short range. Because these devices form a mesh network, each device repeats the signal it receives, letting you form a chain of devices through your home. That means that the signal from your hub only needs to reach one other device to be effective, but the signal does get weaker across multiple devices. So, even for a network that branches across devices, the closer to the hub a device is, the better.

OpenAI released a study on November 16, 2026, covering 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 academic scientists and survey responses from 251 participants. It examines how the scientists used ChatGPT and Codex across research work and how they expect AI to affect discovery.

The full paper analyzes activity from August 20 through September 22, 2026, and a survey conducted September 30 to October 7, 2026. Researchers used privacy-preserving classifiers to categorize messages without reading the underlying exchanges, and program data were not used for model training by default.

Reading and knowledge acquisition accounted for 27.7% of the average scientist’s scientific messages, while writing and communication represented 26.9%. Scientific analysis contributed 16.7% and research software and code development 13.8%; together, those four workflows represented 85%.

That concentration puts most current AI use in discrete knowledge-work tasks rather than coordinated research projects. Explaining scientific concepts was the leading individual task at 18.8% of messages, with 89.7% of scientists sending at least three such requests.

Ideation tells a different story about breadth. It represented 5.3% of messages, but about 68% of scientists sent at least three ideation messages during the study. Across those messages, scientists supplied a specific question 35% of the time, asked ChatGPT to refine an existing idea 36% of the time, and asked it to suggest a research question 23% of the time. The remaining 6% could not be categorized.

The paper also identifies a stage-dependent division of labor. Scientists often asked AI to propose directions during early exploration, then supplied their own questions or ideas for refinement when developing specific research problems.

The paper’s scientist-involvement score, measuring the average share of initial requests followed by clarification, checking or revision, was 31.8%. Writing and communication had the highest score at 38.0%, followed by scientific analysis at 34.4% and research software at 33.7%.

For comparable tasks, requests requiring advanced or frontier expertise had higher involvement in 28 of 32 cases, by an average of 6.5 percentage points. More specialized work therefore generally drew more follow-up, not less.

The score considers only the next scientific message in the same conversation within seven days and does not capture revisions made outside the AI application. Checking or correcting outputs was also the most commonly selected difficulty in obtaining value from ChatGPT, chosen by 45.4% of surveyed scientists.

Those review demands coexist with self-reported time savings. Across eight research activities, surveyed scientists estimated that one hour of work with AI would have taken about 1.9 hours without it at the same quality. Reported productivity ratios ranged from 1.36 times for administration to 2.85 times for research software and code development.

Expectations for discovery were also high. Of the 250 scientists answering the discovery-timeline question, 98% expected AI to accelerate discovery in their field. Asked to imagine a discovery that would take ten years without AI, 72.0% expected it at least three years sooner with AI, including 21.6% who expected it within three years.

Written explanations emphasized execution over full automation. Among 367 reasons identified in 224 responses, 52% concerned faster research execution, while automating research loops accounted for 2.2%. Another 23% concerned finding more promising research questions and 14% pursuing a wider range of questions.

The reported bottlenecks extend beyond model capability. Limited time or staffing was selected by 51.8% of respondents and limited funding by 43.8%; validating or reproducing findings affected 25.5%, while access to equipment, laboratories or computing resources affected 24.3%.

OpenAI cautions that participants were a selected group who applied for expanded AI access and chose to answer an AI survey, so the results may not generalize to all scientists. Usage shares describe requests rather than research value or time allocation, and the findings do not establish causal effects.
