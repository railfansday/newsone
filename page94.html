Democratic sweep of Congress forces crypto industry to rethink Washington strategy

After two years of a pro-crypto, pro-innovation climate in Washington, the digital asset industry faces a sharp political jolt as Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress, forcing the sector to seek new allies within a party that has been more divided on crypto.

As results rolled in on Tuesday, crypto advocacy group Stand With Crypto said 19 pro-crypto lawmakers in the Senate had been elected and 226 in the House as Democrats took control of the House and Senate. Eight anti-crypto Senators were also elected alongside 106 in the House.

Following the results, some in the crypto industry have taken the position that crypto is bipartisan and that lawmakers on both sides of the aisle are pro-digital assets. But a Democratic sweep means more oversight, and there is close to no chance of the crypto industry getting legislation it wants on the table.

"Nobody has their head in the sand," said Kara Calvert, vice president of U.S. policy at Coinbase, in a post on X. "There will be headwinds for any bipartisan legislation next year, but crypto is 100% bipartisan."

Many of the new members in Congress are pro-crypto, said Kevin Wysocki, head of policy at Anchorage Digital.

"The younger members are more used to this technology, are more open to new technology that maybe rivals some of the existing technology," he said in an interview with The Block on Wednesday. "It's very bullish, I think, for crypto overall, so I think that's one of the great things from what happened yesterday, and I think it's only going to get better over time, regardless of who's in power, Republicans or Democrats."

However, one of the biggest races the crypto industry had its eyes on, and money on, was the Ohio Senate race between former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown and SWC-endorsed Republican Sen. Jon Husted. The crypto political action committee spent tens of millions of dollars on that race against Brown, but ultimately lost when Brown defeated Husted later in the night.

Brown was previously Senate Banking Committee Chair, which has jurisdiction over digital assets and has been viewed as critical of crypto. He could have a leading role in oversight and regulation of the industry. And since he won in a special election to fill Vice President JD Vance's Senate term, that means Brown could return to the Senate as early as Dec. 15.

Brown's return is significant, Wysocki said. When he was chair of the Senate Banking Committee, Brown and his Senate staff were open to constructive conversations about crypto, he said.

Pro-crypto political action committees reportedly spent over $60 million alone in the Senate race in Ohio against Brown. On its own, crypto PAC Fairshake raised $106.2 million, according to Americans for Financial Reform. And crypto PACs overall spent $170 million on the midterms, according to Fortune, with millions going to Republican Sen. Pete Ricketts in Nebraska.

Fairshake spent $154.5 million in both primaries and the general election, with over $13.5 million going to Ricketts.

"Fairshake played a meaningful role in winning competitive races across the country in 2026 and we will do it again in 2028," said Geoff Vetter, spokesperson for Fairshake, in an emailed statement to The Block. "Newly elected and current members will have a choice: protect consumers and American jobs or fight progress. We will be watching and '28 is a new map."

One crypto industry source alluded that PACs and trade associations will have to "recalibrate" and need to "double down on being bipartisan."

Specifically on Fairshake, the person criticized funds being used on Brown's race.

"What did we ultimately get out of that?" they said. "Where could that money have been better spent? To be perfectly honest with you, I think it could have been better spent on shoe-leather lobbying."

That money could have been spent to lobby lawmakers on the Clarity Act, broad crypto legislation that passed the House, but got stuck in the Senate in September, they said.

"You spend a fraction of it, and you could have effectively hired all of DC to lobby on Clarity," they added.

Focus will turn to taxes and security and hacks, Anchorage's Wysocki said. The election, too, comes just days after the largest crypto hack on record of Binance for over $6 billion.

"Congress is going to be asking those questions, like how do we make security at our financial institutions stronger?" Wysocki said.

The Clarity Act that passed out of the House last year and stalled in the Senate a few months ago is unlikely to advance over the next two years given Brown's win, said investment bank TD Cowen.

Oversight will also come into view, including investigations into Trump-family-run World Liberty Financial, said TD Cowen’s Washington Research Group, led by managing director Jaret Seiberg.

"Democrats will investigate Trump's crypto entanglements, including World Liberty Financial," Seiberg wrote in a note on Wednesday. "We believe the resulting political environment may make it impossible to advance a version of Clarity that the crypto sector could back."

Hong Kong artists create a poetic moment in Venice

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kingsley Ng’s installation Laundry Nocturne (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

For Fermata: Hong Kong in Venice, the Hong Kong-born artists Kingsley Ng and Angel Hui are training visitors’ attention on the quiet moments and subtle details connecting both cities. The exhibition, marking the 25th anniversary of Hong Kong’s first participation in the Venice Biennale, is curated by the Hong Kong Museum of Art (HKMoA) and continues until 22 November. It is housed in a historic Venetian residential building in the floating city’s Campo della Tana neighbourhood, a setting that underlines the meditative, lived-in quality to the works.

“What we hope visitors take away from the exhibition is a moment of reflection,” says Dr Maria Mok, the director of the HKMoA. “Through Kingsley and Angel’s attention to everyday details, we hope visitors feel invited to slow down and become more attentive to their surroundings, even within the hustle and intensity of urban life.”

One of Kingsley Ng’s installations, for instance, Laundry Nocturne (2026), consists of a projection and soundscape that evoke the play of light across laundry hung between buildings. It was inspired by the artist’s experience visiting Venice, where billowing laundry on clotheslines is still common, though for Ng it also evoked memories of Hong Kong.

Kingsley Ng in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“The laundry becomes a point of entry into a wider soundscape of nighttime labour: recordings from a wastewater treatment plant, a newspaper-printing facility and a postal sorting centre evoke the work that continues while much of the city sleeps,” Ng says. “These modest, often overlooked processes that keep a city alive do not only connect Venice and Hong Kong. They are like a low hum in almost every city, to be picked up by an ear that doesn’t only listen to loud grandeur.”

For her installations, Angel Hui shines a spotlight on humble features of Hong Kong’s everyday life, including goldfish in plastic bags and a neighbour’s ornate but faded window frame. In her magical rendering, glowing plastic bags embroidered with goldfish motifs are suspended overhead, while a handwrought iron window made by Hui and traditional metalsmiths rotates slowly in the gallery.

“In Hong Kong, a goldfish bag is an everyday object; in Venice, it seemed almost precious—delicate and exposed,” Hui says. “The experience reminded me that a work does not need to be grand to hold meaning. Sometimes, its poetry lies in inviting us to look more attentively, and to meet the familiar with tenderness.”

Angel Hui’s installation I Would Like to Open a Window for You (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

Both artists, through their site-specific installations, encourage visitors to mark a pause while heightening their attention to details of light, sound, colour and texture. This invitation to contemplation is echoed in the exhibition’s title, Fermata, which is a type of musical symbol instructing a performer to hold a note or a pause for an extra moment, and the term for the places where Venetian vaporettos stop to let off and pick up passengers. For Hui, this title and its dual meanings resonate across the exhibition’s disparate locales.

“The difference in pace is pronounced: Hong Kong often presses forward, while Venice seems to make room for pause,” she says. “This is why Fermata—a moment of stopping, of stillness—felt so resonant. In both cities, the ordinary endures, and in that endurance, everyday objects acquire a quiet radiance.”

The exhibition’s title and installations also complement the theme of this year’s Venice Biennale, In Minor Keys, set by the late curator Koyo Kouoh.

Angel Hui in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“I come to see the works as experiences to return to and live alongside,” Ng says. “For me, that is one meaning of In Minor Keys: making time for the ordinary and immediate—the air, light and weather around us—and becoming more attentive to our presence within them.”

The artists’ ability to turn familiar objects and moments into tableaux where time pauses and senses are heightened is key to the exhibition’s power. It allows their installations to resonate across localities, from the Venetian Lagoon to Hong Kong’s cityscape.

“The artworks merge into the Venetian neighbourhood, from laundry hanging across nearby alleys and above our courtyard exhibition space to the view of the neighbourhood outside Kingsley’s Laundry Nocturne gallery, to wrought-iron windows that echo Angel’s work,” Dr Mok says. “All these details are blurring the boundary between the exhibition and its surroundings. They also show how small moments of daily life can quietly synchronise across different cities and audiences.”

A Frightening Encounter on a Dark Road Nearly Took Night Running From Me. This Year, I’m Taking It Back.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I N THE DARK, the headlights cast a distorted version of my silhouette on the industrial building to my left as the vehicle makes its way past me. I’m running along an otherwise desolate road, not far from my house. The car comes to a halt at the stop sign about 300 feet in front of me—and idles. My legs instinctively slow down, my body reacting before my mind has a chance to catch up. I quickly calculate what to do next. There are no side streets I can turn into—I either have to run past the car or turn around, which would add a few miles to my run that I’m not ready for. Maybe the driver is just checking his phone, I tell myself.

But the broad-shouldered man with a long graying beard at the wheel is waiting for me with the window rolled down, his arm resting in its frame. He starts shouting before I can make sense of the situation: What do I think I am doing, running in the dark? Am I trying to get killed? My body tenses, anticipating the man’s possible next move. He has daughters my age, he continues, and he’d kill them if they did that. Could I at least carry this with me, he roars, throwing a key-chain alarm to my feet, then driving off before I have a chance to react.

I sprint the last half a mile home, propelled by anxious energy. My inability to focus only fuels my confusion; so does the blue egg-shaped alarm in my trembling hand that I don’t have a clear memory of picking up but am holding nevertheless. I try to replay in my head how it got there—and why. I have reflective bands on both my arms and my ankles, so drivers can spot me. I use my phone’s light to illuminate the ground in front of me whenever I need to.

The next day, I don’t run outside in the dark. Too cold, I convince myself when I step on the treadmill I hate so much in my apartment complex gym. Too rattled, is what I am unwilling to admit. Because do I have any right to feel that way when technically nothing happened to me? The stranger, in the end, didn’t hurt me.

Still, something changed on the road in the dark.

With every rhythmic thump the belt makes, I am less determined to keep going. My mind can’t handle the monotony. I start skipping days when I cannot make it out for a daytime run. At some point over the next few weeks, the key to the gym falls behind the shoe rack, and I don’t even notice.

THE LIKELY DIAGNOSIS is seasonal affective disorder. It could also be winter blues, but that one is milder and more temporary. My somber mood and low energy seem constant. For most people, it passes by spring, my therapist says. What helps? For one, exercise! But at that point, my couch’s cushions have resigned to accepting my body’s indentation as their new shape.

By the time spring comes, my fitness is at odds with my enthusiasm for the prolonged daylight, and I end up with shin splints. I spend the rest of the year trying to heal and return to form.

I don’t forget about the gray-bearded man. In fact, I share the story, framing it as a silly encounter. “Would you believe that?” I say with a forced smile.

“That’s not okay,” my friends and coworkers answer with their words as much as their faces.

My feelings start morphing from self-blame into anger. What should have been clear to me from the beginning dawns on me slowly: Vigilance is important, but the solution to a stranger’s concerns about my safety is not to instill more fear in the name of “protection.” Women running at night are not the problem. We’ve long ago dismantled the patriarchal notion that men should be our protectors when, in fact, they often pose the danger.

And so when the clock falls back an hour at the start of November, I’m determined not to fall back into a cycle of seasonal depression and injury.

BY MID-DECEMBER, it’s dark by 4:30 p.m. I wrap my torso in color-changing LED tubes. Today I pick emerald. As I descend the trail that clings to the bank of the Delaware River, the historic cantilever bridge that crosses the water comes into view, itself wrapped in green color-changing lights. I smile for no other reason but that we match.

I run up a semicircle road where, for a moment, the world is reduced to cones of warm yellow glowing from vintage streetlights. My chest lamp illuminates just enough ground in front of me to fit my next two steps. Beyond that, I trust that my feet remember what my eyes cannot see.

The darkness has a way of taming my unruly mind. The stillness mutes my whirling thoughts, a nearly impossible achievement among the hectic noise of daylight. It’s the closest I come to meditating, to understanding what it means to be present. It doesn’t matter where the road leads or how long is left of my run; it’s only here and now. The quiet is mostly disrupted by my own two feet. They take turns striking the ground, but their thuds meet in the echo that reverberates off building walls before getting swallowed up by the endless nightfall blue encircling me.

How did I ever allow this to be taken away from me?

Someone lingers at the end of the sidewalk in front of me, and I instinctively switch to the opposite side, picking up speed.

The person pays me no mind, looking in the direction of a leash they’re holding, the end of which I cannot make out. The black road becomes deserted again, and I slow my pace. What was born out of necessity has grown into my favorite part of the day. I love this. I need this. This is the world I want to live in.

Where voters stand on state ballot measures, from abortion to LGBTQ rights to guns

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Voters weighed in on 146 statewide ballot measures across dozens of states. Mario Tama/Getty Images hide caption

Tuesday's midterm elections were a huge sweep for Democrats, among other takeaways.

And across 39 states — plus Washington, D.C. and the Northern Mariana Islands — voters weighed in on 146 statewide ballot measures spanning issues from taxes to transgender rights to traffic cameras to data centers and AI.

Three states moved to preserve abortion access

The U.S. Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in 2022, leaving the legality and regulation of abortion up to each state. In subsequent elections, over a dozen states have put access to abortion on their ballots — and overwhelmingly moved to secure it.

This year, four states considered abortion measures. Three passed, while Idaho voted to preserve the state's abortion ban from 2022.

In Missouri, voters rejected a Republican-led amendment that would have banned abortion with few exceptions. The move affirms support for abortion just two years after voters mobilized to reject Missouri's ban, which was one of the strictest in the country.

Nevada residents voted to enshrine abortion up to the point of viability in their constitution. They did so for the second election in a row, since state law requires a proposed constitutional amendment to pass twice.

Virginians voted to create a "fundamental right to reproductive freedom" in their constitution. That includes the right to an abortion during the first two trimesters (and afterward in certain cases), as well as the right to contraception, fertility, postpartum care and more.

Many states considered changes to how they run their elections.

It was too early to call Wednesday afternoon whether voters had approved some of the more far-reaching changes, in Alaska and Arizona.

Other states — including Nevada, North Carolina and Ohio — passed measures toughening identification requirements for voters, while California voted against such an effort.

Several states — including Alaska, Arkansas, Kansas, South Dakota and West Virginia — approved measures reinforcing that U.S. citizenship is required to vote in their elections.

Redistricting was also top of mind for voters. President Trump kicked off a redistricting race earlier this year by urging Republican-led states to redraw their districts ahead of the midterms. Republicans and Democrats in multiple states did so. But many of the districts altered to boost GOP prospects ultimately went to Democratic candidates.

Missouri voters rejected a Republican-friendly congressional map, while Maryland voters passed a redistricting amendment that could pave the way for a Democrat-leaning map in 2028.

Virginians passed a measure automatically restoring voting rights to anyone convicted of a felony once they have completed their sentence.

Virginians also passed a constitutional amendment officially repealing its ban on gay marriage and adding language confirming that the commonwealth will recognize marriage between two adults regardless of their sex, gender or race.

Virginia's ban has been moot since the Supreme Court made same-sex marriage legal nationwide in 2015, but this measure is seen as a safeguard in case Obergefell v. Hodges gets reversed (as some have feared in the wake of the court's overturning of Roe v. Wade). Other states have made similar moves, including Colorado and California in 2024.

Vermont overwhelmingly voted to add an amendment to its constitution guaranteeing equal protection under the law "race, ethnicity, sex, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, or national origin."

A number of states considered measures restricting transgender kids' access to school sports teams and gender-affirming care, with Colorado passing both. A Washington state measure to ban trans students from competing in girls' school sports was still leading on Wednesday.

Arizona passed a proposal requiring schools and athletic associations to restrict students, athletes and employees from using facilities — including restrooms, locker rooms, and other private spaces — that are not designated for their sex assigned at birth. Arizona law already bans trans girls from playing on school sports teams designated for girls, and this measure would extend that prohibition to recreational organizations like Little League.

One of the most high-profile ballot measures asked Floridians to weigh cutting property taxes for homeowners, which critics say would take billions of dollars in local government revenue away from much-needed public services like food banks, libraries, transportation and infrastructure. Voters overwhelmingly passed the amendment.

In California, voters pondered whether to approve a one-off 5% tax on individuals with more than $1 billion in assets, with most of the money raised going toward health care services. The fight against that ballot measure was one of the most expensive in the state's history. The contest is too close to call as of Wednesday afternoon.

Tennessee voters approved a constitutional amendment prohibiting its government from levying a statewide property tax, which it has not collected since 1949. Arizona voters passed a measure that bans cities and towns from raising taxes on groceries until July 2027. North Carolinians approved an amendment to cap the maximum personal income tax at 3.5%.

Washington voters considered whether to repeal an income tax on individuals and households earning more than $1 million, which is scheduled to take effect in 2028. Early results indicate they want to keep that tax.

A polar-opposite pair of gun ballot measures

Massachusetts voted to keep a 2024 law tightening firearm regulations. The law made Massachusetts gun control measures among the strictest in the nation, as it also included requiring "ghost guns" to have a serial number, broadened the definition of "assault-style firearm" and expanded the state's red flag laws.

Meanwhile, in Idaho, a ballot measure asked voters for help choosing the official state gun from six different brands and models. The Winchester Model 1873 — aka the "Gun that Won the West" — leads in the polls as of Wednesday, though the state legislature would need to pass a separate law to make the winner official.

The ballot initiatives about data centers

At least 38 jurisdictions voted on the sprawling facilities needed to power AI, and Tuesday revealed how many voters dislike them. Jurisdictions in California, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Nevada, New Jersey, North Dakota, Ohio, and Wisconsin voted to restrict data centers.

Of these, at least 12 localities voted to restrict the size of data centers, while six others voted to halt development and ban construction of all facilities, according to Data Centers on the Ballot.

The one community that supported data centers is in Harwood, North Dakota, where 75% of voters did not want to place restrictions on industrial projects, including data centers.

Ohio, one of the frontline states on the issue, had 21 ballot measures mentioning data centers. Voters there moved to restrict the AI facilities: Trenton, Ohio voted to ban data centers over 25 megawatts, while Hubbard moved to ban construction of data centers of any size, including converting existing buildings. Ohio voters also rejected rezoning areas from rural residential to light industrial for data center usage.

Candidates spent up to $40 million on ads about data centers this election cycle. They were a contentious issue in many statewide elections, as 77% of Americans say they are concerned about data centers raising electricity costs, according to a June Reuters/Ipsos poll. The poll also showed two-thirds of Democrats and half of Republicans said they'd oppose a data center in their community.

Rails, or Ruby on Rails, is a Web application framework built on Ruby. The vulnerability (which is tracked as GHSA-f3w6-w445-fwh9) is critical, with a CVSS score of 9.8, and is considered so severe because an attacker could upload a crafted image file to write arbitrary files, read sensitive files, access secrets such as secret_key_base files, external system credentials, database passwords, and third-party tokens. These secrets could then be used for lateral movement or remote code execution into said external systems.

As a GitHub advisory published by the Rails maintainers explains, "A Rails application that processes attachments with ImageMagick may allow an unauthenticated attacker to write arbitrary files on the server and, when attachments are stored with the Disk service, to read arbitrary files readable by the application process."

The vulnerability exists because ImageMagick doesn't necessarily decide how to process a file (i.e., which coder it uses) based on the filename or extension, but rather on its contents. This means an attacker can upload an image file named "maliciousfile.jpeg" but contains content that causes ImageMagick to invoke a different coder. This can potentially allow arbitrary file reads or writes on the server.

Vulnerable versions of Active Storage include those 8.0 versions prior to 8.0.5.2 and 8.1 versions prior to 8.1.4.1. Those affected organizations running Rails applications that use ImageMagick for Active Storage image processing run with a security policy that does not block the affected coders, and accept uploads from untrusted users.

Active Storage patches are available now. The GitHub page includes further mitigation requirements for applications that use MiniMagick and ImageMagick.

Additionally, if an application stores attachments through the Disk service, users should "change secret_key_base and every secret readable by the application process, including the master key and everything config/credentials.yml.enc holds, storage and database credentials, and third-party tokens," the GitHub advisory said. Users should not retain exposed secrets as a fallback.

If a user requires a workaround, the GitHub page offers system rules the user can apply into ImageMagick's system policy.

Jake Knott, head of threat intelligence for exposure management vendor watchTowr, tells Dark Reading the firm has already reproduced the vulnerability in a lab environment, and "that against the right target, exploitation won't be difficult at all."

"The hard part is finding software and deployments that are common enough, and configured in a vulnerable way, to deliver impact at scale indiscriminately."

A Pediatrician, a Pastor, and a Paralympian Just Did Wonders for Kids’ Mental Health

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

— The midterm elections offer lessons in decency and difference

My son is 32. Early Wednesday morning he forwarded me a New York Times story about the new faces Tuesday's midterm elections sent to Washington and to statehouses.

He added a note: "Some themes for your next article, like the Knicks one. A message about being decent human beings. A message about how what makes us different brings us together." Then he quoted one line from the Times article: "There's a pediatrician, a pastor, a Paralympian and a former Navy helicopter pilot."

In June, I wrote that the Knicks' championship handed parents a teaching moment. My son was right: Tuesday handed us another.

Our kids and pediatric patients need the lesson. They have lived through a full campaign season. Attack ads. Comment sections. Adults yelling at screens. Before the last presidential election, 77% of American adults named the future of the nation a significant source of stress. Children absorb what we carry home. In the Trevor Project's 2024 national survey, 90% of LGBTQ+ young people said politics had hurt their well-being in the last year. Let's call that the un-teaching.

First, start with the line my son quoted. The pediatrician is Annie Andrews, MD, of South Carolina. She ran for the Senate on a platform focused on children's health after a 2025 measles outbreak sickened some 1,000 children in her state. Andrews, a pediatric hospitalist, saw harm to kids and asked for the job of stopping it.

The pastor is Adam Hamilton, who leads the largest United Methodist congregation in the country. In January he was a registered Republican. Now he is the first Democrat to win a New Hampshire Senate seat in Kansas since 1932. He campaigned on his faith and promised to work across the aisle.

The Paralympian is Josh Turek of Iowa. Two gold medals in wheelchair basketball. A working-class kid. A man who once voted for George W. Bush. He is headed to the Senate.

The helicopter pilot is Rebecca Bennett. She flew for the Navy, ran a healthcare business, and now heads to the House from New Jersey.

Then come the firsts. Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia will be the first Black woman ever elected governor in this country. Chris Pappas of New Hampshire will be the first openly gay man in the Senate; he told the Times he is living a life he could not have pictured as a boy. Abdul El-Sayed, MD, DPhil, of Michigan, a physician who ran Detroit's health department, will be the first Muslim senator. Melat Kiros, 29, becomes the first Gen Z woman elected to Congress. James Talarico, a former middle-school teacher who quotes Scripture in his stump speech, will be the youngest member of the Senate at 37.

Ask a child what these people share. Different faiths. Different bodies. Different races, ages, loves, hometowns. Each one walked into rooms full of strangers and asked for their trust. The strangers said yes. What makes each of us different brought them together.

Talk about Amy Acton, MD, MPH. A physician, Ohio's former health director, now its governor-elect. She has spoken openly about a traumatic childhood and sexual abuse at the hand of her stepfather. Late in the race, an ad from her opponent put Acton's estranged mother on camera to call her a liar. Acton held her ground. She said she told her story so other people might see a way out.

As a child psychiatrist, I treat children with histories like hers. Most of them believe the worst thing that happened to them is the truest thing about them. Let's tell these patients Acton's story.

Adolescents build an identity partly by borrowing one. They pick role models whose life stories connect to their own, and they gravitate toward role models who resemble them. For some kids that is a matter of life and death. LGBTQ+ youth who have an older LGBTQ+ role model report lower rates of considering and attempting suicide. In an earlier Trevor survey, more than 80% said positive public role models made them feel better about who they are. A gay teenager in Manchester watched a gay man win a New Hampshire Senate seat Tuesday night. A kid in a wheelchair in Des Moines watched a Paralympian win one in Iowa. That registers.

Difference, met up close, heals. Cross-group friendships rank among the strongest known antidotes to prejudice in childhood, and they support children's well-being. Civic life protects too. In a national study that followed more than 9,000 adolescents into adulthood, those who volunteered and voted went on to have better mental health and engage in fewer risky behaviors. Community engagement in adolescence predicted fewer depressive symptoms years later.

My son also pointed out another crucial point: Kids across the country just witnessed an incredible underdog story. Talarico and El-Sayed were up against hundreds of millions of dollars in opposition spending. Several of the winning candidates represent groups historically left out from Congress, including gays, Blacks, Hispanics, and women. There is something immensely powerful about marginalized candidates running campaigns built around taking care of their communities and defeating the wealthiest individuals in the country. It doesn't matter how powerful you are, compassion for others and belief in self is what can change the world. That's a story our young patients need to witness.

Of course, half of the country may have woken up disappointed Wednesday. But even for that half, the lesson holds. Hamilton was a Republican 10 months ago. Turek voted for Bush. A child can admire a person and still argue with the platform. Disagreement is healthy. Contempt is the toxin. After the last election, the head of the American Psychological Association warned that pulling away from people over politics can cost us the relationships we need most.

Decency. Service. Faith worn in the open. Grit in a wheelchair. Honesty about one's own pain. A hand across the aisle. Strangers saying yes to someone unlike themselves.

There's the message. Everyone in this country has a reason to bring it up this week, and a son or daughter or patient will be ready to hear it.

My son is 32. He still sends me lessons. That's how it's supposed to work. Don't let the moment pass.

‘Godzilla Minus Zero’ Review: Oscar Winner Takashi Yamazaki’s Vision Of Japan’s Most Iconic Monster Is Back Bigger Than Ever Battling An Even Deadlier Foe

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

With 2023’s monster hit Godzilla Minus One, Takashi Yamazaki became only the second director after Stanley Kubrick and 2001: A Space Odyssey to take home the Visual Effects Academy Award, and his film became the only Godzilla movie in 70 years to win an Oscar.

Naturally, that all calls for a sequel, doesn’t it? The filmmaker wasn’t sure since he didn’t want to disappoint the legions of fans of the first film, a Japanese Kaiju cinema masterpiece which, unlike most of the Godzilla films, really succeeded in part because of the emotional human story it also had to tell. Well, it is nice to say he hasn’t forgotten that aspect, even as Godzilla Minus Zero, set two years after the first, has gone much bigger and was “filmed for IMAX”. The fabulous Scope visual palette is still there but adding the IMAX brand turns out to complement it rather than smother it.

The irony here is the titular star, Godzilla, is really just a supporting player in his own movie. This one, for much of the 135-minute running time, is more obsessed with another Kaiju, the regally named King Ghidorah (one of my favorite Godzilla foes ever). Ghidorah, in the wake of the apparent death of Godzilla in Minus One, is ruling the skies over Matsumoto, a truly terrifying creature who proves three heads are better than one. He seems invincible but the head of the Large Scale Disaster Response Bureau, Kenju Noda (Hidetake Yoshioka) is convinced he can be banished through electrocution and sets the plan in motion with associates, including Weather whiz and meteorologist Masami Nagasawa (Makoto Aoyama), and Professor Kanji Murakami (Min Tanaka), a survivor of unspeakable horrors including the atom bomb dropped on Hiroshima by the Americans to end WWII. The U.S. Army is around too, but with their own motivations, just as Murakami has his. The film’s first half is all about Ghidorah, and also the returning Shikishima family who so movingly figured in Minus One. Watch on Deadline

Koichi Shikishima (Ryunosuki Kamiki) is now married to Noriko (Minami Hamabe), sporting an eye patch and her own secrets, the parents of now five-year-old Akiko (played again by Sae Nagatani). Koichi is happy to put his past as a kamakazi pilot behind him, although still riddled with guilt over still being alive. Noriko’s connection to Godzilla will soon thrust this loving family into crisis as they are drawn into the new disaster when it becomes clear Godzilla is still around, and actually needed by the powers that be to destroy Ghidorah, a complicated plan concocted when all else fails. How the hell do they lure Godzilla out of hiding and to cooperate in this ambitious scheme. This all leads to the film’s truly spectacular Kaiju battle for the ages. Yamazaki had only 35 artisans on board for the equally thrilling fights in Godzilla Minus One, and here only really adds a handful, who all contribute to the remarkable visual effects on display, at an ounce of the cost of major studio productions on this scale. I smell another Oscar.

Still despite all the histrionics on display, it is the human element that makes this a step above the usual monster mashups. In a heartbreaking Solomon-style moment, circumstances force the parents to give up Akiko to an orphanage as their duty to country (and I won’t give the exact details of that but it is pretty persuasive) trumps duty to family. The separation scene is gut wrenching as Akiko fails to understand why she is being abandoned and gives one wail of a goodbye scene.

The craft of Godzilla Minus Zero is stunning to say the least and if ever there was a reason to head out to the local IMAX, this movie gives you one. The good news is this may not be the last of Yamazaki’s dance with Godzilla as the ending leaves room for a third edition, which considering the artistic and crowd- pleasing success of the first two is something to look forward to.

Producer is Kazuaki Kishida. In Japanese with English subtitles.
