Is ‘I Play Rocky’ Original or Adapted? Stallone’s ‘Rocky’ Origin Story Faces a ‘Barbie’-Style Oscar Dilemma (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sylvester Stallone wrote “Rocky” in three days. Figuring out which Oscar screenplay category the movie about him writing it belongs in is taking a lot longer.

Amazon MGM Studios’ “I Play Rocky” has been the subject of multiple conversations among the studio, the Writers Guild of America, the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences and the film’s creative team over whether Peter Gamble’s script will compete as an original or adapted screenplay. No determination has been made. Thursday, Nov. 12, is the final deadline for studios to submit Official Screen Credits (OSC) forms to the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences for the 99th Academy Awards in the best picture, animated feature and general entry categories. No films afterward will be considered.

Gamble conceived and wrote the script as an original, and it isn’t based on a book, article, memoir or any other single published work. He drafted it on spec, and a spot on the Black List carried it to producers Christian Baha and Toby Emmerich and eventually to director Peter Farrelly, who helmed best picture winner “Green Book” (2018). It’s Gamble’s second produced feature script after “Office Uprising” (2018).

But “Rocky” is one of the most established pieces of IP in Hollywood, and sources say some within each organization are weighing whether the screenplay is a better fit for adapted, the category for scripts built on previously established material. Rocky Balboa, Adrian and Apollo Creed are preexisting characters and Farrelly’s film restages some of the 1976 movie’s best-known scenes, written half a century ago.

Amazon MGM and the Academy declined to comment.

“I Play Rocky” expands nationwide Nov. 20, the same day WGA submissions close, but the guild won’t be weighing in. The film wasn’t produced under a WGA signatory agreement, so the guild can’t classify the screenplay or consider it for the WGA Awards, even though Gamble is a member. That leaves the decision entirely to the Academy’s Writers Branch executive committee, which makes its own determinations and has broken with the WGA before.

Elsewhere on the circuit, the question will carry less weight. The Golden Globes put all screenplays in a single category, while BAFTA and the Critics Choice Awards, which split original and adapted, generally go with the category the studio submits.

According to the Academy’s screenplay submission FAQ for the 99th Oscars, films don’t submit to a specific writing category. Instead, the Writers Branch executive committee reviews each eligible title and generally defers to the WGA’s classification, except when the guild hasn’t classified a film, as is the case with “I Play Rocky” or its classification “has been called into question.” The guidelines cut both ways for Gamble’s script.

A script is generally deemed adapted if it’s based on a sequel, a remake, an existing film or “pre-existing characters,” but it’s considered original if it tells a true story in which the writer “performed their own research and used multiple sources.” While a determination is under review, the committee may ask the film’s credited writers for a statement. Once it rules, the decision is final and no appeals or additional materials are accepted.

We seem to have one of these every awards season.

In January 2024, Greta Gerwig’s “Barbie” was moved to adapted after Warner Bros. campaigned it as an original and the WGA classified as well. Barbie and Ken existed as Mattel dolls long before Gerwig and Noah Baumbach turned them into a billion-dollar sensation, and there were multiple cartoons that existed with the characters. Ultimately, the script earned an adapted nomination and lost to Cord Jefferson’s “American Fiction.”

Category reversals aren’t new. Barry Jenkins’ “Moonlight,” Damien Chazelle’s “Whiplash” and Joel and Ethan Coen’s “The Ballad of Buster Scruggs” all carried original labels from the WGA before the Academy shifted them to adapted. The Academy has also long treated sequels as adaptations because their characters come from earlier films, which is how “Before Sunset,” “Toy Story 3,” “Top Gun: Maverick” and “Glass Onion” all landed in adapted.

That rule is being tested again this season. Aaron Sorkin’s “The Social Reckoning,” which dramatizes whistleblower Frances Haugen’s 2021 Facebook leak and Wall Street Journal reporter Jeff Horwitz’s coverage of it, was initially marketed as a sequel to “The Social Network” (2010), which won Sorkin the adapted screenplay Oscar. The WGA has classified the new film as an original. There’s no word yet on whether the Academy’s committee will accept that designation.

That’s the precedent Gamble’s script has to get around, and there’s a strong counterargument. “I Play Rocky” isn’t a sequel, a prequel or a spinoff. It’s a biographical drama about a real person, and biopics have routinely competed as originals. Dustin Lance Black won original screenplay for “Milk,” even though Harvey Milk’s life had already been chronicled in the Oscar-winning documentary “The Times of Harvey Milk.” Anthony McCarten’s “Darkest Hour” also competed as an original, despite covering Winston Churchill’s first weeks as prime minister, a chapter that fills every high school history textbook.

Movies about the making of movies are interesting territory, and they have produced originals, too. “Saving Mr. Banks,” about the making of “Mary Poppins,” competed as an original but didn’t land a nomination. Neither did Jason Reitman and Gil Kenan’s “Saturday Night,” which follows the 90 minutes leading up to the first televised episode of “Saturday Night Live.” Netflix’s “Mank,” written by the late Jack Fincher, father of director David Fincher, chronicles screenwriter Herman J. Mankiewicz’s work on Orson Welles’ “Citizen Kane” (1941). It was also deemed an original, and it also missed in the category, even as the film led the field with 10 nominations.

When a making-of film does land in adapted, there’s usually a book behind it. Scott Neustadter and Michael H. Weber’s “The Disaster Artist,” for example, was based on Greg Sestero and Tom Bissell’s 2013 nonfiction book of the same name. A public figure’s life, however, is fair game, as illustrated by A24’s Chris Hansen story, “Primetime,” which Ajon Singh based on an article by Luke Dittrich.

Interestingly, Amazon MGM owns the “Rocky” franchise. The 1976 original earned 10 Oscar nominations and won best picture, and Stallone was nominated for best actor and original screenplay. Director Farrelly knows the original side all too well, as he shared the original screenplay Oscar with Brian Hayes Currie and Nick Vallelonga for “Green Book,” another true story that drew from no single published source.

Where the film lands could reshape its trajectory, and both races are stacked. In original, Jesse Eisenberg (“The Debut”), Jordan Firstman (“Club Kid”), Mike Leigh (“Tender Loving Care”), Martin McDonagh (“Wild Horse Nine”) and Simon Rich (“Artificial”) are all in the conversation. A move to adapted wouldn’t clear the road. It would put Gamble up against Christopher Nolan’s “The Odyssey,” Drew Goddard’s “Project Hail Mary” and Rashida Jones and Will McCormack’s “The Invite,” along with Los Javis’ “La Bola Negra” and Denis Villeneuve’s yet-to-be-screened “Dune: Part Three.”

“I Play Rocky” has been gaining momentum since its world premiere at TIFF. It finished first runner-up for the People’s Choice Award behind “La Bola Negra” and was the runner-up at Middleburg. It was also the audience choice winner at Cinéfest Sudbury International Film Festival, and recently Ippolito was honored with the breakthrough award at SCAD Savannah Film Festival. It recently opened on Nov. 6 in seven theaters, capturing $345,000 from seven theaters, averaging $49,200 per location. The film has also made one category call already: it’s submitted at the Golden Globes as a musical or comedy, joining a field that includes “Wild Horse Nine,” “The Debut” and Amazon MGM stablemate “Project Hail Mary.”

The Academy’s final word likely won’t surface until nominations voting opens Jan. 11, 2027. Before that, the WGA announces its screenplay nominees Jan. 8. Oscar nominations will be announced Jan. 21, and the 99th Oscars take place March 14.

Sarina Wiegman receives honorary damehood from Prince William at Windsor Castle - after leading Lionesses to back-to-back European titles

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Published: 14:28 EST, 10 November 2026 | Updated: 19:04 EST, 10 November 2026

Lionesses manager Sarina Wiegman has been awarded an honorary damehood by Prince William after leading England to back-to-back Euro wins.

Wiegman is Dutch and therefore cannot be made a substantive dame. Instead she has picked up the honorary title, because the Netherlands does not have a British monarch as its head of state.

She said upon being awarded the title at Windsor Castle on Tuesday: 'I feel very privileged to receive my honorary Damehood from His Royal Highness The Prince of Wales at Windsor Castle today.

'It is a day I will never forget and made even more special by sharing it with members of my family.

'I would like to again express my sincere gratitude for this honour and for the continued respect and warmth I experience from the English people. I thank everyone for their support.'

Wiegman was named in the King's New Year's Honours list at the end of 2025, alongside several members of her England team.

England manager Sarina Wiegman (left) has been awarded an honourary damehood by Prince William (right)

Captain Leah Williamson was awarded a CBE, with Alex Greenwood, Keira Walsh, Georgia Stanway and Ella Toone all being honoured with MBEs.

Wiegman said at the time: 'I would like to express my sincere gratitude for this honour. When I first arrived in England, I could never have imagined the respect and warmth I've experienced from the English people. I deeply thank the fans for their support.

'This title is a reflection of my exceptional team of players and staff. My congratulations go to Alex, Ella, Georgia, Keira, Leah and also Dr Ritan Mehta who have all been celebrated for their brilliant contributions.

'Our team values mean that when there is recognition for one, then there is recognition for all. I want to pay tribute to every member of our EURO squad. They should all be celebrated for everything they brought to the pitch and for the positive role they play in society. I'm proud to work alongside such a dedicated group of people.'

The former Netherlands coach took over the England team in 2021 and led them to success at Euro 2022 and Euro 2025.

She also reached the World Cup final in 2023, and signed a new two-year contract extension earlier this year, which will take her to the end of the 2029 Euros.

a giant circle frames NIKO’s timber guesthouse and tiny curved sauna in japan

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

NIKO design studio's 'marumi house' in japan features a giant circular facade opening, exposed cedar logs, and a tiny sauna.

NIKO Design Studio has completed Umino Marumi, a two-story wooden guesthouse on a hillside overlooking the Pacific coast of Chiba, Japan. With its enormous circular facade opening and exposed timber structure, the house makes an unusual addition to the surrounding residential neighborhood. In the garden, a much smaller companion building takes shape as a sauna hut, its curved roof designed to accommodate the trees growing around it.

Completed in 2025, Marumi was conceived as a second home where the owners could gather with family and friends, while also creating a welcoming presence within the local community. The architects approached this ambition through an engawa, the traditional Japanese veranda, transforming it into a series of staggered outdoor platforms that climb through the house and connect to the upper floor.

The Marumi House’s most recognizable feature is the enormous circular opening cut into its dark facade, revealing a network of timber columns and elevated walkways beneath the pitched roof. Around one-third of the covered building is dedicated to the semi-outdoor engawa, where visitors can sit, gather, or move between levels without entering the enclosed rooms.

With this layout, the architects at NIKO Design Studio give the house an unusually open relationship with its surroundings. From outside, the veranda offers glimpses of people moving through the timber structure, while its various levels accommodate both intimate gatherings and larger groups. The circular opening brings these activities into view, turning the facade into an inhabited space that faces the neighborhood.

NIKO Design Studio worked with substantial cedar logs measuring approximately 400 millimeters in diameter, assembling the structural frame using traditional kanuki joinery. Horizontal timber members pass through openings in the round columns, creating a robust structure that retains the natural geometry of the wood. The holes were cut using chainsaws, following an approach suggested by the carpenters and inspired by forestry techniques.

The same hands-on approach extends into the landscape. To address the sloping site, a stone retaining wall was assembled during a two-day workshop using nozumi, a traditional stone-stacking technique. The rough stonework sits alongside the large timber structure, which brings a sense of craftsmanship to a building designed for communal use.

Beyond the main house, the architects faced a different challenge when designing a freestanding sauna for the garden. The owners initially wanted a cabin for three or four people, but commercially available models appeared surprisingly bulky when placed within a model of the site. Looking more closely at the surrounding landscape, the team noticed a neighboring palm tree extending over the property boundary. Its continued growth became the starting point for the sauna’s unusual curved roof.

Working with carpenter Murakami of Murakami Carpentry Workshop, the architects sourced naturally bent timber from trees that had grown along a hillside. These curved members formed the roof structure, allowing its profile to rise and dip around the surrounding vegetation. When the timber arrived thicker and longer than anticipated, the team decided to retain the excess length, leaving the beams projecting beyond the roofline. The unexpected detail gives the small building much of its character.

Despite its expressive roof, the sauna occupies remarkably little ground. Its foundation measures just two by two meters, with a total floor area of five square meters including the stepped portion. Inside, timber-lined surfaces and a tall ceiling make the small space feel surprisingly generous. Although originally intended for three or four bathers, the completed sauna can accommodate five or six people.

The hut combines electric and wood-fired heating, while a skylight brings daylight into the compact interior. It connects with the surrounding terrace and the house’s bathroom, which can serve as a cold bath during sauna sessions. Marumi and its miniature companion together explore two scales of wooden architecture, with one opening itself toward the neighborhood and the other adjusting its form to the trees already growing in the garden.

Big Walk AI Controversy Is a GOTY Awards Twist Sparking a Bigger Debate

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An IGF voter stance marks a new hardline for the industry

Big Walk, one of the best-reviewed games of 2026 and by all measures a Game of the Year darling, faces a crossroads on the eve of awards season. Following a recent disclosure of AI use from its developer, Big Walk’s chances at one awards show seem fraught. And its shot at the big one (December’s Game Awards) might be impacted. But it’s not just a chapter in 2026’s awards show saga. In many ways, it’s a boiling over of a debate that’s been bubbling in the games industry for several years now.

On Monday, a group of 19 Independent Game Festival (IGF) jurors, including Immortality director Sam Barlow and Friends at the Table host Austin Walker, shared a statement saying they would not vote for any game that used generative AI tools in any capacity — including for code.

On Bluesky, Barlow said that this discussion among IGF jurors is always lively, but that this year’s “conversation was even more intense.”

Why? It did not take long for at least part of the reason to become clear: Big Walk used generative AI tools in development, developer House House revealed in its voluntary disclosure to the IGF. (In a follow-up statement, Barlow noted that the impetus for the conversation was more broadly about the volume of games that make use of generative AI, and that Big Walk was the "tricky edge case.")

In a statement to Aftermath (also cross-posted on the developer’s website), House House said that “well under 0.1% of Big Walk’s code” had anything to do with generative AI tools, and those tools were used solely to “help work through difficult technical challenges and bugs.” House House also noted that the studio used large language models (LLMs) to generate a baseline, which the team then “modified into game code.” House House’s statement gets into it in full, but the studio pointed to a lighting issue as an example of how LLMs helped with the final phase of Big Walk’s development, amounting to “about 10 lines of code.”

That might seem small in the grand scheme of things, but a hard line is only a hard line if you don’t cross it. And for 19 members of the IGF jury, that’s the line.

The group of jurors cite several reasons for its stance against generative AI, including the technology’s widely documented negative impacts on the economy, the labor market, the environment, and the global informational pipeline. They also say that AI use is “not a foregone conclusion” in the games industry, and, as independent developers, see this as an opportunity to take a stance that many people working at larger companies might not have the leeway to take.

Critically, the inclusion of code as part of the hardline is not something you see every day. Generally, the colloquial argument against generative AI use is reserved for disciplines that are considered more “creative” in nature: art, writing, composition, voice acting, and so forth. You often see people make exceptions for technical work, like programming. This stance from part of the IGF jury marks a clear public expansion of the tent among an influential games industry group, and it’s not lost among a subset of the community that isn’t always public-facing. Responses to Walker’s Bluesky post of the statement are full of programmers thanking the jurors for considering coding an art form.

The debate isn’t merely broken down among clean lines of “AI good” vs. “AI bad.” Some see it as a betrayal not just that House House used these tools but that it didn’t disclose that use upon release. If the use of an LLM was no big deal, the argument goes, why hide it? And if the company hid this instance, what else was hidden? (Though Steam requires a disclosure for generative AI use, it does not require that disclosure for the use of code or chatbots.)

For others, it’s just about the best case scenario for a worst-case situation. It’s a fairly minimal use, they say, and if strict delineations are made for the public disclosure of 10 lines of AI-generated code, wouldn’t that disqualify many video games? Plus, they say, House House seems reflective and open to change, pointing to the studio’s philanthropic efforts in the past as a sign of good will. (In 2020, following the release of Untitled Goose Game, House House pledged to donate 1% of its profits to organizations that help members of indigenous communities make rent.)

Will this ultimately affect Big Walk’s chances during awards season? Maybe! Though the statement was issued by fewer than 20 IGF jury members, it’s gained traction across both social and traditional media. And now that it’s become a matter of public interest and widespread discussion, there’s always the chance other voters are swayed by their arguments or otherwise hesitate to vote for Big Walk.

How this affects the Game Awards horse race is a cloudier issue. On Monday, Big Walk was all but a lock for a Game of the Year nomination, according to Polygon’s ongoing analysis of the race. But an indie game securing a GOTY nomination is always an uphill race — even without the albatross of controversy.

Yes, the technically indie game Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 smashed records and swept the 2025 Game Awards, but it also broke into the mainstream in an undeniable way. Big Walk, despite its impressive critical reception, has not. To secure the nomination, it needs every vote it can get from nominating jurors who are part of the indie gaming community, which largely rejects generative AI. To wit: Clair Obscur may have run the tale during the 2025 awards circuit, but ultimately had its Game of the Year award from the Indie Game Awards (note: not the same thing as the IGF) stripped over inclusion of AI.

Beyond awards, the entire saga seems an inflection point for an industry that has struggled to grapple with the encroachment of generative AI. Developers of all strata — from the creator of The Last of Us to the studio behind the Wendy’s JRPG — have taken public stances against the technology, refusing to use AI whatsoever in any part of development. They stand in contrast to prominent firms like Embark Studios, which caught heat for using AI in its 2023 shooter The Finals and then immediately used the same tools for its 2025 shooter Arc Raiders, or Epic Games, which is run by a CEO whose stance on LLMs could fairly be summarized as “Claude = Jesus 2.0.”

A weekly cheat sheet to what you’ll eventually love to play. No buying, no commitment — just saving for later.

All the while, AI tools have proven a third rail for large portions of the gaming audience, often prompting public apologies from developers that disclose their use. House House joins that club.

“Big Walk was developed without an internal AI usage policy in place, and we consider that lack of policy a failure,” House House said in its statement. “We will work to have a more comprehensive policy going forwards.”

That’s good on House House. Stating some clearly defined rules for this sort of thing helps settle internal debate, and provides a north star for anyone working at the studio. It could even serve as a model for others in the industry to adopt. But the lack of policy shouldn’t land entirely on the shoulders of a small outfit in Melbourne — or any developer of any size, for that matter. At this point, generative AI tools have been publicly available for nearly half a decade. Until governments start taking the technology seriously and passing real regulations around what counts as acceptable use, this will keep happening.
