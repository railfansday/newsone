Boy Meets World Cast Reveals Major Salary Differences in Final Season

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Boy Meets World alum Rider Strong revealed how a misunderstanding of his contract led to him receiving a much lower salary than his costar Ben Savage.

Rider Strong was meeting a world of salary differences.

The Boy Meets World alum got candid about the vast pay disparity between himself and costar Ben Savage on the seventh—and final—season of the series in Book Meets World: The Definitive Inside Story of Hit Sitcom Boy Meets World, a book he co-wrote with costars Danielle Fishel and Will Friedle.

As Strong explained, he was uninterested in starring on season seven of the show—which ran from 1993 to 2000—but had “misread” his contract and believed that he was already “locked in” for the season, according to an excerpt from the book shared by Entertainment Weekly. The misunderstanding led to a call from creator Michael Jacobs, who assured him there would be no season eight, only the final, seventh season.

“I was already in college,” Strong wrote, per the outlet, “and I didn’t want to do the show anymore.”

However, 46-year-old recalled that Jacobs pitched him “hard,” telling Strong he could head to college the following year.

“I stopped him, and I said, ‘You realize, they’re talking about adding an eighth season.’ He was like, ‘No, we’re not. We’re talking about adding a seventh,’” Strong wrote, adding that Jacobs allegedly told him, “‘Okay, so we’re just thinking about adding a year that you already thought was there, so we’re good.’”

The meeting left the former child star feeling “confused,” although he knew his manager would be happy that he signed on for another season. Still, he felt alone throughout the negotiation process.

“I thought, why is nobody looking out for me and just saying, ‘Rider, if you don’t want to do this, you don’t have to?’” he said. “There was no sense of that.”

“I had my own relationship with my manager, my own relationship with my agent, which is why I walked into a meeting with Michael by myself and got completely screwed,” he continued, noting that “if he could go back, it would have ended differently.”

And while the Pod Meets World host wishes he hadn’t stayed on for a seventh season, he did notice that costar Savage “held out” on committing to the final season after getting accepted to Stanford University, which Strong said led to Savage receiving a “huge pay increase.”

“There was a lot of miscommunication. If we had actually talked to each other, we probably should have just said, ‘We’re all equal here, let’s go [negotiate together].’ But there was no sense of that,” Strong claimed in the book. “It seemed that Ben getting into Stanford was his biggest negotiating tactic, because the contract ended. They wanted the seventh season, but he was like, ‘Are you going to not allow me to go to college?’ He stuck to his guns, and it worked out.”

As for how much Savage’s pay was increased? According to Strong, he was able to negotiate a salary close to that of costar Bill Daniels—an established actor who was the highest paid actor on the show—earning “close to six figures per episode.”

And as Strong’s costar Friedle noted in the book, he and the rest of the cast “were making nowhere near that,” adding that he regrets not entering negotiations with Strong “so that the two of them were making the same salary.”

E! News has reached out to Savage and Jacobs for comment, but has not heard back.

For a look at what more stars have shared about their own salaries through the years, keep reading.

The Jackass star reflected on the "comical" amount of money he was paid for doing death-defying stunts on the MTV series' first season in 2000.

"When it was all said and done, after taxes, I was paid less than $1,500 for the entire first season of Jackass," the daredevil told Playboy in June 2026. "Before the show came out, my sister kicked me out of the house. I was broke, unemployed, and homeless, and a star on this big MTV show."

Oscar winner Jennifer Lawrence was paid $25 million to star in Adam McKay’s ensemble disaster comedy Don’t Look Up. But her costar and fellow Oscar winner Leonardo DiCaprio got $30 million.

“Look, Leo brings in more box office than I do,” Lawrence told Vanity Fair for its’ December 2020/January 2021 issue. “I’m extremely fortunate and happy with my deal. But in other situations, what I have seen—and I’m sure other women in the workforce have seen as well—is that it’s extremely uncomfortable to inquire about equal pay. And if you do question something that appears unequal, you’re told it’s not gender disparity but they can’t tell you what exactly it is.”

Her sanguine attitude was hard-fought: When the disparity between Lawrence’s compensation and that of her male costars for 2013’s American Hustle was revealed in the 2014 Sony email hack, it prompted an industry-wide conversation about the gender pay gap in Hollywood.

"I'm so fortunate to have my job. My problem is not money," Lawrence told the U.K.’s Channel 4 News in 2017. "I wasn't upset that I only got this many millions for a movie. That's ridiculous. I was angry about the unfairness and inequality."

The *NSYNC alum revealed that he and his fellow bandmates JC Chasez, Joey Fatone, Chris Kirkpatrick and Justin Timberlake didn't make much money during their years with the group.

“We had a per diem: $35 a day,” he revealed on The Zach Sang Show in 2026. “Our first check was a $10,000 check after years of No. 1 albums, sellout tours. I don’t know how many millions they made off of merchandise because merchandise was insane.”

“And then they finally gave us a check for $10,000,” he recalled. “You’re like, ‘Oh, thanks.’ We should be grateful for that, been working for free for two, three years now.”

Less comparable to DiCaprio’s salary: The $60,000 “before commissions and taxes” that Jonah Hill was paid for 2013’s The Wolf of Wall Street, while the titular wolf made $10 million.

“They gave me the lowest amount of money possible,” Hill recalled on The Howard Stern Show in 2014. But it was the chance to appear in a Martin Scorsese movie, so the Moneyball alum wanted to get the deal done before anyone could change their mind.

“I would sell my house and give him all my money to work for [Scorsese],” explained Hill, who earned his second Best Supporting Actor nomination for his turn as a squirrelly substance-abusing stockbroker. “This isn't what you make money for. You do 22 Jump Street or you do other things, and you can pay your rent. I would have done anything in the world. I would do it again in a second. This isn't about money. You should do things that you care about."

According to Dylan Sprouse, his residuals from filming The Suite Life of Zack & Cody with twin brother Cole Sprouse is anything but suite.

"The only residual check I've ever received for The Suite Life at all was a crossover episode with Hannah Montana for five cents," the Disney Channel alum shared during a 2026 episode of his Wildmen podcast. "In a time where residuals would have been great, not a single paycheck for being arguably one of the most popular shows on television."

While there's no cash prize for nabbing Dancing With the Stars' mirrorball trophy, “whoever makes it to the finale, you all get paid up until then," Jenna Johnson, who won season 33 with partner Joey Graziadei, shared. "And you do get a nice bonus.”

"If you win," Johnson, who noted pro contracts are dependent on seniority, continued. "it’s not like you get a million dollars. You’re just getting a cute trophy together.” As for those who say farewell to the ballroom early, well, as she explained, "You’re guaranteed until a certain amount of weeks.” According to multiple reports, dancer salaries start at around $1,200 to $1,600 an episode, which can increase to up to of $100,000 per season. (ABC has not confirmed these reports.) On the flip side, celebrities receive a starting salary of $125,000. As they cha-cha further into the competition, stars  receive additional bonuses with the semifinalists and finalists getting paid up to $50,000 extra.

The actress seemingly confirmed that the Friends cast—including Jennifer Aniston, Courtney Cox,David Schwimmer and Matt LeBlanc—get $20 million in residuals per year from the show re-airing, quipping to The Times in April 2026 that if she had to guess why, it's “Because Phoebe Buffay was so great?"

But in actuality, she praised the talents of her costars for their performances.

“I felt I did OK, but Jennifer and Courteney? Amazing,” she raved. “David and Matt? They had me laughing so hard. And then Matthew [Perry]—he was just beyond us all.”

To hear Joe Giudice tell it, his and ex-wife Teresa Giudice's salary for season one of The Real Housewives of New Jersey was nothing to flip over. "We got paid nothing the first season," he detailed on daughter Gia Giudice's Casual Chaos podcast in August 2025. "I'll even say, it was like $25,000. It was ridiculous. But, whatever."

And the ingredientses they put into that initial fame-making season weren't cheap. "I think the first year we had that big party in my great room," the father of four reflected. "I think that show cost me $150,000 the first year."

Oprah Winfrey happily accepted only a little bit of green to star in 1985’s The Color Purple.

“They were only offering $35,000 to be in this film, and it is the best $35,000 I ever earned,” the billionaire media mogul told Essence in 2023. “It changed everything and taught me so much.”

The actress revealed that she made about $20,000 per episode of Two and a Half Men with a 5 percent raise each season, making about $43,000 for one episode in the final season.

When she scored the role of late Tejana singer Selena Quintanilla in the 1997 biopic Selena, Jennifer Lopez became the first Latina actress to earn $1 million for a film.

“I was too young and didn’t know what the hell was going on,” Lopez told Variety in 2019, reflecting on her breakout movie. “It was great they offered me a million dollars. I feel like everyone was making a statement.”

And it paved the way for her to make hundreds of millions more, though sometimes her participation doesn’t cost a thing.

“I do things because I love them,” Lopez explained to GQ in 2019. “I didn't get paid a whole bunch of money for Hustlers. I did it for free and produced it. I bank on myself, you know? Like Jenny From the Block—I do what I love."

Adrien Brody didn’t do The Brutalist for the money. Rather, he took on the role of a Hungarian architect suffering for his art in post-WWII America for $250,000 and won his second Oscar for Best Actor.

“I need a studio movie now, because I’ve poured it all into this,” Brody quipped to Variety, referring to the “barn that looks like a castle” he’s renovating in upstate New York.

“Do you guys know what they pay us?” Pete Davidson cracked to New York magazine when asked ahead of Saturday Night Live's 50th anniversary special how he spent his money when he was first hired at the age of 20 in 2014. “It’s like three grand an episode, so I think I got dinner.”

Interestingly, the salaries have remained proportional for SNL newcomers, with season one stars John Belushi, Jane Curtin, Gilda Radner, Dan Aykroyd and Chevy Chase reportedly earning $750 an episode back in 1975. Which was roughly equivalent to $3,300 in 2014, according to the Consumer Price Index Inflation calculator. (Though the $750 of 1975 is $4,428 in 2025.)

While Kenan Thompson makes a lot more than $3,000 per episode of SNL now that he’s in his record 22nd season, the former Nickelodeon star had to start somewhere.

“That first commercial when they paid me, it was $800,” the All That alum revealed in Demi Lovato‘s 2024 documentary Child Star. “I was 12 so that may as well have been a million dollars."

It turns out Gilmore Girls’ status as a beloved rewatchable classic is literally priceless.

“There really are no residuals on Netflix,” Lauren Graham, who played Lorelai Gilmore for seven seasons and then reprised the role for 2016’s Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life, said on Jimmy Kimmel Live in March 2025. “But I’ve been paid in love and appreciation.”

Feeling not so appreciated, Tommy Dorfman got specific about her 13 Reasons Why paycheck during the 2023 SAG-AFTRA strike—which was prompted in part (as was the WGA strike) by streamers like Netflix not making it a practice to pay additional compensation to cast and crew once a show had become a big hit.

"my earnings for the entire first season of 13 reasons why were $29,953.24 prior to agency and manager fees (20%) and taxes," Dorfman wrote on Threads July 24. "8 episodes over six months." She continued, “i did all of the promo and had KEY ART for this show, flew round trip from NYC to SF to shoot for every episode, was kept for days without pay/working. i barely qualified for insurance."

And, Dorfman added, “within the first 28 days of release, the show's season 1 garnered a total of 476 million view hours. this is why we strike."

Without naming names or numbers, Dax Shepard told fellow Parenthood alum Lauren Graham when she appeared on his podcast that he knew he made “among the lowest of all the actors” on their NBC drama, which ran for six seasons.

But, the Armchair Expert host stressed that he did not mind.

“Every job I had ever had in my life prior to Parenthood, I made a point to find out what everyone was making,” he said. “I'd always figure it out—either a conversation where I get it out of them or I backchannel through an agent."

So he made a point to purposely not seek out such info about his Parenthood family, which helped with enjoying the experience even when he did get an inkling of where he was on the pay pyramid.

“I got a dollar for writing the script,” George Clooney told the Los Angeles Times in 2005 about the financial strings he pulled to get his film Good Night, and Good Luck made. “I had to endorse my check for directing and turn in my acting salary. [Actor, cowriter and coproducer Grant Heslov] and I each made a buck for doing it.”

His labor of love about CBS News journalist Edward R. Murrow speaking truth to power in the 1950s had legs, though: Making his Broadway debut, Clooney wrapped his months-long stint playing Murrow in a stage adaptation of the film  in June 2025. And with the play having grossed a Broadway record (for a nonmusical) $3.3 million during a preview week before it officially opened April 3, he made a lot more than $1.

For his chilling turn as Patrick Bateman in 2000’s American Psycho, Christian Bale was paid “the absolute minimum they were legally allowed to pay me” because no one in the production really wanted to hire him except director Mary Harron, the Welsh actor told GQ in 2022.

He made so little, Bale shared, that he remembered “sitting in the makeup trailer and the makeup artists were laughing at me because I was getting paid less than any of them.”

No, the Game of Thrones cast didn't get paid $300,000 per episode, according to Emilia Clarke.

“We didn’t earn that much," she told Variety in 2026. "Can you imagine? I’d have been driving a couple of Porsches!"

However, the actress—who played Daenerys Targaryen for eight seasons between 2011 and 2019—noted that the money she made throughout the HBO show did provide her with enough financial security to pay off her parents’ mortgage.

His uncle may have been Rico, but Jon Heder confirmed in 2010 that he was “initially” only paid $1,000 for Napoleon Dynamite.

After the irreverent comedy became a sleeper hit, he was able to renegotiate for a piece of the profits, telling the New Zealand Herald, “They went a little bit higher.”

Djimon Hounsou said he’s “definitely underpaid” in Hollywood despite his prestigious list of credits, including Amistad and Blood Diamond.

"I've been in this business and making films now over two decades," the Beninese actor told CNN’s Larry Madowo in January 2025. "And still—with two Oscar nominations and been in many big blockbuster films—and yet I’m still struggling financially to make a living."

Rebel Wilson detailed in her 2024 memoir Rebel Rising that she was paid $3,500 to do Bridesmaids, “a fee that I then had to pay directly to the Screen Actors Guild to join the union. So really, I got paid nothing.”

Still, she wrote, the experience was “everything!”

And the Australian actress’ star rose ever a-ca higher, with her fee going from the SAG minimum scale of $65,000 for 2012’s Pitch Perfect to $10 million for 2017’s Pitch Perfect 3. Wilson wrote that she raked in $20 million total for the threequel, Isn’t It Romantic and The Hustle, though—reality alert—she “lost almost 50 percent to taxes, 10 percent to agents and 5 percent” to her lawyer. But even after those obligations and paying her publicist, business manager and assistant, Wilson “netted what to me was an absolute fortune.”

In late 2017, Ellen Pompeo negotiated a new Grey’s Anatomy contract for $575,000 per episode, a seven-figure signing bonus and two full backend equity points worth an estimated $6 million to $7 million.

“I’m 48 now, so I’ve finally gotten to the place where I’m OK asking for what I deserve, which is something that comes only with age,” Pompeo told The Hollywood Reporter in 2018. “Because I’m not the most ‘relevant’ actress out there. I know that’s the industry perception because I’ve been this character for 14 years. But the truth is, anybody can be good on a show season one and two. Can you be good 14 years later? Now, that’s a f--kin’ skill.”

Reflecting more recently on how she always made less than costar Patrick Dempsey, even though she was the Grey in question, Pompeo said she didn’t begrudge him his money.

But, she said on a March 2025 episode of Call Her Daddy, “Just being that I was the namesake of the show, I deserved the same and that was harder to get. I wasn't salty about him getting what he got. I was salty that they didn't value me as much as they valued him and they never will.”

Jamie Lee Curtis recalled getting paid $2,000 a week—so $8,000 in total—for 1978’s Halloween, her movie debut. “I mean,” she told Rotten Tomatoes in 2018, “nobody got paid, anything.”

Yet while the scream queen earned four figures, director John Carpenter’s friend Nick Castle felt like a king making $25 per day to share the role of Michael Myers. (It’s Anthony Moran whose face you see when the killer’s mask comes off for a freaky second, and editor Tommy Lee Wallace also logged screentime as Michael.)

“That was a lot at the time!” Castle, who attended film school at USC with Carpenter, told Vanity Fair in 2018. “You have to remember: My interest in doing the film was being on set, so I could demystify the experience of filmmaking and directing. I expected to hang around the set for no money. But hey, $25 per day, and all I had to do was wear a rubber mask.”

Taraji P. Henson earned a Best Supporting Actress Oscar nomination for 2008’s The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, but years later she was still fired up by the $150,000 she accepted to be in the movie—after producers rejected the $500,000 quote she brought to the table.

As for the film’s leads Brad Pitt and Cate Blanchett, “You don’t hear a lot of $20 million quotes anymore, but at this time that was happening,” Henson said on the Ladies First With Laura Brown podcast in 2021. “And rightfully so—I’m not saying they shouldn’t have paid Cate and Brad what they deserved.”

But, she continued, “I’m bringing a certain amount to the seat too and I felt like what I was asking at that time in my career was fair, was fair to the ticket sales that I would contribute to this big film. Damian Lillard is back and Ja Morant is here. How exactly is this going to work? | Bill Oram

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the history of basketball, we have certainly seen some why-would-they-do-that? pairings.

Jerseys and sleeves. Kawhi Leonard and a tree sponsorship. Utah and "the Jazz."

Once, there was even Detlef Schrempf and a headband, if you can believe that.

We're about to find out if they belong on such a list.

Because of all the things you might have said the Trail Blazers needed as they prepared to welcome Lillard back after making a playoff run without him, a slight-of-stature ball-dominant guard with a questionable jump shot would not have topped anyone's list.

Depth on the wing? Yes. Shooting? Definitely. Defense? Always.

Morant doesn't particularly address any of those, and yet, he's here, a dynamic two-time All-Star in need of a second act, being paid a team-high $42.1 million to play the same position as not just Lillard, but Jrue Holiday and Scoot Henderson.

Heck, the Blazers' most valuable point guard last season was forward Deni Avdija.

"I'm sure there will be questions about fit," General Manager Joe Cronin said Monday.

The Blazers have little more than three weeks before the regular season begins in which to figure that out. At the team's annual media day on Monday, Morant appeared in a fresh white No. 1 Trail Blazers jersey. It's happening.

"It's easy for people to be like, 'Man how is this going to work,'" Lillard offered. "'They can't do this, they can't do that.' But being on the floor with them … we can be a dangerous team and I really believe that."

The onus is on the Trail Blazers starting this month to prove that they bet right in acquiring Morant. And that pairing him with Lillard isn't the boondoggle conventional wisdom suggests it will be.

Lillard noted how many teams that succeeded in the playoffs last year, think San Antonio with its four point guards, relied on numerous playmakers. To his credit, Morant said he would be comfortable coming off the bench, if necessary.

But they would not be the first NBA team to fall into the trap of believing they are capable of adjusting or that they will be comfortable sacrificing. It isn't uncommon to see players in those situations default to familiar habits and sensibilities once the season is underway or struggles come.

That's what made a lot of the positive energy around what sure seems like an awkward roster ring so hollow. We're going to need to see it to believe it.

Morant tried to lay out a vision for how it could look. If he drives to the paint and is double-teamed, someone else is inevitably open. That's how basketball works.

"That allows me to have more space to be the best Ja I can be," Morant said.

The 36-year-old Holiday, who would be a more natural complement next to Lillard or Morant than they are to each other, made his own case for why the point guard rotation makes sense.

"It's about being a complete basketball player for myself," he said, "and I think we have a lot of guys like that."

There is a long history of teams talking themselves into superstar additions that make little sense. The Los Angeles Lakers once made a conscious decision to acquire Russell Westbrook and pair him with LeBron James. Nobody made them do that!

The talent and star power of a Lillard-Morant backcourt is undeniable. Still, I remain unconvinced that Cronin, with a very hands-on new owner in his ear, made the deal with winning basketball games at the front of mind.

Although, admittedly, Cronin's biggest wins as a GM have been in the trade market.

The haul of assets he collected for Lillard, only to get him back two years later, will go down as an all-time great swindle. So, too, will the oft-questioned Avdija trade a year later.

Maybe he sees something in a Lillard-Morant pairing that many of us can't.

Morant could return to the All-Star form he had achieved before injuries and off-court issues derailed his ascent. And if he does, the Blazers can either trade him for pieces that fit better or clear out the logjam and build around him.

But to sell the current roster as a basketball strategy doesn't quite pass the smell test.

Neither Lillard nor Morant has achieved stardom due to his defensive acumen. Each player needs the ball in his hands to thrive. If the duo is on the floor together, Avdija is no longer the secondary playmaker, he becomes tertiary.

So, you are left to wonder whether the Blazers sold themselves on Morant because of how he can help the current collection of talent or if they simply saw the opportunity to land a 27-year-old All-NBA talent for cheap, regardless of what it meant for the existing roster construction.

And if you're being honest, you probably don't need to wonder which of the two it is.

"We were very focused and we'll continue to be on … adding to our talent base as much as we can," Cronin said.

That thinking right there may tell you just how far ahead of everyone else the San Antonio Spurs and Oklahoma City Thunder are in the Western Conference. They are positioned to dominate for the next half-decade, if not longer. The only chance any other team has of disrupting that is taking bold risks.

But I thought the goal was to go all in on the twilight years of Lillard's career?

"Dame literally just wants to win," said first-year head coach Micah Nori, whose charge is to sort out arguably the most talented glut of point guard talent in NBA history. "I think that's the only thing left he really has to (do). He's already proven he's a winner, but at the highest level."

Does adding Morant move Lillard closer to a title? Or simply give Portland a potential off-ramp if Lillard is no longer the same player after tearing his Achilles tendon?

Morant was without question a low-risk, high-reward addition. He came at the reasonable price of Jerami Grant and Kris Murray. If it becomes clear that Morant is one point guard too many, or that he's just not the player he once was, some will argue that it didn't cost the Blazers much to try.

However, there is an opportunity cost to messing with Lillard's swan song. If you don't get it right, you risk wasting the gift of his voluntary return last summer. And, not for nothing, Lillard has the option to become a free agent again after this season.

The franchise icon certainly hasn't said anything that would suggest he is unhappy about Morant's presence, although he stopped short on Monday of a full-throated endorsement. He said he knew about the trade before it went through.

When I asked if he thought it was a good idea right away or if he needed time to get on board with the plan, Lillard said, "My position on it was, I'm prepared for whatever."

It's good that he's already thinking about other positions. Because his old one is getting awfully crowded.
