Inflation, the steady rise in prices, shrinks what each dollar you hold in cash can buy, even at the modest pace the Federal Reserve aims for. Keep your emergency fund and money you’ll spend within five years in savings, and invest the rest, since stocks have grown faster than prices over long periods.

The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of 2% a year over the long run, and even that pace doubles prices in about 35 years, so a dollar kept in cash buys half as much. In the 12 months through June 2022, prices rose 9.1%, the biggest jump in 40 years, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported.

Prices climb when spending outruns what businesses can supply, or when costs like energy and wages rise and get passed on to shoppers. Expectations push them up too, since people who expect higher prices ask for bigger raises and buy sooner. Savers holding cash and retirees on a pension that never rises lose ground. Borrowers with fixed-rate loans gain, since they repay in dollars that buy less, and workers whose raises keep pace hold their own.

Prices rose about 92% from January 2000 to December 2025, by the government’s Consumer Price Index. By the end, $10,000 left in a checking account that paid no interest bought only what about $5,200 bought in 2000, as the BLS’s CPI Inflation Calculator shows. Interest shrinks that loss, but only a rate above inflation, a positive real interest rate, adds buying power. So sort your money by when you’ll spend it.

Keep your emergency fund and money you’ll need within five years in a high-yield savings account, where it earns interest and can’t drop with stock prices. Invest the rest in low-cost index funds, which own hundreds or thousands of stocks at once, through your 401(k), an individual retirement account (IRA), or a brokerage account.

With dividends reinvested and before fees and taxes, $10,000 invested in the S&P 500, an index of 500 large U.S. companies, grew to about $73,900 over those years, by our math from NYU professor Aswath Damodaran’s return data. That $73,900 bought what about $38,500 bought in 2000, despite a 37% loss in 2008, which is why only money you can leave alone for years belongs in stocks.

Total your emergency fund and the next five years of planned spending, keep that much in savings, and invest every dollar beyond it.

How long is a new Fitbit actually supposed to last?

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Longevity is a major factor to consider with any fitness wearable. It's meant to be used 24/7, and its subject to conditions that would wreck many other products, from accidental knocks and scrapes to heavy deadlift sessions. As gadgets go, there are few things worse than discovering you're out a few hundred dollars because your tracker wasn't sufficiently sweatproof.

There's more to longevity than durability, though. There's also battery health, not to mention software support, since a tracker is useless if you can't sync and browse its data. At some point, end of support is virtually guaranteed — it's just a question of whether that will be before or after you're ready to move on.

How long can you expect a new product like the Fitbit Edge or Fitbit Air to last? While we obviously can't say for certain, info from Google, third parties and my own personal experience with wearables may be helpful.

Let's return to durability for a moment. Most impacts are only going to do cosmetic damage, as long as you don't destroy the screen or button(s), so the greater concern is water resistance. A product like the Edge has an IP68 rating, meaning it can be submerged and keep running, but that's only for as long as its liquid protection seals hold up.

Over time, the seals on any device are bound to degrade, especially with repeated exposure to moisture. It's just a question of how quickly. While a person who only wears their Fitbit for walking might never run into trouble, someone who swims daily might encounter a sudden breakdown a few years down the road. Sweat killed three generations of my Beats earbuds, despite their supposed IPX4 sweat and water resistance.

As for battery health, all lithium ion batteries have a finite number of 0 to 100 percent charge cycles. In wearables, this is typically about 300, so charging a device roughly once per week could in theory get you over five years of runtime. That's not the whole picture, however. Apart from variable cycle counts, your device's health can be prolonged if you rarely let your battery go dead, but its lifespan can also be harmed by the issue of practical capacity.

Before you hit that 300 mark, various chemical factors will probably reduce capacity to the point you no longer consider a Fitbit usable. Most people don't want to charge a wearable multiple times per day. Also, while the Edge has a replaceable battery, it's the exception to the rule. There's no choice but to dispose of most Fitbits once they cross this threshold.

Google explains that it offers security updates for at least two years after a Fitbit product is no longer on sale. If you buy one of the company's current models, updates are going to last through 2028 and probably beyond, depending on when Google refreshes its hardware. The Inspire 3, Versa 4 and Sense 2 will presumably be first on the chopping block, since they launched in 2022.

Note that security updates aren't the same thing as active feature support. Companies like Google tend to prioritize the newest hardware, both to encourage you to spend money, and because older products can only keep up with feature changes for so long. For example, Google Health Guardian features like irregular rhythm notifications only work with a narrow range of Fitbits, despite some unsupported models having heart rate sensors too. Meanwhile, the original Fitbit app has been replaced by Google Health. There's no telling how long legacy devices will remain compatible as the new app evolves.

You may eventually find features so basic or broken that you end up buying a new device regardless of damage, battery health or security vulnerabilities. Realistically, I wouldn't count on owning a new Fitbit for any more than five years, but I hope I'm proven wrong.

What Are The World’s Tallest & Highest Hotels? The Answer Is Tricky…

There’s something I really love about staying in a hotel room on a really high floor, where you’re so high up that you almost feel like you’re in an airplane. So, what are the tallest and highest hotels in the world? I want to talk about that in this post, and also discuss why the answer isn’t that straightforward.

The question of which hotel is the tallest and highest isn’t as straightforward as you might assume. And even once we do define it, there’s the question of the extent to which the answer is actually useful. First let’s cover the distinction between the tallest hotel and the highest hotel.

Generally speaking, the tallest hotel would be defined as the tallest building where a large percent of the structure is made up of the hotel. Most commonly, I see people suggest that this would include situations where at least 80% of the building is the hotel.

This is different than the highest hotel, which would be the hotel that is highest above the ground, even if it only takes up a small percentage of a mixed-use building, where there might be apartments, residences, etc.

In other words, a 50-story building that consists exclusively of a hotel would fall into the former category, while a 110-story building where the top 10 floors are a hotel would fall into the latter category.

With that out of the way, let’s talk about the properties that are remarkable in these categories…

Let me of course mention one downside of very tall standalone hotels — you could end up being on a very low floor. After all, if a high-rise consists exclusively (or almost exclusively) of hotel facilities, some people will end up on very low floors. The other downside, of course, is that these hotels are necessarily huge. If you prefer boutique hotels, you won’t find any on this list.

Currently, the tallest hotel in the world is Ciel Tower Dubai. The 1,042-room hotel opened in 2024, rises to a height of 1,227 feet (374 meters), and has 82 floors.

Mauricio Pochettino's Manchester United reign got off to a disastrous start as the Red Devils suffered a humiliating 4-0 defeat against arch-rivals Liverpool at Anfield in the Premier League on Sunday.

The Argentine took charge of the club earlier this week after Man Utd parted ways with Michael Carrick, leaving him with barely a few days to work with his new players on the training ground.

Pochettino had won just three of his previous 19 games before Sunday's encounter, and the final whistle brought no improvement to that record.

The defeat also had a damaging impact on the league standings, with Man Utd dropping to 19th in the Premier League table on eight points after 11 matches, with only Crystal Palace faring worse.

According to Opta Joe, Pochettino became the first manager in Premier League history to see his team concede twice and receive a red card in the first half of his opening match in charge of a club.

The game was only eight minutes old when the Man Utd backline was caught napping, forcing Leny Yoro into a challenge on Alexander Isak that resulted in a red card.

Liverpool capitalised on United's defensive frailties, scoring twice before the break through Dominik Szoboszlai and Florian Wirtz.

The hosts enjoyed greater control after the interval and added two more goals through Rio Ngumoha and Victor Munoz, with the former also setting a Premier League record of his own.

Pochettino also entered the Man Utd history books for the wrong reasons, suffering the joint-heaviest defeat by any Red Devils manager in his first game in charge, matching Lal Hilditch's 4-0 loss against Bolton Wanderers in October 1926.

Considering Man Utd's struggles so far this season, the defeat against Liverpool need not spell the end of the world for the Red Devils or their new manager.

Pochettino's immediate priority will be to collect as many points as possible and create some breathing space between his side and the teams at the bottom of the table.

There were precious few positives to take from the performance, aside from Senne Lammens's display, but Pochettino will hope to see greater improvement from his players in the coming days.

Investor Bill Miller Says Bitcoin Is Easier to Value than AI

📅 2026-11-22  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Famous Bitcoin (BTC) advocate and investor Bill Miller IV said that it is much easier to value the world's largest cryptocurrency than AI investments, even though Bitcoin does not produce any cash flow.

He said that this has to do with the expectations investors have when it comes to these two assets, specifically regarding their capacity to deliver future profits.

In a recent X post, Miller, who is the chairman and chief investment officer of Miller Value Partners, broke down why he thinks that it's easier to make an investment case for Bitcoin than for AI companies.

"Bitcoin and AI owners have different expectations. The owners of AI expect companies will one day produce free cash flow. Bitcoin owners never did or will expect this," Miller said.

And the end result is that "the valuation case is still much easier for Bitcoin than AI."

Simply put, when investors buy stocks in AI companies, there's an expectation that at some point these companies will deliver big profits and free cash flow.

Free cash flow is the money a company produces from its operations after capital expenditures. This is a significant metric for understanding the value of a company.

But when it comes to AI companies, it can be difficult to assign a value because investors need to try to determine what kind of revenue these companies will generate, how much they will invest in infrastructure and when these investments will start to deliver sufficient returns.

Bill Miller has made it a point to push back against the argument that Bitcoin does not have value because it doesn't produce cash flow.

In a commentary from June 30, Miller explained that the traditional way of looking at intrinsic value, which revolves around future cash flows, doesn't really capture the potential of Bitcoin as a decentralized monetary network.

Instead, Miller said that Bitcoin's value could be determined by comparing its potential role within the global financial system to that of gold.

In his view, Bitcoin could theoretically reach about $1.4 million per coin if it achieved a valuation similar to gold's. At least, that's what he calculated at the time.

Cannabis use disorder is linked to higher odds of suicidal thoughts

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Adults with cannabis use disorder reported suicidal thoughts far more often than other adults, and the gap held steady for a decade.

Adults with cannabis use disorder had 57% higher odds of reporting serious suicidal thoughts in the past year than adults without the disorder, even after researchers accounted for depression and other factors.

Across U.S. surveys from 2014 through 2023, researchers found no clear evidence that the link grew stronger.

Cannabis use disorder is not the same as using cannabis. It means having trouble controlling the habit even when it is clearly causing harm.

The survey measured it with nine yes-or-no questions about the past year.

Tosin Philip Oyetunji, a doctoral student in public health at the University of California, Merced, led the study with three colleagues there.

The team pooled ten years of the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, which the federal government runs every year. After minors and incomplete answers were set aside, 415,861 adults were left.

Among them, 4.0% met the criteria for the disorder. And 4.6% said that in the past 12 months they had seriously thought about killing themselves.

“What we would most like people to understand is that struggling with cannabis use can go hand in hand with mental health challenges that are not always visible,” Oyetunji said in an interview with Earth.com.

Adults with the disorder had 1.57 times the odds of reporting suicidal thoughts. The range around that figure runs from 1.44 to 1.72.

The comparison is adjusted, which matters more than usual here. The model accounted for depression, serious psychological distress and heavier drinking that met the criteria for alcohol use disorder.

The team also controlled for income, education, insurance, self-rated health, religious attendance and whether a person’s state had legalized recreational cannabis.

So the gap isn’t just depression showing up in a different column. Nobody was followed over time, though, so the survey can’t say which came first.

The team expected the link to strengthen. Cannabis potency has risen sharply since legalization began, and Canadian data had suggested a tightening link.

An earlier national study had also tied cannabis use to suicidal thoughts, plans and attempts.

The researchers found no clear evidence that the link strengthened. In the 2016 and 2022 surveys, the odds ran higher than in the first survey year.

Neither of those two survived the correction the team applied for testing ten years at once. Across the decade, the differences from one survey to the next were too small to rule out chance.

“A public health concern does not have to be getting worse to deserve attention,” Oyetunji said when Earth.com asked why the link had held while the drug changed.

Sidra Goldman-Mellor, a professor of public health at UC Merced and the study’s senior author, put the other half of it plainly.

“People with cannabis use disorder are still at a very substantially increased likelihood of experiencing serious suicidal thoughts, and that’s not something that is widely understood,” she said.

The disorder was not the only measure that showed the pattern. The survey’s criteria changed partway through the decade, so the team ran the analysis again using how often people used cannabis.

Nondaily users had 1.39 times the odds of reporting suicidal thoughts, compared with people who had not used cannabis at all. Daily or near-daily users had 1.65 times the odds.

“This suggests that patterns of cannabis use may also be important to consider when examining mental health, and it raises questions about how the frequency of use relates to suicidal thoughts,” Oyetunji told Earth.com.

The team also split the sample by sex, by age and by race and ethnicity.

Adults with the disorder had higher odds in every one of those groups, and the differences between the groups were too small to call real.

Everyone answered once, so nobody can say which came first. Every answer was also the person’s own account of a year they were remembering.

The study focused on serious suicidal thoughts. The link with cannabis use disorder may differ for suicide plans, attempts or deaths.

The survey also records nothing about what people were using. Potency, product type and whether someone vaped concentrates are all missing, so the study cannot test the potency question it raises.

Studies of high-THC products disagree anyway. A recent review found strong links between high-THC cannabis and the disorder itself or psychosis, and weaker ones for depression and anxiety.

Craving and withdrawal were left out of the disorder measure so the questions would match across years, which probably undercounts it.

The survey excluded people living in institutions and people experiencing homelessness who were outside shelters.

The authors raise one more possibility. As cannabis use spread, the group meeting the criteria may have shifted toward milder cases.

A shift like that would hold the average down even if nothing had changed for the most severe cases.

Two people can give this survey the same answers. In one of them, cannabis use deepened a crisis. In the other, a crisis drove heavier use.

A single round of questions cannot tell them apart. Researchers would have to follow the same people for years, with the potency and the products written down.

Oyetunji is widening the question rather than narrowing it.

“I want to better understand how these risks overlap, what drives them, and where we may be missing opportunities to help people before they experience serious harm,” he said.

Clinics don’t have to wait for that. Someone asking for help with cannabis may be carrying something they have not mentioned, and the authors argue that the right response is to ask.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs.
