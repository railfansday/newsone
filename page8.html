Huddersfield Town told to make January transfer move for ‘aggressive’ Barnsley star

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Optimism is beginning to build that Huddersfield Town can gain promotion and finally make their return to the Championship.

The Terriers currently sit at the summit of the third tier standings after taking 29 points from their opening 14 league games, while having a game in hand on some of their promotion rivals after not playing last weekend.

It had been a disappointing last couple of weeks in Yorkshire after Huddersfield were held at home in the league by Luton Town to end a run of four successive victories, while they also suffered defeat in the FA Cup at the hands of Exeter City, going down 2-1.

Martin Drury’s men were able to respond in their final group game of the EFL Trophy last week however, putting Liverpool's U21’s to the sword in a 4-0 victory that helped them top Northern Group H.

League action finally returns for Huddersfield this coming weekend, with the Terriers hosting Leicester City in a massive game for both teams towards the top end of the table.

Meanwhile, to solidify their place in the promotion picture after a hectic run of winter fixtures, the January transfer window could be the perfect time to make a statement signing and highlight their intent of returning to the second tier this season.

With that in mind, FLW’s Huddersfield Town fan pundit has backed the Terriers to complete a raid of a fellow Yorkshire club for an ‘aggressive’ star.

Much of Huddersfield Town’s excellent form leading into the festive period has been down to their recruitment in the forward areas, with the Terriers looking so much more potent compared to their previous couple of seasons in League One.

The addition of Derensilli Sanches Fernandes looks inspired at the moment, with the creative Cape Verde midfielder contributing seven goals and six assists from 14 matches, while the signing of frontman Ashley Fletcher has added much-needed experience and allowed Martin Drury to chop and change between him or Joe Taylor leading the line, who himself has 10 goals in 13 league games.

Martin Drury wasn't on the touchline for Huddersfield's recent EFL Trophy win, and the reasoning has sparked a reaction amongst the fanbase

Their quality moving forward and doggedness in their defensive duties has seen them lose just once all season so far in the league, with eight wins and five draws collected from their other 13 outings.

There’s such a feel-good factor at the Accu Stadium at the moment, with Huddersfield fans in awe of the job Drury is doing that could finally see them be back in the Championship setup next season.

Speaking to Football League World, Huddersfield Town fan pundit Graeme Rayner has urged the Terriers to raid fellow Yorkshire outfit Barnsley for winger Reyes Cleary in January, believing the addition of the 22-year-old can help the team be even more dangerous in attack, and that the player has ‘all the attributes’ to help the club achieve a top two spot come the end of the campaign.

When asked who Huddersfield should try and sign in January, Graeme told FLW: “I think it’s a player I’ve mentioned before in the summer, I would love us to take a really good hard look at Reyes Cleary, who plays for Barnsley.

“He’s a left-winger, 22-years-old, was the top assister in League One last year. He was playing in a pretty poor Barnsley side and still managed 15 assists and seven goals in 50 appearances.

“This season, he’s playing in a Barnsley side that are right at the foot of the table, they’re joint-bottom of the league, and he’s scored a couple and assisted one in a team that’s only scored 12 goals, he’s contributed in a way to a quarter of those goals.

“He’s quick, he’s capable, he’s clearly got a good finish on him, he’s aggressive, I think he’s six-foot, he’s a big unit, and got every kind of attribute that we’d want from someone, and what we lack at the moment is a really good option on the left that would give us what Derensili Sanches Fernandes gives us on the right.

Huddersfield Town fans, would you welcome a move for Reyes Cleary in January? Could he be the signing that helps get you over the line for promotion? Drop your thoughts below!

“In our front three we’re quite often playing Fernandes and then a big man, little man combo with an Ashley Fletcher, or Bojan Radulovic, alongside (Joe) Taylor. What this would give us is the option to play Fernandes and someone like Reyes, with Taylor through the middle, and I think we’d be even more dangerous, and he (Cleary) would be the exciting player that could help us get over the line for automatic promotion.

“I think he’s got capabilities and potential to play at a higher level. If he’s doing that well in a pretty abject, woeful side, then he could do really well in a team that are playing on the front foot as much as we are these days.

“He goes hunting for the ball, he closes down defenders, he wins the ball, he’s a good finisher, he’s got all the attributes that we need, and he wouldn’t be hugely expensive, I wouldn’t have thought.

“He’s been at Barnsley for a season and a half, his history is not many games at West Brom where he came through the youth setup I believe, half a season with Hartlepool and then onto Barnsley, so he’s not going to be a massively expensive signing, maybe a million quid would do it? I don’t know.

“So, I say let’s have a look at him. He could be really exciting.”

As another transfer window rolls around, the future of Reyes Cleary hangs in the balance, and Huddersfield Town might be the club to finally take advantage.

Cleary had been tipped to depart Oakwell in the summer, but elected to remain with the Reds, but after a diabolical start to the season that leaves them joint-bottom of the division, Cleary may well want to move on and link up with a promotion-contending outfit to then have a route into playing Championship football next season.

And Huddersfield would seem the perfect place, with the Terriers currently so dangerous in the forward areas and having a line that rips apart opposition defences.

Not to mention that a left-winger is the one area that Huddersfield are missing at the moment despite having such an impressive attack, and you feel if that is addressed, then there is no way of stopping them blowing away most of their opponents and dominating the division for the rest of the campaign.

While Cleary’s numbers have been nowhere near the levels he managed to notch last season with Barnsley, you have to feel that is because he is playing in such a struggling side, and those returns would surely get back to their best when playing in a forward line that is so front-footed and dominant.

Huddersfield’s current setup would be a perfect one for Cleary to come into, with the 22-year-old able to use his pace, power, directness and clinical eye for goal to great effect, and it could be the missing piece that makes Huddersfield a shoo-in for promotion back to the Championship.

If the Terriers were able to pull this deal off in January, then their chances of automatic promotion are bound to considerably increase, and it’s a piece of transfer business that the club must get over the line if they are serious about achieving promotion.

DJI spinoff Amflow has spent the last couple of years shaking up the electric mountain bike (eMTB) market with ridiculously high-torque, lightweight models. Today, it’s bringing that same overengineered energy to pavement with the UE Series, its first urban commuter line.

The series consists of two models: the UE Carbon and UE Carbon Pro. Both pack 125Nm of torque, 1,100W of peak power, and an 8x max assist ratio capable of climbing 30 percent grades — all while operating quietly under 45dBA (quieter than a household refrigerator). A swappable 600Wh battery provides up to 132km (about 82 miles) of range and fast-charges from zero to 100 percent in 2 hours and 23 minutes.

While most city e-bikes deliver their power modestly, Amflow is strapping a mountain-bike-grade motor onto a 20.9 kg (46 pounds) upright step-through frame — and pricing it slightly below its flagship trail bikes. The UE Carbon starts at €3,099 (about $3,500 USD), while the Pro will sell for €3,499 ($3,900 USD).

But this isn’t just another commuter bike. With its proprietary Avinox drivetrain, DJI has been ahead of the curve in blending consumer gadget UX — features like wireless phone charging, Apple Watch proximity unlock, Find My integration, and camera controls — with serious hardware specs. That’s one of the reasons why The Verge’s own Thomas Ricker has been so bullish on the brand for the past few years.

With 125Nm of max torque and 1,100W of peak power, the Avinox M2 in the UE Series is slightly more dialed back from Amflow’s 150Nm flagship eMTBs. That said, 125Nm is still massive overkill for flat city streets — but it won’t hurt to have it when encountering any grades over 30 percent. A new Auto Boost mode automatically dumps extra power when starting at a traffic light or hitting a steep hill. And traction control will help prevent any spin-outs on wet asphalt.

The 600Wh removable battery is good for up 82 miles in Eco mode — and likely less than that while relying on higher levels of assist. It’s worth noting that the 1,100W peak power and 8x assist ratios will likely be software-capped depending on where you live and what regulations allow.

As a brand within the larger DJI ecosystem, Amflow loves to integrate a lot of tech into its bikes. A 1.4 inch touchscreen is integrated into the handlebars alongside a 15W wireless phone charger — plus a 20W USB-C charger, if you prefer. The cockpit also features plenty of Apple tie-ins, like the aforementioned Find My tracker and Apple Watch proximity unlocking. The UE can also pair with a heart rate monitor, allowing riders to set a target heart rate zone that will dictate how much pedal assist the bike provides. And, of course, there’s direct recording controls for DJI Osmo cameras, in case you like to film your rides.

Additional security is provided by a built-in 10mm hardened steel wheel lock keyed to match the removable battery. And if the bike is paired with “Lost Mode” in the Amflow app, the user can remotely brick the drive system if the bike ever gets stolen.

There’s plenty of commuter-friendly touches, like a 65mm suspension fork, suspension dropper seatpost, and heavy-duty racks rated for 20kg (44 pounds) on the front and 27kg (60 pounds) on the rear.

The bikes are on sale globally starting today via Amflow’s website and retail partners. The company says a smaller XS frame size is coming in 2027 for shorter riders.

OpenAI is unveiling its most advanced AI model after scrapping a prior version

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

OpenAI said GPT-6.2 Astra scored 5.5 points above Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 on a professional-work test at 5% of the cost

OpenAI made GPT-6.2 Astra available on Tuesday to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users of ChatGPT Work and Codex. The company released the model alongside GPT-6.2 Sol, a cheaper version for everyday work. Developers can reach both models through the OpenAI API.

OpenAI scrapped GPT-6.1 Astra in late September, saying the model fell short on "staying within scope and authorization, and how it communicates back to the user about the type of work it's done," according to Bloomberg.

OpenAI said GPT-6.2 Astra set a new high on Agents’ Last Exam, a test of multistep professional work. It scored 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, and 5.5 points above Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.2 at 5% of the cost per task, the company said. It also said the model produces slides, spreadsheets and documents that follow user templates and professional conventions more closely.

API prices for GPT-6.2 Astra are $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 per million cached input tokens and $40 per million output tokens. The company said that is 20% below GPT-6 Astra's input and output rates. GPT-6.2 Sol costs $2 for input, $0.10 for cached input and $10 for output per million tokens, unchanged from GPT-6.1 Sol.

OpenAI said both models meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity and the High threshold for biological and chemical capabilities under its Preparedness Framework. GPT-6.2 Astra is its first externally released model to meet the High threshold for AI self-improvement, which measures a model's ability to speed up AI research.

The company said it is launching the models with tighter safeguards for higher-risk cybersecurity requests. Those protections restrict some legitimate vulnerability research and security testing, it said. OpenAI also built a system called Control Room that tracks research workloads and can halt model output when intervention is needed.

OpenAI said both models were more likely than GPT-6 Astra to omit mentioning obstacles, such as a missing file, in two alignment tests. It added instructions in Codex that substantially reduce the omissions and said it is investigating the behavior.

OpenAI briefly halted training on its strongest models and resumed only recently, once it had disconnected them from the internet, according to Bloomberg.

OpenAI began rolling out GPT-6 Astra on Sept. 3, the first model it designated Critical under the Preparedness Framework. The company said at the time that it had delayed parts of the model's development to strengthen protections against cyber misuse.

Amazon CEO Andy Jassy feared being ‘a lousy dad’—so he did this

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When Amazon CEO Andy Jassy became a parent for the first time 26 years ago, he didn’t want to choose between being a good worker and being a good father.

That left him with only one option.

“I realized that I had to change the way I worked,” Jassy recently said during an appearance at Amazon’s Bring Your Parents to Work Day. “[Otherwise,] I was either going to have to leave Amazon, or I was going to be a lousy dad.”

Jassy was used to working grueling hours—often staying at the office late into the night—and he wasn’t sure if he could do his job effectively on a tighter schedule.

“I didn’t know how I would do my job without [working all the time],” he said. “But like everything else, if you think about it and you rework it and you find ways to be more efficient and you make trade-offs, you can do it.”

One step was to ensure that, whenever he was at the office, he was using his time efficiently.

“I did spend a lot of time in the hallway talking to people, and I wasn’t always prioritizing the things that mattered most,” Jassy said. “It forced me to get more efficient in what I did.”

He also established rules to help him prioritize family: being home for dinner every day, having weekly breakfasts with each of his children, and maintaining a standing Tuesday date night with his wife.

The challenge Jassy faced will likely be familiar to any parent struggling to balance career ambitions with responsibilities at home. And it is increasingly common among men, a growing number of whom say they are interested in being parents or caregivers. 

In one recent survey, over two-thirds of men said they consider caregiving to be as important as paid work and that both parents should contribute equally—yet only 40% of men who are caregivers said they shared the responsibility equally with their partners.

In the same survey, nearly two-thirds of male caregivers said work obligations interfered with their family time. A third reported having “no control” over their schedules, and only 40% said they had a hybrid or remote work arrangement. (Amazon issued a five-day RTO policy in 2024.)

Meanwhile, the study found that male caregivers are increasingly missing work or cutting back on their work hours. The percentage of men who care for adults or children with extra needs who reported missing work rose from 64% in 2019 to 76% in 2026.  

And, as Fast Company reported recently, men in some states are filing at least as many claims for parental leave as mothers.

For fathers who want to keep their career ambitions and still be equal parents, Jassy said there may be no one-size-fits-all solution.

“There are times you have to be flexible with what’s happening in your life and at work,” he said. “But it’s doable if you want to do it.”

Ayumu Imazu Talks ‘musubi’ & The Belief that Music ‘Brings People Together’: Billboard Japan Monthly Interview

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song & what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

Singer/songwriter Ayumu Imazu spoke to Billboard Japan for its Monthly Feature interview series spotlighting artists and releases on its radar.

Imazu’s latest single “musubi,” released digitally on Oct. 2, has become the biggest hit of his career. “musubi” is a love song portraying the bonds between people through the motif of the “red string of fate.” A demo posted to TikTok in late August, when only a single verse and chorus existed, spread widely and led to an “emergency release.” The track swept the charts across streaming services immediately after it dropped and earned the ​26-year-old artist his first-ever No. 1 on Billboard Japan’s Streaming Songs chart (dated Oct. 14, 2026), marking the biggest breakthrough of his career to date.

2026 has also been a milestone year for him — it marks the fifth anniversary of his major-label debut, he announced a partnership with SKY-HI-led BMSG in February, and in May he released CLASSIC, his first album in four years. After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

In the Codename MDASH pilot, GitHub and Microsoft turned thousands of raw AI findings into 99 checked, private reports with suggested fixes for six widely used open source projects. Maintainers have already fixed more than half.

AI has made finding potential vulnerabilities fast and cheap. Fixing them still takes human judgment, and in open source, that work falls to maintainers. Many are already flooded with AI-generated security findings, including duplicates and reports that aren’t vulnerabilities at all. Another raw scan doesn’t help them. Anyone pointing a new model at a project should ask its maintainers first and come with fixes, not just findings.

Maintainers are under intense pressure from this flood of reports. As Christian Grobmeier, a maintainer of Log4j, has put it, the AI defending open source has to be better than the AI attacking it.

AI shouldn’t add to maintainers’ workload. So, with maintainers, GitHub and Microsoft ran a pilot together: could AI help maintainers ship fixes instead of handing them more findings?

GitHub and Microsoft are using Codename MDASH, Microsoft’s multi-model agentic scanning harness, to find and fix security vulnerabilities in widely used open source software, strengthening the foundation that much of the world’s code is built on.

Our pilot had two goals: send only findings that matter, and never send a maintainer an unverified finding. Here’s how it worked:

Reports and fixes stayed on GitHub, where maintainers already work.

The result: Codename MDASH, using Microsoft’s MAI Cyber model, flagged thousands of possible issues across the six projects. After AI triage and hand review by seven Microsoft FORGE Lab researchers, maintainers received 99 private reports. As of November 15, they had fixed 55, and 14 of those fixes were already in a release.

Six projects took part, all alumni of the GitHub Secure Open Source Fund. Their maintainers agreed to the scan, received every report privately, decided what to fix, and shared what worked:

These lessons come from what the six projects fixed and what their maintainers chose to share. Six projects is a small sample, so read them as early lessons.

Fixing is where maintainers spend their time. So every report arrived already checked, with a suggested fix to review, adapt, and merge. Maintainers started from a well-scoped patch, not a raw finding.

The first fixes shipped about a week after the initial scan. Caddy’s maintainers fixed all 22 of the reports they received in a week or less. Maintainers used the suggested fixes, some as written and some with changes.

AutoGPT’s maintainers had recently shipped a new check, and it was broken. The report landed three days later and pointed at the right line. They took the fix further than the suggested patch, wrote it within a day, and had it in production less than a week later.

Finding vulnerabilities is no longer our bottleneck. Fixing them is the hard part, and that depends on maintainers.

Maintainers got a short list, not a flood. Express maintainers said the reports cut through the noise. Caddy maintainers said they surfaced obscure bugs and filled gaps that earlier reports had missed. For FastMCP, the scan went beyond what AI scanners had given them before. ImageMagick, maintained by a two-person team, received 22 reports.

AI did the finding, researchers did the checking, and maintainers stayed in charge.

One ImageMagick report looked like a kind of issue the maintainers had seen before. Its summary showed it wasn’t and pointed to the exact lines of code. They were able to push a fix in 15 minutes.

Normally, a security advisory would take us around 30 minutes to 1 hour on average to read, understand, and make a fix. It is a huge difference.

Codename MDASH was able to find unknown, obscure security bugs in our project with succinct reports, and it filled in gaps from some prior reports that hadn’t been noticed.

The pilot worked because people did careful work at every step, and that work doesn’t scale yet. Seven researchers spent about two days per project checking findings by hand. Meanwhile, low-quality reports still reach maintainers every day.

For open source, fixes need to be delivered via private vulnerability reporting (PVR). Maintainers need to run CI/CD tests on private forks to verify those fixes. And to close the gap between defenders and attackers, maintainers need access to cyber-capable models to verify a proof of concept, before and after the fix.

The Codename MDASH scan provided unique insights into the security of our project above and beyond what AI scanners have provided us in the past

If you’re a maintainer, this approach changes what a security report costs you. A checked, private report that comes with a suggested fix takes less of your time. You shouldn’t have to triage a scanner’s output. That’s the sender’s job.
