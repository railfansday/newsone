GoFundMe CEO sees ‘new era of desperation’ after 40% spike in essentials fundraisers

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As more Americans struggle to put food on the table, crowdsourcing money for groceries has become consumers’ balm to an ongoing affordability crisis.

Food-related GoFundMe fundraisers have increased 35% year-over-year in the platform’s English-speaking markets of the U.S., Canada, UK, Australia, and Ireland, including a 40% jump in the U.S., according to the company. The amount of money given to these campaigns increased 50% since the year before.

“The costs of regular things—food, rent, gas prices, obviously, particularly this year, and not just here but elsewhere in the world—have gone up, and folks are struggling in many cases to be able to afford all of that,” CEO Tim Cadogan told Fortune. “Things are more expensive, and a lot of people’s incomes have not moved up at a commensurate rate.”

This year’s growing fundraising demand for essentials is part of a larger trend that began during the pandemic, when the intersection of widespread job losses,health emergencies, and a transition to virtual community led more people to seek help online.

GoFundMe introduced the food, rent, and monthly bills tab in October 2020 in response to an increased need for necessities during the pandemic. Martin Lukk, a postdoctoral research fellow at the Data Sciences Institute at the University of Toronto, said while the company created the new category as a result of increased demand, it also was a catalyst for even more people seeking help through the platform.

“In response to an overwhelming number of campaigns for exactly these things, they invented this new category, and that seemed to usher in this new era of desperation that probably really didn’t exist during the pandemic,” Lukk told Fortune.

Fundraisers for essentials also increased last year, when the platform saw a 20% rise in the number of fundraisers to cover essentials from rent to utilities to groceries, which quadrupled the year before. The “monthly bills” category on the platform was among the fastest-growing, second only to individual support for nonprofits.

“We have been seeing that trend now really for about four years, really since the late stage of the pandemic had a lot of impacts on supply chains that’s flowed through,” Cadogan said.

There are also variables outside the economy that may have contributed to the shift toward more necessity-related campaigns, including more people simply knowing about GoFundMe.

Crowdsourced fundraising was documented as early as the 1700s, when the Irish Loan Fund gave small loans to low-income rural families who would pool resources together. The U.S. saw perhaps its most famous crowdfunding campaign in 1885, when publisher and lawmaker Joseph Pulitzer launched an effort to raise money for the pedestal on which the Statue of Liberty would rest. More than 160,000 donors would raise $100,000 in about five months. But the rise of GoFundMe, which was founded in 2010, coincided with the financial crisis tightening lending requirements, forcing artists and entrepreneurs to turn to the internet to raise money. Ventures soon expanded from creative endeavors to philanthropic initiatives and charity drives.

This year’s increase in fundraising correlates with ongoing affordability woes stoked specifically by the Iran war and tariff uncertainty. Americans now spend nearly half their income on food and housing alone, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)’s annual Consumer Expenditures Report published last month. Compared to the year before, food spending increased 4.6%, and housing expenditures climbed 5.8%, with the share of spending on these necessities reaching its highest in 15 years.

While much of that increase is related to rising costs, it’s also a result of stagnating wages. Minutes from the latest Federal Open Market Committee meeting found wage growth for most industries was lagging behind the inflation rate of 3.7% over the last 12 months. According to BLS data, the average wage growth in the U.S. over the last 12 months ending in September 2026 was around 3.1%. Inflation-adjusted wages and salaries fell 0.6% over the same period.

Economic pressures tend to mount around the holidays. Walmart CEO John Furner told investors on Thursday that customers are “stable, resilient,” ahead of the holiday season, but according to the company, about half of Americans say their finances are the greatest stressor as they are expected to serve large holiday meals and buy presents for loved ones.

Just as widespread factors like a pandemic and economic tensions can spur crowdsourcing, other variables like political shifts can also impact fundraising demand. Lukk posited that with a wave of Democratic lawmakers moving to Washington, there’s greater opportunities for policy that could eventually alleviate the economic pressures that cause people to turn to GoFundMe campaigns. Republicans largely conceded that they lost so many midterm elections because they failed to address Americans’ affordability concerns.

“I wouldn’t be surprised if, with greater leverage in the Senate and the House, that any kind of social policy initiatives the Democrats might be able to pass in the coming years, that those might be able to absorb some of this, and potentially reduce the the amount of campaigns that we see on GoFundMe,” Lukk told Fortune.

In his book GoFailMe: The Unfulfilled Promise of Digital Crowdfunding written alongside Erik Schneiderhan, Lukk explains that in the Canadian province of Quebec, where there are more social programs, crowdfunding is less prevalent than in provinces with fewer social safety net programs.

GoFundMe has seen a similar pattern in its own data. Between November 2025 and 2026, food-related fundraisers in the U.S. have actually plummeted by about 20%, a trend the company attributes to the Trump administration freezing SNAP benefits last November, which caused a spike in food-related campaigns on the platform.

According to Lukk, GoFundMe is unlikely to see a reduction in campaigns as the platform continues to grow, but rather, there’s an opportunity for less need for people to start fundraisers if there are changes to social policy.

“GoFundMe really seems to respond to those gaps in the safety net. So, if there were a kind of systemic framework to think about what leads people to GoFundMe and what increases the kind of pressure on those fundraisers, it’s about where the gaps in safety net might be coming up, where the net might be fraying, and where potentially future policy interventions might be able to repair some of that,” Lukk said.

“I wouldn’t give up on social policies,” he added, “and at least to continue imagining a world where some of these could reduce the kind of desperation that we see flowing out onto GoFundMe.”

Sasha Rogelberg is a reporter and former editorial fellow on the news desk at Fortune, covering retail and the intersection of business and popular culture.

Your AI agent has a wallet. Who answers for its decisions?

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last week, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet, its second attempt at agentic payments after shelving in-chat checkout earlier this year. Its agents can now pay merchants on a user’s behalf, but every purchase still requires a human in the loop to approve it.

That safeguard will not last, and it shouldn’t. An agent that needs your approval for every purchase is a faster checkout button, not an agent. Once approval moves from the transaction to the instruction, payments lose the assumption they were built on: that a human decides, at the moment of payment, that this is what they want.

The industry is making rapid progress on how agents pay with existing payment methods. It has not yet solved what agents are allowed to decide, or who pays when they decide wrong. The companies that solve that problem will define the agentic economy.

Imagine asking your agent to plan a family vacation within a $5,000 budget. It books nonrefundable flights on the wrong weekend, a hotel 40 minutes from the beach, and accepts a counteroffer from the airline’s own agent that quietly strips out checked bags.

Every payment was authorized. Every merchant delivered exactly what was ordered. The trip is still nothing like what you asked for.

OpenAI’s terms leave delivery and returns with merchants and preserve existing card dispute protections. However, agents introduce a different kind of failure: a purchase that was properly authorized and fulfilled, but did not reflect the user’s instructions. Existing dispute mechanisms may offer remedies, but they do not clearly establish who should bear the loss when the failure lies in the agent’s judgment.

Authorization to spend is not authorization to judge. Today’s infrastructure only knows how to verify the first.

The industry is beginning to build verifiable mandates. Google’s Agent Payments Protocol, for example, uses cryptographically signed mandates to record what a user asked for and approved. That is necessary, but not sufficient. A signature can prove what was authorized. It cannot prove that the agent interpreted the user’s preferences correctly, acted in their interest, or was free of outside incentives. Closing that gap requires a broader idea of what the industry has started calling Know Your Agent. Today it means verifying whose agent is acting and what it was authorized to do. It must also mean verifying whether the agent exercised that authority as intended. That requires three layers.

First, mandates that capture intent. In practice, agent permissions still center on price limits, timing and product parameters. A meaningful mandate also encodes intent: the user’s priorities, the trade-offs they accept and reject, and the conditions that require escalation, such as “nothing nonrefundable without asking me.” Relevant constraints must be verifiable across the transaction, by the merchant’s agent, the payment provider and any later arbiter, without unnecessarily exposing the user’s private instructions. A spending limit tells the system how much. A mandate tells it what for.

Second, a liability framework. When an agent acts inside its spending authority but outside its mandate, the loss could land in many places: the user who gave an ambiguous instruction, the provider whose model misread it, the merchant whose agent extracted worse terms, the platform that ranked the options, or the payment provider that executed the transaction. No rule decides among them today, so every failure is likely to become a dispute. To earn consumers’ trust, the allocation should be settled in advance, for example by asking which party can prevent the harm at the lowest cost. Other industries have done this. The card industry’s EMV liability shift made the party that had not adopted chip technology bear certain counterfeit-fraud losses, creating a powerful incentive to upgrade. Product liability law allocates defects along the supply chain. Clear allocation also creates a market: risk that can be assigned can be priced and insured.

Third, a duty of loyalty. OpenAI is not taking a cut of Wallet transactions at launch. But business models evolve. Once an agent’s platform earns money from merchants, through fees, paid placement or advertising, the agent faces a conflict of interest its user cannot see. Financial regulation addressed a similar conflict for brokers and advisers through disclosure and best-interest obligations. Consumer agents need enforceable standards for whose interests they serve, how paid influence is disclosed, and how compliance with the user’s mandate can be independently verified.

AI labs, payment companies and commerce platforms are writing the agentic protocols right now. Private standards are well suited to identity, credentials and payment flows, because every participant benefits from interoperability. They are structurally weak on liability, because every party at the table would prefer the loss to land somewhere else.

I have worked on both sides of the rulebook, as chair of the FATF’s Risk Group and as a fintech executive. In both roles, the rules that worked were the ones that settled, in advance, who absorbs failure. Systems that answer that question scale. Systems that leave it open stall, or have the answer imposed by regulators after a visible failure, usually in terms written for the last crisis.

The companies that build mandates, clear liability rules and loyalty into their agents now will not be slowed down by it. They will be the ones consumers trust with the wallet. Card payments became global infrastructure because people believed that when something went wrong, someone would make it right. Agents need the same promise, across consumers, AI platforms, merchants and financial institutions that must trust decisions none of them fully controls.

Whoever makes that promise credible will do more than enable agentic payments. They will help write the rules of the new economy.

Can Washington steal the NFC East crown? Hogs Haven readers weigh in after Thursday’s in over the Giants - poll results

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Here's what Ben Standig had to say earlier this week:

The 2026 NFL regular season is past the halfway point, but the finish line remains too far away to make sweeping declarations about how the NFC East race will unfold.

Suggesting that the eventual division winner will have at least eight victories doesn't seem like a stretch, in theory.

There's a solid argument that the Washington Commanders are the best all-around team among the remaining contenders. (Step aside, Eagles.)

When Washington sat at 1-3 following a dismal overseas loss to Indianapolis in Week 4, talk of an NFC East title felt like pure fantasy. Yet six games later, the burgundy & gold have quietly stitched together a 3-2-1 stretch highlighted by Thursday night's 24-21 win over the New York Giants at MetLife Stadium that has breathed a sense of renewed optimism into at least part of the fan base.

Now at 3-5-1, Washington still occupies third place in the division, trailing New York by 2 games. Even so, the underlying progress is real. Quarterback Jayden Daniels engineered a clutch late drive last Thursday night — connecting with Stefon Diggs on a 32-yard touchdown with 1:05 remaining — while Washington converted 7 of 14 third downs and held New York to 4-of-12 on third down.

Moreover, over the past 6 games, the Commanders have largely protected the football — carrying a +7 turnover differential on the season while leaning on young contributors like tight end Ben Sinnott, wideout Antonio Williams, and cornerbacks Trey Amos and (the not-so-young) Amik Robertson.

In this week's Hogs Haven Reacts survey, we asked readers two questions to gauge the community's temperature entering Week 11. The first was our weekly benchmark: Are you confident in the overall direction of the team?

This week, 46% of respondents answered "Yes," while 54% voted "No".

That 32-point surge from last week's 14% approval mark mirrors the 21-point jump we saw in Week 9 after Washington beat the Eagles. Nothing cures midweek angst quite like beating a division rival in prime time!

Still, the fact that 54% of voters remain in the "No" camp shows that skepticism runs deep. As several commenters and fans have mentioned, Washington has yet to beat a clear playoff contender outside of Seattle in Week 3, and they let winnable games against the Colts and Giants slip away earlier in the fall. Until the Commanders can achieve sustained winning, a majority of the fan base is keeping its guard up, and the heat will stay turned up on head coach Dan Quinn and GM Adam Peters.

Our second question asked which team respondents believe will win the NFC East division title in 2026. Despite Washington sitting behind both New York and Dallas in the standings, the results were fairly decisive:

That 54% vote represents a dramatic reversal from earlier in the season, but it stems less from sudden perfection in Ashburn than from the vulnerabilities of Washington's rivals.

First, Washington owns the head-to-head tiebreaker over the Giants (5-3-1) after Thursday's win. Second, the schedule-makers did New York no favors. Over the final 8 weeks, the Giants must run a gauntlet that includes Jacksonville, San Francisco, Seattle, and Detroit, whereas Washington gets much softer matchups against Arizona, Tennessee, and Atlanta.

Meanwhile, Dallas (4-6) has been unable to win back-to-back games since November 28, 2025, and its defensive struggles reached a boiling point this week when head coach Brian Schottenheimer stripped first-year coordinator Christian Parker of play-calling duties in favor of senior defensive assistant Mike Pettine. To beat out Washington for the division title, the Cowboys will have to win as many times in their remaining 7 games as the Commanders win in their final 8. Don't forget that the Commanders still have a chance to inflict a loss on the Cowboys when they meet in Landover for the final game of the regular season in Week 18.

The math is straightforward: if Washington can match the win totals of both the Giants and Cowboys, the Commanders will be the division winner!

Washington holds the tiebreaker over the Giants, and, because of the Week 5 tie, matching Dallas in the win column would put Washington a half-game ahead of the silver & blue.

If, for example, the Commanders can win 5 of their final 8 games to finish 8-8-1, they would be division champs if the Giants go 3-5 and the Cowboys, who have only 7 games remaining, finish out the season 4-3. Of course, with 8 weeks to go, there are sure to be a lot of twists & turns before things are decided, but the Cowboys' games in Week 17 vs the Giants and Week 18 on the road against the Commanders could prove to be decisive in determining the ultimate division champ.

Readers in the survey article comment thread broke cleanly along familiar lines.

The optimistic Pollyanna camp — voiced by Savo_UK, Redskins21Life, and BillWard among others– pointed directly to the schedule imbalance. Their consensus is that if Washington takes care of business against Arizona, Tennessee, Atlanta, and Dallas (in Week 18), stealing just 1 upset against Cincinnati, Houston, Minnesota, or Jacksonville would be enough to reach 8 wins, which could be enough to claim the division in this 2026 season.

The skeptical Cassandra camp — led by theFan4Life, WillThomas, and Greengmork among others — warned against mistaking a single prime-time escape against the Giants for a turnaround. They argue that Washington's defense still struggles to generate consistent pressure, that the roster lacks the talent to beat playoff-caliber offenses, and that finishing around .500 risks trapping the franchise in draft-position purgatory without building a true contender.

I think the Commanders defense has looked completely different since the return of Nick Cross and Trey Amos to the lineup.

In the first 5 games of 2026, the Commanders surrendered an average of 29.8 points per game. Since Week 6 in San Francisco, when Amos and Cross took the field together for the first time, Washington has given up 18.5 points per game. While that stretch did include games against the struggling Eagles and last week's road trip to New York, it also included games against the Niners and Rams.

While passing yards surrendered since Week 6 are not dramatically improved (255 per game in Wks 1-5; 232 per game in Wks 6-10), the number of passing TOUCHDOWNS surrendered has changed dramatically, from 2.8 per game (14 total) in the first 5 weeks to 0.75 per game (3 total) in the four most recent games; the secondary has not allowed more than one passing touchdown in any of those contests.

If you only look at the scoreboard, it is easy to argue that Washington's offense is still spinning its wheels. To my eye, though, the underlying efficiency and rhythm of David Blough's unit have taken a noticeable step forward over the past month.

Across the four-game stretch straddling the Week 7 bye, the Commanders have pushed their average to 7.6 yards per pass attempt—a marked jump from the 4.8 to 6.3 yards per attempt they managed during the opening month—while completing 66.7 percent of their throws. Even in the Week 9 loss to Los Angeles, Jayden Daniels posted a 101.4 passer rating (24-of-35 for 253 yards) with explosive strikes of 42 yards to Stefon Diggs, 21 yards to Van Jefferson, and a 45-yard burst from Rachaad White, before following that up on Thursday night in New York by going 23-of-33 for 235 yards and three touchdowns in a 24–21 road win over the Giants.

That progress reflects a deliberate coaching emphasis on sharper situational execution; prior to Week 10, head coach Dan Quinn noted that the staff added extra practice repetitions specifically targeting red-zone spacing against man and zone looks so that "the decision-making process happens quickly" and the offense stops settling for field goals.

Just as encouraging has been the growing trust between Daniels and his first-time play-caller, paired with Daniels' emergence as the unit's vocal leader. There were plenty of justifiable questions early on about Blough's learning curve, yet Daniels emphasized ahead of the Giants game that their weekly game-planning loop is actively tailoring the playbook to his strengths: "I think just stuff I don't like throughout the week, he'll take out. Stuff I really do like, he'll call… If we got all 11 people going in the same direction, we have a chance."

After some early-season failures, that buy-in has shown up recently when it has mattered, most notably during Week 8's 27–10 win over Philadelphia, when Daniels gathered the offense during a sluggish 10–0 start—calling their opening series "complete BS"—and sparked 27 unanswered points.

Rookie wideout Antonio Williams credited Daniels' command in the huddle for uniting the group ("All the guys respect what he has to say, because we know the work he puts in"), while Quinn praised the quarterback's resilience in Thursday night's comeback after overcoming a turnover on downs and two interceptions to engineer the game-winning drive, saying, "To see us battle and stay in the fight, that's what I was looking for."

All in all, I see a team that struggled early and is still finding its feet, but one that is on the rise. The Commanders still haven't put together that full 60-minute game that was the hallmark of the 2024 season, but I believe that they're moving in that direction, and, with 8 games left to play, there's plenty of time to finish putting it together.

The redesigned compact sedan features Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant, Gleo AI, and an updated regenerative braking system with EV-like one-pedal driving.

Hyundai pulled back the cover on the new 2027 Elantra sedan and 2028 Elantra Hybrid this week at the LA Auto Show, and, wow, it looks like nothing else in its class. The bold styling may not be for everyone — though, personally, I dig it — but economy car shoppers will certainly be interested in the compact hybrid sedan’s targeted 53 mpg and Gleo, Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant that controls the automaker’s new Android Automotive OS-based dashboard tech.

The humble Hyundai Elantra hasn’t historically been a sexy car, but it remains an important one for the brand. It’s the automaker’s second-bestselling vehicle, behind only the Tucson. More importantly, the Elantra is one of the stalwarts of the affordable compact sedan class, a segment that many US automakers have abandoned in favor of more profitable SUVs. At a time when buyers are more sensitive than ever to inflated fuel costs and sticker prices, economy car options like the Elantra and its second-generation hybrid powertrain are worth our attention.

The updated hybrid powertrain pairs a 1.6-liter four-cylinder gasoline engine with a 6-speed dual-clutch automatic gearbox. Combined output at the front wheels rises to 155 horsepower (up from 139 hp in the current car), thanks to a more powerful 45-kilowatt electric-assist motor (up from 32kW).

An updated regenerative braking system recaptures energy when slowing and now offers an “EV-like one-pedal driving experience” that can bring the car to a complete stop at its maximum setting. Additionally, the hybrid now features a new Stay mode that lets the climate control and infotainment systems run while parked for up to 30 minutes without firing up the gas engine.

Sharing the stage in LA, the new nonhybrid Elantra arrives with the same 2.0-liter four-cylinder engine and continuously variable transmission as the current generation. At 147 hp with a target of 35 mpg combined, it’s no more powerful than before, but it should also be no less efficient, despite the sedan’s increased size. I’d say the hybrid is the better choice, though it will likely also be the more expensive powertrain option.

Inside, a new cockpit design moves the now-hoodless digital instrument cluster, a 9.9-inch “Slim Information Display,” away from the driver and toward the windshield on the dashboard. The new placement, similar to what I’ve seen on recent Toyota models, puts vehicle information closer to the driver’s line of sight.

Pleos Connect, Hyundai’s new Android Automotive OS-based infotainment suite, lives on a 12.9-inch center display. Despite being based on Android, it still features the automaker’s bespoke navigation and media software, rather than Google Maps. The system is said to be over-the-air updatable and features standard wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto for drivers who’d rather bring their own apps on the road.

The Pleos dashboard OS is matched with Gleo AI (who names these things?!), which is Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant that lets drivers and passengers talk to the car rather than occupy a hand on the touchscreen. Hyundai says the system features “contextual language support, multi-command capability and zone-specific recognition,” which basically means the car knows who’s talking to it and which seat they’re in and can assign context to voice prompts based on the situation.

“Affordable doesn’t have to be anonymous,” said Olabisi Boyle, Hyundai’s senior vice president of product, at the unveiling. I agree: Hyundai’s designers clearly aren’t afraid to push the design envelope, and the new Elantra is anything but bland.

The sedan introduces the automaker’s Art of Steel design language, with chunky flared fenders that feel inspired by the brand’s recent new SUVs. The tall greenhouse looks a bit cartoonish if you catch it at the wrong angle, but it provides increased headroom inside. From certain angles, the sedan’s beefy fenders remind me of an angular Korean take on the Subaru WRX. From other viewpoints, I can see inspiration from Hyundai’s N Vision 74 concept and the hydrogen-fueled Nexo, particularly in the triangular rear windows.

The sedan measures 188.8 inches bumper-to-bumper on a 108.3-inch wheelbase that is 1.2 inches longer than the current model’s. Hyundai says the stretch for this generation improves rear-passenger space, now rated at 39.1 inches of legroom on the second row. The Elantra, according to its designers, now approaches the interior volume of a midsize car, a claim I’d take with a grain of salt had I not watched multiple six-foot-plus attendees slip into the back seat with relative ease.

Hyundai did not announce pricing or detailed specs at the auto show debut, and availability is still vague. However, based on the stated model years, which are usually slightly offset from calendar years, I expect the 2027 Elantra sedan to arrive in early 2027, with the 2028 Elantra Hybrid following in late 2027.

S&P 500 correction: Five forces behind the market sell-off

📅 2026-11-20  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Esteemed Wharton professor Jeremy Siegel was more animated than usual on Friday afternoon... with good reason.

The normally staid author of "Stocks for the Long Run" spoke to CNBC on Friday as the market careened toward some ugly milestones. The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average closed in correction territory, off more than 10% from their records. The Nasdaq Composite lost its gain for the year and the more concentrated Nasdaq-100 entered bear market territory on an intraday basis.

"These things are like straws that break the camel's back. You know what I mean? Everyone [is] all, 'I don't care, I don't care cause everything else is good' and when those straws keep on going... Bang," said Siegel.

Just a month-and-a-half ago, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite were at record highs. But the straws were already adding up by then and a market in denial is just now recognizing them.

Here are the reasons behind the sell-off:

The breaking point came this week in the form of the initial public offering of artificial intelligence leader Anthropic and a pick-up in the selling intensity for one-time AI chip leader Nvidia.

Anthropic upped its offering amount a day before going public despite the already dimming climate around AI-related stocks. The move ensured it would be the biggest IPO ever, but played into the fears that this debut — following SpaceX's monster offering over the summer — was a collective cash-out moment for the AI complex that will leave the investing masses holding the bag.

In other words, it could be the ultimate "sell the news" moment for the AI trade.

On Thursday, Anthropic traded from its $83 IPO price to a high above $115 before instantly rolling over and taking the AI trade with it. Anthropic's sell-off intensified Friday, and when the dust settled Anthropic closed the week at $88.91.

The Nasdaq posted its worst week since the Covid plunge of March 2020, down 10%.

Nvidia fell 14% on Friday with the selling intensifying after a Reuters headline hit the tape that the chipmaker's accounting is the focus of a Securities and Exchange Commission probe.

Nvidia is now down nearly 40% in November alone, a month that saw the AI bull market's leading salesman, Jensen Huang, resign as its CEO because of an affair with an alleged Chinese spy that raised speculation about whether the company's chips were ending up in Beijing's hands despite government restrictions.

To start the week, the Commerce Department tightened restrictions on U.S.-made chips to stop China from accessing them remotely from other countries.

A solid earnings report on Tuesday fell flat as Nvidia's board definitively ruled out any chance Huang would return, disappointing some of the stock's fanatics still holding out hope for his far-fetched return. Nvidia ended the week with a 23% loss.

The S&P 500 traded below its 200-day moving average — what's considered a measure of a security's long-term trend — on Tuesday and made a convincing break of it on Thursday. This turned many on Wall Street— like commodity trading advisors — into automatic sellers on the market. So-called CTAs are individuals or organizations that advise others on trading futures and swaps across a range of assets.

"We know there's a lot of trend riders on these markets," said Siegel. "They have their trend lines. They've ridden it up and as soon as it goes down, they're selling and they're selling and they're selling and they just perpetuate" the decline, he said.

The Iran conflict has caused the price oil to surge and while it's off its highs it still remains above $83 a barrel, while the fees for transiting the Strait of Hormuz and the rest of the region keep persistent pressure on global prices.

A series of numbers earlier in the month showed inflation is still far from the Federal Reserve's 2% target, sparking a run of central bank officials to warn they may have to get more aggressive with interest rates.

After increasing rates in September by a quarter point, the Fed skipped a hike in late October and sparked concern among bond investors it may be falling behind the inflation fight. The 10-year Treasury yield subsequently jumped above 5% and stayed there until a flight of safe haven fixed income buying brought it back below that level on Friday.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said this week that the Fed may need to do a half percentage point hike next month to catch up.

"I'm very, very concerned about inflation. It's kind of headed in the wrong direction, and it's spreading out," Waller told CNBC.

"Don't fight the Fed" has long been a maxim on Wall Street and right now expectations are that the central bank will need to raise rates in December and again in January.

And late-breaking on Friday was a report from the Financial Times that the Trump Administration is considering restarting strikes on Iran.

Before the grim market milestones were met on Friday, it had long lost its mojo on fears that the pace of growth driven by AI companies was slowing.

As Anthropic prepared to go public, its third-quarter figures released earlier this month showed revenue growth last quarter was 47%, down from 144% in the second quarter.

AI momentum trades, like the VanEck Semiconductor ETF and the iShares MSCI USA Momentum Factor ETF, all topped out during the summer.

The fears of slowing growth picked up this month after the Democrats won control of Congress and key governorships, fueling speculation that states — or even the whole country — could implement stricter rules on data center construction.

CoreWeave, which builds data centers using high amounts of debt, is down 71% from its high. Caterpillar, which has reinvented itself as an AI play because of data center construction and generators, is down more than 50% from its high.

The view of Siegel and many market observers is that the economy remains in good enough shape that this will just be a run-of-the-mill correction that resets a bull market that had raised people's expectations too high because of its early ferocity.

The S&P 500 returned more than 20% in 2023 and 2024 and then gained 16% last year.

"The average over long periods for the stock market is 8%, 9% a year up," said Siegel. "People began to think that 15 to 20 [percent] was going to be the norm with AI and we have to bring that down to reality."

The only problem with that theory is that the stock market and its wealth effect was one of the major factors underpinning the economy. Stocks are a greater source of household wealth — especially for baby boomers — than they ever have been before.

With the S&P 500 clinging to a 1% gain for the year, this stock market rout could end up tipping off a virtuous cycle that slows the economy, and in turn, leads to bigger equity losses.

Non so quanto abbia senso andare a spendere 30 o 40 milioni per un giocatore che magari poi non ti dà quello che ti aspetti e che sopravvaluti anche nell'acquisto, perché alla fine non vale tutti quei soldi. Per me ha più senso se l'allenatore conosce bene un giocatore e dice di volerlo perché può aiutarlo da subito fino a giugno. In quali ruoli ha bisogno il Napoli in questo momento? Penso a un attaccante, sicuramente, ma per me serve anche un leader difensivo che possa gestire tutta la squadra. Un leader difensivo".
