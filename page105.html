Fun things to do with your VM/370 CE machine – Fun with big computers

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This page assumes you have a machine (emulated or not) running the VM370 Community Edition OS available. There are a couple ways to get one, and you might want to choose the one that appeals to you the most (or the one that’s less work - it’s up to you).

The Community Edition is a huge set of improvements and fixes applied on top of the last VM/370 that was released by IBM without a license restricting its use. These improvements and fixes have been developed and published in a variety of ways since the release of the original OS. If we were to compare it with Linux, VM/370 R6 (the release CE is based on) would be Linux 1.0 as published by Linus from his bedroom and VM/370 CE would be latest Fedora (insert your favorite maximalist distro here). It has many features that didn’t exist in R6 and a couple things that would have been called science fiction back then. It’s quite capable, even if the machines it can run on are limited to a single CPU and 16M of memory (IBM calls it “storage”).

The easiest way to run this is with Docker installed. How to install it depends on your platform, and we won’t cover it here. Besides, Docker is only one option - you can use Podman as well - it has some nice features, and can pretend it’s Docker, so you can use the same commands with both. When you are set up, you can just issue a shell command to have your own machine.

This will bring up the machine and display the machine’s console on your terminal.

The container image we are using is based on the excellent work of the VM/370 Community Edition team. You can see their releases on vm370.org. The Community Editions runs only software that’s either open source or public domain.

If you work in an organization that’s cool enough to have an actual IBM mainframe, you can check if your operations team will allow you to have a small VM running nested VM/370 Community Edition. IBM mainframes have legendary backwards compatibility and there should be no (technical) issue bringing up an environment for you. There might be a lot of process hurdles, however, so tread carefully and have a good excuse at hand. “Doing fun stuff” is not it. Trust me, I’ve tried this approach and it didn’t end well.

Another possible source of used mainframes is eBay or your local IT asset disposal company, but, seriously, you probably don’t want the hassle of a dedicated power line for the machine, or the basement space to have one. And, if you get a real machine, you might also get a valid license to run something more modern, like z/VM, z/OS, and a fairly modern Linux. They are also quite expensive and require expertise to set them up, and, many times, to fix them (people who decommission these machines often don’t bother disconnecting cables when just cutting is faster).

IBM mainframes use screen-oriented terminals. They worked more or less like a web browser - the mainframe would send you a screen with a form, you’d write some information on the fields of the form, and the terminal would send the information back to the mainframe, restarting the cycle. If you think this looks like how HTML forms work, you are right, but this was in the late 1960s, not the mid 1990s.

Unlike most terminals, a 3270 doesn’t scroll. If you fill the screen, you’ll need to press the PA2 key (don’t look for one on your keyboard - there isn’t one). PA2 clears the terminal and receives the next page of text from the mainframe. On x3270, PA2 is Alt-2. On c3270, it’s Control-C.

CMS offers a flat file system, so we can’t have subdirectories. This is a big difference for people who are used to Unix or Windows systems and to organize files using them. 4 Action Movie Masterpieces That Define The Entire Genre

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The action genre is an exciting and evolving subsection of cinema that has continued to develop in exciting ways since the dawn of the medium. There’s no clear consensus on when the modern action film began because examples of high-wire setpieces and incredible stuntwork are much older than anything resembling modern blockbusters. The silent films of Buster Keaton and Charlie Chaplin featured remarkable precision and experimentation when it came to actual stuntwork, even if they wouldn’t technically be considered “action stars” by modern pundits. The Western genre, which has been an active part of the industry since the ‘30s, is also where a lot of “action stars” got their start; actors like John Wayne, Steve McQueen, Charlton Heston, Gary Cooper, and Yul Brennar were all accomplished dramatic actors who could do their own fight scenes.

Action cinema began to become more stylized in the 1960s, as the era of World War II adventure films started to sunset in favor of franchises like James Bond, which promised an increased degree of spectacle and theatricality upon each new entry. The ‘70s introduced a darker component to the genre with the rise of vigilante films like Rolling Thunder and the Dirty Harry series, which were more grounded in reality because they drew from contemporary anxieties.

The action genre is often defined by stars, as it seems as if each generation has a few major actors who continuously push the genre forward. Arnold Schwarzenegger and Sylvester Stallone dominated the ‘80s, and their reign continued well into the ‘90s, even if they found new competitors in Tom Cruise and Nicolas Cage. Although today there’s been action stars like Jason Statham and Gerard Butler who have essentially become genres on to themselves, there’s also an appeal in seeing older, more grizzled dramatic actors return to their pulpier roots, including Denzel Washington and Liam Neeson. There are still a few specific action films that have not only stood the test of time, but have a significant degree of influence on pretty much everything that has followed.

Raiders of the Lost Ark was an attempt to do for ‘30s adventure serials what Star Wars had done for science fiction, and producer/creator George Lucas knew that there was only one person who he could trust with bringing the character to life. Steven Spielberg had long since lamented that he was unable to direct a 007 film, as MGM had consistently turned him down because they were not accepting American filmmakers. It was famously when Spielberg and Lucas were on vacation during the opening weekend of Star Wars that they came up with the idea of what the Indiana Jones franchise would look like. Unlike Bond, Indy was a straight-laced professor with a deep knowledge of history and archeology, but he also had a strong desire for justice. Although multiple possibilities were floated for who could possibly take on the role, Harrison Ford eventually became the only real choice, and it’s perhaps the defining part in a career that includes some of the most beloved films of all-time.

Raiders of the Lost Ark is a film that Lucas described as having “just the good parts,” and that is evident within a perfect screenplay by Lawrence Kasdan. There is no fat on the script, as the story moves at a propulsive pace without ever feeling impersonal. Although there are several great installments in the series, Raiders of the Lost Ark remains the best because it has the most exciting MacGuffin, which brings to mind issues of philosophy and faith that add an existentialist level to the film. Many of the action scenes, such as the truck chase and the bi-plane fight, are absolutely brutal in their viscerality, but Spielberg did not forget to also make a film that is equally funny and romantic. The romantic chemistry that Ford has with Karen Allen is nothing short of remarkable, and it’s a major reason why Marion Ravenwood is the love interest that the series has kept coming back to.

Die Hard is a film that has come to represent an entire era of action cinema because of its bullet-proof premise of an ordinary man trapped in a confined location and forced to use his wits and wry wisdom to defend himself and get people to safety. Die Hard was such a massive cultural success that the next generation of action films could all be pitched with their comparisons to Die Hard; Speed was “Die Hard on a bus,” Con Air was “Die Hard on a plane,” Under Siege was “Die Hard on a boat,” and The Rock was “Die Hard in Alcatraz." What distinguished the original Die Hard from all of its imitators was the instantly iconic, brilliant performance by Bruce Willis as John McClane. Although at the time few thought that the guy from Moonlighting would be the next great action star, Willis understood the heart, humanity, and humor needed to play McClane, who somehow felt like a more relatable character than the types of roles that Schwarzenegger and Stallone were playing at the same time.

Die Hard’s genius was understanding that having a great villain was just as important as having a great villain, and cinematic antagonists rarely get better than Alan Rickman as Hans Gruber. Rickman had never acted in a film before, but became perfectly suited to be the cruel, sarcastic, and deviously clever criminal mastermind whose motivations aren’t exactly what they appear to be at first. The dynamic between Willis and Rickman is why Die Hard hits on another level, even if they don’t share that many scenes together. It’s also why none of the Die Hard sequels were able to quite live up to the promise of the first film; the best of the other films in the series is the third installment, Die Hard With A Vengeance, which honored Rickman by introducing Jeremy Irons to play his brother, Simon Gruber.

The Matrix came at a time in which the science fiction genre needed a boost of energy due to it being overrun by maximalist blockbusters and established franchises. The Wachowski sisters made a deeply philosophical warning about the rise of artificial intelligence that also drew from the philosophy of Plato and the type of martial-arts that was traditionally found in East Asian action cinema, as well as strong components of the cyberpunk aesthetic. The result was a groundbreaking achievement in worldbuilding and spectacle that both looked like something that cinephiles had never seen before and got them to question the nature of their reality. Due to the new rules for what its universe looked like, The Matrix was able to reinvent the way that fight scenes were choreographed in the digital age with cutting-edge techniques, such as the now-famous introduction of “bullet time.”

The Matrix is cutting edge as a work of slick, propulsive action filmmaking due to the inclusion of advanced and superhuman fight techniques within a tactical environment, but it is also a classic “hero’s journey” story that benefitted from the charisma of Keanu Reeves. Although Reeves had already proven himself to be a talented action star with Point Break and Speed, The Matrix perfectly cast him to play the avatar for humanity who has to accept his destiny to become “The One.” Although the later installments in The Matrix franchise got a little too wrapped up in their own mythology and strayed away from the emotional sincerity of the original premise, the first film remains a perfect masterpiece that has held up just as well today.

Mad Max: Fury Road was a film that came 30 years in the making, as George Miller had desired to make a fourth installment in the Mad Max franchise ever since he wrapped on the third entry, Mad Max Beyond Thunderdome, in 1985. Although a massive script was written that included an outline for the prequel film that he would end up making, Miller was constantly faced with setbacks and issues regarding funding; after 9/11 delayed shooting and Mel Gibson became too old to play the iconic titular character, it seemed like his dream would never come to fruition.

It was against all odds that Miller ventured into the Australian desert once more on a famously grueling film shoot in which wild rumors suggested that there was a massive disaster in place, and that skepticism didn’t let up until Mad Max: Fury Road debuted at the Cannes Film Festival in 2015 and solidified itself as one of the greatest films of the 21st century, regardless of genre. Mad Max: Fury Road is pure adrenaline and propulsiveness, as it is essentially one long chase. On a deeper level, Mad Max: Fury Road follows a drifter who gets caught up in a revolution, and the launching of Charlize Theron’s Furiosa as one of the defining action heroes of all-time took the great franchise to another level.

NEW YORK, Nov. 9, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- An AI tool can analyze patients' narratives about injuries that sent them to the emergency room to predict who will later develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a new study found.

About 43 million patients are admitted to a US emergency department (ED) each year with physical injuries (trauma), and more than a quarter of them develop PTSD, according to past studies. That makes the ED a checkpoint for catching the condition early, but clinicians face time constraints severe enough to rule out the use of standard screening questionnaires.

Led by NYU Langone Health researchers, the new study found that the team's set of AI models â each analyzing the trauma narratives of 145 ED patients â correctly identified more than 95.4 percent of at-risk patients. In a head-to-head test, the AI tool, called PREVIA, flagged 100 percent of 30 cases from the same patient group that went on to develop PTSD, while an ED clinician specializing in PTSD correctly identified 89 percent of these cases.

"This is the first study to show that a patient's spontaneous answers to a single, routine care question â 'What brought you to the ED?' â carries enough of a psychological signal to forecast their PTSD risk a month later," said senior study author Katharina Schultebraucks, PhD, associate professor in the departments of psychiatry and population health at NYU Langone. "PREVIA can serve as an AI screener that identifies at-risk patients and explains why, without adding any time to intake in busy emergency rooms."

Published online November 9 in Nature Communications, the new work examined the capabilities of generative AI, which predicts options for the next word in a sentence based on how billions of people used that word in context. This sensitivity to language lets the models analyze a person's description of what happened to them to find patterns linked to their risk of developing PTSD, the researchers said. The patients' answers were recorded and transcribed for analysis.

"The emergency department is often the only point of contact a trauma survivor has with the healthcare system before symptoms become much harder to treat," added Dr. Schultebraucks, who is also co-director of computational psychiatry at NYU Grossman School of Medicine. "By identifying at-risk patients at that first encounter, PREVIA could open a window for timely care approaches, when preventive intervention is most effective."

The first month after trauma is a key window for finding those at risk for PTSD because many patients are no longer in contact with the hospital after that, the researchers said. Current questionnaires â such as the Peritraumatic Dissociative Experiences Questionnaire and the Posttraumatic Adjustment Scale â effectively forecast PTSD development, yet only about 28 percent of EDs routinely screen for PTSD at all, and only two-thirds of those use validated tools.

This likely reflects overcrowding and limited clinician time, typically about 15â20 minutes per patient, which forces clinicians to prioritize immediate medical concerns over lengthy mental health assessments, the study authors said.

PREVIA was designed as a pipeline of steps that use different AI tools based on their strengths (e.g., the Llama model tends to emphasize signs of PTSD risk, while Mistral highlights protective and contextual factors that could buffer against it). Specifically, the tool's first layer scans the language of a patient's narrative for symptoms that happen during the initial trauma, like loss of control or amnesia, as well as early symptoms that show up while a person is in the ED, such as feeling disconnected from the body. Both types of symptoms have been linked to later PTSD development.

The second layer of the team's AI tool also weighs risk factors such as pre-existing mental health problems or lower socioeconomic status, alongside protective factors such as coping skills or strong social support. A third AI model synthesizes these assessments into a structured summary, reducing the chance that any single model dominates the results. A final layer combines that summary with demographic information (e.g., age, sex), trauma type, and known PTSD prevalence rates to produce a risk score with a plain-language explanation.
