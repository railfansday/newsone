When was the last time each of the Big Six were in the relegation zone?

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For the first time in 40 years, Manchester United find themselves in the relegation zone, so when was the last time each of the Big Six were in the bottom three?

United’s 4-4 draw at Aston Villa condemned them to the drop zone for the entirety of the international break.

So we’ve gone back over the history books to find the most recent time each of the Big Six were in the drop zone.

Thirteen years after their most recent Premier League title, United are now more concerned with leaving the competition than winning it.

Carrick’s team have won one game all season and even that was not without elements of luck.

They have been shockingly poor, the players are arguing with the board in public, Carrick looks out of his depth and mediocrity has become the accepted norm for a club that once held the very highest of standards.

United rescued a point against Villa, but that feels like plugging one hole on the Titanic while there are still several very large holes to deal with.

That draw, coupled with Nottingham Forest’s beating of Manchester City, put United into 18th, the first time United ended a day in the top-flight relegation zone since November 1986.

United probably won’t get relegated (probably) but the fact they will spend the long two weeks of the international break down there is a very visual sign of how far this club has fallen.

If you happened to tune into talkSPORT at any point, you will have been well aware that Spurs were in the bottom three at the start of this season.

Roberto De Zerbi’s team started the year not only without a win but failed to score in the opening four games.

By the third game, they had at least managed to stop conceding them too and had risen to the dizzying heights of 17th come the end of the four-game day.

But a 3-2 defeat to Aston Villa put them down at 20th. Drawing 1-1 with Manchester United – which should put them in the relegation zone automatically – kept them in the bottom three.

But a first win of the season against Everton followed by two more rose Spurs up and away from danger (for now).

They are 11th after their weekend defeat to Leeds, but relegation looks a long way off now.

Under the watchful eye of Amazon Prime’s cameras, Arteta’s second season in charge did not get off to the best of starts.

They lost the first three games of the year, the latter of which was a 5-0 thumping away at Manchester City and a result that left them dead last in the Premier League table.

Things turned after though and a porous Arsenal defence began to look like the one that is synonymous with the team today.

One goal conceded in the next four matches, with three wins and a draw, pushed Arsenal up the table.

The Gunners then put together a run of three wins to move into the European spots, but they still struggled against their fellow Big Six opponents.

In late November they were pumped 4-0 at Anfield before losing 3-2 to Manchester United a few weeks later.

A 5-0 win away at Norwich on Boxing Day boosted the spirit and a 2-1 defeat against City on New Year’s Day kickstarted a good run for Arteta, putting together five wins in their next six games.

By the end of that, they were fourth and in the Champions League spot but in a very Arsenal way, the wheels fell off.

Four defeats in five put them down to fifth and come the end of the season, Arsenal just missed out on the Champions League.

2019 was a summer of change for Chelsea. Maurizio Sarri had left to go and smoke cigarettes at Juventus while Eden Hazard moved to Real Madrid.

The Chelsea hierarchy gave the job to Frank Lampard and he was handed the unwanted present of a trip to Old Trafford for his first match in charge.

Marcus Rashford was the star as United recorded their biggest win at home against Chelsea since 1965, beating the London side 4-0 in Lampard’s first Premier League match.

The only team to have a worse start to the year was West Ham, who got battered 5-0 by Manchester City. That meant the Hammers were bottom while Lampard’s Chelsea were 19th.

A draw against Leicester in the next game did not boost too much confidence, but Lampard did manage to turn it around with six wins on the trot.

Chelsea moved into fourth in matchday nine and stayed there seemingly forever, briefly flirting with third near the end of the year but ultimately finishing in the Champions League spots.

Brendan Rodgers was billed as the next great British manager, but Liverpool’s fans were right to be a little worried when the team started his tenure without a win in their first five.

Liverpool lost every other game to kick off the Rodgers era, including defeats at home to Arsenal and United, leaving them flirting with the relegation zone.

They were in 18th first, third and finally the fifth matchweek before going on an unbeaten run of eight games and moving clear of the drop.

Liverpool would go on to finish seventh that year and kicked on under Rodgers.

Give it a few months and this may be due an update but for now, the last time City were in the relegation zone was back in 2008 in the first season under the Abu Dhabi ownership.

City, managed by Mark Hughes, started the year badly with a 4-2 defeat away at Aston Villa, which put them 18th, but they did manage to rise to fifth with a few decent results, including a 6-0 win against Portsmouth.

One win in eight games sent them tumbling back down and despite a momentary respite of beating Arsenal 3-0, City’s run of three defeats in four had them 18th at Christmas.

City spent big in January, bringing in Wayne Bridge, Craig Bellamy, Nigel de Jong and Shay Given, which shored them up.

They continued to lose a lot of games in the second half of the year but were able to rise to 10th come the season’s end.

Excluding pre-takeover City, which doesn’t really count (2001 if you were wondering), the last Big Six club to go down was Chelsea back in 1988.

The Blues were sent into a relegation playoff against Middlesbrough but lost it 2-1 over two legs and their fans stormed the pitch.

For the pedants out there that say Chelsea pre-takeover also doesn’t count, then you are looking at Tottenham in 1977.

Before that, United went down in 1974, Liverpool in 1954 and Arsenal in 1913.

The Latest Shift in How Appraisals Are Handled Could Save Homebuyers Hundreds in Closing Costs—Eventually

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s typically the last hurdle for homebuyers—and the process is about to get a face-lift.

Last week, the Uniform Appraisal Dataset (UAD) 3.6, a major update to property valuation standards introduced by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, became mandatory for all new UCDP appraisal report submissions.

UAD 3.6 is a new, digital form that appraisers will use to evaluate homes. Now, rather than the appraiser meticulously jotting down notes in longform paragraphs, the new system will allow appraisers to use extensive drop-down menus and checkboxes to make their evaluations.

A proper appraisal can determine not only whether a buyer can qualify for a loan, but also whether they can save significant money at closing.

After a buyer makes an offer, their mortgage lender will order an independent appraisal to confirm the home is worth at least what's being borrowed against it.

If the appraised value comes in at or above the purchase price, the process moves forward as planned. But if it comes in low, that gap has to be resolved before the loan can close.

The logic behind the UAD 3.6 update, according to Fannie and Freddie, is to create a “data-driven, dynamic reporting structure to capture property and market information.”

This new form includes significantly more data fields, helping lenders and buyers better understand the property’s characteristics.

But with more detail comes a longer commitment.

“Be prepared for a lot more questions from the appraiser during the inspection,” explains Michael Mignogna, president of the Appraisal Institute.

“There’s a lot more data to include, so be prepared to answer questions about renovations, repairs, new systems like heating and air conditioning, appliances, etc., or have that information ready if no one with that type of information can be at the inspection.”

To that end, with UAD 3.6, appraisers will now not only grade individual rooms, but they can also highlight upgrades and features that might’ve been lost in the old system. So, features of a newly remodeled kitchen or new bathroom flooring get specifically noted, which will aid in the ease of deciding the home’s value.

Still, Mignogna is skeptical that this will have much of an impact on the final value.

“Not as much information is always known about the comparables, so expectations about condition/quality adjustments to the comps on a room-by-room basis should be tempered,” he adds.

The buyer almost always pays for the appraisal. The cost is typically a few hundred dollars, and it’s often rolled into closing costs. This is the case even though the appraiser is hired by the lender, not the buyer.

The hope is that because this new appraisal process is a clean, digital checklist, banks can review and approve it much faster, reducing delays.

For example, under the former process, lenders had the option to kick back the report for corrections if anything about the report was unclear, which could happen if the appraiser’s report didn’t cover every facet necessary.

When that happens, buyers may find themselves having to pay for expensive mortgage rate-lock extensions to keep their rates while they wait for the appraisal to clear.

Extension fees typically range from 0.125% to 0.250% (or up to 0.500% or more) of the buyer's total loan balance per extension period, often broken into 15-day increments.

On a $450,000 mortgage, a 15-day extension would usually cost between $500 and $1,000.

Given that closing costs already run a buyer anywhere from 2% to 5% of the total loan amount, or about $8,000 to $20,000 for a $400,000 loan, according to Bankrate, another couple hundred to a thousand dollars is nothing to scoff at.

But as the saying goes, all good things come with time, and right now, this new digital infrastructure may actually be coming at a cost to buyers as it finds its footing.

Clear Capital, a real estate valuation technology company, reported via National Mortgage Professional an average $65 increase in appraisal fees in markets where it has enough production volume to compare UAD 3.6 with the older UAD 2.6 format.

Additionally, Michael Farrell, president of the National Association of Mortgage Brokers, said that the number of field appraisers equipped to do UAD 3.6 is so limited at this point, demand bottlenecks are driving up prices for modernized reports. While older appraisals are being priced at around $550, modernized reports may cost borrowers closer to $850.

“It’s basically all roads lead to chaos,” Farrell explained to Scotsman Guide.

“From an originator standpoint, it's a delay. Delays cost money. Delays cost the consumer money.”

Mignogna, meanwhile, says it’s important to remember that fees are affected by far more than just the process.

“Appraisal fees are very specific to markets and are affected by the number of appraisers in a market, the demand for appraisals in that market, interest rates, and a host of other factors,” he says.

Having said that, the transition to the UAD 3.6 format continues to be slow and Mignogna concedes that lenders may bid up prices for the few field appraisers who are fully onboarded like in any “supply-demand situation.”

But while delays are happening as appraisers go through a “transition mode,” Mignogna says this won’t affect buyers' need for expensive mortgage rate-lock extensions.

“This is really only meaningful or significant in times of interest rate uncertainty or expected rate increases,” he says.

“The overall UAD 3.6 process is longer right now for the reasons above, but as the learning curve flattens, turnaround times will improve. It remains to be seen if the increased data requirements offset by UAD 3.6 familiarity gets us to the turnaround times seen before UAD 3.6 or faster turn times.”

With that in mind, it’s a good idea to ask your loan officer upfront if their specific appraisal management company has fully integrated the new digital system into their team.

While the UAD 3.6 mandate took effect on Nov. 2, 2026,  lenders may continue to submit revisions for existing UAD 2.6 reports through May 19, 2027.

Bournemouth's Amine Adli should be subject to review by an independent panel following a late moment against Ipswich Town.

That is the opinion of former head of PGMOL and FIFA referee, Keith Hackett, speaking exclusively to Football Insider.

Goals from Evanilson and a goal from Leif Davis had put Marco Rose's side 2-0 up at Portman Road.

The Tractor Boys would get one back via Jack Clarke from the penalty spot, and would go to push for an equalising goal.

In added time, Ipswich had found themselves on the edge of the Cherries' box, but Adli would relieve pressure by drawing a foul.

Albeit through deceiving referee Simon Hooper.

Simulation is a part of football that every fan will want to be rid of in the game.

However, when incidents are missed, those players can get away with punishment, with Adli the latest example.

In the added time of Bournemouth's 2-1 win against Ipswich, Adli had gone down as if he was struck in the face.

However, replays showed that Julio Enciso had merely grazed his chest.

Bournemouth were awarded a free-kick, with Hackett left unamused by the footage he'd seen.

He said: "I just think that what we're seeing in the game is this unacceptable act by players of simulation, attempting to get their opponent yellow-carded or in an attempt to unfairly win a free kick or a penalty kick.

"Therefore, I think that the authorities, at some point, should have an independent panel operating on a Monday morning, and where there are incidents of this type, to have the power to suspend a player for a minimum of one match."

Hackett believes the problem extends far beyond the Premier League.

Hackett detailed how he feels this issue is becoming more commonplace across all of English football, sharing: "I do think that these increased acts of simulation are making life difficult throughout the whole tier of football.

"Not only are we seeing it at the elite level, but we're actually seeing it at the grassroots level and young referees coming under pressure from parents because of these acts, which are unfair and warrant a caution, a yellow card."

No football fan wants to see simulation on the field, and on this occasion, Adli certainly made the most of a limited touch, portraying it as a head injury, when there was no contact at all.

In a session at NAR’s 2026 NXT conference on Sunday, Woodward explored what AI can do for real estate professionals and the housing industry, as well as what it could do to agents and the industry if not used without proper caution and guardrails.

Woodward began by exploring the business model used by AI companies, because, he argues, you cannot understand the risk until you understand how AI companies make their money.

“There ain’t no such thing as a free lunch,” he says, before reaching for a darker metaphor. Two pigs in a barn admire the free shelter and the great food — right up until breakfast. “For those of you who had bacon this morning, you know how that story ends. You’re the bacon.”

As he put it, large language models are “voracious beasts” for data. Feed a free service your documents and it can ingest them, learn from them and surface what it learned to the next user.

Due to this, for anything real or confidential, pay for the service and tick the data-retention opt-out boxes, which stop the model from keeping or training on your inputs.

If the business model is the first trap, the overconfidence of LLMs is the second. Woodward warns against treating AI like “the Oracle of Delphi” — gods walking among us, dispensing truth.

To prove the point, he ran the same kinds of queries his clients run. He asked Copilot about supermarkets and schools near a Massachusetts town; it took the question too literally, omitting stores and schools in neighbouring towns. He asked Claude to pull up records for his home and five of the seven facts came back wrong.

The lesson for agents, he said, is less about the errors themselves and more about their clients.

Buyers now arrive armed with AI-generated impressions of a town or a property and it is in the agent’s interest to know what those buyers think they know.

Woodward also sought to dispel the belief that one AI model is better than the others, noting that agents need to simply pick out and learn how to make it work for them and their business. In addition to this, he also noted that there were no fancy tricks for prompting, instructing agents to “talk to it like a child”: short sentences, simple words, clear instructions.

But AI isn’t all just doomsday scenarios, according to Woodward, who noted that there were many low-risk and genuinely valuable use cases.

The caveat running through all of it: on free tiers, use dummy data, and never feed in anything confidential. Fixing technical faults like exposure, white balance or a tilted horizon are things industry professionals have long done using tools like photoshop. But corrections like these are now accessible to everyone thanks to AI regardless of their proficiency with photoshop.

Corrections like these, according to Woodward are fine, but removing temporary blemishes or even inventing property features like adding a fireplace, moving a window or dropping in a swimming pool can cross firmly into misrepresentation, a breach of the Realtor Code of Ethics.

What’s Fogging Your Brain? Signs of Aging vs. ADHD

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cognitive changes may stem from perimenopause, ADHD, or early dementia, each of which requires distinct treatment.

Q: How do I know whether hormonal changes related to perimenopause are causing my cognitive function problems, or my ADHD is getting worse, or I might be experiencing mild cognitive impairment or early dementia?

ADHD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) exhibit clinically different cognitive deficits that help me differentiate one from the other. While ADHD symptoms involve forgetfulness, inattention, and distractibility, MCI has specific cognitive symptoms of word-finding challenges, misspellings, and the use of generic terms like “stuff” and “things” because of word-finding difficulty. Also, MCI is progressive over two to three years, whereas ADHD doesn’t generally worsen.

In perimenopause, a decline in estrogen leads to cognitive difficulties. In contrast to MCI, we know that the cognitive changes from estrogen decline are not the result of neurodegeneration.

However, the specific deficits related to declining estrogen haven’t been well differentiated from those associated with ADHD. So if you have pre-existing ADHD and you experience heightened cognitive difficulties in perimenopause and menopause, you may think, “My ADHD is getting worse.” But it’s likely not worsening ADHD. It’s declining estrogen.

Okay, so what do you do about it? There is a paucity of research for clinical guidance. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) was not always recommended because it was alleged to increase risk for breast cancer and other illnesses. That claim has now been refuted by the most recent research, which has led to the removal of the black box warning from the FDA on HRT product labels. And so, if I have a female patient with ADHD who is 50 years old and says, “For the past two years, I’ve had cognitive decline,” the question to consider is this: “Do I increase her stimulant medication or do I put her on HRT?”

I am inclined at that point to put her on HRT for three months, keep her stimulant medication dosage stable, and monitor the qualitative changes in her cognitive functioning. If there is improvement on HRT, then the question becomes, “Does she stay on HRT for some extended period of time along with the stimulant medication?” If the HRT doesn’t work or she can’t take HRT for medical reasons, then I might increase the stimulant medication dosage to see if we can’t overcome the perimenopausal changes from the estrogen decline. This advice comes from my clinical experience, and it is unfortunate that we don’t have more research for clinical guidance here.

Q: Could ADHD stimulant medication mask symptoms of mild cognitive impairment?

There is very limited research on stimulants and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). My clinical impression is that stimulants likely improve symptoms of MCI, but do not take that as an endorsement to seek treatment for MCI with stimulants. There is emerging research on MCI and stimulants that has not yet been published. The follow-up question to that is, if you have ADHD and MCI, will treating the ADHD delay the development of MCI or Alzheimer’s? There is no clinical research for me to answer that question. But if this were me, I’d be treating my ADHD, keeping my fingers crossed, and appreciating the fact that the quality of my life on medicine is better than it would be without my ADHD treated.

ADHD should not get worse; however, the experience of ADHD may be worse if life’s demands start to increase and exceed your ability to compensate. However, if you’re having cognitive changes that are beyond the scope of your ADHD, you may have two neurologic processes going on: your ADHD and something else. In this case, your ADHD may, indeed, be getting worse because you have a second neurological process that needs to be evaluated.

The Nature Communications paper, also published November 9, reports 78.5% AUROC and 46.8% precision across three cross-validation folds and calls for validation in larger, independent cohorts. Sensitivity measures the share of later PTSD cases detected, precision measures the share of flagged patients who actually developed PTSD, and AUROC summarizes how well the scores separate the two outcomes. That precision means many positive screens would be false positives, potentially adding follow-up staff time, patient burden and resource-allocation costs, although the next step would typically be a brief assessment rather than invasive intervention.

PREVIA is designed around a practical gap: only about 28% of US emergency departments routinely screen for PTSD, and only two-thirds of those use validated questionnaires, according to the study. Standard assessments compete with immediate medical needs when clinician time is typically around 15–20 minutes per patient. The first month after trauma is a critical identification window because many patients are no longer in contact with the hospital by then.

Senior author Katharina Schultebraucks, co-director of computational psychiatry at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, said: “PREVIA can serve as an AI screener that identifies at-risk patients and explains why, without adding any time to intake in busy emergency rooms.”

Research coordinators asked patients at two New York City level-1 trauma centers to describe the event that brought them to the emergency department. Participants were enrolled within 24 hours of trauma and given up to three minutes to respond while an iPad recorded video. Narratives averaged about 374 words. Whisper transcribed patient speech, and native English-speaking research staff reviewed the transcripts. One-month PTSD outcomes were determined using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5, a clinical diagnostic interview.

PREVIA divides each transcript across three layers. In the first, Llama and Mistral independently assess early post-traumatic stress symptoms, reactions during the traumatic event, risk factors and protective factors. DeepSeek reconciles their eight assessments into structured summaries, while the two models’ numerical scores are averaged with equal weighting. A final assessor combines the summaries with age, sex, trauma type and PTSD prevalence information to produce a risk score from 0 to 100 and a short explanation.

The pipeline uses zero-shot prompts, with no parameter training or fine-tuning on the study sample. Cross-validation was used to optimize and evaluate the classification threshold.

The paper reports broadly comparable overall performance between PREVIA and single zero-shot configurations of O3, Llama and Mistral on many metrics, with PREVIA’s clearest advantage in sensitivity. For subthreshold PTSD, meaning clinically meaningful symptoms below the full diagnostic threshold, PREVIA reached 89.7% sensitivity and a 69.4% F1 score, a combined measure of precision and sensitivity.

The study reports a separate comparison of 30 randomly selected narratives, stratified to include nine PTSD cases, in which PREVIA recorded 100% sensitivity and 84.4% AUROC. One emergency clinician recorded 88.9% sensitivity and 83.9% AUROC; both recorded 50% precision. The clinician had more than 24 years of emergency-medicine experience and specialized PTSD research experience. The single-rater design leaves open whether the comparison generalizes across experience levels and clinical settings.

Participants were English-speaking, medically stable adults aged 18–80, and women accounted for 31% of the sample. Medically unstable patients were excluded, potentially omitting some patients at highest risk of chronic PTSD. The authors identify testing in other languages, longer-term PTSD trajectories, comorbidities such as depression, and integration with clinician judgment and emergency workflows as future work.

The authors propose a clinical workflow that pairs a brief audio recording with those demographic and trauma variables from the electronic health record, then returns a risk score and explanation without additional clinician input. The study did not establish that such screening improves patient outcomes. PREVIA ran in a HIPAA-compliant high-performance-computing environment on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Self-hosting supports control over patient data, but adoption depends on hospitals having suitable computing and governance infrastructure.

The public PREVIA repository provides the complete pipeline and prompts for open-weight models that run locally, plus five fabricated narratives for an end-to-end demonstration. Identifiable clinical narratives and psychiatric assessments remain restricted for privacy and consent reasons, so the demo can test operation but cannot reproduce the paper’s quantitative results.

PREVIA’s immediate contribution is an independently testable screening approach built from a routine emergency encounter and clinician-assessed outcomes. Its clinical value now hinges on independent validation: whether larger cohorts preserve its sensitivity, whether services can manage the false positives, and whether hospitals can incorporate recording, follow-up and secure computing without disrupting emergency care.
