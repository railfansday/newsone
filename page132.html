When (or if) Ring’s security camera drone flies into our homes in January, you won’t get the option to pilot it yourself. The Ring Drone Cam relies entirely on an “AI Pilot” system to follow pre-programmed routes and navigate autonomously around obstacles, with no manual control option available — and an embarrassing demonstration with CBS News might reveal why.

The video, broadcast on CBS Mornings and published on Threads, shows Ring founder Jamie Siminoff allowing technology reporter Jo Ling Kent to manually fly an early version of the drone via a mobile app with integrated controls.

Siminoff: “Yeah, so we can load manual controls.”

The demo then shows the drone crashing twice while being manually piloted. In the first instance, Ring reportedly told CBS News that the drone’s collision avoidance technology malfunctioned, and declined to share the video recorded by the drone prior to crashing. CBS News caught the second crash on camera, with the footage showing the drone colliding with a wall after failing to respond to manual controls. According to Siminoff, this occurred because the drone lost internet connectivity — which it also relies on to fly autonomously.

“If it loses connectivity, it’s supposed to stop and land,” Siminoff said in the video. “It did not there.”

Ring declined to show us the app in our own demonstration of the Drone Cam, and said it only works on pre-programmed flight paths. It seems that while manual flight controls were included at some stage of development, Ring won’t be making the feature available to customers when the Drone Cam launches in January.

“We explored a lot of features throughout the development, but autonomous flight to patrol points using the AI Pilot is the best customer benefit and experience,” Ring spokesperson Sam McGee said in a statement to The Verge. Ring didn’t respond to our question regarding whether the decision to not include manual controls was made following the CBS News demo.

Gut bacteria plays a huge role in vaccine side effects

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria that make a protein called flagellin keep low-grade inflammation going, and the people carrying more of them ran a fever.

For some people, a COVID shot means a night of chills and a headache. Others barely notice theirs, and the split holds, dose after dose. To some, this has always looked like luck.

Researchers have now found part of the reason, and it was in the gut before the needle went in. Certain bacteria there build whip-like tails out of a protein that the immune system reads as a warning.

People whose gut bacteria made more of that protein were the ones who ran a fever.

Kelsey Huus and her colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Biology Tübingen followed 171 healthy adults in Germany through an mRNA vaccination against SARS-CoV-2.

In people they can only show that gut microbes and fever go together, so they tested cause in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

If gut bacteria set the risk of a fever, that risk may be something a person can change, though that has not been shown in people.

In an interview with Earth.com, Ruth Ley, the study’s senior author, said the bigger idea isn’t really about vaccines at all.

“More broadly, I think the interesting idea coming from the study is that the gut microbiome helps set our baseline inflammatory ‘immune tone,'” she said. “That could potentially be relevant well beyond vaccination.”

Participants took their own temperature three times a day for two weeks, before and after their shot, and wrote every reading in a diary. The diaries came to 7,079 readings from 171 people.

The median was 97.7°F (36.5°C), which is below the 98.6°F (37°C) most of us were taught to expect.

Instead of using a clinical cutoff, the team compared each person against their own normal for that hour of the day.

Anyone whose temperature climbed 1.4°F (0.8°C) or more above their own baseline counted as fever-high. That line left 72 people above it and 99 below.

People who said earlier doses had given them a fever ran hot here too. The two groups also differed on a stool test for gut inflammation. This was higher in the fever-high group two weeks after the shot and tilted the same way beforehand.

Many gut bacteria grow whip-like tails to move around. Those tails are built from a protein the immune system treats as a sign that bacteria are on the move. The protein is called flagellin.

The team read the RNA in each stool sample to see which bacterial genes were switched on. In the fever-high group the flagellin genes were running harder, before the vaccine as well as after. Those gut microbiomes carried a wider variety of flagellins to start with.

One genus accounted for most of that. Waltera, a member of the Lachnospiraceae family, made 67.2 percent of the flagellins that were more active in fever-high people. The family as a whole made 76.3 percent.

That genus was also the only one reliably more common in the fever-high group.

The same thing turned up in a second group of people. Among 1,014 Canadian adults in the CHILD Cohort Study who answered questions about side effects after a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, the ones who reported any had more Waltera in their stool.

Measuring flagellin isn’t the same as showing it does something, so the team ran two experiments.

First they grew human colon cells in a dish and bathed them in stool extracts from the participants. Extracts from fever-high people pushed those cells to release more of an inflammatory signal, and the amount tracked how much active flagellin each sample held.

Then they transplanted human microbiomes into germ-free mice and vaccinated them. A mouse with an inflammatory reaction doesn’t run a fever. Its temperature drops instead.

Mice carrying a fever-high person’s gut bacteria dropped further, and how far they dropped tracked the fever that person had run.

Mice bred without the two receptors that detect flagellin still reacted, though less, so flagellin carries part of the signal and something else carries the rest.

The animal work was done at Georgia State University, and the gut cells and the additive cultures were handled with Institut Pasteur in Paris.

Diet changed the same thing. In culture, the common thickener carboxymethylcellulose helped Waltera grow, and raised the amount of inflammatory flagellin it made.

In tanks seeded with stool from two donors, that thickener on its own barely shifted those bacteria. A mix of it and 13 other additives of the kind that go into ultra-processed foods raised both the bacteria and the flagellin they made.

Mice fed a Western-style diet, high in fat and low in fiber, reacted harder to the vaccine than mice on standard chow. Their temperatures dropped further, and a day later they carried more of a second inflammatory signal.

Among the 171 people, 46 said they ate a plant-based diet. Temperatures rose less in that group than in the 125 omnivores.

The gap held even after the team adjusted for gender, age, body mass index, dose, fever medication, earlier fevers and starting antibody levels.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most, Ley pointed at how fast diet changed what the bacteria produced in the laboratory. “That gives us a plausible way in which diet could influence inflammatory immune tone through the microbiome,” she said.

None of this reached the part of the vaccine response that protects you. Fever-high participants carried somewhat higher antibody levels before and after their shot, but the booster lifted both groups by the same amount,. Diet made no difference either.

The mice ended up in the same place. Germ-free animals given fever-high or fever-low microbiomes finished with matching antibody levels, and the ones that cannot detect flagellin made antibodies anyway.

“This suggests that the mechanisms that make someone feel ill after vaccination are at least partly distinct from those responsible for generating protective antibodies,” Ley told Earth.com.

The human diet data are the thinnest part of the work. Participants picked from broad categories rather than filling in a food diary, and Ley said as much herself.

“But the dietary findings in people are much more preliminary: we only have broad, self-reported dietary categories, so we cannot conclude that diet caused the differences in fever that we observed,” she said.

The group was narrow by design. Everyone was a healthy adult between 18 and 40, with a median age of 25 years. Anyone immunocompromised, living with a gut disorder or recently on antibiotics was left out.

Huus and her co-authors wrote that their definitions of fever were built for research and may not meet the standard for a clinical diagnosis.

Ley said she wants to know first whether the same pattern shows up in other groups of people.

“One important next step is to determine whether the relationship between microbial flagellin and vaccine reactions holds across different populations, age groups and vaccines,” she said.

Her team also wants to know whether a person’s inflammatory tone can be shifted at all, which would take a controlled diet trial.

For now, a fever after a shot may say as much about the gut it arrives in as about the shot itself.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

Popular Weight-Loss Drugs May Have a Surprising Effect on Your Brain

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Popular weight-loss drugs may offer benefits that extend beyond metabolism, potentially helping preserve memory and other cognitive abilities.

Medications used to treat obesity and type 2 diabetes may also influence memory and other cognitive abilities. But whether drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide can actually protect the brain remains uncertain, particularly after major clinical trials failed to demonstrate a benefit in patients with early Alzheimer’s disease.

In Novo Nordisk’s EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials, semaglutide did not slow Alzheimer’s progression, even though it produced changes in several biological markers associated with the disease. Researchers from the University of Gothenburg and the University of Copenhagen have now examined the broader evidence in a review published in Science, exploring whether these medications could still help preserve cognitive function in other groups of patients.

GLP-1 medications influence biological processes involved in both metabolism and brain function. Animal experiments suggest they can affect learning and memory, but studies involving humans have produced inconsistent results. Analyses of patient health records have linked GLP-1 treatment to a reduced risk of dementia among people with type 2 diabetes, although these observational findings cannot establish that the drugs directly provide protection.

The potential benefits may differ depending on the cause of cognitive difficulties. People with obesity or type 2 diabetes sometimes experience problems with attention, planning, and mental processing speed. These difficulties are frequently associated with metabolic disturbances and poor vascular health, raising the possibility that treatments targeting these underlying problems could help maintain cognitive abilities.

Stina Börchers, the review’s first author and a researcher affiliated with both universities, believes that identifying the right patients and treatment timing will be essential.

“We need to find out whether these treatments can help people with obesity or diabetes preserve their cognitive abilities, particularly if treatment begins before cognitive problems become established. It is also important to identify which patients might benefit,” says Stina Börchers.

A central unanswered question is whether any cognitive benefits arise from direct effects on the brain, improvements in metabolic health, or both. Better regulation of blood sugar and other metabolic processes could indirectly support brain function, while GLP-1 signaling may also influence neural processes more directly. Separating these mechanisms could help researchers determine which effects are most relevant to preserving cognition.

Christoffer Clemmensen, the review’s senior author and a research group leader at the University of Copenhagen, sees an opportunity to use increasingly advanced obesity medications to investigate these connections.

“Obesity medicines are becoming increasingly sophisticated, offering new opportunities to understand how metabolic regulation influences brain function. By disentangling the direct and indirect effects of these drugs on the brain, we may ultimately be able to develop treatments specifically aimed at preserving cognitive function,” says Christoffer Clemmensen.

The researchers are calling for clinical trials that measure memory and other cognitive abilities from the beginning of treatment. Such studies could help establish whether these medications preserve cognitive function, which patients are most likely to benefit, and whether starting treatment before cognitive difficulties appear makes a difference.

They also caution that potential benefits must be weighed against risks in older or frail patients. Rapid weight loss and declining muscle mass can reduce physical functioning, potentially limiting the overall advantages of treatment. Future studies will need to account for these effects when assessing whether obesity medications can support long-term brain health.

Reference: “Obesity pharmacotherapies and cognition” by Stina Börchers, Giulia Mazzini, Henrik Zetterberg and Christoffer Clemmensen, 5 November 2026, Science. DOI: 10.1126/science.aek9987

At the New York premiere of Focker-in-Law, the singer and actor made her first red carpet appearance with Álvarez since rekindling their romance earlier this year. And if their constant embraces, adoring glances, and kisses were anything to go by, the pair are very much still in the honeymoon phase.

Stylist Law Roach selected a vintage 1990s Badgley Mischka gown for the occasion. The delicate, spaghetti-strapped design featured a pale silver-gray windowpane check, a fitted bodice scattered with tiny beaded flowers, and a voluminous floor-skimming skirt. She accessorized with Swarovski Matrix hoop earrings—a recent favorite—and traded the flipped bob she’s been sporting of late for a sleek micro ponytail, tied with a little ivory ribbon. Makeup was also her go-to style of glowy and highlight-dominant.

Roach is on a particularly impressive archival streak of late. He’s been mining the fashion vaults for Kim Kardashian’s All's Fair press tour, pulling everything from 1990s Dolce & Gabbana to Paco Rabanne couture. Just last weekend, he dressed Anya Taylor-Joy in a spectacularly ruffled spring 1997 Pierre Cardin ensemble for the LACMA Art+Film Gala.

Grande has long been a devotee of fashion’s more whimsical, romantic history. Her love of vintage reached particularly fanciful heights during the Wicked press tours, but the singer has since held firmly to her soft, ultra-feminine aesthetic with 1950s silhouettes and diaphanous fabrics. At one point, her dedication to the look was so pronounced that some wondered whether she was quietly auditioning for a rumored Audrey Hepburn biopic. Roach revealed that he’d bought the Badgley Mischka gown months earlier, drawn to its connection with the era of the original Meet the Parents film.

Álvarez, for his part, kept things considerably more low-key. The dancer and photographer wore a relaxed black blazer over an open-neck white shirt, tucked into dark indigo jeans with rolled cuffs, a leather belt, and brown shoes. Some classic easy-going boyfriend dressing. He spent much of the evening with an arm around Grande’s waist, while she leaned into him, held his hands, and beamed up at him for the cameras.

Of course, the couple’s love story has its own history. Grande and Álvarez first met when he worked as a backup dancer on her Honeymoon Tour in 2015. They dated for around a year before splitting in 2016, and he later received a memorable name-check in her 2018 breakup anthem, “thank u, next.” Almost a decade later, however, things have come full circle. Rumors of a reunion began circulating this summer when the pair were spotted together in Austin, Texas, during Grande’s Eternal Sunshine tour, before more sightings followed in Florida and New York. By August, they were Instagram official, and Grande had debuted a new tattoo reading “I do love you”, seemingly matching a similar inscription on Álvarez’s collarbone. The two were also spotted arm-in-arm around London, where Grande completed her 10-night residency at the O2. She even began rewriting the Ricky lyrics in “thank u, next” onstage. At one Brooklyn show, she sang, “Wrote some songs about Ricky / We always find our way back.” By the tour’s September finale in London, the sentiment had become considerably more definitive: “I’m tryna make him my last.”

More recently, they’ve been enjoying some more ordinary couple activities, including cheering on runners at the New York City Marathon earlier this month. Grande has also enlisted Álvarez for a particularly kiss-heavy R.E.M. Beauty lip-product guessing game on Instagram. The adoration and affection is showing no signs of slowing down.

Between a decade-old romance finding new life and a beautiful dress pulled from the archives, Ariana Grande is making a persuasive case for revisiting the past.

Lei Lei’s Hybrid Doc ‘Vertigo Garden,’ About a Botanist’s AI-Replicated Memories, Lands at Asian Shadows Ahead of IDFA Bow (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Boutique sales company Asian Shadows has acquired “Vertigo Garden,” the new feature from Chinese filmmaker and animation artist Lei Lei, ahead of its world premiere in the Envision Competition at the International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA).

This is Lei’s second feature. IDFA has also nominated it for the Beeld en Geluid IDFA ReFrame Award, which honors creative work with archival material. Related Stories From 'Dil Se' to 'Ramayana': How Indian Cinema Shattered the U.K., Ireland Box Office Glass Ceiling Rajinikanth’s 'Jailer 2' Sets Global Streaming Premiere Date on Prime Video

“Vertigo Garden” unfolds at the Lushan Botanical Garden and follows the life of its botanist. His body is found in the snow and ice at the top of Lushan Mountain, with both body and brain intact. Scientists study the brain, use AI to reproduce his memories and set them loose in a digital world. Through conversation, the film pieces together the botanist’s storied bond with the metasequoia tree and his efforts to communicate with the planet’s memories through these ancient trees.

Lei uses the metasequoia to probe how memory, life and the planet connect, combining documentary, 3D animation and experimental techniques.

“I believe that Asian Shadows and its team truly love the film. They will treat this film like their own child and promote it to meet audiences around the world, which for me goes beyond what is called a business partnership,” Lei said.

In a director’s statement, Lei wrote that he started filming at the garden, in his hometown, with an 8mm camera in 2024, making long-term field recordings there. He credited Chris Marker’s “Sans Soleil” (1983) as the inspiration for the screenplay, and the film uses video letters to stage an imagined correspondence between the filmmaker and the botanist. The statement also set out plans to 3D-scan metasequoia fossils, trunks and the garden’s greenhouse, then move through the digital models with a virtual camera to create effects that resemble a video game. Elsewhere, Lei described training AI to generate footage of the same location again and again, cutting the results into a flickering effect he likened to the work of artist Rose Lowder.

Lei wrote and produced the film with Chai Mi. It is produced by See-Ray Studio Production, the animation and visual art company Lei founded.

Maria A. Ruggieri, head of sales and acquisitions at Asian Shadows, called Lei a singular voice among Chinese filmmakers without any doubt. “His talent as an animator, his search for new representations of China, his love and care for the recreation of lost emotions and history are perfectly assembled one more time in ‘Vertigo Garden,'” she said.

Born in Nanchang, Lei earned a master’s degree in animation from Tsinghua University. He went on to teach experimental animation at CalArts Film & Video School and has been a professor at Tsinghua’s Arts and Design Academy since 2022. His short “Missing One Player” won a Prix Ars Electronica and a Silver Dove at DOK Leipzig in 2015.

His mid-length documentary “Breathless Animals” debuted in the Berlinale Forum in 2019 and was named best mid-length documentary at Iran’s Cinéma Vérité. His first animated feature, “Silver Bird and Rainbow Fish,” premiered in the Tiger Competition at the International Film Festival Rotterdam in 2022.

Founded in 2014, Asian Shadows sells Asian films by Asian directors worldwide. Its recent titles include Eddie Cahyono’s “Ibuku,” which won the Busan Award Special Jury Prize at this year’s Busan International Film Festival, and “The Seoul Guardians,” directed by Kim Jong-woo, Kim Shin-wan and Cho Chul-young, which picked up a Netpac Award special mention in Rotterdam. The company’s catalog also includes Wang Bing’s “Man in Black,” Soda Kazuhiro’s “The Cats of Gokogu Shrine” and Mouly Surya’s “This City Is a Battlefield.”

“Vertigo Garden” is the third Lei film handled internationally by the company, after “Breathless Animals” and “Silver Bird and Rainbow Fish.”

Michael Carrick has officially been sacked by Manchester United after less than six months as the club’s permanent manager, with Mauricio Pochettino expected to replace him.

In a statement released on Thursday, United said that the club “has come to the extremely difficult conclusion that a change is necessary in order to achieve our ambitions” after a “disappointing start to the season” and that they “intend to appoint a permanent successor as quickly as possible”.

United added in tribute to Carrick: “Michael is, and always will be, a Manchester United legend. Just as he did as a player, he has conducted himself with the highest standards of dedication and professionalism throughout his time back with the club.

“His leadership and achievements last season will always be fondly remembered.”

Carrick took over as United’s interim head coach in January after Ruben Amorim’s departure and was promoted to the full-time job in May after qualifying for the Champions League last season.

He has failed to carry that momentum into the new campaign, though, and departs with United mired in the Premier League relegation zone having won only one of their first 10 matches and sitting 17th in the Champions League table. They also exited the Carabao Cup at the third round stage.

A 4-1 defeat by Como in Europe last month left Carrick on the brink and a 3-0 home loss to Bournemouth then initially looked as though it would be the end of the road, but he was spared the sack and retained for the visit to Chelsea, which the Red Devils lost 2-0.

A dramatic 3-2 comeback victory over Roma in the Champions League last week looked to restore some faith - at least temporarily - but a topsy-turvy performance against Aston Villa in a chaotic 4-4 draw, only salvaged at the death by Patrick Dorgu’s last-gasp equaliser, further underlined his side’s significant issues heading into the final international break of 2026.

United parted ways with Carrick following a meeting with director of football Jason Wilcox and CEO Omar Berrada held at Carrington, where, despite the intense speculation over his position, Carrick had continued to take training this week with United’s players not currently away on international duty.

Wilcox and Berrada had been in Monaco on Wednesday for crunch talks with United co-owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe, who controls the club’s sporting policy, where it was decided that a change in the dugout was needed to turn around their fortunes.

Pochettino is expected to replace Carrick and become the new manager at Old Trafford, despite only signing a four-year contract extension to remain in charge of the United States national team following the World Cup on home soil in the summer.

An official approach is believed to have been made to US Soccer, with Pochettino’s deal containing a release clause worth $10million (£7.5m). Pochettino is keen on the move and has spoken to United about their managerial position on multiple occasions in the past, with an agreement over personal terms reported to have been reached.

Former Chelsea and Tottenham boss Pochettino - who has seen off competition for the job from the likes of ex-Newcastle manager Eddie Howe and former Germany coach Julian Nagelsmann - would become United’s eighth permanent manager since Sir Alex Ferguson retired in 2013.

Singapore Airlines Reconsiders New Business Class Seat Design After Backlash

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last week, Singapore Airlines unveiled all-new cabins, with the most exciting developments being a new first class and new business class. The new cabins look great (nothing revolutionary, but a huge improvement), with one exception — the new business class seat colors. Not only do they look cheap, but they just seem completely mismatched to the rest of the cabin.

While the airline vehemently defended the color choices for several days, it seems that they’re at least now reconsidering it, though it remains to be seen if it’s too late to make a change…

On Wednesday, November 11, 2026, Singapore Airlines CEO Goh Choon Phong had a town hall with employees, as reported by The Business Times. During this, he was asked about the negative feedback the airline has received about the new business class seat design.

In response to this, he reportedly said “we’ll look at it, we’ll see what’s feasible.” So that signals some willingness to make changes, though admittedly with how far out seats are ordered, that’s not necessarily so straightforward, so I wouldn’t count on anything happening. He did, however, emphasize that customers will come to like the seats after experiencing its comforts for themselves.

At the town hall, Singapore Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Lee Lik Hsin said this was a good learning point for the airline, but indicated that he hopes customers will mind the colors less after experiencing the seats. He also reportedly said “I suppose, you know, it’s the 80-20 rule, you can’t please them all.”

At the time of launch, Singapore Airlines VP of Product Experience, Ong Wah Li, explained the new design was in response to customer feedback, and described it as “modern, fresh, yet elegant and refined, that’s suited to business travel.”

BMW Designworks was behind the design of the cabin, and CEO Julia de Bono defended the color choices at the launch event, describing them as “more refreshing and more progressive.” As she viewed it, “what I see when I look at it, it’s definitely like a nice suit, with the white and the metal really popping out, you know, it’s more like a suit with a sneaker, so a bit more modern and fresh.”

To state the obvious, the trim and finishes on a new business class seat are hardly the most pressing issues facing the world. However, Singapore Airlines prides itself in being perhaps the most detail oriented airline in the world, because management has an obsessive attention to even the smallest things.

So I can’t help but be a bit confused by all of this:

It also just feels to me like the mandate for the design of this product was off, as shared by the CEO of BMW Designworks. Premium travel demand is through the roof, and a lot of people in premium cabins aren’t traveling for business. For that matter, these cabins will be operating the world’s longest flights, so this isn’t a case of a business traveler hopping on a plane in a suit, and going straight to a meeting (at least that doesn’t make up a meaningful percentage of the traffic here).

Is “suit with a sneaker” really the vibe you want to go for with a new business class product that’s on an airline that’s known for its incredible warmth? It’s almost like the airline views warmth and modernity as being at odds with one another, which I don’t at all agree with.

I would’ve loved to be a fly on the wall when this design was finalized. Was everyone immediately onboard with this, and thought it looked great? Or was there some skepticism, but someone said “trust me, people will love this, this is what a modern cabin looks like?”

I just don’t understand how you can commit to investing in a project that costs billions of dollars, reportedly get feedback from hundreds of customers, and still end up so far off the pulse of public sentiment.

Last week, Singapore Airlines unveiled its new cabins, which is an exciting development. Everything looked pretty great, except the new business class seat finishes, which seem terribly mismatched to the brand. The airline claimed it wanted something modern and progressive, but I’m not sure “suit with sneakers” is really the aesthetic that the public wanted (or that the chosen finishes actually reflect that).

Singapore Airlines leadership claims it’s now looking into possibly updating finishes, but I wouldn’t count on anything coming of that, given how far along this is.
