HOMESTEAD, Fla. (AP) â Kyle Larson is now a three-time NASCAR champion.

Larson clinched his third Cup Series title in six years early in the 400-mile season finale at Homestead-Miami Speedway on Sunday. He became the first back-to-back champion since Jimmie Johnson in 2010; Johnson won five in a row beginning in 2006.

The 34-year-old Larson also became the 11th driver in NASCAR history with three Cup titles, joining a list that includes seven-time champs Richard Petty, Dale Earnhardt and Johnson. Jeff Gordon has four. Lee Petty, David Pearson, Cale Yarborough.

Darrell Waltrip, Tony Stewart and Joey Logano have three.

Larson was the overwhelming favorite to hoist the Bill France Cup in Homestead long before the green flag dropped.

He entered the weekend with a 67-point lead over Christopher Bell and needed only nine points to wrap up the title. He got one point for starting the race and seven more by finishing fourth in the opening stage. But because Bell didn’t win the stage, he had no path to catch Larson the rest of the day.

Larson responded to crew chief Cliff Daniels dropping the news over the radio by saying, “That was anticlimactic.”

Larson put himself in position for his latest title with a historic postseason, dubbed NASCAR’s Chase for the championship. He ended a 51-race winless skid by winning three of the previous nine playoff events and finished in the top 10 in eight of them.

He enjoyed a similar finish while winning it all in 2021, his first season at Hendrick. He won four of the last five races â including the finale at Phoenix Raceway â while still dealing with questions about how his time ended at Chip Ganassi Racing.

His second title came in stunning fashion last year at Phoenix. He took advantage of a late caution â his crew put on two new tires instead of four â and leaped sentimental favorite Denny Hamlin on pit road with two laps to go. Hamlin didn’t have enough time to catch Larson.

Larson’s third title was far less shocking. Securing the title was so much of a slam dunk that Larson was the only Cup driver invited to NASCAR’s championship media day Thursday, leading to a parade event that Larson called awkward.

The biggest question was how Larson and his No. 5 Hendrick Motorsports team would handle the pressure. Larson put that to rest during qualifying Saturday.

“They were debating on if a hot dog is a sandwich and if cereal is a soup,” Larson said. “So I would say they were pretty relaxed. I was surprised by all that.”

A better question: How does Larson compare to the best drivers in motorsports history?

Parnelli Jones, Mario Andretti, A.J. Foyt and Tony Stewart are widely regarded as the best of the best, guys who proved their worth by winning across varying racing series.

Jones won in sprint cars and stock cars around the same time he won the 1963 Indianapolis 500. Andretti won four IndyCar titles, the 1967 Daytona 500 and the 1969 Indianapolis 500. Foyt won four Indy 500s and the 1972 Daytona 500. Stewart won the 1997 IndyCar championship, three Cup Series titles and is currently racing dragsters at the top level, the NHRA.

Larson raced twice at the Indy 500 and has won the Rolex 24 at Daytona, three Chili Bowls and four Knoxville Nationals. And now he’s in elite company with three Cup titles.

SAN FRANCISCO (AP) â Nvidia is opening an investigation into reports that CEO Jensen Huang was the “target of a foreign intelligence effort,” the company’s board of directors said Sunday.

The announcement came after Huang acknowledged to the board that he had been intimate with a business executive whom U.S. intelligence determined was gathering information for Beijing, The New York Times reported.

Huang apologized in a message to the company, according to the Times. “I was unfaithful to my wife,” he said. “My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry.”

Nvidia’s board said in a statement it has “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised,” but that its inquiry would examine all available information. Huang “fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating with it,” the board said.

Huang, 63, will remain CEO during the investigation.

The Times reported last month that U.S. intelligence agencies had discovered that Huang had developed a close relationship with Alice Huang, a Chinese citizen and chip buyer, and determined that she was feeding information directly to Beijing. The two are not related, according to the Times.

Alice Huang was previously a television reporter in China before becoming a top executive at Singapore-based data center company Megaspeed, which has become one of Nvidia’s biggest customers in Southeast Asia. Megaspeed bought more than $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia chips over the past two years, according to the Times.

UCLA stays unbeaten by speeding past Minnesota with a two-play, 150-yard burst by Aidan Mizell

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

MINNEAPOLIS (AP) — Once Ty Dieffenbach saw the Minnesota secondary align and react the way he expected, it was time for the UCLA quarterback to let it fly.

Aidan Mizell was going to run it down. Dieffenbach had no doubt about that. And when the Bruins got the ball back about five minutes later, well, it was time to try it again.

With two 75-yard touchdown passes from Dieffenbach to Mizell on consecutive possessions in the fourth quarter, the Bruins sped away from the Gophers and showed the Big Ten — and the College Football Playoff selection committee — yet another strength of their unbeaten breakout team in the 34-20 victory on Saturday.

"We knew it was going to be a big finding-the-gaps-in-the-defense game, and we just worked all week on the timing," said Dieffenbach, who had a career-high 303 yards and three touchdowns on just eight completions.

Said Mizell: "We knew the whole game we were able to get behind the DB. We were just waiting for the right time to call it."

Mizell, a transfer from Florida who entered the week with only seven receptions all season developed a strong connection during training camp with Dieffenbach while they worked frequently with the backups. Dieffenbach, who took over last month for the injured Nico Iamaleava, quickly realized he could hardly overthrow the 6-foot-1 senior.

"He can really run, so you've just got to put it out there for him," Dieffenbach said.

Bruins coach Bob Chesney said offensive coordinator Dean Kennedy was trying to set up those plays to Mizell earlier in the game, and the strategy clearly worked on the Gophers — undoing more than 47 minutes of solid defense that had the game tied at 17 early in the fourth quarter.

"How we practice is how we play, and we knew by the way we practiced this week it was over with," Mizell said.

On the first one, the Gophers were in a two-high formation with quarters zone coverage underneath. But Mizell caught safety Kerry Brown flat-footed and blew by him on a post route, and safety Zack Harden — who was supposed to be playing deeper — bit on a crossing route in front of him.

On the second one, both Harden and safety Aidan Gousby lurched forward on the run fake by Dieffenbach. That left no safety help for cornerback Aydan West, who was badly beaten by Mizell on a post route from the other side of the field.

"When you have a blown coverage, you're going to get beat," Gophers coach P.J. Fleck said. "We've got to have depth to the defense, and we didn't have it."

Papua New Guinea’s Most Expensive Film, ‘Mussau: The First Song,’ Wraps Shoot, Unveils First Looks (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Indian filmmaker Bijukumar Damodaran directs the $4.5 million period drama, shot on remote Mussau Island and in Goroka, which is eyeing a 2027 festival premiere.

Papua New Guinea’s most expensive film to date, the $4.5 million historical drama “Mussau: The First Song,” has wrapped principal photography after a 40-day shoot across two provinces.

Filming took place on Mussau Island in New Ireland Province and in Goroka, in Eastern Highlands Province. PNG-based producer NAFA Productions celebrated the wrap at a Thanksgiving event in Port Moresby on Saturday. Government figures at the event included Belden Norman Namah, Minister for Arts, Culture and Tourism; Chief Secretary Ivan Pomaleu; and Chief Censor Jim Abani. Related Stories Taylor Swift's 'I Knew It, I Knew You' and 'La Bola Negra's 'La Nieve' Among Nominees for Hollywood Music In Media Awards

Indian director Bijukumar Damodaran, a three-time Indian National Film Award winner, is at the helm. Parul Agrawal produces, and Noelene Taula Wunum is executive producer.

Inspired by a true story and set in the 1930s, the film centers on Taula Livovoa, a young islander who walks away from tribal violence and takes up a faith that is new to him. His choice sets off a sweeping social change that the producers rank among the South Pacific’s most remarkable.

The dialogue is in six languages: Mussau, Emirau, Agarabi, Gadsup, Tok Pisin and English. Some of them are indigenous languages with small numbers of speakers that are at risk of dying out. The producers present the film as a way to protect the country’s linguistic and cultural heritage and bring it to a global audience.

Much of the cast was drawn from the local community, and many are acting on screen for the first time. Some had never watched a film before. Several roles are played by great-grandchildren of the real people whose lives inspired the story. Two British actors round out the ensemble.

Mussau Island has little infrastructure and patchy communications, which made the shoot hard to pull off. The 120-person crew, 20 of them technicians from India, spent 30 hours at sea crossing the Pacific to reach the island. Temporary lodging and facilities were set up there. Islanders helped throughout, including on sequences shot in the sea at high tide. The unit then moved on to Goroka to finish filming.

“This has been one of the most challenging and rewarding journeys of my filmmaking career,” Damodaran said. “The people of Mussau welcomed us into their lives, their culture and their history, and their contribution has been invaluable. Having descendants of the original historical figures portray some of these characters, alongside community members who had never seen a film before, made this journey even more profound.”

Hilda Tanimia wrote the story. The screenplay is by Jennifer Litau, who also served as translator, along with Joses Imona and Damodaran. The cinematographer is Yedhu Radhakrishnan and the editor is Davis Palakal Manuel. Arnold Laena handled production design and Gladice John designed the costumes. The music comes from Music Masters of PNG and the Murat Choir. The cast includes Freddy Sutchiff, Candy Ali, Daniel Lee Hird, Kevin Jackson, Jamie Napo, Calvin, Alan Balalua, Frank Eke and Dolvin Aite.

NAFA Productions wants the film to bow at a top-tier international festival in 2027. It also hopes the film will be Papua New Guinea’s official entry for best international feature at the 100th Academy Awards.

Damodaran has made that run once before. His earlier PNG–India co-production “Papa Buka” was the first film Papua New Guinea ever submitted to the Oscars’ international feature race.

Appalachian Trail Gear: What Worked, What Didn’t, and What I Changed (Part 1)

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I found that, just like in the real world, things break, wear out, and sometimes need to be replaced. Just as you budget for an unexpected car repair, you need to plan for gear replacements on the trail. Your shoes aren’t going to last the entire hike (mine lasted about 500 miles!), and sometimes things break when you least expect them.

Take my sleeping pad, for example. It unexpectedly started losing air, and thankfully, my hiking buddy let me borrow his “trail couch,” a foam sleeping pad, until I made it to Daleville, Virginia. Then there are the upgrades you might choose to make along the way. Maybe you realize you can get by with a smaller, lighter tent and decide it’s worth the investment to shed some weight from your pack.

The point is, preparing for a thru-hike isn’t just about buying all your gear before you start. It’s also about budgeting for the things that will wear out, break, or simply need an upgrade along the way. Just like maintaining a car or keeping up with home repairs, taking care of your gear is part of the journey.

I started and ended with a Base Weight of about 13.5 lbs without food, fuel, or water.

So, let’s look at the gear I started with, what held up, what I replaced along the way, and what I carried to the finish line.

I won’t go into a deep dive on my entire gear list, but if you’re curious about the specifics, you can check out my full gear list that I started with on my profile here and what I finished with on LighterPack here.

What I Started With: Big Agnes Tiger Wall UL2

What I Finished With: Hyperlite Mountain Gear Crosspeak 1

I had plenty of experience with the gear I started with, and honestly, it worked! I loved pretty much everything I owned, so I didn’t see a reason to replace perfectly good gear just to upgrade to the latest and trendiest options. That said, I did budget for a new tent.

Originally, I planned to start with the Zpacks Duplex, a tent my fiancé and I use on our backpacking trips. The only catch? He’s usually the one who sets it up for us. Since this was going to be a solo adventure, I needed to be confident setting up my own shelter. It was the middle of winter in Vermont before my hike, and after a few unsuccessful attempts at practicing on my own in the snow, I just wasn’t feeling confident enough to bring it along.

Instead, I decided to start with my Big Agnes Tiger Wall UL2. At 2.5 pounds, it’s a freestanding tent that’s practically dummy-proof, offers plenty of space, and, most importantly, was something I had tons of experience setting up on my own.

First time pitching my new Crosspeak 1 tent.

I love freestanding tents, but I had my eye on a Dyneema version. The one I wanted wasn’t scheduled to hit the market until April, so my plan was to use the Tiger Wall until it launched. Easy enough, right? Well, then a material issue delayed the release.

Meanwhile, Hyperlite Mountain Gear launched its CrossPeak 1 at Trail Days, and they were offering a deal on it. I didn’t hesitate for a second. I may have downsized on space, but this tent checked a lot of boxes for me and was worth the upgrade.

What I Started With: Gossamer Gear Gorilla 50L

What I Finished With: Gossamer Gear Mariposa 60L

I’ve been a huge fan of Gossamer Gear packs ever since hiking the Wonderland Trail in 2023. In 2024, I invested in the Gorilla 50L for my Tahoe Rim Trail thru-hike, and it turned out to be the perfect pack for me and my needs. So naturally, I figured I’d take it along for the Appalachian Trail. No regrets… at least at first!

The first two months on the AT were pretty dry, and I sometimes found myself carrying up to 4L of water to ‘dry camps’. Resupply options were also more spread out than I expected, which meant carrying five to six days of food at a time. The Gorilla is designed to comfortably carry around 25 pounds, with a recommended maximum of 30 pounds, and I found myself pushing those limits pretty regularly.

My beloved Gorilla started ripping on both sides of the bottom corners.

Eventually, my lower back started chafing from the pack rubbing against it, and the side seams began to split. I was so sad because I genuinely loved this pack! The removable back foam pad doubles as both a frame and a sit pad, which is such a great feature. The side pockets are positioned at just the right height, making it SUPER easy to grab my water bottle and slide it back in without stopping or asking someone for help. And the large exterior mesh pocket? Another favorite.

Rather than give up on Gossamer Gear altogether, I decided to stick with the brand and upgrade to its larger pack, the Mariposa 60L. With a maximum recommended load of around 35 pounds, it seemed like the perfect solution for my heavier carries. I never ended up maxing it out, and it gave me the extra capacity I needed.

Eventually, I also got better at dialing in my resupplies, and my water carries dropped back down to around 2L. But even with the lighter loads, I struggled with the Mariposa for a long time. I’m not entirely sure why, but it felt and sat so differently from my Gorilla. I even had the Gossamer Gear team fit me at Trail Days, yet I still felt like the pack I’d purchased was too small for me.

Over time, it seemed to get more comfortable. Or maybe I just got used to the pain and discomfort. Honestly, I’m still torn about whether the Mariposa will be the pack I reach for on my next thru-hike or if I’ll be looking elsewhere. I loved my Gorilla, but my experience with the Mariposa left me with some mixed feelings about what pack comes next.

Navigating the large boulders on the way up Mama K, featuring my Mariposa.

What I Started With: Enlightened Equipment Enigma 20-degree down

What I Finished With: Enlightened Equipment Revelation APEX 40-degree synthetic

I started the trail March 9th with a 20-degree down quilt, but by the time I reached Pearisburg, Virginia, I was ready to make the switch to my 40-degree synthetic quilt.

For me, the 20-degree quilt just wasn’t the right fit for the entire trail. I tend to run hot, and even early on, I found myself getting too warm at night. I was more than happy to swap it out for the lighter, cooler 40-degree option when I had the chance.

Of course, there were still colder nights ahead, especially once I reached the White Mountains in New Hampshire. When temperatures dropped, I made do with what I had, occasionally pulling my rain pants over my wool baselayer tights for a little extra warmth.

Looking back, I’m glad I made the switch. I didn’t need a 20-degree quilt for the entire trail, and having the option to adjust my sleep system as the seasons and temperatures changed worked out really well for me.

What I used: Therm-a-rest NeoAir Xlite Regular Wide

Good sleep is SO important on the trail. When you’re hiking all day, having a comfortable place to rest and recover at night makes a huge difference. I’ve had this sleeping pad for a few years now, and I absolutely love it.

I originally had the regular size but found it too narrow, especially as a woman and a side sleeper. So, I invested in the Regular Wide, and it’s been perfect for me. This version also has an improved inflation valve, and I find it a bit quieter than the original model and some of the other sleeping pads on the market.

Remember what I mentioned earlier about budgeting for gear repairs and replacements? Well, here’s a perfect example.

5-mile trail NERO, it dumped rain, and many of us called it a day and squeezed into a shelter by 2 pm.

I’m guessing that on my last night before reaching Pearisburg, Virginia, I managed to puncture my sleeping pad. It was pouring rain, and honestly, I was just happy to find a spot, get my tent up, and crawl into something dry. Somewhere in that process, I must have pitched my tent over a stick or something sharp.

I didn’t notice anything was wrong until my first night back on the trail after taking a zero in Pearisburg. My pad was noticeably deflated. I figured it was probably just the temperature change, so I blew it back up and went to sleep. Except I found myself doing that two more times before morning.

I tried all the usual tricks to find the leak. I inflated the pad, pushed down on it, listened for escaping air, and ran my hands all over the surface, hoping to feel even the tiniest bit of air escaping. I even attempted the water-and-soap trick, but no luck. I just couldn’t find the hole.

Thankfully, my trail buddy Viking came to the rescue and lent me his foam sleeping pad until we reached Daleville, Virginia. I was incredibly grateful, but I’m not going to lie, those few nights sleeping on what felt like the bare ground were rough!

When it came time to replace it, there wasn’t much debating what to buy. I loved this sleeping pad enough to purchase the exact same one. Sometimes you find gear that just works for you, and there’s no reason to mess with a good thing.

These trekking poles were a gift from my fiancé, and one of the things I loved most about them was how incredibly lightweight they are. They’re among the lightest options out there, weighing 4.9oz per pole. I was a little surprised that I didn’t see more of them on the trail. Maybe it’s the nearly $200 price tag, or perhaps people are hesitant about investing in carbon fiber poles because of the possibility of them breaking.

I saw plenty of bent poles and broken tips along the way, but guess what? My LT5s survived all 2,200 miles without a single bent shaft or even needing a replacement tip!

Well, almost all 2,200 miles. One of my poles did decide to go on its own solo adventure by leaping into the Delaware River. I had set the pair down while stopping to take a photo at the Pennsylvania–New Jersey state line, and apparently, one decided it had somewhere else to be. I’ll give Gossamer Gear this: these poles do float! Unfortunately, I couldn’t get the attention of any of the paddlers below to help rescue it.

The one pole that decided to stay with me to Katahdin. The other went on its own solo adventure into the Delaware.

I reached out to Gossamer Gear, and they were incredibly kind and sent me a replacement. So, while I technically lost one pole to the Delaware, I was able to finish with a full set.

My one complaint with these poles is the twist-lock adjustment mechanism. It tends to get stuck, and there were plenty of times I had to wrestle with it to get the poles adjusted. Sometimes I’d even hand them over to Jerzy to manhandle them for me!

Despite that one frustration, I was really happy with these poles. They’re lightweight, held up remarkably well over the entire trail, and I’d happily recommend them to someone looking to save weight on their setup.

What I Finished With: Altra Olympus 275 Matryx

I’ve been an Altra fan since 2018. I’ve tried Hoka, I’ve tried Topo, and nothing has quite compared to the zero-drop platform and roomy toe box that Altra offers.

I started the AT with the Olympus 6. I’ll admit, I wasn’t completely sold on this version when I first tried it the summer before my thru-hike. The fit felt a little different from previous versions, and I wasn’t a fan of that odd little heel pillow. But overall, the shoe worked well for me, and I got about 500 miles out of that first pair.

New on the right vs. old pair on the left that I had since Marion, VA, to 501 Shelter in PA.

One of my favorite things about the Olympus is the extra cushioning. When you’re putting in miles day after day, that added cushion makes a big difference. I did experience some heel blisters, particularly along the sides of my heels. Over time, those eventually turned into pretty impressive calluses. As the shoes approached the end of their life, the blisters and foot fatigue became my signal that it was time for a new pair.

That decision came when I stopped at Marion Outdoors to replace my shoes. That’s where I was introduced to the Olympus 275 Matryx. It offered the familiar fit and features I loved, but instead of the heel pillow, it had a built-in gaiter guard.

I still preferred wearing my separate gaiters with these shoes. Although the built-in gaiter guard meant there weren’t traditional gaiter attachment points, I found a workaround. I simply hooked the front of my gaiters onto my shoelaces. The outfitter initially gave me some adhesive Velcro to help secure them, but that eventually peeled off. Surprisingly, the gaiters still stayed pretty well in place without it.

Altra Olympus 275, featuring how I’d attache my gaiters to the shoe laces.

All in all, I went through five pairs of shoes during my thru-hike. I probably could have squeezed the entire trail into four pairs, but I wanted my final 120 miles to be as comfortable and pain-free as possible. My general rule of thumb became replacing my shoes around every 500 miles. If I tried to push them much further, I’d quickly pay for it with blisters and foot fatigue.

After 2,200 miles, I was pretty happy with that system. Your shoes are one piece of gear I’d absolutely budget for before starting a thru-hike. They’re not going to last forever, and waiting until your feet are telling you it’s time for a new pair isn’t always the best strategy!

That covers the gear that carried me through the miles, but what about everything I wore along the way? From socks and underwear to the layers that got me through everything from hot Virginia afternoons to chilly New Hampshire nights, I’ll be breaking down my clothing system in the next post.
