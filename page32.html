Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: Why One Big Meal Isn’t the Real Holiday Health Problem

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

In the classic Thanksgiving comedy Planes, Trains and Automobiles, hapless hero Del Griffith (played by the late, great John Candy) delivers an emotional riposte to Steve Martin’s uptight ad man Neal Page that stands as a beautifully dignified defense of kindness, empathy and self-love in the face of his own perceived shortcomings.

“You want to hurt me? Go right ahead if it makes you feel any better…I could be a cold, hard cynic like you. But I don’t like to hurt people’s feelings. You think what you want about me, I’m not changing. I… I like me. My wife likes me…because I’m the real article. What you see is what you get.”

For many of us around the holidays, particularly after a night of excess, Del and Neal’s exchange could read as our internal monologue – your kinder inner voice battling the internal bully who judges your every overindulgence. The jury’s out on whether the kind voice is ever as potent as Del’s is in that moment. But it should be, because when it comes to your health around the holidays, the bully has usually got it wrong.

The truth is, for most of us, one day of immoderation during Thanksgiving is unlikely to matter in the grand scheme. As such, you would do well to avoid the litany of holiday season health myths out there, many of which seem hell-bent on punishing you for taking a day off:

Like all misinformation, these kinds of medical myths are loud and tend to die hard, but they almost always trade on quick fixes that target the wrong thing.

A 2020 review of 23 observational studies found that most showed significant weight gain during festive periods, averaging roughly a pound and a half. One of the original studies, a landmark NIH study from 2000, found that much of that weight tends to stick around. So the question is not what you ate on Thanksgiving Thursday and how quickly you can “undo the damage,” but rather whether the extended holiday season becomes a two-month period where your healthy routine goes out the window. Technically, feeling good on Black Friday starts on Thursday. A short and light walk after a feast can help smooth out post-meal blood sugar – the earlier the better, ideally within an hour of eating. Staying upright for a couple of hours to help with digestion, particularly if you’re prone to reflux, is also a great idea. If you’re on medication, remember to take it on time. Reuters described Anthropic as the world’s first publicly traded frontier AI company. Reuters identified three existing pressures on AI stocks: Jensen Huang’s departure from Nvidia, momentum behind the anti-data-center movement following Democrats’ sweep of the US midterm elections, and concerns about rising debt loads across the sector.

Goldman Sachs analysts said no single headline drove the retreat. The contrast between Anthropic’s strong debut and the broader retreat separates demand for an individual listing from confidence in the sector as a whole. For private AI companies and their backers, that distinction is consequential: public investors embraced a single offering while reducing exposure to the wider sector, with data-center opposition and debt concerns still unresolved.

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

A common question with low-communication training is whether allowing learners to train independently between synchronizations degrades model quality—especially on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where independent updates could cause expert routers to drift apart.

MaxText provides a native JAX and Flax NNX implementation of both Vanilla and Streaming DiLoCo—requiring no external central parameter servers.

You can enable Streaming DiLoCo in your MaxText YAML configuration or CLI overrides by setting enable_diloco: true and enable_streaming_diloco: true. As a best practice, set both num_diloco_fragments (P) and diloco_sync_period (H) to N_layers + 1 so that exactly one fragment is synchronized per training step (Δh = 1):

In MaxText, diloco.py splits each global batch across the M learners so each learner processes GBS / M tokens per step. To overlap cross-slice communication with TPU computation in Streaming DiLoCo, weight deltas for the scheduled fragment are offloaded to M-way sharded syncer workers running on the learner host CPUs. Rather than routing through a single central coordinator, the host-CPU syncer shards execute a distributed all-reduce across the network in the background to average the fragment deltas while the TPUs continue running inner-loop training steps. Each syncer shard then applies the outer Nesterov momentum update locally and writes the updated fragment weights back to its colocated TPU learner. To dive deeper or launch your first run:

Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText decouples large-scale LLM pre-training from the requirement of a single, monolithic, high-bandwidth fabric. By combining use of an inner and outer optimizer with fine-grained parameter streaming, engineering teams can pool TPU slices across congested datacenter networks or geographically distributed metros—sustaining high hardware utilization without compromising model convergence. Streaming DiLoCo is available today in MaxText v0.2.5 on PyPI, the corresponding Docker images and on GitHub main. Try it today for experimenting with distributed training workloads.

OAuth Consent Phishing: How Malicious Apps Pass MFA and How to Shut Them Down in Entra ID

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at iamdevbox.com

OAuth consent phishing never asks for a password. The attacker registers a multi-tenant application, sends the victim a link to the real login.microsoftonline.com authorize URL, and waits for the user to click Accept. Every step the victim sees is genuine, including MFA, which is why this attack class keeps working against organisations that think they are covered by phishing-resistant authentication. What matters is not who the user is, but what they just authorised an unknown app to do.

This guide covers how the attack works at the protocol level, the Entra ID consent settings that close it, a KQL detection you can deploy today, and the exact commands to clean up after a successful grant. For the related token-theft pattern that abuses RFC 8628 instead of the authorization code flow, see OAuth Device Code Flow Security.

The attacker's app is just an ordinary registration with a redirect URI they control. The lure is a standard authorization request:

From then on the attacker calls Microsoft Graph as the app. The grant is independent of the user's password and sessions, so a password reset does not remove it. This is the same refresh-token persistence problem seen in MFA bypass attacks, except here Microsoft itself issued the token to the attacker's client at the user's request.

Microsoft documents three user consent settings in Entra ID: do not allow user consent, allow consent only for apps from verified publishers requesting low-impact permissions, or let Microsoft manage your consent settings (the default for new tenants, backed by the microsoft-user-default-recommended policy). Check which one your tenant is on before assuming you are protected.

If this returns ManagePermissionGrantsForSelf.microsoft-user-default-legacy, users can consent to almost anything that does not require admin consent. Move to a stricter setting.

This is the strongest control and fits regulated tenants. Pair it with the admin consent workflow below, otherwise users hit a dead end and look for workarounds.

Note the weakness: it depends on the verified-publisher signal, and the permission classification (which scopes count as low impact) is something you must review rather than assume. Mail.Read should never be classified low-impact in a tenant that cares about mailbox confidentiality.

In the Entra admin center go to Enterprise applications > Consent and permissions > Admin consent settings and set Users can request admin consent to apps they are unable to consent to to Yes, then pick reviewers. Users get a request button on the consent prompt instead of a block, and reviewers see the app, publisher and exact scopes before approving. Make sure reviewers know to decline anything requesting offline_access plus mail or file scopes from an unverified publisher.

Microsoft's risk-based step-up consent turns a user consent request that Microsoft flags as risky into a requirement for admin consent. It only changes behaviour when user consent is enabled, and the BlockUserConsentForRiskyApps setting is controlled through the Graph PowerShell beta module with the Privileged Role Administrator role. Treat it as a useful extra layer, not a substitute for restricting consent: the flag is advisory and attackers iterate until their app is not flagged.

Apps nobody on your team can explain are candidates for removal.

Consent phishing succeeds because identity teams spend their effort on authentication strength and treat authorisation as a user decision. Passkeys and number matching do nothing here: the user authenticated correctly and then authorised the wrong party. The durable fix is making consent an administrative decision for anything beyond trivial scopes, and watching the audit log for the exceptions. 📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at nodus-compute.ai

The best SF Compute alternatives for an NVIDIA H100 cluster are Nodus Reserved Compute (Beta) for a quoted reservation from one GPU to a connected cluster, Together AI GPU Clusters for the lowest published short H100 reservation we found ($3.69 per GPU-hour), and Lambda 1-Click Clusters for 16 to 2,000+ GPUs on InfiniBand. Nodus also lists the H100 SXM at $2.60 per GPU-hour for on-demand jobs, which beats every cluster rate here when your work fits on one 8-GPU machine. SF Compute stays the better pick if you want to set your own price on an open orderbook and sell back hours you end up not using.

We build Nodus, so weigh this accordingly. Every competitor fact links to its own page, and every cost is written out so you can redo it with today's rates.

SF Compute sells compute time, not machines. Its docs describe pools that hold a schedule of node-hours, and orders that add or remove time from that schedule. One node is 8 GPUs, and you set a maximum rate in dollars per node-hour when you buy, per its orders docs. The alternatives below sell fixed terms at published or quoted rates.

Prices checked November 19, 2026: Nodus pricing, Nodus Reserved Compute docs, Together AI pricing, Lambda pricing, CoreWeave pricing, SF Compute specs and SF Compute orders docs. CoreWeave's figure is its North America on-demand price for the 8-GPU HGX H100 instance.

SF Compute does not publish a fixed H100 rate. Its pricing page asks you to sign in, and its orderbook API requires a Bearer token. The price you pay is whatever matches your order: buy orders fill if the book has supply at your max rate, and by default are cancelled if it does not, per the orders docs.

So the useful question is not "what does SF Compute cost" but "what rate does SF Compute have to beat". Multiply any per-GPU-hour price by 8 to get the node-hour rate SF Compute quotes in.

How AIClaw 3.12.13 Loads Selected Skills Before the First Model Request

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A skill catalog is useful when the agent needs to discover a workflow. The timing matters when the user already picked one. If the instructions arrive through a tool call, the first model request still has to decide to make that call from the catalog and the user's message alone.

AIClaw v3.12.13 gives an explicit selection a different path. This behavior ships in the tagged release, and the source links below point to that version.

Imagine a mounted skill named release-check and a request like “$release-check summarize these changes.” The relevant instructions can shape the model's first response or tool choice. There is no prerequisite load_skill round trip for the excerpt. If the user just asks for a summary, the model can discover an applicable skill from the catalog and call load_skill as needed.

The kernel matches complete $skill-name references against mounted skills. It appends the selected text to the outgoing request in memory. The stored user message keeps its original text. A retry after context compaction can still carry the selection, and the next turn starts with fresh selection state. See the request assembly and skill prompt code.

The catalog uses an estimated budget of up to 2,000 tokens, reduced to 2% of the configured context window when that yields a smaller budget. AIClaw shortens descriptions to retain more names. If names alone exceed the budget, it can omit entries. An exact reference to a mounted skill still works even if the catalog leaves it out.

Selected text has a separate limit of 8 KiB per skill excerpt and 32 KiB across selected blocks. An excerpt shortened by the byte limit directs the model to load_skill for more detail. These are prompt assembly limits. The catalog uses a token estimate, and supplying instructions gives no guarantee that a model will follow them.

The kernel regressions inspect requests captured by a test model. They cover first-request inclusion, turn scoping, a compaction retry, catalog limits, and UTF-8 truncation. Those checks establish what the kernel sends. They do not measure live-model instruction following, latency, or provider token usage.

Humpback whale song's extra structure vanishes once its repeats are removed

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Earlier this week I reported that the "language-like" Zipf law found in humpback whale song (Arnon et al. 2025, Science 387:649) is matched by a simple second-order Markov chain. Three more pre-registered tests on the authors' own data have now come back, and together they trace the song's extra structure to its source: repetition.

First, a held-out prediction test (does knowing more than the last two elements help predict unseen song?) finds structure in whale song that a second-order chain cannot reproduce. The Zipf statistic could not show this.

Second, the song repeats itself far more than that chain does. Collapsing every tandem repeat (a cell of 1 to 8 elements sung twice or more in a row) to one copy removes 63% of the whale's elements, against 50% for the chains. 35 of the 80 most frequent whale "words" contain an immediate repeat or an x-y-x alternation, such as gr(s)-dws(s) sq sq sq.

Third, the decisive one: with the repeats collapsed, the held-out test finds nothing beyond a second-order chain. In all 8 years, looking 3 to 5 elements back predicts unseen song worse than looking 2 back. The test still had power: it found English words in 5 of 5 samples of the same size. And song made of second-order chains with random repeats added did not fool it (0 of 3), which we had not expected.

The structure behind the whale's "language-like" chunks is phrase repetition, which whale biologists have described since Payne and McVay (1971), plus short-range rules between elements. Because random repeats do not reproduce the signal, it seems to lie in how the whale repeats. One guess, not yet tested: a chain that remembers two elements cannot count to three, so a whale that sings a cell a set number of times would look like longer-range structure. None of this says whether humpback song has meaning.

The graders were published before the jobs ran and re-derive every label, prediction and score from the raw output. Read RESULTS-HOLDC.md on GitHub and the working manuscript.

One whale data set coded by human listeners, each year treated as one recording; one English corpus in phonemes. "With power" means power against structure as strong as English words; weaker long-range order could be missed. The surrogate null leans against detection (every negative control gave p = 1.000), so p values are conservative, not exact. 4 of 7 registered predictions came true in the last test (Brier 0.189).

22 Years Later, the Greatest Quote in TV Western History Is Still Untouchable

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Yellowstone may have pop culture in its grasp, but how soon fans forget about the best Western series that graced television. That honor, of course, goes to David Milch’s Deadwood, the gritty three-season masterpiece on HBO. Milch famously wanted to do a series about Ancient Rome, but when HBO’s Rome went into production, his hopes were dashed. Instead, he delivered the now cult classic series about the real town in the Old West, full of debauchery, crime, and gold prospecting.

If there was one thing that Deadwood was famous for, however, it was the dialogue. The Western has been compared to the likes of Shakespeare, though somehow even more swear-riddled. The controversial quotes have cemented the series into an unmissable classic, though there is one line of dialogue to rule them all.

In a series that is defined by its dialogue, there are many memorable quotes, but only one gets to the heart of the vicious world the series takes place in. Deadwood follows a former Marshal, Seth Bullock (Timothy Olyphant), who wants to start a hardware business with his business partner, Sol Starr (John Hawkes). Deadwood is the new land of opportunity, but Bullock is immediately blown back by the owner of the Gem Saloon, Al Swearengen (Ian McShane). Al swears by the idea that this world is cruel and he will respond in kind.

Deadwood is, of course, a lawless town full of murder, laudanum, and sex work, but all Bullock wants to do is run his small business. Despite his insistence on remaining impartial, his principles can’t help but continue to crop up. Bullock is characterized by these seemingly contradictory personality traits. As principled as he is, he is also about the angriest man in the gold mining town.

This puts him in the crosshairs of Al and all others looking to take advantage of the townspeople. Bullock resists his destiny for quite a long time before it is impossible to ignore any longer. Corruption is so rampant in the town that he has no choice but to take control.

“Fine, I’ll be the f***ing sheriff,” Bullock swears in a character-defining quote. His reluctance not only defines the character but also the complexities of the mining camp as a whole.

Good is very hard to come by down in these here parts. Deadwood becomes so rife with corruption that there is really only one person who can be entrusted to keep the peace. With the profanity that the series is known for and Bullock’s no-nonsense attitude, this quote distills Deadwood into the gold standard for Western television series.

Bullock accepting this new post was also the end and start of a new era. The lawlessness made Deadwood famous, but his acceptance of the post signaled that some civility would be coming to the town. Bullock sets the tone for Deadwood, which is a place in between being civilized and no man’s land. This is the sweet spot for one of the best HBO series, which has always played by its own rules. Seth Bullock began what the series was in earnest and created an iconic character that no one is likely to forget.
