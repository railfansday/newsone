NOAA Just Tweaked Its Winter Forecast. Here’s What’s Changed for Skiers.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) has some encouraging news for skiers hoping for a major rebound after last winter’s dismal snow season, particularly in California and the Southwest.

The agency’s Climate Prediction Center released its latest winter outlook on Thursday, Nov. 19, updating its forecast for the months ahead. The biggest development? The odds of above-average precipitation have increased across California and the Desert Southwest, offering a more promising outlook for a couple of the ski regions that have struggled with snow in recent years.

The new forecast tweaks a few things from its previous iterations. Here’s a cheat sheet, but be sure to read on for more specifics and a regional breakdown:

Finally, NOAA says that the new precipitation forecasts are more reliable than their temperature forecasts this winter, which they attribute to the strong El Niño and an active southern storm track. In other words, there will be moisture, but there’s no guarantee it’ll come down as snow.

The latest outlook favors above-average precipitation across the southern United States, stretching from southwestern Oregon and California through the Desert Southwest and into the southern Plains and Southeast.

For ski country, California and the Southwest stand out. NOAA increased the probabilities of above-average precipitation in both regions compared with its previous outlook. Southern California and southwestern Arizona now have greater than a 60 percent chance of finishing the December-through-February period wetter than average.

That could be welcome news for resorts in the Sierra Nevada, including Mammoth and the Lake Tahoe region, as well as Arizona Snowbowl and New Mexico’s Taos Ski Valley and Ski Santa Fe. The forecast also offers some encouragement for southern Colorado, including Purgatory, Wolf Creek, and Telluride, although it’s far less clear for the Front Range and Northern Rockies.

It’s worth noting here that NOAA is forecasting total precipitation, not snowfall, per se. Whether that moisture falls as snow or rain will depend on temperatures, snow levels, and where individual storms track. Still, a wetter-than-average winter would be a step in the right direction.

NOAA continues to favor below-average precipitation across parts of the northern Rockies. The region also faces an increased likelihood of higher than average temperatures, a combo that’s not music to the ears of resorts in Montana, Idaho, and Wyoming. The Pacific Northwest also faces odds of warmer-than-average temperatures, particularly across Washington and Oregon.

Elsewhere, the outlook favors wetter-than-average conditions across much of the East Coast and Southeast. However, warmer temperatures could complicate snowfall prospects, particularly at lower-elevation ski areas.

This is the latest chapter in a winter forecast that skiers have been watching closely for months. Earlier this summer, NOAA’s increasingly confident projections of a potentially historic El Niño raised hopes for a snowy rebound across parts of the West following last season’s exceptionally poor conditions.

In July, SKI spoke with meteorologists and ski-travel experts about what the developing climate pattern could mean for the upcoming season. The early consensus pointed toward the Southwest, particularly southern Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, and California, as regions with favorable odds for an active storm track. Much of that remains the same.

But there were plenty of question marks, and there still are, namely what will happen in central Colorado and Utah. NOAA’s earlier outlook showed average precipitation for those destinations, and the latest forecast is slightly more encouraging. We still recommend choosing higher elevation ski areas to pad your odds for snow rather than rain.

NOAA’s next seasonal outlook is scheduled for December 17. Until then, skiers will have to keep watching the shorter-range forecasts and hope the promising signals translate into the kind of winter the West desperately needs.

Long-term memory for an AI companion without a vector database: 40 messages verbatim + 15 distilled facts

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I run Lovora, an AI companion service for adults in nine languages. The number one thing people ask an AI companion is "will she remember me?", and "memory" turns out to mean two different things. Here is the design we use, and why.

1. The recent conversation, word for word.

On every reply the model receives the last 40 messages of the chat, both sides, exactly as written. In voice calls it is the last 24, because spoken turns need to be answered faster and the prompt has to stay small.

This layer is what keeps a scene or an argument coherent. A summary would lose the exact wording, the jokes and the thing she promised two messages ago, which is exactly what people notice in a conversation.

Every 5 messages the user sends, a separate pass reads the latest part of the conversation and updates a short list of lasting facts: at most 15 short facts, about 1,500 characters. That list goes into the prompt of every later reply, in chat and in calls.

That is the layer that lets her ask about the job interview you mentioned last week, hundreds of messages later.

The distillation pass is instructed to keep facts about the user, never about the character, whose personality and backstory are fixed:

Small talk is discarded. A fact that is contradicted later is updated or dropped, not appended: the list is rewritten, not grown. The character is told to use what she remembers naturally, by asking about it or referring to it, and never to recite it as a list. Reciting is the fastest way to make a companion feel like a database.

A small fixed budget forces the distillation step to decide what matters, which is exactly the judgment retrieval skips.

The verbatim window is the expensive part: 40 messages is a real share of the context on every reply. The fact pass is cheap: it runs once every 5 user messages, as a separate call on a short excerpt.

If you are building anything conversational (support bots, tutors, companions), I would start here: a verbatim window sized to your latency budget, plus a small, rewritten list of facts about the user. Retrieval can come later if you ever need it.

In a Midtown Office, Artists Sift Through the Junkspace of Capitalism

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An intergenerational group of artists confronts technology as both a pathogen and a cure for today’s cultural ailments in the husk of an office at 260 Madison.

A few hours before 260 Madison opened on September 24, I trekked to its namesake address in Midtown to meet the curators. A security guard directed me to the 14th floor of the 1950s office building, where drop ceilings, an exposed HVAC system, and two people I assumed were the exhibition organizers dressed in corporate drag greeted me. They quickly clarified they were not; they were from AmTrustRE, the real estate company that offered the derelict 23,000-square-foot office space to the artists for free while preparing it — and three other unoccupied floors — for their next corporate tenants.

The three artists-turned-curators, Shaina Tabak, Dayana Matesheva, and Edison Niebla Rodríguez (the latter two of whom make up the duo DemonLovers Inc.), organized 260 Madison as an intergenerational, “inter-everything” show with work by over 50 artists, including self-taught and non-traditionally educated practitioners. On view through this Sunday, November 22, the show arrives just as the Whitney Museum of American Art opens artport: A History of Internet Art, signaling a new wave of institutional recognition for internet-grown art. With 260 Madison artists Maya Man, Rachel Rossin, and Eva and Franco Mattes among those included in the Whitney’s 25th-anniversary exhibition for its net art commissioning platform, the Midtown show feels especially urgent, if only because it takes place at the precipice of a paradigmatic shift within the art world.

Upon entering the first room on the left, I was immediately met with artist Jon Kessler’s towering nine-foot-tall sculpture, in which four plastic male dolls in Adidas tracksuits dangled from a weighted contraption somewhere between a carnival ride and a torture device. Titled “It Takes a Global Village Idiot” (2018) after philosopher Marshall McLuhan’s early ’60s theory that information-age technology “recreates the world in the image of a global village,” the sculpture forces viewers to confront the fact that the quick fixes of amusement proffered by technology rely on the same methods of data extraction that undergird systemic violence. It’s an unsettling introduction to a show where, even in its more humorous, delusionally conspiratorial, or enchanting moments, discomfort is always in the periphery.

“We don’t want to make a show or art that tries to be some form of escapism,” Rogríguez told Hyperallergic during an interview the week after the opening. “It’s like the [Werner Herzog] quote, ‘The poet must not avert his eyes.’ If some art in the show seems depressing, it’s because we live in what seems a depressing time.”

Rodríguez and Matasheva, who are also a couple, began making work together seven years ago while studying film in Montreal. Their video-art duo is named after Olivier Assayas’s 2002 film Demonlover, where multiple global corporations compete for a new immersive Japanese hentai company through extreme methods of espionage, exploitation, and blackmail.

“DemonLovers is about the libidinal economy and how algorithms are weaponizing our very basic instincts and desires,” Matasheva told Hyperallergic, echoing Rodríguez’s interest in what he calls “financial Freud,” or the psychoanalytic dimension of digital capital flows.

Both DemonLovers’s own two works in the show and those they curated certainly don’t shy away from the degradation associated with extractive economies, be it of the self, physical infrastructure, or the proverbial global village. Nearby Kessler’s work, Theresa Tomi Faison’s “The Long Tail” (2026) climbs up one of the floor’s load-bearing columns. Across the curved sculpture’s six screens, images of memes, forum screenshots, and phrases ruminating on contemporary capital flow all accumulate and dissolve downstream. The river of digital spam ends at the viewer’s feet, inculcating the audience, but also the space itself, which the curators characterize as shaped by "junkspace,” which architect Rem Koolhaas describes as the fallout that “remains after modernization has run its course.”

Among works that are either readymades or play with the form’s history — including Anthony’s Discenza’s windowsill pile of unopened credit card and loan offer letters in “Offers” (2026) and Eva and Franco Mattes’s distended taxidermied cat, “Longcat” (2026) — the corporate detritus can be difficult to parse from the artistic interventions. At one point, I mistook a few square feet of wallpaper with the Champion logo for a new work. The curators clarified during a later conversation that it was actually kept from the office’s previous tenants, HanesBrands, which owns Champion.

Having also inherited the space’s trove of gutted bathrooms, broken drywall, and stripped concrete floors (in short, its junkspace), the curators said they view 260 Madison through “layers of site responsiveness” more than anything. With some works, that connection emerged during the eleventh hour of installation. With others, the site occasioned the work itself. Ivana Dama used exposed air vents for “The Air Was Already Here” (2026), a sound work that channels air through reeds, transforming the invisible, inaudible rhythms of the space into what the artist describes as a “sculptural presence.” Pictures Generation artist Matt Mullican took a 47-foot span of unused wall space as an opportunity to reflect on his five-decade practice of constructing, deconstructing, and classifying systems of representation and perception with largely nonverbal symbols.

“He spent three days here drawing his entire practice on the wall, right from the beginning to the end,” Matasheva said regarding Mullicant’s monumental graphite piece, “Representing The Work” (2026). “He said he will give a lecture about it and that it's a very important work for him. It's kind of a conclusion of his practice.”

Meanwhile, the show’s video works are lodged into broken columns, ceiling alcoves, liminal backrooms, bathroom plumbing, abstract paintings, and even a hanging pink punching bag, a veritable reunion of the last decade of post-internet art movements. Alumni from Joshua Citarella’s publishing platform Do Not Research, the New Museum-affiliated Rhizome, and the traveling video and film screening series Open Secret, including Faison, Dana Dawud, Yehwan Song, and Ruba Al-Sweel, have contributed works. Many of them feature themes of surveillance, internet-induced conspiracy, and social media performance, as well as the technological, often AI-abetted methods of production that have come to define such enclaves.

Yet, for all the immateriality and speculative knowledge that flows through these works, 260 Madison equally plumbs an idea of technology with a long, craft-driven history. Tabak, a sculptor with a background in furniture design, is the driving force behind this facet of the show — and the currents of divination, enchantment, and even sublimity that come with it.

“My practice is very handmade and material-focused, so I always know that that’s what I would contribute to the curatorial vision,” Tabak told Hyperallergic. “And just being in Midtown, you shouldn’t separate a show from what’s happening around it.

Tabak was particularly interested in curating works that “blur the line between the hand and technology,” inviting artists who “haven’t had a lot of chances to show in art contexts, whether it’s because they are in the design world or have nine-to-fives” to participate in the exhibition. She explains that many of them complicate the very notion of technology and its use, citing Brian Oakes’s “Vessel 9” (2025), which gathers 3D-modeled circuit boards into a suspended “techno-Gothic” chandelier, and Ryan Decker’s “The Wavemaker Falters” (2026), which uses a motorized, rudder-like instrument to swoosh water through a raised vessel resembling a spaceship.

“We view technology not as something that has arrived in the 20th century, but something that has made humanity possible,” Matasheva said, expanding on Tabak’s comment. “Language is a technology, a chisel is a technology, everything is a technology.”

While many of the show’s craft-driven works conjure the anthropologist Alfred Gell’s idea that art is a technology that enchants, and in turn, becomes enchanted, others wield technology in the service of care. Kylie Mitchell repurposes a light-seeking solar robot and discarded electronic parts to move a found bird’s nest and egg toward a life-sustaining heat lamp. A few dozen feet away, Gregory Kalliche’s installation “Black-tailed gull nest; Ring-necked Pheasant Nest; Mallard Nest” (2016) suspends an LED light over three metal folding chairs printed with images of bird egg nests. Ben Gould’s “Ark II” (2026), on the other hand, augments a hospital gurney with eight aluminum armatures that hold fresh red roses inside coil-shaped glass vials. Doubling as a literal manifestation of Shakespeare’s “this mortal coil” phrase in Hamlet, the sculpture’s sterile infrastructure butts against the transitory visual comfort proffered by its flowers, mirroring a healthcare ecosystem wherein privatization, optimization, and tech automation have whittled medicine’s promises of care, healing, and empathy into mere aesthetic veneers.

It’s no coincidence that many of the works in 260 Madison position technology as both a pathogen and cure for today’s cultural ailments. In fact, when the real estate developers initially contacted Columbia University’s School of the Arts, it was to invite artists to “activate” a 36,000-square-foot gutted Duane Reade on the building’s ground floor. The developers eventually retracted their offer and moved the exhibition to the 14th floor, citing ongoing construction. Meanwhile, the original plan for 260 Madison to occupy the husk of the Duane Reade will be realized in a different form next month. Now serving as the building’s new temporary lobby, the former pharmacy will play host to a different group exhibition curated independently by Tabak. Opening December 3, HOW I FEEL will feature artists working across sculpture, social architecture, photography, painting, performance, and programming, including Jessica Vaughn, Quay Quinn Wolf, André Magaña, and 260 Madison’s Grayson Cox and Luke Murphy. How the forthcoming Duane Reade show contends with this moment of tech-induced economic entropy, so intense that even an entity as extractive as a real-estate company cannot commit to its own speculations, remains to be seen.

How to Run Long-Running AI Agents in Production (2026): Runtime Limits, Durable State, and Surviving Restarts and Outages

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at controlplane.com • AI-assisted

Long-running AI agents need three things that serverless request handlers do not give them: a process with no short execution cap, state checkpointed outside the process so a restart resumes from the last step, and presence in more than one location so a provider outage does not stop the agent. Most teams find these gaps after a prototype works in development. In production it dies mid-run when a function times out at 15 minutes, a deploy kills the container, or a zone goes offline.

Disclosure: Control Plane publishes this guide.

Long-running AI agents fail in production when the infrastructure treats them like short-lived request handlers.

Production agents need a runtime that does not stop them after a few minutes, a state layer that survives process death, and enough redundancy to stay up through infrastructure failures.

Execution limits vary widely, and hitting a cap mid-run loses work unless a durable layer sits on top.

Short-lived serverless functions need a durable execution layer on top, and long-running container runtimes still need graceful shutdown and external state.

The right state layer depends on your existing infrastructure, how complex the workflow is, and how long the agent needs to pause.

A team that already runs Postgres can add durability with DBOS or the LangGraph Postgres checkpointer and little new infrastructure. Complex multi-day orchestration with many branches fits Temporal. The OpenAI Agents SDK documents integrations with Temporal, Restate, DBOS, and Dapr for long-running agents, and the OpenAI Responses API background mode runs long requests that are polled or delivered by webhook. A single cloud’s durable service keeps agent state inside that provider’s failure domain, so a provider outage stops the workflow along with the agent.

Eleven failure modes regularly stop production agents, and each has a control that prevents lost work.

Every control depends on state living outside the process and outside any single failure domain.

Graceful shutdown, rolling updates, multi-location replicas, and checkpoint resume work together.

When a deploy, scale-in, or node drain happens, the orchestrator sends SIGTERM to the container. The agent should catch it, stop picking up new steps, and complete or checkpoint the current step. A preStop hook runs before SIGTERM, and its time counts against the termination grace period, which defaults to 30 seconds on Kubernetes.

Rolling updates keep capacity during deploys by replacing replicas in batches (on Kubernetes, by default no more than 25% unavailable at once) and waiting for each new replica to pass readiness probes. A PodDisruptionBudget limits how many replicas are down at once during voluntary disruptions such as node drains; it does not throttle a Deployment’s own rolling update.

Multi-location replicas protect against zone, region, and provider outages. If the agent runs in two locations and one fails, the other keeps serving. Checkpointed state must live in a store the surviving replicas can reach, such as a Postgres database replicated across regions. Asynchronous replication can lose the latest checkpoints on failover, so the resumed step must be safe to repeat, and leases belong on one strongly consistent primary at a time. When that primary’s location fails, promote a replica in a healthy location and advance the fencing epoch so writes from the old primary’s era are rejected.

Resuming from checkpoints ties these together. A replica in a healthy location loads the last checkpoint and continues. Steps whose completion was recorded are skipped, the interrupted step runs again, and idempotency keys let that retry run without duplicate side effects. Routing users to the nearest healthy region covers the traffic side.

Bitcoin miners face a new profitability cliff as difficulty heads for an 8.5% jump

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin miners face shrinking revenue and tighter electricity margins as a projected difficulty increase threatens less efficient machines.

Bitcoin miners’ expected dollar revenue for the same computing power fell about 6% from Nov. 16 to Nov. 20 in two readings of Luxor’s revenue index.

A difficulty cut had lifted expected Bitcoin earnings earlier in the month, but with the next adjustment now tracking higher, less efficient machines could soon need cheaper electricity to cover their power bills.

Simple Mining’s Nov. 20 reading of Luxor’s hash price index put gross mining revenue at $28.58 per petahash per second (PH/s) per day, with Luxor’s Nov. 16 roundup listing $30.45.

To estimate a machine’s gross daily revenue, multiply the hash price by the machine’s computing power in PH/s. One PH/s equals 1,000 terahashes per second.

The slide also appeared in CloudMineCrypto’s daily series, which estimates revenue from Bitcoin blocks. In its partial Nov. 20 reading, dollar hash price had fallen to about $28.90 from $30.30 per PH/s per day on Nov. 16, while the coin rate held near 0.00047 BTC.

The paired readings explain why a miner can earn roughly the same amount of Bitcoin for a day of work and still have fewer dollars to pay an electricity bill.

Bitcoin’s Nov. 14 adjustment lowered difficulty by 9.13%, from 148.36 trillion to 134.82 trillion. Holding the reward per block steady, the same amount of work could then earn about 10% more Bitcoin. Luxor’s Nov. 16 roundup found that the BTC hash price had risen from 0.00042763 a week earlier to 0.00046839 BTC per PH/s per day.

Over the same period, the dollar hash price fell from $31.95 to $30.45.

The expected coin payout depends on the block subsidy, transaction fees, and how hard it is to find a block. The dollar payout also depends on Bitcoin’s price, since fees accounted for only a small fraction of block rewards in both data sets.

Luxor reported a weekly average of 0.0229 BTC in fees per block on Nov. 16, and the partial Nov. 20 data imply about 0.022 BTC in fees on top of the 3.125 BTC subsidy.

As of Nov. 20, MineOrNot counted 1,008 blocks, halfway to the next adjustment. Blocks were arriving about every nine minutes and 14 seconds, compared with Bitcoin’s ten-minute target.

If that pace persists, MineOrNot estimated difficulty would rise 8.47% to about 146.24 trillion around Nov. 27.

If Bitcoin’s price, the subsidy, and average fees per block stay fixed, an 8.47% rise in difficulty would lower the $28.58 benchmark to about $26.35 per PH/s per day. A 10% increase would put it near $25.98 and bring difficulty almost back to its pre-cut level.

For a machine using 30 joules per terahash, the observed benchmark translates to a power-only break-even rate of 3.97 cents per kilowatt-hour.

With an 8.47% increase in difficulty, the same machine’s ceiling would fall to 3.66 cents. A machine using 13.5 joules per terahash can absorb higher electricity rates, with its ceiling falling from 8.82 cents to 8.13 cents under the same assumptions.

Is Early-Season Skiing Worth It? Here’s the Case for Going.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

There’s a lot to love about skiing: being outdoors, pushing yourself to improve, finding your flow, and being part of ski culture. But too often, the things that make skiing great get overshadowed by cost and crowds. It’s hard to find your flow when the run is crowded, and even the best ski day is hard to enjoy when lodging, lift tickets, and lunch leave you financially stretched.

The solution is early-season skiing. Few people book a ski trip in November or early December, and even local skiers tend to be unenthused. But I think they’re missing out. In fact, early season is one of my favorite times to ski. Early season turns have led to breakthroughs in my technique, and it’s one of the only times when the mountain feels small enough to meet up with friends.

Plus, everything that makes skiing great still stands: you’re outside, you’re improving, you’re dipping into your flow state, and you’re more a part of ski culture than if you were to come for President’s Weekend like everyone else.

And the things that make skiing less appealing are non-issues.

The cost of an early ski season trip can be half or even a third of what you’ll pay just a few weeks later. In Jackson Hole, for example, if you visit in early December, rather than Christmas week, you’ll pay $85 for a lift ticket instead of $340. Similarly, a room at the ultra-luxe slopeside Hotel Terra Jackson Hole increases by $600 a night based on whether you stay in early or late December. The same goes for big ski weeks like spring break and Presidents’ Day—and the weeks in between aren’t much cheaper.

And then there’s the fact that when you’re not skiing at the same time as everyone else, every aspect of the day (or your entire trip) becomes more enjoyable. You’ll get in more skiing thanks to shorter (or nonexistent) lift lines, and you don’t have to worry about running into someone on the way down. And when you aren’t one of 10 people waiting in line for rental gear or a table at the restaurant, you’ll get warmer, more personalized service. (Plus, in early season, everyone is fresh and excited for the season ahead, not tired and over it.)

When the mountain is fully open, and everyone’s ski legs are firing, the pressure to go big is real. It can be difficult and dangerous to try to keep up, especially if you’re new to skiing or it’s your first (or only) ski trip of the year.

But in the early season, everyone is still getting their ski legs under them, and the skiing is more about fine-tuning your technique and rebuilding muscle. There’s nothing showy about it, and most people leave their egos at home.

The biggest argument against early season skiing is the lack of snow. Indeed, in the early season, the snow hasn’t had time to accumulate. Often, only the lower parts of the mountain are open, and sometimes only thanks to snowmaking. It’s not the time to do a hike-to, and you’ll rarely find any powder, so show up with your expectations (and ego) in check.

But if you’re willing to rethink what a ski day should look like, you can enjoy everything that makes skiing great while avoiding its biggest drawbacks.
