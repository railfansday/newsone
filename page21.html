Pixel phones getting new icon styles, Create My Widget, and more in November Pixel Drop

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Spruce up your home screen with expanded custom icons and AI-generated widgets.

Google’s Pixel phones get an update every month, but most are just security patches and bug fixes. The bigger changes are bundled into quarterly Pixel Drops, and one is rolling out today. The update brings a few new things, and many of the Gemini features previously exclusive to the latest Pixel phones will also expand to Google’s Pixel 10 lineup.

The Pixel 11 was Google’s first foray into “Gemini Intelligence” branding, something we’ll see a lot more of going forward. Some of the features under that heading were limited to Google’s newest phones (and in some cases, Samsung’s phones). The November Pixel Drop will expand these AI features to the Pixel 10 lineup, but importantly, that doesn’t include the budget-oriented Pixel 10a.

For starters, the Pixel 10 series will get the improved personalized suggestions (previously known as Magic Cue) that debuted on the Pixel 11. This enables things like sending grocery lists to Google Keep from other apps and generating summaries from the context menu. Rambler, Google’s AI-assisted voice input system, is also coming to the 2025 phones. Likewise, Gemini app automation will roll out on Pixel 10 with this update. This feature is still limited to select apps in rideshare, food delivery, travel, and a few other categories.

The AI-powered icon theming that Google added to Pixels back in March is also getting an update. Initially, this feature just matched icon colors to the system theme when developers didn’t include the necessary theming assets. With this update, Google says it has moved the feature to a more powerful Nano Banana model, unlocking 10 new icon styles for your home screen.

The update also brings Create My Widget to Pixel phones. Google announced this earlier in 2026, and it shipped on Googlebooks a few weeks ago. The idea is that you describe a home screen widget you want, and Android builds it for you on the spot. However, the use cases are pretty narrow. You’re limited to data accessible in search (e.g., stock prices, sports scores, and weather) and select Google account data points like Gmail or your calendar.

So that’s a lot of AI. Google does have some non-AI additions with this update, but don’t get your hopes up. One of the headlining additions is (drumroll) HiLight support for Google Messages. Yes, Pixel 11 phones will be able to illuminate HiLight when you get a message from important contacts. Ignoring that we had more functional notification LEDs 20 years ago, this is a good and necessary improvement for Google’s least useful hardware feature.

If you have a Pixel Watch, Google says this update will also improve your workout routine. The Pixel Watch 3 and newer will be able to display step-by-step guidance for your workouts. The wearable can also log reps and weights as you go without interrupting live metrics. While Create My Widget is not yet available on the watch, Google promises it’s also coming soon.

The update is beginning its rollout today and should be available widely in the coming days.

Report - It's over: Liverpool finally ready to call Richard Hughes signing a failure

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Liverpool are reportedly looking to replace one Richard Hughes signing. They've made an approach and are ready to rule the deal a failure.

Richard Hughes did a lot of big-money business in charge of Liverpool. He essentially oversaw a complete squad restructuring as the Reds looked to overhaul Jürgen Klopp's group and move onto something new.

Now did that work? No. Was it a good idea? No. Did the job even get completed? No, again.

Liverpool have unquestionably gone backwards since Klopp left. Their squad makes use of virtually none of the youngsters who were being brought through in 2024, while the squad also looks very imbalanced.

The reason? Most of the signings just haven't worked out. There are many, many reasons for that but sometimes they're just the wrong player. And Liverpool are willing to accept failure on at least one of them.

Whether that deal happens or not, it's a sign of the thinking at Anfield. They wanted a new right-back, just 18 months after Hughes signed Jeremie Frimpong for the role from Bayer Leverkusen.

It was a deal that always raised eyebrows. Frimpong was a right wing back in Germany and never really showed an ability to defend in a back four. He was also the sole wide player for Leverkusen and that was never going to be the case at Liverpool.

So the Reds were really asking him to be completely different. Shockingly, that didn't work and Frimpong has struggled to establish himself at the club, ultimately becoming a failed signing - despite a ridiculously unfair situation for him.

Liverpool are apparently trying to do that and it's a sensible move. Even if it does mean giving up on Frimpong.

6-Part BBC Spy Miniseries Is So Good, They Revived It After A Decade

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Between Sherlock, Peaky Blinders, and Luther, the BBC has produced plenty of wonderful dramas that have made a global impact, but one 6-part miniseries was so good, it was revived 10 years after its first season. In an era full of lengthy waits between seasons, fans have become used to shows being off-air for several years, but not a whole decade.

However, fans of The Night Manager had to wait that long for their beloved project to return. Despite an impressive first outing, the BBC spy thriller looked destined to stay a limited series as the years passed, but The Night Manager finally came back for season 2 in 2026, making it even more popular than before.

The adaptation of John le Carré's novel stars Tom Hiddleston as former soldier and undercover agent Jonathan Pine attempting to infiltrate an arms ring led by Richard Onslow Roper (Hugh Laurie). While season 1 was a classy affair that saw Hiddleston play a James Bond-like role in The Night Manager, season 2 proved to be much darker.

That elegant presentation and unquestionable charm remained, but there were moments that no one saw coming, completely changing the entire narrative.

Although the show underwent a lengthy absence, this gap proved valuable, as it was picked up for season 3, which is already in development. As a result, The Night Manager has become one of the best spy thrillers around, and while it already had a strong legacy as a limited series, it's now a mainstream hit that keeps on getting better.

While the BBC could've easily just left The Night Manager as the perfect one-season show, it was so impressive, it simply had to be revived 10 years after it started. Considering le Carré never wrote a sequel, it's possible there was apprehension about bringing the series back, since this time around, it would have to be an original story.

However, season 1's ratings were fantastic, earning a 91% critic score and an 89% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes, highlighting just how much people enjoyed the project. All six episodes were incredibly consistent, capturing that tense espionage feel from the book with arguably even better pacing, capped off by countless stellar performances.

Hiddleston and Laurie are the standouts as expected, but with names like Olivia Colman, Tom Hollander, and Elizabeth Debicki all involved, it's no surprise the characters felt so memorable. Viewership was also strong during the show's initial run, and it never seemed to fade into obscurity, especially when finding an international audience.

Everything about the first season went smoothly, which made the wait for season 2 more understandable, as the BBC clearly wanted to bring back its main stars and get things right. It's rare to see studios show this kind of restraint rather than cash in right away, but this only speaks to The Night Manager's quality, which undoubtedly left a lasting impression.

Both seasons of The Night Manager are available to watch on Prime Video in the US.

Letting the series end how it did 10 years ago would've still been effective, cementing it as a great book adaptation. Fortunately though, the powers that be recognized its popularity and allowed it to become a huge project like it was always destined to be, making The Night Manager a must-watch for any spy thriller fans who haven't yet seen it.

As a completely fresh story, The Night Manager season 2 does a great job of continuing on from the first and maintaining the franchise's legacy. The second entry really ups the intensity, raises the stakes, and provides a genuinely shocking cliffhanger that left fans feeling divided, but despite all that, it still falls short of season 1.

That isn't to say the 2026 return was poor or disappointing; it just had an extremely high bar to clear, and to its credit, season 2 did come close. Alongside introducing more compelling characters, the story was much bolder this time around, bringing back seemingly dead foes, killing off key heroes, and delivering a rather bleak ending that sets up an unpredictable future.

All in all, The Night Manager remains compelling throughout season 2, yet it has a few more notable flaws than the first. The pacing suffers compared to season 1, since the show was clearly brought back with plans for a third installment. Likewise, some of the big twists undo le Carré's original vision, which many viewers weren't too happy about.

If you enjoy realistic spy action, then season 2 shines in isolation, especially if you have an appetite for more. However, those with nostalgia for season 1 and the original novel may have more complaints, explaining why the audience score was much more divisive for this entry.

Still, regardless of whether you loved it or hated it, The Night Manager season 2 broke BBC viewership records and proved it was worth resurrecting. Even if season 2 didn't quite live up to the first, season 3 is in the works, confirming the spy thriller isn't going away quietly as it did 10 years ago, and its next chapter looks very promising.

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy Meets Discworld In This Sci-Fi Comedy Releasing Today

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Cher Thompson is a Senior Staff Writer at Screen Rant, where she covers everything from explosive reality TV moments to cast interviews about the twists and turns of scripted dramas. With a background in creative writing, marketing, and teaching, Cher knows how to spot both the storytelling genius and the guilty pleasures in pop culture. Her work blends wit with insight, making readers feel like they’re chatting with a friend who just happens to know way too much about TV.

While sci-fi novels have never been short of stories that strike a humorous chord with readers, there are a few standouts in the genre that have a lighter appeal than others, like Douglas Adams' The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy or Terry Pratchett's Discworld series, along with this new release. Though many of the elements that come with sci-fi fiction tend to be deeper and darker than the average story, readers often get the trade off that sci-fi novels have a specific sense of humor, one that indulges in chaos to explore a novel's larger ideas. Both Adams and Pratchett bring that irreverent quality to their writing, as does Andrew H. Murray.

Murray, whose novel Everything Happens So Much, debuts today, November 10, has built a world where absurdity and philosophical observations are able to co-exist, in a similar vein to Adams, Pratchett, and some of sci-fi's best. While the combination of serious and silly is often tough to balance, sci-fi authors have long been able to do so with imaginative world building, unconventional humor, and stories that have genuine emotional stakes unfolding as things unravel. Bringing Pratchett's distinctive approach to fantasy and Adams' sense of observation together, Everything Happens So Much is able to create something special.

While Murray's focal point in Everything Happens So Much, Esme Good, may be a fairly ordinary character, the way in which her story takes shape is reminiscent of the greatest sci-fi comedies of all time, bringing the absurdist multiverse comedy to life.

Murray's novel, Everything Happens So Much, focuses on Esme Good, a fairly ordinary young woman whose life has become defined by its pure lack of excitement. Esme's dead-end job leaves her with a standard life in an unremarkable city, and her circumstances leave her looking for more. Hoping for an escape from the bleak monotony of her everyday life, Esme finds herself indulging in The Elsewheres, potions that allow her to disappear from her life into alternate versions of herself for brief 15 minute intervals. The alternate realities allow Esme to feel some sense of excitement, if only for a few moments.

While the idea of alternate realities may be affirming for some, visiting other versions of herself leaves her concerned that she's living her worst possible life. As Esme moves through balancing her everyday with doses of alternate realities, she finds herself at the center of an incident that makes her markedly more important in her own universe, with consequences that ripple out to her alternate realities. Murray's distinctive voice gives Esme's journey, which ultimately finds her in uncharted territory fulfilling a prophecy, an ironic tilt that ensures readers are never entirely certain about what's coming next.

Although there are high-level comparisons to be made between Everything Happens So Much and Hitchhiker's Guide because of their elements of a cosmic journey, the bigger comparisons between Murray's style and Adams' has to do with their shared sense of humor. Murray's moments of absurdity stand out against the larger implications of the story, and while they have shared sensibilities, are vastly different than the ways that Adams' weaves his own absurdist humor throughout Hitchhiker's Guide. Murray, who has openly shared that he was influenced by Adams, is able to take his own style and infuse it with a similar absurdist quality.

Everything Happens So Much isn't just inspired by the humor and the journey of Adams' most famous novel, but also takes Terry Pratchett's Discworld into the mix. With genuine slapstick humor written into Esme's journey, Murray was able to find himself inspired by elements of Pratchett's strange and delightfully funny universe, infusing them into the narrative in his own way. With a combination of alternate reality fiction and a sense of humor that stands out among the more serious sci-fi titles of the current era, Murray's Everything Happens So Much puts the genre on an exciting new journey.

AI’s future starts to look surprisingly old-school: theCUBE’s KubeCon day one keynote analysis

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The cloud-native community that turned Kubernetes into an enterprise standard is now being asked to carry artificial intelligence into production. As the underlying technology looks more alike from one company to the next, the hunt for domain-specific AI value is spotlighting how AI is put to work – and how to do so safely.

That shift is on display this week in Salt Lake City, where the Cloud Native Computing Foundation has added a dedicated AI inference and agentic track to its flagship conference. The plumbing built for microservices is proving to be the substrate for AI factories and agents, along with a renewed focus on tried-and-tested security and business principles, according to John Furrier (pictured, left), co-founder and executive analyst of theCUBE Research.

“The AI-native world that we’ve been covering and following and living — AI factories, the rise of agentic, which involves coding and now tasks — are all based fundamentally on the substrate of cloud-native technologies,” Furrier said. “This community is the heart and soul for the AI generation.”

Furrier spoke with theCUBE’s Rebecca Knight (right) as a part of a keynote analysis at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon NA, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how enterprises can secure and govern AI agents while turning proprietary knowledge and workflows into measurable business value rather than simply maximizing token consumption. (* Disclosure below.)

Kubernetes has matured from a niche orchestration project into the reference platform for major clouds and neoclouds, and enterprises are now standardizing on it. That maturity is what makes it useful for AI, which needs the same control planes, scalable data layers and telemetry the community already worked out for microservices, according to Furrier.

“Kubernetes got boring, which means it’s working,” Furrier said. “A lot of things that are happening in cloud-native that were involved in microservices in Kubernetes world are actually the same challenges in the AI world.”

Those lessons now apply to a pressing question: how much freedom enterprises will give AI agents inside real systems. Agents that cause trouble are usually poorly instructed rather than malicious, and the answer lies in context, identity and guardrails, according to Furrier. Perimeter-style security thinking is expected to make a comeback, along with a move away from token consumption as a measure of value and toward tangible business outcomes.

“We’re gonna get back to an era of, ‘Build something, show me what you built, present it for value,'” Furrier said. “If it’s worth something, there’s an exchange for value.”

Archaeologists Found 2,500-Year-Old Battle Lines From a Conflict No One Knew Ever Happened

The Iron Age palisade likely blocked an invading army. But who built it—and who were they afraid of?

Archaeologists sifting through portions of Denmark’s western peninsula ahead of a highway expansion keep uncovering remnants of the area’s past, often in small snapshots: a grave here, an axe there, or maybe sometimes even the foundation of an ancient home. They certainly weren’t expecting to find the remains of battle lines in West Jutland, indicating a conflict they didn’t know ever transpired.

“The find came as a complete surprise to us,” Michael Alrø Jensen, curator at Museum Vest, said in a translated statement. “It points to previously unknown conflicts in West Jutland dating back to the earliest part of the Iron Age.”

The find showed up via a 2,500-year-old line of pits, which experts believe fighters created during the Iron Age to block hostile incursions along an ancient West Jutland thoroughfare. The discovery came as archaeologists from the museum examined fields along Route 11, between Varde and Korskro, before the Danish Road Directorate began a highway expansion.

Experts dubbed the battle lines, which were found beneath a field near Schaefergården, a “post-hole alignment,” likely from around 500 B.C.E. The lines feature a long series of closely spaced holes in which one end of pointed wooden posts were buried to form a defensive palisade.

“Our best theory is that there was a fear of an invading army arriving from the north via the ‘Drivvej’ route, and that attempts were therefore made to control and block the passage,” Jensen said.

More evidence may still lie buried at the site. Experts note that the current Route 11 alignment between Varde and Korskro matches the ancient “Drivvej,” or Drove Road, that was the main transportation line through West Jutland for centuries. The modern road project adds an additional lane to the two-lane highway and shows how closely the ancient road—whose origin date remains unknown—matches the modern setup.

Still, the find raises questions. Archaeologists and historians don’t know who developed the defensive structure—or why. The team hopes that additional work in the area may offer fresh clues about the ancient mystery.

Attempts to pinpoint answers to a 2,500-year-old question got a bit muddy as the excavations revealed artifacts from a range of eras in spots that hadn’t been searched previously. Archaeologists now have evidence of human activity along the road from the first Stone Age farmers, who buried their dead in small mounds through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and into the Viking Age (including the discovery of a 4,500-year-old battle axe buried alongside a Stone Age warrior).

“What we are most excited about is that we have found traces from so many different periods within a relatively small area,” Jensen said. “We have suddenly gained a wealth of new pieces that provide much greater insight into how people lived and moved about in this region over several thousand years.”

Every new discovery provides nuance to the historical record, whether the remains of a house, farm, or grave. Museum Vest hopes to learn more by excavating additional burial mounds. The various discoveries in the region together form a narrative of humans’ transformation of the land, first into farming plots and then into villages. “Once our work is complete, we hope to be able to offer the local community a more cohesive narrative about the area they live in,” Jensen said.

Speech Processing Pioneer Bishnu Saroop Atal Dies at 93

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

IEEE also bids farewell to innovators in space, power, and telecom

Bishnu Saroop Atal received the 2013 IEEE Jack S. Kilby Signal Processing Medal for “fundamental and pioneering contributions to speech coding.”

Bishnu Saroop AtalAcoustics and signal processing pioneerLife Fellow, 93; died 19 August

Atal was an acoustics and signal processing researcher at Bell Labs in Murray Hill, N.J., and later at AT&T’s Shannon Laboratory, in Florham Park, N.J. His pioneering work on voice-coding techniques became the foundation for efficient, high-quality voice transmission over cellular networks. He received the 2013 IEEE Jack S. Kilby Signal Processing Medal for “fundamental and pioneering contributions to speech coding.”

He joined Bell Labs in 1961 as a communications researcher, and his early work there focused on speaker identification technology. During the 1960s he helped develop linear predictive coding, a powerful method for analyzing and compressing speech. LPC is now the most widely used method for encoding speech at a low bit rate.

Atal developed increasingly sophisticated low-bit-rate speech-coding techniques. They included multipulse LPC, which improved the quality of compressed speech.

He also worked with his colleague Manfred R. Schroeder to develop code-excited linear prediction, a method for producing high-quality speech at very low bit rates. CELP became a foundational technique in modern speech coding, letting digital communications systems carry voice using less bandwidth. The extra efficiency expanded cellular capacity, allowing wireless networks to handle more simultaneous calls.

Later in his career, he led the acoustics and speech research departments at Bell.

The company named him a Fellow in 1994. When AT&T Bell Laboratories split into separate research organizations in 1996, Atal continued his career at the AT&T Florham Park headquarters, and he was named a Fellow the following year.

He retired in 2002 and became an affiliate professor of electrical engineering at the University of Washington, in Seattle. There he continued his research in speech processing, speech recognition, and high-dimensional computing.

Atal was elected to the National Academy of Engineering in 1987 and the National Academy of Sciences in 1993. He received numerous awards including the 2018 International Speech Communication Association Medal for Scientific Achievement and a 2003 medal from the Franklin Institute.

He received a bachelor’s degree in physics in 1952 from the University of Lucknow, in India, and a master’s degree in 1955 in electrical engineering from the Indian Institute of Science, in Bengaluru. While working full time at Bell he earned a Ph.D. in EE in 1968 from the Polytechnic Institute of Brooklyn (now New York University’s Tandon engineering school), in New York City.

Is this the end of the DCU? Probably not, but it's a sad day all the same

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

You'll have heard the news by now... As announced yesterday, James Gunn and Peter Safran are stepping away from DC Studios to be replaced by former Marvel exec Jeremy Latcham. The first phase of the DCU, it seems, is over. What comes next? Well, that's still very much TBC.

This is not entirely shocking news, but it’s still – for me at least – something of a downer. Gunn and Safran's DCU has been surprising, strange, daring, occasionally baffling, and often thrilling. I loved Superman and Lanterns and liked Peacemaker, Clayface, and Creature Commandos a great deal.

OK, the Supergirl film was a misfire and proved that Gunn's winning style was not so easily aped by other directors, but even that had its moments: Milly Alcock was terrific in the lead role, and Krypto is always fun.

The Marvel Cinematic Universe has been frequently criticised for the homogeneity of its output, but that was never a criticism that could be levelled at the DCU, which veered from family-friendly superhero flicks to rated-R horror movies with any given project.

That's probably not good news if you're a Warner Bros. exec desperately trying to forge the next big mega-franchise, however. And it's true that it has sometimes been hard to understand exactly what the criteria were for a DCU project making it to the screen or not. We exist in a strange timeline where we have a fairly solid Clayface movie, but no DCU Batman to go with it.

Still, when the DCU was good, it was great. Gunn's Superman managed the difficult task of delivering a fresh take on the Man of Steel. David Corenswet charmingly embodies Supes' decency, humor, and willingness to take a beating in a way that echoes Christopher Reeve's character-defining performance, but feels more in tune with the comics.

It helps that the supporting cast in that film – Rachel Brosnahan as Lois Lane, Edi Gathegi as Mister Terrific, and especially Nicholas Hoult as a scheming, raging Lex Luthor – were all pitch-perfect.

Then there was Lanterns. I will fully admit to being a die-hard fan of Damon Lindelof's work (The Leftovers is up there with the greatest shows of all time and I will never shut up about it) so I came to this with high expectations and was not let down.

Sure, some fans may have disagreed with the decision to squeeze the cosmic grandeur of the Green Lantern mythology into a self-consciously "grounded" cop show format, but it worked – and then some. Kyle Chandler's spiky, flawed Hal Jordan and Aaron Pierre's stoic, determined John Stewart were the dynamic duo we didn't know we needed, and their investigation into extraterrestrial weirdness in small-town America was both gripping and thought-provoking. This wasn't just a great comic book show, but excellent TV full stop.

As for the rest... I had a lot of fun with Clayface, a gloopy, gruesome, enjoyably schlocky horror that still found time for solid characterization. It's not the sort of film you can build a cinematic universe around, but that's OK. Peacemaker season 2, meanwhile, did not hit quite the same heights as that bolt-from-the-blue first run, but John Cena remains hilarious and heartbreaking in the role. I hope that the character isn't left stranded on Salvation for too long.

So what does a post-Gunn, post-Safran DCU look like? Obviously I'm purely speculating here, but I suspect this will be a case of Latcham refocusing the brand, rather than relaunching the entire universe for a third time. And no, I don't see Zack Snyder returning any time soon...

It doesn't take the World's Greatest Detective to figure out that we'll likely see a sharper focus on the A-list DC characters: Batman, Wonder Woman, and probably reinventions of Aquaman and the Flash at some point. I would hope that it doesn't mean the end of DC trying out interesting lower-budget experiments like Clayface, but I suspect the days of somewhat obscure-sounding TV spin-offs like the upcoming The People v. Gorilla Grodd are over.

The Superman movies will also surely continue, perhaps even with Gunn involved in a third instalment – while his departure letter talked of "trying" times behind-the-scenes, he notably did not close the door on working with DC again and has already referenced another, unnamed project in progress. Frankly, Latcham would be foolish to try and find someone else to steer that character when Gunn is right there.

James Gunn was working on Superman 3 and a Justice League team-up when he was ousted at DC

The biggest question, of course, is what becomes of the Dark Knight – and that does feel like a particularly tricky knot to undo. Matt Reeves' The Batman: Part II (which is set in a different "Elseworlds" continuity) was originally slated to come out in 2025, leaving the way clear for the introduction of a new DCU Batman in a film, helmed by Gunn, titled The Brave and the Bold.

Alas, The Batman: Part II suffered a series of major delays and is now eyeing a February 2028 slot, while the fate of The Brave and the Bold is unclear at this time. Ultimately, the DCU needs to find its Caped Crusader sooner rather than later, and you can be sure that figuring out how to introduce this new Batman, while also promoting Reeves' project, will be top of Latcham's to-do list at DC.

I'm not ready to write the future of the DCU off just yet, but I will miss the unpredictable energy that Gunn and Safran have brought to these movies and shows over the last four years. It's been a bumpy ride at times, but never a boring one, and I'd take a series with a strong authorial voice like this over a play-it-safe franchise any day.

Is that what we'll get with the DCU Chapter Two? Only time and a former Marvel exec named Jeremy Latcham will tell.
