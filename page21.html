Review: Apple's M6 iMac is pricey, but it's still a good choice for a clean desk

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple’s M6 keeps the iMac current without shaking things up much.

The Apple Silicon iMac got its fourth refresh this month.

Every other Apple Silicon Mac has found a way to move forward over the last half-decade—we’ve gotten a more Pro design for the MacBook Pro, a mini-er design for the Mac mini, and a Mac Studio that has finally made Apple admit it doesn’t want to make the Mac Pro anymore.

But the Apple Silicon iMac has always been more interested in looking backward. It’s the thin, screen-on-a-stand iMac design by way of the original Bondi Blue G3 iMac. It’s a clean and pretty desktop computer, one I see all the time in small offices and on TV shows that depict small offices. We’re long past the days when it was Apple’s primary workstation computer.

So I wanted to write about this new iMac from the perspective of that entry-level iMac buyer, who chooses the iMac because it’s simple and then uses it until it breaks. What does a new iMac get you if you’re stepping up from the M1 model or comparing it against an older refurbished M4 model? Without any real external updates, what makes this iMac iteration interesting?

If you haven’t used an Apple Silicon-era iMac before, here’s a brief primer.

The new iMac is much thinner than the older aluminum Intel models. Its front is mostly one big 24-inch screen, surrounded on all sides by a relatively thick white bezel (I still wish black were at least an option, but Apple apparently doesn’t agree).

White bezel aside, it’s by far Apple’s most colorful computer. The iMacs ship with color-matched fronts, backs, stands, keyboards, mice, trackpads, and cables. Apple has tweaked the color offerings and changed the tints of each specific color a few times over the years, but the M6 iMac uses the same six color tints (plus silver) that the M4 iMac did.

The basic $1,499 iMac is essentially a MacBook Air in terms of ports—it has two Thunderbolt ports on the back and a headphone jack on the left side. Choosing this model also locks you into a lesser version of the Apple M6 with 10 CPU cores and 10 GPU cores instead of 12 of each, a maximum of 24GB of RAM, a minimum of 256GB of storage, and a maximum of 1TB. Its default keyboard doesn’t have Touch ID support, and the only way to get it is to buy the version with the built-in number pad, an $80 upsell.

The $1,699 version is the one to get if you still intend to use the iMac as a workstation. It includes the full-speed version of the M6 with all its CPU and GPU cores intact (though there are performance caveats here we’ll talk about later), plus options for 32GB of RAM and up to 2TB of storage. This version also includes four Thunderbolt ports on the back for connecting additional accessories. Both available keyboards on this version have Touch ID.

A 2.5 gigabit Ethernet port can be added to any iMac for $30. This port is located on the desktop’s power brick rather than the iMac itself, an elegant solution that keeps even a wired Internet connection hidden beneath your desk with the other cables. All iMacs also offer a VESA mount adapter option in place of the default stand at no extra charge.

I still really like the effect created by the color-matched keyboard, mouse/trackpad, and cables—even the white bezel complements the white keycaps and mouse. Just know you’re paying quite a lot for how the desktop looks. The $899 Mac mini is $800 cheaper than the iMac with the same chip, the same amount of RAM, and the same amount of storage. Sure, it would cost something to add a nice-looking 4K monitor, but not $800, even if you go for extra frills like a high refresh rate.

It’s fine that the iMac upcharges you for style and convenience. That’s the point of the computer. Just make sure that’s what you want to pay for.

Here are the basic specs for every entry-level iMac Apple has shipped, going all the way back to the last of the Intel models.

The iMac’s evolution has been slow and gradual during the Apple Silicon era. The computer has never gotten a base storage upgrade, and the base M4 and M6 models still only support a single external display (the upgraded models support two external displays, finally surpassing the old Intel model).

If you have any kind of Apple Silicon iMac and you’re happy with how it’s performing and you’re not hankering for more RAM or more displays, I don’t think the M6 model really adds anything worth upgrading for. It’s definitely faster, but even six years on, the Apple M1 remains perfectly serviceable for daily use, especially when it’s attached to 16GB of RAM.

And if you’re in the market for an iMac now, I think the M4 model still gets you the best bang for your buck if you can find one refurbished or on sale. That one still gets you a minimum of 16GB of RAM, Thunderbolt in all four ports for the four-port model, the 32GB RAM option, the nano-texture display option, and support for multiple external displays (again, in the four-port model).

The M6 iMac builds on that, and it comes with real performance benefits (more on that shortly). But the brightest dividing line in the Apple Silicon iMac model history is between the M3 and M4 model, not the M4 and M6 model. And that’s true despite the two-generation silicon upgrade.

A quick recap on the M6: a total of 12 CPU cores (two super cores, four performance cores, six efficiency cores), either 10 or 12 GPU cores, 16/24/32GB RAM options, and a doubled Neural Engine NPU with 32 cores instead of 16. For the iMac, the M6 also introduces some of the architectural tweaks introduced with the Apple M5, including the addition of neural accelerator hardware to the GPU cores. This boosts the speed of some kinds of local AI workloads compared to the M4, including MetalFX upscaling and frame generation in games.

The M6 in the Mac mini and the M6 in the 14-inch MacBook Pro both perform similarly within a roughly 28 W sustained power envelope. The version with 16GB of RAM will perform slightly worse in games and other workloads sensitive to memory bandwidth—the 16GB M6 offers 153 GB/s of memory bandwidth, and the 24GB and 32GB versions offer 170 GB/s. But that’s a pretty minor difference.

The iMac is slower than either of those two Macs, particularly in multi-core CPU benchmarks and graphics benchmarks, and the HandBrake video encoding test shows the likely culprit. The iMac’s M6 consumes an average of 18 W of power under sustained heavy load. It won’t throttle its speeds as aggressively as a MacBook Air because the iMac still includes an active cooling fan, but the reduced power envelope does mean that the Mac mini or MacBook Pro are 5 to 8 percent faster than the iMac in heavy multi-core CPU tasks and about 8 to 12 percent faster in graphics benchmarks.

One benefit of the smaller power envelope is that the iMac’s chip is even more power-efficient than the regular M6 and the iMac’s version of the M4, using much less power to do the same amount of work.

That still makes it a solid upgrade over the M4. The iMac’s M6 is 15 to 20 percent faster in our single-core CPU benchmarks, between 20 and 30 percent faster in multi-core CPU benchmarks, and between 20 and 40 percent faster in graphics benchmarks (the Blender GPU benchmarks do even better—they seem to like the neural accelerators in the M5 and M6 GPUs). Especially with 24GB or 32GB of RAM, it’s perfectly capable of being the heart of a multi-monitor workstation setup or a light photo/video editing rig.

The M6 iMac changes almost nothing about who the iMac is for, its position in Apple’s lineup, or what it’s technically capable of. It’s a box-ticking upgrade, and the box being ticked is “keep the insides up to date.”

That means today’s iMac is best for the same kinds of computer users it was good for in 1998: people who don’t want a laptop, people who need a friendly and approachable kiosk or office computer, and people who just want something pretty that doesn’t require a lot of fussing with screens or cables.

That’s the main thing I miss about being an iMac user. I like how my current Mac Studio desktop works, and I need flexibility and complexity in my specific setup to get the Mac and a gaming PC connected to all the same stuff. But despite my best efforts, the cable situation has always been a barely managed mess.

If someone you know has a cable management problem and doesn’t need the complexity, congratulations, you’ve found someone the iMac is for. I just wish Apple would use these kinds of pretty, saturated colors in a laptop or desktop that meets my needs, you know?

How Short Runways Limit Boeing 787-9 Payloads On Hot Days

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

William Perugini | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

Ethan is an aviation professional with a degree in Aviation Management with Flight from the Florida Institute of Technology. He previously wrote aviation and tourism features for Florida Tech’s student newspaper, The Crimson, where he developed a clear, reader‑focused reporting style. Aviation journalism has remained a consistent priority throughout his career.

He currently holds a Restricted ATP and Commercial Multi‑Engine Pilot certificate and works in aviation supply chain and aircraft component repair. Alongside his industry role, he is pursuing his Certified Flight Instructor rating and actively building flight experience.

A lifelong aviation enthusiast, Ethan spent countless hours at Raleigh–Durham International Airport’s observation deck studying air traffic and practicing amateur photography and videography.

How long it takes a Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner to lift its wheels off the ground depends on numerous factors. An aircraft's performance must fall within the manufacturer's defined performance limits. These limitations are usually outlined in the Pilot Operating Handbook (POH) for general aviation aircraft, or a series of publications that cover heavy commercial airliners in collaboration with Dispatch teams:

Consider a flight that is hot, high, and heavy. Higher than standard 59 degrees Fahrenheit (15 degrees Celsius) temperatures contribute to decreased overall performance. A takeoff roll conducted at a high field elevation further reduces a jet's ability to lift off efficiently. Further, a mission that has every seat occupied or near its maximum certificated takeoff weight (MTOW) will further extend a takeoff roll, presenting a challenge to operators in such environments.

Although humidity is a closely related factor when examining hot versus cold days, when comparing the two solely based on temperature, cold days provide increased aerodynamic and engine performance. The terms "hot" and "cold" are relative in aviation. The FAA outlines that temperature effects on aircraft performance are best evaluated using actual temperature values and density altitude calculations, as the resulting performance impacts vary by aircraft type and model. Aircraft performance data published in the POH are specific to each aircraft and are based on defined atmospheric conditions.

Hot temperatures reduce air density by causing air molecules to spread farther apart. According to the FAA's Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge (PHAK), this increase in density altitude results in fewer air molecules passing through the engine and over the wings. Consequently, the 787-9's General Electric GEnx -1B variant or Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engines produce less power with fewer air molecules available to process. Further, the wings create less lift, leading to longer takeoff distances and reduced climb performance.

However, there is no defined temperature window that is necessarily bad. A 41-degree Fahrenheit (5 degrees Celsius) day with high humidity may be unpleasant for a pilot operating in such conditions, but that same 5-degree day with low humidity would likely result in decent overall performance when looking at temperature and humidity alone. But in general, the colder it is at the airport, the performance curve increases.

However, temperature is only one piece of the puzzle. Atmospheric pressure can produce similar performance penalties even when temperatures are relatively mild. As pressure decreases, the air becomes less dense, reducing the amount of air flowing through the engines and over the wings. This is why aircraft performance calculations rely on pressure altitude rather than field elevation alone. Two airports at the same physical elevation can produce different takeoff performance results if the atmospheric pressure differs on a given day.

For a Boeing 787-9, pressure variations are incorporated into dispatch and performance software before every departure. Lower pressure conditions effectively make the aircraft behave as though it is operating from a higher airport. Less dense air reduces engine thrust output and aerodynamic efficiency, requiring higher true airspeeds to generate the same amount of lift. While day-to-day pressure changes are generally less dramatic than large temperature swings, they can still materially affect takeoff distances when combined with hot temperatures, heavy payloads, or high-elevation airports.

The challenge for operators is that pressure rarely changes in isolation. As AOPA notes, while pressure rarely matches from one location to another, atmospheric pressure is relatively uniform across geographical areas. A low-pressure weather system often arrives with other unfavorable conditions, raising the question of how elevation itself changes aircraft performance before temperature and weight are even considered.

Some routes are long, and some push aircraft to the limit. United Airlines has pushed its 777-300ER so much that on this route, fuel stops are likely.

That question becomes particularly important at airports located thousands of feet above sea level. Even under standard atmospheric conditions, higher-elevation airports contain less dense air than airports at sea level. In practice, this means a Boeing 787-9 departing from Denver International Airport (DEN) will require more runway and achieve lower climb performance than the same aircraft departing with the same weight from a coastal airport.

The difference can be substantial. Denver sits at approximately 5,434 feet above sea level, while airports such as San Francisco International Airport (SFO) operate essentially at sea level. Although both airports routinely accommodate long-haul widebody aircraft, the thinner air at Denver requires airlines to pay closer attention to payload restrictions, fuel planning, and runway performance calculations. A 787-9 departing Denver on an ultra-long-haul mission may face limitations that would not exist from a lower-altitude airport using the exact same aircraft; United Airlines operates more long-haul flights from SFO for this very reason.

Altitude alone does not necessarily prevent a successful departure. The real challenge emerges when altitude combines with high temperatures, creating density altitude values that can dramatically exceed an airport's actual elevation. This brings the discussion to perhaps the most controllable factor of all: aircraft weight.

Unlike temperature, pressure, or field elevation, aircraft weight is one factor airlines can directly influence. Every additional passenger, checked bag, cargo container, and pound of fuel increases the amount of lift required to become airborne. As a result, heavier aircraft require higher takeoff speeds and longer runway distances to lift off safely.

For the 787-9, the maximum certified takeoff weight varies depending on the specific configuration and option package selected by the airline, but it exceeds 500,000 pounds in every configuration. At these weights, performance planning becomes a balancing act between payload and fuel. Airlines may occasionally choose to leave cargo behind, block seats, or schedule a fuel stop if environmental conditions prevent a fully loaded aircraft from departing safely within performance limits.

The consequences of excess weight become most apparent on demanding routes. When high temperatures, high elevations, and near-maximum takeoff weights occur simultaneously, airlines may be forced to find creative solutions that maintain both safety and profitability. In United's case, many international routes served by the Chicago-based carrier are simply not possible from Denver; the required fuel weight could exceed the maximum allowable fuel weight for a takeoff from Denver on a hot day, preventing the jet from reaching its destination.

The phenomenon is a consequence of the aircraft's design.

Airlines have several tools available when performance calculations indicate a 787-9 cannot safely depart at its desired takeoff weight. The simplest solution is reducing aircraft weight. This may involve limiting cargo loads, carrying fewer passengers, reducing fuel, or planning an intermediate refueling stop. While these decisions can affect profitability, they allow airlines to continue serving challenging destinations without compromising safety.

A real-world example can be found at United Airlines' Denver hub. While Denver supports an extensive network of long-haul international flying, its combination of high elevation and summer temperatures can present operational challenges. Routes with extremely long stage lengths, such as a hypothetical nonstop Denver-Manila service, would require significantly more fuel than many existing transpacific flights. SFO-MNL is significantly easier to plan and dispatch as a result, not to mention a slightly shorter trip distance as well.

Even these solutions have limits. As aircraft missions become longer and passenger demand grows, airlines must decide whether operational workarounds are enough or whether different aircraft are fundamentally better suited for certain environments. Boliviana de Aviaciónand Aeromexico struggle with this, as their primary hubs of Vicecomodoro Angel D. La Paz Aragonés Airport (LPB) and Mexico City International Airport (MEX), respectively, are situated at high altitudes. With generally warm temperatures at field elevation to supplement the challenge, both carriers can be somewhat limited in range and aircraft weight.

The 787-9 was designed to maximize long-haul efficiency, but aircraft such as the Boeing 757-200and Boeing 737-700 became known for their strong runway performance and ability to operate from airports where environmental conditions limit larger aircraft. The 757, in particular, remains one of the most respected performers ever produced by Boeing. Equipped with powerful Rolls-Royce RB211 or Pratt & Whitney PW2000 engines and a relatively modest wing loading for its size, the aircraft developed a reputation for operating from hot-and-high airports while still carrying meaningful payloads over long distances.

The smaller 737-700 similarly benefits from a favorable thrust-to-weight ratio and lower overall weight compared to larger members of the Boeing 737 family. For airlines serving airports with runway constraints, altitude challenges, or frequent high-temperature operations, these characteristics can often matter more than maximum passenger capacity.

The challenge facing airlines today is that there is no true modern replacement for the niche the 757 occupied. While aircraft such as the Boeing 787-9 can fly substantially farther and carry more passengers, they cannot always replicate the same combination of field performance and operational flexibility.

Is There a Hookup to Relationship Pipeline?

Yes, it’s true: my relationship started off as a hookup. My boyfriend—who I now love, am fully committed to, and have way too much fun in Costco with—used to send me 2AM “WYD” texts and avoided any sort of conversation about the future.

Yes, I made it out of the situationship trenches. I’ll hold for applause.

While I would like to pretend that I’m better than everyone else, the reality is that there is no secret method to turning your situationship into a real relationship. Sometimes—like in my case—it’s just science.

A 2018 study revealed that sexual desire can actually encourage people to engage in behaviors that help initiate romantic relationships. Could Your GLP-1s Make You Blind? Here’s What to Make of the FDA’s Latest Notice.

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

For years, there have been rumblings and even research about a rare but serious condition that’s been linked to taking a GLP-1 medication. Now, the Food and Drug Administration is doing something about it.

The FDA just released a drug safety communication to warn patients and healthcare providers about the potential increased risk of developing non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) when you take a GLP-1 medication. NAION is a rare condition that can lead to vision loss in one eye, and it can be permanent.

Again, this isn't the first time concerns have been raised about the risk of NAION. But it is the first time the FDA has issued a warning about it.

Given that up to 11 percent of Americans currently take a GLP-1 for weight loss, this news stands to impact a lot of people. How concerned should you be if you're one of them? Eye experts and a doctor who prescribes GLP-1s break it down.

Meet the experts: Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system; Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute; and Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

The FDA's notice is designed to flag the potential risk of NAION to people who use GLP-1s. The FDA also notes that the agency will require all manufacturers of GLP-1 medications to add information about the risk of NAION to the Warnings and Precautions section of their labels.

"Patients and caregivers should be aware of a potential increased risk of NAION, a rare condition that can result in acute, painless, decreased vision in one eye, including permanent vision loss, with GLP-1 RA use," the notice reads.

The agency cited a 2024 study published in JAMA Ophthalmology as well as its own meta-analyses and retrospective cohort study in the notice. They also noted that “NAION cases have been reported for all currently marketed GLP-1 RA products.”

However, the complication remains rare: incidents from the FDA’s meta-analysis were just 6.4 events per 100,000 per year for patients using the medications.

The warning is just that—a warning to patients and physicians that this could happen, explains Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system. "Most outcome-related issues in medicine should be thought of as being probabilistic—there is a certain chance that events can occur," he says. "This warning indicates that the FDA has reason to believe, at least at this time, that the probability of NAION occurring is higher in patients who take GLP-1 RA drugs."

But Dr. Rizzo stresses that there's no evidence that these drugs cause NAION—just that there's a link.

So…why issue a warning if it's not proven that GLP-1s cause NAION and it's so rare? "The severity of a complication matters alongside its frequency," says Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute. "Even a rare risk deserves attention when it can permanently affect vision." The warning is likely designed to give healthcare providers a heads-up that it could be NAION if a patient has symptoms, he explains.

"It's their duty to report any adverse effects, just so people are aware of them," says Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

While NAION is scary, doctors say it's important to put the data in perspective. "It's extremely rare, but it can happen," Dr. Ali says. Given how much GLP-1s can help with blood sugar management, weight loss, and downstream health effects, Dr. Ali says that the risk of NAION is "not a reason not to seek these medications."

Dr. Rizzo agrees. "I essentially never discourage the general use of GLP-1 RA drugs," he says. "Ultimately, the risk-to-benefit assessment must be decided by the patient, but physicians should provide sufficient information to the patients to better inform their decisions."

People should stay aware of the risk without panicking, Dr. Youssefzadeh says. "It's also worth remembering that the people most likely to be prescribed these drugs—people with diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnea—already carry a higher baseline risk for NAION, so the risk isn't starting from zero," he adds.

In a major change to screening guidelines, the American Cancer Society recommended on Thursday that American men between ages 50 and 69 at an average risk of prostate cancer should test their prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood levels every two years.

The prior guidelines, released in 2010, left testing for average-risk men to their discretion, after assessing cancer risk with a physician.

Similarly, PSA testing recommendations were strengthened for high risk groups — Black men and men with a strong family history of prostate cancer. They are now encouraged to get PSA-based screening yearly starting at 45. Black men in particular disproportionately die from prostate cancer, and the guideline authors said they hope that increased surveillance will reduce the number of Black patients presenting with cancers at advanced stages.

Then, starting at 70, the decision on whether to continue screening becomes individualized as the balance of benefit and harm is not as clear after that age. Screening should be interrupted after 80, or in men who are not expected to live another 10 years.

The changes reflect recent findings that screening through PSA levels reduced disease specific mortality for certain men, bringing the indicator back into specialists’ good graces. Once a preferred biomarker to identify risk of prostate cancer, routine PSA screening had fallen out of favor — and was dropped from the guidelines in 2010 — for its lack of specificity, which resulted in many men undergoing follow-up procedures when they had no cancer, or very slow-growing tumors.

The U.S. Preventive Service Task Force, a federally appointed panel of outside experts, has not changed its guidelines since the new evidence became available in 2025, and continues to recommend against routine, population-wide PSA screening, suggesting instead individualized decision-making. But the panel, whose recommendations determine the preventive services that insurers must cover, has announced an upcoming update.

The cutoff for PSA levels to be considered high is established as 3.0 nanograms/milliliter or higher for men aged 45 to 69 years old. This is an important clarification, said James Eastham, chief of urology at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. “Most PSA blood tests in the United States, when they’re reported, say under 4.0 [ng/ml] is normal, but it really should be under 3,” he said. “I think [the update] will hopefully help primary care physicians refer folks a little bit earlier.”

To limit the risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment, the guidelines also now recommend two elevated PSA readings, followed by an MRI scan and/or testing for other biomarkers of prostate cancer, with the aim of reducing the number of patients undergoing biopsies. “We just wanted to give a little bit of flexibility for folks who cannot easily obtain an MRI,” said William Dahut, chief scientific officer at the ACS and a contributing author of the guidelines, about including both biomarkers and MRIs as a possible step following elevated PSA levels. However, he noted that an MRI would still be recommended prior to biopsy, should a biomarker test come back abnormal.

Though there are a variety of biomarkers, the guidelines don’t recommend one over the other. Dahut said ACS researchers  intend to look more closely at biomarkers to establish which may be more effective in predicting a more aggressive cancer.

“I think the new guidelines are certainly a balanced approach for early detection of prostate cancer,” said Eastham. “There have been many men undergoing unnecessary biopsies, and many low risk cancers that were subsequently overtreated,” he said. By recommending some steps between an elevated PSA and a biopsy, he said, the new recommendations reflect the evidence that while being a good biomarker, PSA alone would identify too many men as biopsy candidates.

Finally, the guidelines emphasize the importance of active surveillance in men with slow growing cancer: “If you screen more men, you’re going to find more men with early-stage disease, and we need to be strong in our position that that does not mean that you need to rush for therapies or that you need to have therapies at all.”

For Eddie Baynes, a 56-year-old prostate cancer survivor and advocate, the new guidelines “hit the nail on the head” when it comes to recommending routine PSA screening. As a Black man, he knew he was at higher risk of developing prostate cancer, yet it wasn’t until the fraternity he belonged to organized a screening event that he, at 52, started getting regular PSA tests with his bloodwork. Though routine screening wasn’t part of the ACS recommendation at the time, it led to a cancer diagnosis and a prostatectomy.

That’s why he’s been advocating for men to get PSA levels tested, and relatively frequently. With the new guidelines, men like him will start routine screening earlier than he did. “Part of my advocacy is getting tested more frequently. So now, just to have that announcement coming from the American Cancer Society, it just makes what I’ve been urging individuals to do for a while stronger,” he said.

Hello, good local-time-zone-adjusted afternoon, and let's just say it's been a while. But I come back to you now at the turn of the technological tide. Well, sort of. Sadly I'm not here to predict an end to the ridiculous AI-fuelled chip crisis. That whole mess has a couple of years to run, surely.

Instead I speak of screens, where there is actually some very good news. In short, have you seen how cheap PC gaming monitors are? I mean remarkably, almost stupendously, cheap. Also bear in mind we've had a fair old bit of inflation in recent years. A 4K panel bought today for £200 is equivalent to paying roughly £150 back in 2020. All the while, that same £200 / $200 right now won't buy you a current-model gaming graphics card at all, even the most feeble example, and will barely secure a basic dual-channel 16GB DDR5 memory kit. Youch.

So, I think we can all agree monitors are cheap in the current chip-catastrophe context. The immediate question is why? The answer isn't all that complicated, actually. Most obviously, the display market isn't being devoured by the AI craze. All those data centres are stuffed with GPUs, memory chips and NAND flash, not monitors.

By way of example, one of the largest new AI data centres on Earth is Microsoft's new Fairwater site in Mount Pleasant, Wisconsin. It's thought to pack hundreds of thousands of Nvidia Blackwell GPUs. The cost doesn't bear thinking about, but those GPUs are built into what's known as "headless" server racks. In other words, they have no screens at all. Ultimately, the key compute hardware is all managed remotely, in which capacity the Fairwater facility is reported to currently have around 375 employees. If we generously assume each has two PC monitors, that's only 750 for the entire site.

AI isn't really driving demand on the end-user side of the equation, either. Put it this way: you don't need to buy a new phone, laptop or PC to access the latest models. At the same time, the display market is suffering from something of a glut of overcapacity. Chinese companies built out huge capacity in LCD panel manufacturing over the past decade, to the extent factories are now running underutilised. Meanwhile, even the OLED market is arguably oversupplied.

In fact, Samsung and LG, the two big noises in large OLED panel manufacturing (as opposed to OLED displays for tablets and phones), have been shifting emphasis away from TV panels to PC monitors because the latter offer better margins. Meanwhile, the AI boom has almost definitely meant lower demand for new PCs. Certainly, the big research houses point to cratering PC sales thanks to higher component costs. Long story short, that's oodles of capacity combined with weakening demand. Like I said, the explanation for cheap monitors isn't exactly rocket surgery, if you know what I mean.

What is a bit more complicated is making sense of what's available today in the gaming monitor market. With so much choice, where exactly is the very best value to be found? And are there any remaining pain points?

Monitor specifications, to be sure, can be tricksy at the best of times. But if you're looking for some blanket rules to fling casually over the whole LCD monitor market - I'll be digging deeper into OLED tech another day - try these for size. Look for, at minimum, 300 nits brightness and 1ms response times. Refresh rates are a slightly more complicated call, but for the vast majority of games, 144Hz is plenty. For "esports-grade multiplayer fare like Counter-Strike or Dota 2, or I think a refresh in the high 200s will get the job done for most. If you'll benefit from more than that, you almost certainly know who you are already.

Oh, and heavily favour IPS over VA panels. Exceptions exist: there are some decent "Rapid VA" panels these days and Samsung, in particular, does some nippy VA models. But a lot of the cheaper stuff is pretty slow and smeary, making VA a risky buy. Finally, don't worry too much about claimed HDR capability, save for the fact that HDR 400 certification at least means the monitor can do 400 nits. Unless a monitor has local dimming, it won't be truly HDR capable.

Anywho, one immediate and rather ironic issue is the growing mismatch between monitors and GPUs. For sure, it's great you can grab a high-refresh 4K monitor for such little money. It's almost hard to believe that you can have this 27-inch IPS MSI for sub-£200 (you're looking at $250 for the same model in the US), and it even has a 288Hz 1080p dual mode.

I actually favour 32 inches with my 4K gaming, and that's naturally a little pricier, especially if you insist on an IPS - which again, you probably should. But even $350 for that kind of panel (here's a comparable model for £340 in the UK) is a fraction of what you'd have paid for a similarly specced model a few years ago. And it probably wouldn't have included dual-mode functionality.

Here's the problem, though. How are you going to drive the thing, what with its eight million-plus pixels? After all, the cheapest RTX 5090 on Newegg right now is over $6,500. So high-end Nvidia GPUs now cost about as much as a decent used car.

Of course, you might be surprised just how effective the likes of an AMD RX 9070 XT or Nvidia RTX 5070-class GPU is at 4K thanks to upscaling technologies including DLSS and FSR. The latest transformer model upscalers really do knock out pretty stellar image quality. Add in a bit of frame gen and you have a compelling combination of visual sharpness, reasonably low latency and nice smoothframe rates, even in the most demanding of games. Perhaps the one thing you can't do is enable full path tracing in a game like Cyberpunk 2077 and still expect a slick experience, but standard ray tracing remains an option.

The bigger snag, unsurprisingly, is that you can no longer bag a 5070 at (let alone below) its original £539 / $549 MSRP. Instead, you're looking at the thick end of £800 / $900. That's a bit of an ask for pairing with a £200 monitor, isn't it?

I'd therefore argue that, for pure gaming purposes, the bang-for-buck sweet spot remains just where it's been sat twiddling its thumbs for the better part of a decade, namely 1440p. That applies to both its vanilla 16:9 aspect variety and my personally preferred ultrawide format, the latter being so very immersive and also pretty nifty for daily multi-tasking. Indeed, the 27-inch 1440p segment even brings the vibrant colours and infinite contrast of OLED panels into a reasonably affordable window.

By way of example, £179 (or $150) is enough for a circa 200Hz, IPS 1440p panel with super low (by IPS standards) pixel response, even if the 250-nit brightness is lower than I'd like. Meanwhile, as I write these words, pricing for big-brand 27-inch 1440p gaming OLEDs kicks off at about £290 in the UK and roughly $270 in the US. Admittedly, those entry-level OLEDs come with some compromises, which I'll cover in a forthcoming post. But what you might call 'proper' OLED panels can be had around the low £300 / $300 mark - not throw-away money, for sure, but relatively accessible.

The 34-inch 1440p ultrawide segment is a bit trickier, however. The good news is that conventional LCD models can be had for relative peanuts, with prices starting at about £220 or $249 for game-focused, high-refresh models. But those two example both use a VA panel, which usually means inferior pixel response.

IPS models are a little thin on the ground when it comes to 34-inch ultrawide, but sneaking in below £300 / $300 is generally possible. The real bummer is OLED, where going ultrawide invokes a massive premium. The best price I can find on a big-brand 34-inch ultrawide today is $629 for this Asus model in the US and £648 for this MSI option in the UK.

That's awfully toppy, especially when you consider that you can grab a 49-inch, 5,120x1,440 ultrawide OLED for barely any more money. I do actually quite like those 49-inch ultrawides, but I have to concede the ergonomics are challenging. However, 4K OLEDs in 27-inch and 32-inch formats can also be had for as much as £100 / $100 less than the current cheapest 34-inch 1440p ultrawides, which doesn't make much sense to me.

Again, with 4K, there's the challenge of keeping all those pixels updated. If you already have a sufficiently mighty graphics card, though, I a 32-inch 4K OLED can make a fantastic all-round option, and something like this MSI model for £549 would be absolutely lovely. You get good pixel density and lots of space for daily desktopping, plus OLED visuals and a large enough panel to be really get you into your games.

It's just a pity that 34-inch OLED ultrawides remain so weirdly expensive, it being a format that ticks so many different boxes. As for my own personal money-no-object pick, well, I'm typing these words on the new Alienware AW3926QW, the 39-inch 5K2K beast with a Tandem WOLED panel and RGB subpixels.

One could argue it's a relative steal at £1,079 or $1,100. I mean, it's pretty much peak current PC gaming display technology, and it's about half the price of what Nvidia's second-tier RTX 5080 graphics card is going for lately. Assuming you can even find one in stock.

At this point, the one major category I haven't covered is LCD monitors with mini-LED full-array local dimming. Cards on the table, I'm not a huge fan of mini-LED monitors. The detailed reasons for that are, perhaps, the subject for another day. But I've reviewed countless models and have yet to see one that didn't come with pretty serious compromises.

That said, the technology has gotten a lot more accessible in the last 12 to 18 months, to the extent that 27-inch 1440p models start at just over $250. I've had that specific model in and came away pretty impressed. It's very, very punchy, and even factoring in the limitations of mini-LED versus OLED when it comes to lighting control and speed, I cannot deny the temptation.

Just for completeness, one further exception to the general rule of cheap gaming monitors involves ultra-high refresh monitors aimed at serious esports aficionados. I'm talking about panels like this 1,000Hz LG I sampled elsewhere. At £800 / $1,000 for a 25-inch 1080p IPS panel it's inevitably a niche proposition, and poor value by normal standards.

I think a much better compromise for just about everyone, short of an actual esports pro, is to grab a larger, higher resolution monitor that offers one of those windowed 1080p modes running at about 500Hz or thereabouts. That way you've got a nice high-res panel for both work and play, as well as that ultra-low latency mode for when you want to dance around Dust 2 doling out the headshots or whatever.

Incidentally, I realise I've gone in a bit hot and heavy with the OLED content here, the implicit assumption being OLED is all that, and notably without addressing the obvious objections like the risk of burn-in. But all that, I hope you'll be pleased to hear, is very much the subject for my next RPS post on all things OLED.

Until then, all I can say is thank goodness PC monitors provide relative respite from the parlous state of much of the rest of the PC component market. It's absolutely brutal out there if you're building a gaming PC, but at least you'll get a lot of screen for your money.

ASUS had a big year of releases, and while a lot of the attention went to the flashy dual-screen models the brand unveiled, their traditional laptop releases were worth paying attention to as well. The Zenbook A16 has a lightweight design without sacrificing screen size, and the dazzling OLED display also features a touchscreen. Overall, it’s a powerful laptop that suits a range of use cases.

The Pixel Watch does what few smartwatches have been able to pull off — look like an actual watch. With a domed display, the face doesn’t call attention to the fact that it’s a screen, and the latest Pixel Watch 5 packs in all of the features you need, including health tracking and sleep tracking, plus features you want but hopefully won’t need, like car-crash detection and SOS satellite communications.

Best known for their affordable TVs, TCL has been raising the bar to become one of the best all-around TV brands, with the right combination of innovation, quality, and value. Their SQD mini-LED TVs deliver greater brightness and contrast, with up to 3,000 peak nits for a vibrant display even in bright rooms.

Boox has proven that an e-reader can be more than an e-reader. The brand’s versatile e-ink gadgets run the gamut from phone-sized e-readers to large note-taking tablets. Because they run Android, you can download a versatile suite of apps. Their latest device is the itty-bitty Picco, which is roughly the size of a playing card. With support for ePub and a microSD slot, you can take a variety of file types to read on the go.

Gaming consoles are dominated by a few brands, but Nex has shaken up the industry with its family-friendly gaming console. The cube-shaped device features a movement-tracking camera, so each game encourages movement and activity. To protect privacy, the camera doesn’t save or store video or audio, and there’s an added physical camera cover for additional privacy. The Playground console also comes with five games to start.

Skylight bills itself as a family-oriented product, designed to help corral kids’ schedules and organize chores. But it’s surprisingly useful in child-free households, or even at the office. As editors who tend to find appointments and obligations sneaking up on us, the easy at-a-glance organization makes it easy to figure out what we have going on that day, and it syncs easily with a variety of calendar apps. The subscription unlocks more, but you can still do a lot with the free tier.

SwitchBot makes a dizzying array of smart home products, with wide support for a range of platforms. The smart locks let you use everything from fingerprints to facial recognition, while the cameras are a more affordable alternative to other tech brands. You can also build a whole-home ecosystem with accessories like smart window shades and lighting. With so much smart tech, it’s easy to forget SwitchBot started as just a Bluetooth button-pushing gizmo.

XGIMI makes projectors for every kind of enthusiast, from home theater pros to on-the-go gamers, and this year marked some major releases from the brand. There was the cinematic Titan Noir Max, the space-saving short-throw Aura 3 Max, and the ultra-portable battery-powered Vibe One. Across price and class, XGIMI has been delivering stylish design, excellent picture quality, and user-friendliness.

It’s rare to see a headphone brand actually shake up the big players, but Daisy proved to be one of the buzziest if-you-know-you-know brands thanks to its stellar design. The aluminum accents set it apart from the all-plastic construction of most headphones, but they look a lot more expensive than they are. Tactile controls make for a refined experience, while solid sound and noise-canceling hold up the performance end of the equation.

PSB’s iQ bookshelf speakers are some of the most stylishly contemporary speakers we’ve seen, with a unique design that puts the bass driver on top of the tweeter. The speakers also connect to each other wirelessly, adding greater flexibility in where you can plug them in. As for listening to music, they support BluOS for multiroom audio, and they have inputs for HDMI eARC, RCA, optical, USB-C, and USB-A, making them flexible and versatile.

Year after year, Q Acoustics proves to be a hi-fi brand that enthusiasts keep coming back to. Their latest release is the 3040C floorstanding speakers, which are headed to the U.S. market soon. They’re slimmer and sleeker than most tower speakers, but they don’t compromise on sound, with clear highs and distortion-free bass. Eye-catching yet unobtrusive, they’re also a stylish addition to any living room or home theater.

Nanu is all about taking a less-is-more approach. The brand currently offers two products, a mechanical counter and an alarm clock. We’ve been using the Arc alarm clock in our daily routine, and it might be the low-tech answer we’ve been waiting for. It has a sturdy metal build and a pleasing retro look, but the digital alarm adjustment is more precise than most analog alarm clocks. Yes, it’s pricey, but after accidentally breaking a series of cheap digital clocks (we’re not morning people), we’re glad to have something that feels built to last.

I’ve been lucky enough to test a lot of impressive audio products, but it’s rare to find one that truly blows me away. KEF’s R11 Meta fit the bill, delivering an exceptional amount of detail, even in rooms that are less than ideally set up.

As for sound, each speaker gets plenty loud thanks to the four bass drivers. There’s also an aluminum mid-range driver that houses a 1″ vented aluminum dome tweeter, which means the bass doesn’t overwhelm the mids or highs. Given their size and low impedance rating, you’ll want an appropriately powerful amplifier to power them.

They’re strikingly good-looking and are available in glossy black or white finishes, or more classic walnut. They are also, it should be noted, gigantic; getting them home and unboxing them was its own odyssey.

It won’t shock you that the R11 Meta speakers are an investment. They’re $3,500 (for one speaker; the pair will set you back $7,000), so I could hardly blame KEF for making sure the pair was returned promptly after I tried them. I might not be in the requisite tax bracket to justify buying these permanently, but they’ve earned a spot on my wishlist. —Jonathan Zavaleta

Why AI Could Change The Way Utilities Modernize Their Customer Information Systems

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Steve Dawson is the founder and CEO at POWERCONNECT.AI.

​For decades, electric, water and gas utilities have approached Customer Information System (CIS) modernization with a familiar strategy: When technology becomes outdated, replace it.

That decision can initiate lengthy procurement processes, complex data migrations, investments and years of implementation. Yet artificial intelligence (AI) offers another possibility: improving how employees and customers interact with enterprise systems without necessarily replacing the platforms responsible for billing millions of customers.

The next generation of CIS modernization may focus less on replacement and more on unlocking the value already embedded in utility technology.

Many utilities operate CIS platforms that have served them reliably for decades. These systems manage customer accounts, billing, payments, meter readings and service orders. They also contain rate structures, business rules and integrations that would be costly to replicate.

Nevertheless, customer service representatives often navigate multiple screens and applications to answer a question. The billing engine may work correctly while its user experience falls short of modern expectations. That distinction matters. Does a utility need an entirely new CIS or a better way to interact with the one it already has?

Application programming interfaces (APIs) let applications exchange information and perform authorized operations. A modern user interface can use APIs to retrieve billing histories, account balances, payment records, meter data and service requests, while the existing CIS remains the system of record.

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) adds another capability. This open standard enables AI applications to connect with external tools and data sources through a consistent interface. APIs supply underlying system functionality; MCP can expose approved capabilities to AI assistants and agents.

For example, a utility might offer tools for checking an account balance, reviewing consumption or retrieving service order status. An AI copilot could use those tools, subject to the employee’s permissions, without requiring a custom connection for every new AI experience.

MCP is not a replacement for APIs, and neither guarantees interoperability or security by itself. Utilities still need integration architecture, authentication, authorization, monitoring and clear ownership of each transaction.

Imagine a customer calling because a service transfer has not been completed. Today, a representative may need to open the CIS, locate a service order, check field activity and contact another department.

With a copilot embedded in a redesigned interface, the representative could ask: “What’s delaying this customer’s service transfer?”

The copilot could gather authorized information from connected systems, identify an outstanding inspection or pending work order and present the next step for review. It could also explain which source supports its answer, making the response easier to verify.

This approach can support billing inquiries, payment questions and high-consumption investigations. The objective is not to let AI invent answers or bypass established workflows. It is to reduce navigation and give employees useful context when needed.

Copilots generally help people interpret information and prepare actions. Agentic AI can go further by coordinating steps across systems. A governed agent might collect information needed for a service request, validate required fields, prepare an update and route it for approval. Connected through APIs or MCP tools, it could help bridge billing, payment and field operations applications.

But greater capability brings greater responsibility. High-impact actions should require approvals, with safeguards against incorrect data, unauthorized requests and unexpected behavior. Human oversight must match the operational consequences of the task.

Utilities handle sensitive customer information and operate critical services. Introducing AI should not mean copying entire customer databases into another platform.

Where architecture allows, an intelligent interface can request only the information necessary for an authorized task, leaving core records within existing systems. This may reduce duplication, although temporary processing, logging, vendors and model-provider retention policies still need careful review.

Security controls should include encryption, role-based access, audit trails, tool permissions, prompt-injection defenses and transaction validation. The same employee who cannot change an account in the CIS should not gain that power simply by asking an AI assistant.

A general-purpose chatbot may write fluent answers without understanding utility operations. Accurate billing explanations can depend on rate schedules, meter intervals, estimated reads, seasonal usage and local policies.

That is why AI must be grounded in authorized source data and utility-specific rules. Responses should distinguish verified facts from suggestions, and representatives should be able to inspect the evidence behind recommendations.

A polished answer is not necessarily a correct answer. For utilities, trust depends on traceability and operational accuracy.

Timing also matters. By the time an organization releases an RFP for a new CIS, leadership may already have determined that replacement is the answer. Alternative approaches can be harder to consider once requirements and budgets reflect that assumption.

Utilities should carefully assess API readiness, integration constraints, user experience, security and AI opportunities before deciding whether replacement is necessary. Some aging platforms genuinely lack vendor support, essential functionality or a viable security posture. Those systems may need replacement.

Others may support incremental improvements that preserve billing logic, minimize migration risk and provide value sooner. A disciplined assessment should compare both paths against outcomes, cost and maintainability.

The traditional CIS will likely remain central to accurate billing, financial transactions and customer records. What changes is the way people access those capabilities. Modern interfaces can simplify everyday work. APIs can connect systems. MCP can standardize access to approved AI tools. Copilots can explain information, while agentic workflows can coordinate controlled tasks.

Success should be measured by resolution times, accuracy, customer satisfaction, employee productivity and reliability, not by how much software gets replaced. Before asking, “When should we replace our CIS?” Utility leaders should ask, “What could we improve with systems we own?”

Modernization doesn’t begin not with replacement but rather with a more intelligent way to use what is already there.
