Things were going so well for Southampton in the Championship - right up until last couple of games before the international break.

The Saints had well and truly put their four-point deduction from before the season began right behind them and had climbed up the table under Tonda Eckert, but a draw at West Brom, then defeat at lowly Preston North End last week saw momentum slow right down prior to the two-week break.

That loss was an unexpected one, and an early injury to Keven Schlotterbeck may have disrupted the steady nature of the Southampton defence, which saw little-seen Nottingham Forest loanee Zach Abbott come on for a rare appearance alongside Jack Stephens.

Southampton pounced late on in the summer transfer window to bring Abbott to St Mary's Stadium, signing the 20-year-old London-born defender on a loan deal until the end of 2026-27.

We've hit the second international break of the season - how does Opta think the rest of the Championship campaign will play out?

Capable of playing at right-back and centre-back, Abbott played 10 times across the Premier League and UEFA Europa League for the Tricky Trees last season, but with Oliver Glasner adding more defenders to his ranks over the summer, the youngster was sanctioned for a loan away.

Southampton though have not found a starting spot for Abbott, who had to wait until the October 14 clash with Norwich City for his first start - only to pick up a first half yellow card and was taken off at half-time by Eckert.

Relegated back to the bench after that, Abbott was handed a chance due to Schlotterbeck's unfortunate shoulder injury at Deepdale, and that could lead to another start after the international break.

Whether Abbott will be at Southampton for much longer though remains to be seen.

As per a report from John Percy of The Telegraph, Nottingham Forest are considering bringing Abbott back to the City Ground when the January transfer window due to his lack of minutes currently at St Mary's.

That of course could now change, depending on how long Schlotterbeck will be sidelined for, but Abbott will likely be competing with Nathan Wood for the spot next to Stephens in Eckert's starting centre-back pairing.

Another Saints supporter believes that Abbott showed enough against Preston - despite being on the losing side - to be given more opportunities.

Not everyone is in agreement though, with this particular Southampton fan wanting two new defenders in, and for Abbott to be sent back to Forest.

Given Abbott didn't play a competitive game for Forest this season before being sent out on loan, there's the opportunity for him to head to a new club if a recalled is triggered.

There were plenty of clubs keen to hand him the chance of playing Championship football, with two of those being Millwall and Preston North End, as per Alan Nixon's reporting in August.

And whilst neither club got him for the first half of the season, there could be renewed approaches in January given their situations.

Starting Millwall right-back Tristan Crama is struggling with injuries, having been restricted to just five appearances this season, and Abbott could potentially slot in there, giving the Frenchman a chance to partner Jake Cooper at the heart of defence once more when fit.

As for Preston, they've been extremely leaky this season and sit at the bottom of the Championship standings, and bringing in any sort of fresh defender - whether it's on the left or right-hand side - could be beneficial for Graham Alexander when January comes around.

Britain’s coastal floods have a surprising connection to distant skies

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and Arctic help explain where extreme storm surges strike Britain’s coast and northwestern Europe.

Storm surges look like local disasters, but which stretch of Britain’s coast floods can often be traced to air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Those far-off patterns help decide whether northern or southern coasts take the high water.

A surge is hard to forecast, because wind, low air pressure, and the shape of the coast all play a part at once.

Understanding why surges hit certain places, such as East Anglia in eastern England, could give coastal towns earlier flood warnings.

Lisanne Blok of the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the British Antarctic Survey.

Her team studied more than 70 years of sea levels around northwestern Europe.

A computer model rebuilt those levels day by day from 1950 onward, using past weather data rather than tide gauge readings.

The team first removed the tides and the normal seasonal rise and fall of the sea. Then they used machine learning to sort the coastline into regions that tend to get extreme surges on the same days.

They ended up with six regions across the seas of northwestern Europe, plus eight smaller ones inside the North Sea.

“If we can identify conditions emerging in, for example, the Atlantic that might result in a storm surge in East Anglia, then we can pinpoint parts of the coast that should prepare for flooding,” said Blok.

Big, repeating pressure patterns over the ocean are what scientists call climate modes.

“Computer models used to forecast risk of coastal flooding don’t always account for large-scale climate modes, instead focusing on localized weather variables,” she said. “But what our findings tell us is that climate modes are really key.”

A storm surge builds when strong winds and low air pressure push a bulge of seawater toward land.

Sometimes it rises high enough to spill over flood defenses. If that happens at high tide, with big waves on top, the sea can reach extreme heights and flood the coast.

Over January 31 and February 1, 1953, a deep low-pressure system moved down the North Sea and pushed a huge mass of water along Britain’s east coast.

It arrived with a spring tide, the extra-high tide that comes around a new or full moon, and sea levels rose about 10 feet (3 meters) above predicted levels.

Floods swept through parts of Britain, the Netherlands, and Belgium.

Another surge struck Britain’s east coast on December 5 and 6, 2013, with water levels in some places higher than during the 1953 flood, but modern defenses prevented a disaster on that scale.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel shape, tightest between East Anglia and the Netherlands, pushes water toward land. Its shallow floor, roughly 30 to 490 feet (10 to 150 meters) deep, makes the surge taller.

On the worst 1% of days in the rebuilt records, surges added at least 22 inches (57 centimeters) to the North Sea’s water level, more than in any other region.

“The storm surges are very dynamic and complex in this region,” said Blok. “There is also heightened risk, because lots of people live along the low-lying coastal regions bordering the North Sea.”

Next, the team compared each region’s surges with those big pressure patterns.

“We can link regional storm surge extremes to very distinct climate patterns,” Blok said. “I didn’t expect such clear relationships, especially across the large distances involved.”

The strongest links were to pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere, including the North Atlantic Oscillation, a seesaw in air pressure between the regions around Iceland and the Azores.

When Iceland’s pressure is low compared with the Azores, the jet stream, a fast wind high up, moves north, and surges become more likely on northern and central coasts.

“As the storms shift to the north, they also deliver high seas to more northerly coasts,” said Blok.

When pressure over Iceland is unusually high instead, the pressure difference with the Azores shrinks.

The jet stream slows and bends south. Storms then take southern routes, bringing high seas to southern coasts.

Storms behind extreme surges off southwest England and west of Ireland tended to start near Newfoundland, off eastern Canada, thousands of miles away.

Storms behind surges farther north, in the seas toward Norway, more often began off Greenland or in the central North Atlantic.

Those southwestern surges were also more common in autumn, while the other regions saw most of theirs in winter.

Ali Mashayek, an associate professor in Cambridge’s Department of Earth Sciences, was the study’s senior author.

“Conditions in the Arctic have a big impact on UK weather,” he said. “That’s something we’ve known for a while, although we haven’t been able to link it to storm surge distribution before.”

Mashayek said Arctic warming will weaken the pressure difference that powers the jet stream, and “that means it is likely we will see a shift in UK storm patterns, including where storm surges occur, in future.”

But he added that “this is something we are still trying to understand.”

It’s time to view local weather extremes in a global context, Mashayek said. “We now have more evidence that the extreme weather in our backyard may be driven by what happens in very distant parts of the world.”

“This speaks to the broader issue that climate change and the extremes it induces are a truly global problem that need global solutions.”

The surge records left out waves and long-term sea level rise. The team also didn’t check whether the links change from season to season.

Next, the researchers want to run the same analysis on future climate projections, to see whether the surge regions shift as the planet warms.

The full study was published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is nearing its final destination more than 1 million miles (1.6 million kilometers) from Earth, where it will soon scour the universe for a host of cosmological treasures, including an estimated 100,000 new alien worlds.

But even before its mission truly begins, recent developments suggest the state-of-the-art spacecraft could take better images than — and remain operational for more than twice as long as — initially expected, further delighting already-excited astronomers.

"It is fantastic to learn that Roman is not just reaching its design goals but exceeding them," Eamonn Kerins, an astronomer at the University of Manchester in the U.K. who will help lead one of Roman's major surveys, told Live Science.

Roman is NASA's latest flagship space telescope, following in the footsteps of iconic observatories like the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). The $4 billion machine ‪—‬ which is over 42 feet (12.7 meters) long and weighs around the same as a fully grown Tyrannosaurus rex ‪—‬ successfully launched into space on Aug. 30, more than a decade after scientists started building it.

The telescope is now en route to Lagrange point 2 (L2), a fixed point relative to our planet where Earth's gravity and the sun's gravity cancel each other out. There, Roman will join several other important spacecraft, including JWST and the European Space Agency's Euclid telescope.

Roman was launched into space on board one of SpaceX's Falcon heavy rockets. It lifted off Aug. 30 from NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

During Roman's journey to L2, which is currently around 85% complete, mission scientists have remotely switched on the telescope's various instruments to test whether they function as expected. So far, this "commissioning process" has gone off without a hitch, and by the time Roman arrives in less than two weeks, it should be ready to snap its first in-depth images of the cosmos.

Researchers were already pumped to see what Roman will be able to do once it is in place and fully switched on. However, that excitement has further intensified in recent weeks, thanks to two postlaunch developments.

First, a series of near-perfect rocket burns, executed during and shortly after launch, saved more than a decade's worth of fuel that can now be used to extend Roman's mission. Second, after remotely adjusting the telescope's mirror, researchers discovered it should be capable of snapping images with around 30% greater detail than engineers predicted.

The team that designed, launched and remotely commissioned the orbital observatory has "done an amazing job" and "their work has ensured that Roman is more than up to the task," Kerins said.

Shortly after Roman arrived in space on board a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket that lifted off from NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida, mission scientists conducted two major burns, or engine firings, which gave the spacecraft enough momentum to escape Earth's orbit and make the journey to L2.

Roman is around the same size as a small truck and was packed into the nose cone of the Falcon Heavy rocket prior to launch.

Thanks to a combination of "exquisite planning by our orbital dynamics team" and "brilliant execution by the operations team," these burns were so precise that each one saved up to four years' worth of fuel, Jamie Dunn, director of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, said in a Sept. 14 statement.

On top of this, Roman ended up being much lighter than initially planned, which allowed engineers to pack another four years' worth of fuel into the spacecraft before launch. "Since Roman's [weight] was lower than we budgeted for, we were able to fill the propellant tanks to their capacity rather than only filling them as much as we needed," Alison Rao, the Roman propulsion lead at NASA Goddard, said in the statement.

In total, these savings could give Roman an additional 12 years of fuel, which could more than double the mission's planned 10-year lifespan. That means Roman could still be capturing images of the cosmos in 2048.

Given that Roman can collect data up to 1,000 times faster than Hubble, this extended lifespan will greatly enhance the amount of data the new space telescope will be able to capture. "With a 20-year Roman lifetime, we could, in theory, do over 20,000 years of Hubble survey work," Kerins said. "That's a lot of science!"

Roman is now on its way to L2, where the gravity of Earth and the sun cancel out, allowing it to remain in one place without expending much fuel.

This prolonged lifespan will let astronomers compare images of specific objects decades apart — such as expanding nebulas or colliding galaxies — allowing them to detect changes they otherwise might have missed.

It may also let them study objects or phenomena that we haven't even discovered yet, which is particularly exciting, Kerins noted. "The real show-stopper results will be new things we just aren't even thinking about right now," he said. "The possibilities are truly breathtaking."

There is also the chance that Roman's mission could be extended even further, because engineers often find clever ways of making active spacecraft last longer. For example, Hubble is more than two decades past its initial mission parameters, and the recently deceased Swift Observatory lasted 10 times longer than initially predicted before falling back to Earth last week.

Roman's secret weapon is arguably its Wide Field Instrument (WFI), made up of a 288-megapixel camera attached to a 7.9-foot (2.4 m) mirror, which will capture ultra-detailed images with a field of view roughly 100 times larger than Hubble's.

Roman's WFI consists of a 288-megapixel camera attached to a 7.9-foot (2.4 m) mirror. It will be capable of capturing images with a field of view 100 times greater than Hubble.

We have already seen the first WFI images — a series of blurry, green dots captured during a test of the instrument's 18 different light detectors ( see below). However, those photos were nothing like what WFI will capture in the future; those images will be so detailed that you would need dozens of high-definition TVs to properly display a single snap in full resolution, Kerins said.

After Roman completes the initial WFI commissioning phase, those images will be around 30% sharper than designers predicted. "All of the images from Roman's cosmic surveys will be even sharper than we expected," Alden Jurling, a senior optical engineer who works on Roman at NASA Goddard, said in an Oct. 14 statement. "It's a great outcome."

The improvements were first noticed after the WFI's car-sized mirror was realigned by just one-sixteenth the diameter of a human hair, demonstrating just how intricate the commissioning process has been. "Calibrating an instrument is like tuning a piano or guitar before a concert," Maxime Rizzo, a WFI systems engineer at NASA Goddard, said in the statement. "You adjust everything until it's just how you want it and then leave the settings alone so it performs consistently while you use it."

NASA released Roman's first photos on Sept. 15. Each small square represents one of the telescope's light detectors, and each blurry green spot is a distant star. (This is just a test image and does not represent how the telescope's proper photos will look.)

The improved image quality could really come to the fore during Roman's hunt for potentially habitable exoplanets, which is what Live Science's readers are most excited about for this mission.

Kerins, who is leading the Exoplanet Demographics Working Group for the Transits in the Roman Exoplanets Survey, is particularly excited about this development, but he said it does not necessarily guarantee that Roman will find more exoplanets than initially predicted.

The rest of Roman's commissioning phase is also proceeding as planned.

Roman's coronagraph instrument, which will block out the light from distant stars to allow WFI to spot alien worlds, has passed its main commissioning phase and is working as intended, according to a NASA blog post.

Roman has completed around 85% of its journey to L2 and should arrive at its final destination in the next few weeks. (The timeline in this image is a screenshot from Roman's commissioning process website, captured on Nov. 11.)

Meanwhile, a global network of ground stations, which will receive up to 1.4 terabytes of data from Roman every day, has established contact with the spacecraft and is receiving signals as expected, another blog post revealed.

All of these early wins raise hopes that Roman can have a long and successful career, and are a testament to the years of work that went into designing the spacecraft and planning the mission.

"What we're seeing now with the actual optical performance and estimated lifetime of Roman is just how good that design process has been," Kerins said. "It's a remarkable achievement. I can't wait to see what Roman is going to give us."

The game show Family Feud pits two families against each other in a race to find the most popular answers to silly questions like “Name something you might find in a bathtub.” If you guessed “water” and 50 of the 100 people who had been surveyed had also chosen that answer, you would get those points. If you answered “arachnid,” you might get zero points. Even though many children learned the "Itsy-Bitsy Spider" song growing up, "arachnid" would probably not be one of the most-popular answers.

Creative, unconventional, divergent thinkers might be at a disadvantage on Family Feud. Yet research demonstrates that generating creative, original thought and activity can make you happier (Tan, et al. 2021).

Moderate stress, like what you might feel when taking a test, can improve our convergent thinking—the type of thinking that's required when we want to find the right answer to a question or a problem. However, stress can stifle divergent, out-of-the-box, creative problem-solving. Divergent thinking involves generating multiple novel ideas from multiple perspectives and/or combinations of perspectives (Guo et al., 2024). When we feel looser, freer, and more relaxed, we can access our creative mind more readily.

Creativity can lower stress by offering outlets for strong emotions. Poets, authors, songwriters, dancers, and other artists find that strong emotions can fuel creativity as they strive to transform pain into beauty. Psychotherapists often recommend creative writing, journaling, and other artistic outlets as a therapeutic intervention. Creativity can increase well-being, providing opportunities to feel more fully alive and joyful (Tan, et al. 2021).

Every month, a group of friends gathers at our home for a musical jam session. We improvise songs, switch instruments, and often end by drumming on percussion instruments. This shared creativity strengthens our friendship and our sense of playfulness. Engaging in creative arts with others reduces loneliness and supports social connection, an essential requirement for happiness (Perkins, et al. 2021).

Many young people have formed deep friendships from Dungeons and Dragons, Magic: The Gathering, and similar role-playing games that require creative imagination, divergent thinking, and sometimes even elaborate costume creations.

Studies show that group-based arts and creativity programs can improve the health, well-being, and quality of life of older people. These activities strengthen social ties, encourage an active imagination, and promote physical activity, all essential for a healthy life (Sowerby, et al. 2026).

When I suggest to clients that they might improve their happiness by finding a creative outlet, I often hear, “But I’m not a creative person.” I usually ask them a few questions like, “Have you ever substituted a different ingredient in a recipe when you didn’t have the right ingredients? Or have you ever had to try different ways to teach your child how to tie shoelaces?” Typically, people can think of several times in the recent past when they have been creative.

Author, psychotherapist, and writing coach Marni Freedman writes in her delightful new book, Artistic Badassery, “The latest research on creativity from both a psychological and a neuroscientific perspective shows that creativity is not an inborn gift or a fixed trait, but rather a set of skills that can be developed through practice, effort, and the right conditions.”

Like any mental muscle, creativity needs exercise to develop. If you’re just learning to play guitar, you may not yet have enough musical vocabulary to improvise. Yet you can write a song after learning only three chords. All you need is an idea and a melody. It can be fun to create something original, even if it wouldn’t pass critical muster. Try making something just for the fun of it, without criticizing the outcome. Let yourself play in the sandbox of creativity. It might make you happier.

I think there should be a Family Feud game for divergent thinkers. You would try to pick the most novel answers to the questions asked, and the person who picks logical items that no one else chooses wins. Here’s a fun question to start: “What items might be found in a national ballroom?”
