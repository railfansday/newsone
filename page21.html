DW Science explainer on the default mode network: what your brain does when you aren't focusing on anything, and why it's important.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Doing nothing might be the ultimate brain hack. When you're bored, the brain goes into default mode network. It's not only healthy for you, but it may also shape the very essence of your identity.

You know when you lock your phone and the screen goes black? It seems like it's not doing anything. But it's not doing nothing. It's syncing your messages, updating apps, and running other background processes.

Imagine you're sitting on a bus, staring out of a window, taking a shower or walking, and you get that feeling of doing nothing and letting your mind drift?

Well, your mind, the brain, is doing a lot when it feels like you're doing nothing.

It's going over your memories, shaping your sense of who you are, imagining possible futures, helping you understand other people, and connecting ideas.

That's your brain's default mode network (DMN).

Scientists conducted brain-imaging experiments in the 1990s and early 2000s to find out which regions of the brain were active during demanding tasks, like solving a math problem or memory exercise.

But to measure that, they needed a so-called baseline. They needed to know the level of brain activity at rest and during demanding tasks.

So, between the tasks, they asked participants to relax, close their eyes, stare at a fixed point on a wall, or try not to think of anything at all.

And they measured the participants' brain activity both when they were solving a task and doing nothing. That's how scientists noticed the brain was active in up- and downtime.

They noticed that when participants started to focus on a new task, after having done nothing, some brain areas lit up and other areas dimmed down. That suggests those areas that dimmed down must have been active while the participant was apparently — physically and mentally — at rest.

This realization eventually led to the discovery of the default mode network or DMN.

The DMN is crucial because it helps your brain make sense of your life and experiences.

It helps you connect and revisit memories, reflect on who you are, imagine what might happen in future scenarios, understand other people, build meaning from experience, and connect ideas in new ways.

It turns all your memories, experiences, knowledge, emotions and social information into your internal story. Think of that story as a constantly evolving, updated model of who you are and how you engage with the world around you.

But it's important to understand the DMN involves more than one area of the brain. It is a group of brain regions that light up when you're not focused on an external task.

There's another player in your brain that acts as a monitoring system. You can think of it as constantly asking "Is there anything that needs my attention?"

Imagine you're daydreaming while driving, and a car suddenly swerves into your lane.

In a fraction of a second, your daydream disappears and your attention snaps back to the road. That player is called the salience network (SN).

The salience network helps you notice important signals, like a sound, a mistake, pain, an emotion, a threat, or something happening in your body, and helps shift the brain toward attention and control systems when action is needed.

The DMN, SN and attention/control systems constantly interact.

And the DMN doesn't just turn off completely with every hard task. Parts of it can also contribute when tasks involve memory, stories, meaning, or other people.

Smartphones have filled many of the small empty moments of our days — waiting for the bus, standing in line, eating alone, lying on the couch. We are constantly looking at our devices.

So, it seems reasonable to ask: are we crowding out those moments of boredom where DMN kicks in?

Maybe, but, so far, there's no strong evidence that scrolling directly affects mental health by reducing DMN time.

What we do know is that problematic smartphone use can increase stress, anxiety, task-switching, and disrupt sleep.

And sleep loss and stress also have clearer evidence for altering DMN-related brain connectivity.

When your mind wanders, it might produce a great new idea.

But it might also replay an embarrassing thing you did five years ago. Again. And again.

In depression, the self-reflection part of the DMN can get caught in repetitive negative loops that are hard to stop — what's known as rumination.

Scientists have also found that Alzheimer's disease targets key DMN areas, particularly those responsible for your autobiographical memory and remembering other aspects of the past. Which may help explain why memory problems are so central to the disease.

Protect your sleep and take stress seriously. Sleep loss and stress have direct evidence for disrupting DMN-related connectivity.

Take real quiet breaks after mentally demanding tasks, like studying. It can even help you memorize facts for your exam.

Try to reframe boredom as this essential, energy-intensive active work in your brain that builds your internal narrative and sense of self, consolidates memories, creates connections and sparks creativity.

Try waiting in line without checking your phone. Look out the window next time you take the bus or take a walk without listening to anything.

You can also try scheduling DMN time. It may be the closest thing to a real brain hack.

Disney's Legendary 218-Episode Sci-Fi Series Is the Perfect Anti-'Lanterns' Binge

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

All 218 episodes of this classic sci-fi mystery series are available to stream for free

The fate of Lanterns season 2, and the DC Universe in general, has never been murkier. With the sudden ousting of DC Studios' co-CEOs James Gunn and Peter Safran, the status of HBO's hit show series about two mismatched Green Lanterns who get paired together to solve an intergalactic mystery is up in the air like never before. Season 1 was a critical hit that captured the zeitgeist, and Lanterns co-creator Chris Mundy had already assembled a season 2 writers' room. But in a recent interview, Mundy said the odds of the series continuing were likely "50/50.”

That's not great, but thankfully, there's a perfect replacement waiting in the wings that flips the Lanterns formula on its head — and it's got an almost unlimited supply of episodes. That show is The X-Files, and even though it also features aliens and a pair of odd-couple cops, it couldn’t be more different.

Lanterns was a bold new take on DC Comics’ various Green Lantern heroes. While the character has most often been associated with grand, operatic space adventures, Lanterns was a grittier, more grounded affair. Hal Jordan (Kyle Chandler) is the cocky, go-with-your-gut veteran superhero who was selected to be Earth’s first Green Lantern, part of a universal protection force whose members use powerful green rings that can generate pretty much whatever the wearer can imagine out of green light.

Tired of Jordan’s rule-breaking behavior, the Guardians — the alien beings who oversee the Green Lantern Corps — select serious-minded ex-Marine John Stewart (Aaron Pierre) to become Hal’s partner, which is really just a ruse to have Stewart replace Jordan. Together, the two investigate a deadly incident in the small town of Rushville, Nebraska.

The X-Files also starts out with a bit of a ruse. The show is about a pair of FBI agents who investigate cases involving paranormal phenomena. The cases are referred to as “the X-Files” and they’ve become the obsession of Agent Fox Mulder (David Duchovny), who has a strong belief in aliens and other supernatural elements. Prior to the events of the series, Mulder had been a hotshot criminal profiler in the FBI, and the higher–ups at the agency are now looking to bring him back into the fold by giving him a partner who will debunk his work on the X-Files. They select Agent Dana Scully (Gillian Anderson), a trained medical doctor who believes in hard science, and is wildly skeptical about the wilder elements of Mulder's work.

For both Lanterns and The X-Files — and pretty much all media about mismatched cops — the two partners gain a begrudging respect for each other. In Lanterns, Jordan and Stewart come to admire one another's bravery and sense of honor. On The X-Files, Mulder learns to appreciate the tough questions Scully asks, while Scully comes to realize that Mulder’s theories and beliefs aren’t quite as wild as she’d originally thought. Mulder and Scully also develop a romantic relationship over time, but that takes several seasons to come to fruition and it’s done really organically.

While I realize that I’m pointing out a lot of the similarities between the two shows, they have vastly different viewing experiences. Lanterns is just eight episodes and they’re all dedicated to one case. In contrast, The X-Files ran for 11 seasons, totaling 218 episodes. While the show has a central mythology that extends through the series, the vast majority of episodes are procedural, with new standalone cases every week. The series also covers a wide variety of subject matter. Sometimes it’s about aliens, sometimes it’s about ghosts or urban legends or elements of the occult. There’s even an episode where Mulder is seduced by a vampire. (I recommend you skip that one, since it’s pretty terrible.)

The X-Files also has a very different tone. While Lanterns has a moodiness to it inspired by the likes of True Detective, The X-Files had a lot of variety. The show can be deadly serious in one episode, then lean into more of an action/thriller vibe for the next one. Some episodes are even quite funny. The genre-hopping all works really well too, thanks to the chemistry between Duchovny and Anderson. As wild, or as heavy, as the show gets, their partnership is the show’s anchor and that’s why the show lasted as long as it did and why it became a monster success in the 1990s.

In a way, The X-Files is kind of an inverted mirror of Lanterns. With The X-Files, the FBI agents are the most grounded thing about the show, while the cases themselves were more outlandish. In Lanterns, the Green Lanterns and their power set is the most fantastic element, whereas the case is somewhat more grounded. Even with the Manhunter component, Lanterns is interestingly low to the ground.

Plus, there are no power rings in The X-Files, which is a pretty big thing in Lanterns.

Germany slips in climate protection rankings as other countries make gains

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Record renewable energy growth is driving climate progress in some countries, but Germany and many major emitters are falling behind.

Germany has slipped down the climate action ranking for the third consecutive year, with the former green leader now being outperformed by Brazil, Columbia and Romania.

In its worst performance since 2019, the country fell to place 25 and received a "medium" rating overall on the yearly Climate Change Performance Index (CCPI), which assesses efforts to curb global warming in 64 countries plus the European Union.

Still, "a number of countries have been making good progress for years," said Jan Burck, CCPI lead author in a statement. Denmark is leading the way across all four categories assessed — energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, renewable energy and national climate policy. A total of 15 states, including Lithuania, Chile, and Morocco, received an overall "high" rating, which is two more than last year.

The European Union also moved up a spot, while Hungary jumped 21 places after the defeat of former prime minister Viktor Orban's government, which had repeatedly vetoed EU climate plans, dismissing them as a "utopian fantasy."

The top three positions on the CCPI remained vacant again this year, as no country has yet done enough to meet global climate goals aimed at preventing worsening weather extremes and climate damage.

"We are seeing a striking contrast," said Burck. "Progress on climate action is often slow and in some countries, such as the United States, things are even moving backwards. But technologically and economically there is enormous momentum."

That momentum is apparent in record solar energy growth. Last year 75% of global electricity demand growth was met by the green energy source, according to energy think tank Ember's latest global electricity review. Investment in clean energy has also outpaced that in fossil fuels for multiple years.

The rapid expansion of renewable technology, as well as the Iran War and a reduction in deforestation are behind a projected slight fall in emissions for the first time since the COVID-19 pandemic, according to the CCPI.

"Governments have to act now to turn the momentum into sustained emissions reductions," said CCPI co-author Niklas Höhne of the NewClimate Institute, which compiles the report with environmental NGO Germanwatch.

Burck attributed Germany's worst ever performance in the category of national climate policy to current policies being pursued by Germany's economy minister Katherina Reiche.

"Measures that primarily aim to increase the use of natural gas, threaten to undermine progress in the energy transition and risk significantly worsening the conditions for renewable energy and electrification for many years to come," said Burck.

A European Commission report published on November 9 on the sidelines of the UN COP31 climate summit in Turkey said Germany was one of nine EU countries set to miss 2030 emissions reductions targets.

Germany is not alone in its disappointing ranking. The other G20 countries — the world's leading industrialized and emerging economies, responsible for around 80% of global CO2 emissions — also perform poorly.

The only exception among them is the United Kingdom, which ranks seventh and received a high rating, performing well in greenhouse gas emissions, energy use and climate policy. However, study authors rated it low for renewable energy expansion.

The G20's oil- and gas-producing countries occupy some of the lowest positions on the climate action table. Iran, the United States, Russia, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Canada all languish at the bottom of the league, with an overall rating of "very low."

The hosts of this year's COP31, also received a "low" overall score. Australia, a major coal exporter with some of the world's highest per-capita emissions, ranks 54th, while Turkey, which is also heavily dependent on fossil fuels, comes in at 53rd.

China is the world's largest emitter of greenhouse gases and ranks 56th in the CCPI, receiving an overall rating of "very low." Its main weaknesses include its now high per-capita emissions and substantial energy consumption.

However, among the G20, China leads the way in renewable energy expansion. The country, which is five years ahead of schedule in reaching its goal of adding 1,200 GW of wind and solar capacity to the grid by 2030, also produces over 80% of the world's solar panels.

Several oil-producing countries, including Kazakhstan, Saudi Arabia, Uzbekistan and the United Arab Emirates, are also performing better than expected in the renewables category and "are significantly expanding their previously limited" capacity, said the report.

The authors said these countries have significant potential to further expand renewables.

"In the Arab states in particular, very high per-capita emissions and an above-average level of electrification coincide with a relatively low existing share of renewable energy and enormous solar potential," said co-author Natalie Pelekh in a statement. "These are, in fact, ideal conditions for more ambitious climate action."
