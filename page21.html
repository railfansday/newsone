Macao: Ex-Lawmaker Gets 12.5 Years After Unfair Trial

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Former lawmaker and veteran pro-democracy activist Au Kam San during an interview in Macao, October 7, 2024. © 2024 AP Photo/Bertha Wang

(London) – A court in Macao, a special administrative region of China, sentenced the former lawmaker and democracy activist Au Kam San to 12.5 years in prison on November 6, 2026, on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. 

Macao’s Court of First Instance, in the first publicly known case under the city’s national security law, convicted Au on five charges involving “subversion of state power,” “establishing links with foreign forces to endanger national security,” and “violation of secrecy.” The Macao government should quash the sentence and immediately and unconditionally release Au.

“The Macao authorities’ bogus prosecution of former lawmaker Au Kam San reflects Beijing’s escalating repression across its territories,” said Maya Wang, deputy Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “The sentence, which could keep the democracy advocate in prison until he’d be past 80, is both outrageous and cruel.”

The court did not release the full judgment, but only a summary of its decision. It ruled that Au worked with unspecified overseas “hostile” organizations “over an extended period” to disseminate materials advocating subversion and inciting hatred against the Chinese government, as well as to provoke hostile foreign action against China and disrupt Macao’s 2024 election for chief executive.

It also found that, while serving as a lawmaker in 2021, he disclosed confidential information about casino license renewals from a legislative committee meeting to overseas organizations. The court did not identify the organizations or substantiate the charges in detail, but claimed Au did not dispute most of the facts.

Au’s prosecution involved serious violations of his right to a fair trial, Human Rights Watch said. He had been detained since July 2025. 

In July 2026, his family told reporters that the authorities had held him incommunicado and denied him access to the lawyer his family found for him. Au was instead represented by a court-appointed lawyer, who had undergone the government’s required national security vetting, the court statement said. Article 36 of Macao’s functional constitution, the Basic Law, guarantees residents access to legal counsel to protect their lawful rights and interests. In its November statement, the court said Au’s family had been allowed to visit him “regularly.”

Au was tried in a closed-door hearing over three days, from September 16 to 18. A law that Macao adopted in March permits closing court proceedings to the public when Macao’s national security committee determines that national security is at risk. The committee’s decisions are not subject to appeal or judicial review.

Au, a Portuguese and European Union citizen and former primary school teacher, was one of Macao’s longest-serving pro-democracy lawmakers. Directly elected to the Legislative Assembly from 2001 to 2021, he was one of its few consistent voices for democratic reform, government accountability, and social causes.

He campaigned for 15 years for free public education and more public housing, scrutinized government spending, and raised concerns about corruption and the misuse of public funds. After leaving the legislature, he remained engaged in public affairs, including efforts to protect Macao’s historic Guia Lighthouse.

Au also played a major role in Macao’s small civil society. For 30 years, his group, the Macao Union of Democratic Development (澳門民主發展聯委會), organized annual vigils commemorating the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre in Beijing, even as he and fellow organizers experienced abuse for their efforts, including physical assaults and loss of jobs.

Macao was a Portuguese colony until 1999, when its sovereignty was transferred to the People’s Republic of China. Macao’s government has long suppressed critical voices. It has arbitrarily arrested opposition figures and journalists, severely restricted protests, and barred Hong Kong pro-democracy activists and journalists from entering the territory.

The crackdown on political opposition and civil society has intensified in recent years, Human Rights Watch said. Authorities disqualified 21 people in 2021 and 12 in 2025 from running for legislative seats, citing vague reasons such as “disloyalty” to Macao. In 2021, Macao’s Court of Final Appeal upheld the government’s arbitrary ban on the Tiananmen Massacre vigils, ruling that the organizers’ political slogans violated the Chinese Constitution and Macao’s Basic Law by urging the public to “overthrow the existing political system.”

Macao’s draconian Law on Safeguarding National Security, enacted in 2009 and amended in 2023, undermines the rule of law and human rights guarantees enshrined in the Basic Law, and contravenes the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which applies to Macao through the Basic Law. The 2023 amendments extended the National Security law to nonviolent acts; previously, only violent acts were punishable. 

The European Union has condemned Au’s conviction and sentencing, and Portugal has raised the case directly with the Chinese authorities. 

“Portugal, the European Union, and other concerned governments should publicly and forcefully press for Au Kam San’s immediate and unconditional release,” Wang said. “Countries should take note of the recent harsh sentences handed down in Hong Kong and Macao and consider what that means for their long-term relationship with the Chinese government.”

China exports, imports soar past expectations in October — surplus narrows sharply

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

China's exports and imports surged more than expected in October, while its surplus shrank sharply, undershooting expectations even as it crossed $1 trillion for the year so far.

Goods exports soared by 35.2% last month from a year earlier, according to the official data released Tuesday, exceeding expectations for a 33.5% jump in a Reuters poll, but slowing from the 36.3% growth in September.

Imports climbed by 41.6% last month, customs data showed, jumping from 31% in September and sharply beating estimates for a 32% rise by analysts polled by Reuters.

The monthly surplus dropped to $105.3 billion in October, the lowest level since April, according to LSEG data, with the year-to-date figure at $1.04 trillion, compared with a record $1.2 trillion for the full year 2025. Calls from trading partners have grown louder for Beijing to boost domestic demand and balance its economy.

The strength in exports has allowed policymakers to continue relying on moderate policy support to meet the country's annual growth target of 4.5%-5%. Household consumption has remained soft, with retail sales growing a modest 1.1% in September, underscoring the imbalance in the world's second-largest economy.

Much of the surge in exports reflects robust demand for its chips and other artificial intelligence-related hardware. But any slowdown in the global AI buildout would risk denting the momentum and squeezing Chinese exporters' margins.

A separate survey by China Beige Book showed new domestic and export orders for goods slowed in October compared to a year earlier. Factory activity also unexpectedly shrank last month, according to official PMI data, as overseas orders cooled. "The stronger side of the economy got weaker," the New York-headquartered research firm said in a note on Oct. 30.

Washington and Beijing agreed in September to extend a broader trade truce by two months to January, along with a slew of other trade measures, including export-control waivers for certain critical minerals and special port fees for each other's vessels.

Chinese President Xi Jinping and President Donald Trump are expected to meet for a third time this year at the APEC leaders' summit in Shenzhen, China next week.

The European Union also reached an agreement with Beijing last month to limit the imports of Chinese hybrid vehicles over the next few years, as part of the effort to reduce its trade deficit with Beijing.

But the auto import curbs, taking effect on Dec.1, would barely contain China's export boom, according to analysts.

The cap of 350,000 imported cars per year is above the 300,000 hybrids that China exported to Europe last year, though well below the 800,000 units in annualized pace over the last three months, according to Brad Setser, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations.

"Europe still is a long way from a coherent trade and industrial policy with respect to autos, let alone a coherent economic policy towards overall trade with China," said Setser, a former U.S. Treasury official who specializes in global trade and capital flows.

The agency just unveiled new lunar missions, a nuclear-powered rover and $10 billion in contracts to build a moon base for astronauts.

NASA just wrapped up its Max Power event, the agency’s first-ever aerospace technology expo that took place Wednesday through Sunday at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida. Featuring discussions, panels, announcements and technology demonstrations, the biggest part of the event was NASA’s update on the Ignition initiative, which promises to put a base on the moon by 2032.

The Moon Base Program is an expensive endeavor that requires the help of various space companies to complete on time. Max Power discussed the various technologies and projects to make a base work, including the new CLPS 2 contract, a nuclear-powered moon rover and other lunar architecture.

NASA will rely heavily on commercial aerospace companies for many of these components: Blue Origin, Firefly Aerospace, Intuitive Machines, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, SpaceX, Voyager Technologies and ispace-US. Most of those companies have also been supplying tech for the Artemis program, which is slated to launch its third mission in 2027 and send people back to the moon in 2028. While the Artemis missions are important in their own right, NASA is also using them as a precursor to the Moon Base. That’s one of many reasons why it was a huge deal when Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket exploded in May.

“We are building the moon base mission by mission,” NASA administrator Jared Isaacman said during Max Power. “When our astronauts return to the moon, they will arrive with the first elements of the moon base already waiting for them on the surface.”

The CLPS 2 contract is part of NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services initiative, which plays a pivotal role in the moon base’s construction. The goal is to send materials and equipment to the moon ahead of time, so humans can immediately start building the base when they arrive.

The CLPS 2 contract builds on the CLPS 1.0 contract to develop the infrastructure and vehicles, including cargo landers, necessary to continuously ferry supplies and crew between Earth and the lunar surface. To fund these efforts, NASA paired the $10 billion CLPS 2 budget with an expanded CLPS 1.0 budget, which increased from $2.6 billion to $4.2 billion.

The cargo landers get cargo from space to the moon’s surface, but they also have to be transported there. NASA announced $1.3 billion in funding to Blue Origin, Arrow Science and Technology and Impulse Space to perform this step. They’ll make Orbital Transfer Vehicles, or OTVs, that act like “long-distance freight trucks” to carry the cargo landers and eventually deploy them safely to the moon’s surface.

In addition to building the vehicles that do this, NASA says three missions are slated for completion. First, research must assess how to reduce human risk and determine the best ways to deploy the CLPS landers. Second, NASA’s MoonFall drones will assess the moon and find the most optimal spot for a base. The third mission is to test the infrastructure to ensure it works as designed.

NASA took some time to talk about Promise, also known as Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping and In-Situ Exploration, an important vehicle for early moon exploration. The agency awarded Blue Origin a $94 million development contract, plus a performance-based $140 million for lunar delivery. Once deployed, the nuclear-powered rover will be managed by NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory at Caltech, which oversees NASA’s other rovers, NASA’s Deep Space Network and the Voyager probes.

Unlike solar-powered rovers, Promise’s nuclear core allows it to operate continuously through two-week lunar nights and in permanently shadowed regions where the sun never reaches. Its goal is to analyze regolith, hunt for ice and conduct general exploration.

While most of NASA’s recent announcements focused on getting hardware to the moon, the final segment laid the groundwork for surface infrastructure, specifically, long-term habitability so astronauts can actually be there.

To power this presence, NASA awarded contracts for energy storage and transmission to Honeybee Robotics (a Blue Origin company) and Yank Technologies. Their solar arrays, batteries and wireless power hardware will culminate in the Mini-Communications and Solar Tower (miniCast), assembled at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, before being deployed to the moon.

NASA is also trying to tackle the myriad problems that must be solved for a moon base to succeed. One of the initiatives announced at Max Power is the Moon Base Student Challenge, which asks students to help build humanity’s future on the moon, with $1 million in prizes for winners with the best ideas.

“We are inviting students from across the country to help solve some of the problems that stand between humanity and a permanent presence on another world,” Isaacman said during his speech, which was packed with students and kids, according to Space.com.

While the initiative isn’t aimed at anything specific, it challenges students to tackle whatever they can regarding lunar base problems. NASA has tapped students before to help solve problems such as dealing with lunar dust clouds and human-capable landers.
