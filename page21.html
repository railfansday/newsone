As someone who’s used Letterboxd religiously for quite a few years now, the best way to explain it to people who've not heard of Letterboxd (they do exist, especially if you venture outside the online world!) is to say it’s like Goodreads, but for movies instead of books. And most people have heard of Goodreads, even if they don’t themselves have accounts there.

If you're not in the know, then it basically lets you keep track of what you're reading, what you’ve read, and what you'd still like to read. You can rate titles, review them if you want, or even “DNF” them (marking something as “did not finish”). The site is a bit of a popularity contest, sure, but it’s interesting to see what appeals to people, with the following fantasy books all being quite loved on Goodreads, and more than 100,000 ratings each, not to mention generally high average ratings across the board, too.

His Dark Materials was a trilogy that’s since grown into a series, though it’s the first three books that are most essential. The Subtle Knife is the second, and probably the strongest chapter of that trilogy, since it skillfully adds another central character to the proceedings while effectively continuing on from the surprising way the first book (Northern Lights) ended, in relation to the characters who were established in that initial novel.

The third book, The Amber Spyglass. is kind of chaotic, but still interesting, and the way the overall trilogy works in some science fiction elements alongside the fantasy as things go along is also pretty great. All have well over 100,000 ratings on Goodreads, and the only one that might cause some disappointment is The Amber Spyglass, and even then, probably only the final chunk of The Amber Spyglass, so it’s kind of a case of two-and-a-half out of three ain't bad.

Things sure do happen in The Master and Margarita, and it’s hard to call the book boring in any way, but then it’s harder to try to summarize all those things that do happen and articulate how those things converge into something potentially definable as a plot. It’s the kind of fantasy that’s heavy on surrealism and extremely light on explanations for why anything is happening, outside of saying “Oh, this madness is happening because it’s making some kind of satirical point about x.”

And all that can be lost on you, if you're not knowledgeable about the history of the Soviet Union and maybe some other things (The Master and Margarita was written a few decades before it was ultimately published, and so knowing about the Soviet Union in the 1930s is more helpful than knowing its history closer to the middle of the century). Crazy things happen, you will feel like you're hallucinating at multiple points, and maybe it gets labeled as “fantasy” because it’s hard to know what other genre to put this one into.

It is The Hobbit, or The Hobbit, or There and Back Again if you want to use its full title and all, so of course it’s worth mentioning for present purposes. J.R.R. Tolkien sure did write another legendary fantasy book that has an even stronger reputation, and that one will be addressed shortly, but The Hobbit, or There and Back Again is like a prologue or introduction to the whole of Middle-earth, but even if it weren't, it’d still be an extremely compelling work of fantasy in its own right.

It has more than 4.6 million ratings on Goodreads, and then it has a very strong overall score of 4.3/5, so all that really just further cements how readable and essential this is.

There’s a Hobbit here, and then a quest, plus some gold, and a dragon, so, like, lots of expected fantasy things, but the execution is what counts, and then there’s also the influential nature of The Hobbit, or There and Back Again to take into account. It has more than 4.6 million ratings on Goodreads, and then it has a very strong overall score of 4.3/5, so all that really just further cements how readable and essential this is as a fantasy novel.

As is the case for most series, there are fewer ratings given out the further The Dark Tower goes along… but it is curious that the seventh book has a few more ratings overall than the sixth book. The idea of reading the seventh book without the sixth is wild, since the sixth book is almost entirely set-up for all the bombastic things that happen in book #7, but either some people forgot to rate the sixth book, or they treated their rating for The Dark Tower VII as a rating for the entire saga.

If they're in the latter camp, that’s almost understandable, to be honest. The Dark Tower VII is such a grand finale to a weird and compelling series, and it represents The Dark Tower at its best, strangest, and most ambitious. It is one of Stephen King’s best books, without a doubt, and even if you’ve heard parts of it are, um, kind of divisive (to say the least), you still shouldn’t let that put you off finishing your own journey to the Dark Tower.

Like His Dark Materials, Mistborn was a trilogy that later became a whole series, whether you want to judge the Mistborn series as its own thing (thanks to a sequel trilogy and some other stuff), or whether you want to look at the whole of Brandon Sanderson’s Cosmere saga as one massive series. Of that original trilogy, The Final Empire is the strongest entry, with its real sense of unpredictability and the way it works as something of a heist narrative with a fantasy setting.

If you do like The Final Empire, then the chances of you liking books #2 and #3 are strong, too. And for now, Sanderson can only really be appreciated through books, but movie and/or TV adaptations of some of his works are pretty much inevitable (watch this space and all). Anyway, as for Goodreads, 1.1 million ratings, an average score of 4.48/5… yeah, honestly, fair enough.

More than a million people have rated The Odyssey on Goodreads, and it’s fitting that something so old and influential has been widely read. Maybe a fair few of those ratings came about because people were made to read it in high school, sure, but there’s a lot here worth studying and analyzing, to put it mildly. Whether to even call it a book, though, in the traditional sense is something you might be able to debate.

Well, it exists as a book now, even if it was a story that was originally formed and told (rather than written down) orally. You can keep the tradition alive, to some extent, by listening to a recording of The Odyssey rather than reading it, because it’s one of those rare instances where listening to a book rather than reading it is technically an experience more in line with how you're “supposed” to take in the text.

The Stand is another Stephen King book, sure, but this is a pretty different one from The Dark Tower VII, even if both The Stand and The Dark Tower (as a series) feature Randall Flagg, admittedly. The Dark Tower was a bit of every genre, including dark fantasy, whereas The Stand… okay, The Stand also belongs to a good many genres, just not really the Western one, nor a few others.

It’s still very much dark fantasy, though, leaning more into that than science fiction, even if the majority of books about the end of the world (and what comes after) do so with a sci-fi spin rather than a fantasy one. The Stand is also huge, especially if you read the uncut version published in 1990, yet it’s incredibly rewarding and makes those hundreds upon hundreds of pages fly by real fast.

Any of the three parts on their own would be worthy of putting in the top spot here, and then if you add all those parts together, and read The Lord of the Rings as one epic, then yeah, obviously it’s even more worthy of topping a ranking like this one.

All the parts have had more than 1,000,000 ratings each, and each of those three parts has a high average rating overall. If you find The Lord of the Rings as a single volume on Goodreads, then the average rating there is also extremely high, at 4.55/5, and there are more than 700,000 users who've rated it as one complete book. Beyond iconic, essential as both literature and entertainment, timeless, yada-yada-yada, all that stuff can be said because it all applies. It’s The Lord of the Rings, you know?

What Zinedine Zidane Told Marco Materazzi Years After 2006 World Cup Final

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Marco Materazzi has revealed what was said in the first interaction he had with Zinedine Zidane after the Frenchman headbutted the Italian in the 2006 World Cup final.

France and Italy met at the Olympiastadion in Berlin in football's showpiece event, with les Blues bidding to earn a second star on their shirt, while Gli Azzurri were aiming for their first triumph in the competition since 1982. Zidane opened the scoring from the penalty spot just seven minutes into the clash, before Materazzi levelled proceedings with a fine header from Andrea Pirlo's corner.

Following an intense opening 90 minutes, the game went to extra time. With the encounter poised at 1-1 with nine minutes left, the momentum shifted in Itay's direction when Zidane was sent off for aggressively headbutting Materazzi. The referee and the linesman revealed that they didn't spot the moment when the former Real Madrid midfielder's head collided with the defender.

The incident was instead seen by the fourth official, Medina Cantalejo, who advised referee Horacio Elizondo to show Zidane the red card.

This notorious moment ultimately cost the 1998 Ballon d'Or winner, who wasn't available to take a penalty in the shootout, which Italy went on to win 5-3.

Zidane faced backlash for his poor temperament in the 2006 World Cup final, while Materazzi was also blamed for provoking the France international.

This hasty moment understandably left both players sour, and according to the Inter Milan legend, the pair wouldn't speak to each other until 2010, four years after the incident in Berlin.

Speaking to EuroSport, Materazzi detailed his brief interaction with Zidane after a game between Inter and Real Madrid.

"The opportunity came up, as we know," he said.

"It was in November 2010, at the Meliá Hotel in Milan, on the eve of the Milan vs. Real Madrid match. I was leaving the hotel after saying hello to Mourinho. He was parking his car in the back. When he saw me, he hesitated a bit; he even turned around to drive away, but I walked over to him and held out my hand: ‘I’m glad we ran into each other here, where no one can see us.’ He replied, ‘Don’t worry, these things happen, it’s in the past.’ Too bad he later denied everything, claiming he thought I was a fan.”

Materazzi went on to discuss the incident in further detail, claiming that he would've forgiven Zidane had the Frenchman apologised for his actions.

“I would say today what I would have said that night, twenty years ago, or the very next day: ‘I was wrong, but I was provoked. Judge me however you like: that’s how things happened, and I ask for your forgiveness.’

"And he could have avoided that, because I wasn’t the first or the last to hold back an opponent to prevent him from scoring. On the contrary, if I were a coach and had a defender who didn’t foul his opponent out of malice, I’d shake his hand."

EMBT shelters Naples metro station beneath "natural and welcoming" vaulted timber roof

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Spanish architecture studio EMBT has completed the Naples Underground Central Station in Italy, contrasting its modernist surroundings with a wavy timber roof.

The station, which is shortlisted in the Infrastructure and transport category of Dezeen Awards 2026, sits within the Centro Direzionale, a business district in Naples designed in the 1970s by Japanese architect Kenzo Tange.

The 10,000-square-metre building was designed to replace a previously understated metro entrance with a new public plaza that felt more integrated with the surrounding city.

As EMBT founder Benedetta Tagliabue previously told Dezeen during the station’s construction, the curvilinear timber canopy was designed in direct response to what she called the “artificial” feel of the modernist Centro Direzionale.

“Kenzo Tange designed Centro Direzionale, and he built a very Kenzo Tange-type of city with reflective skyscrapers and a piazza, very 1970s, very clean,” she told Dezeen in 2021.

“It didn’t work because it’s so different from the rest of Naples,” Tagliabue continued. “So the idea of making a new station there and connecting with the new subway line was also to try to introduce a piece of nature and a piece of the original part of Naples into this artificial piece of the city.”

The roof of the station was constructed from arches and beams of glue-laminated (glulam) timber, which are partially supported by the existing concrete structure and shelter a floor finished in local Etna and Trani limestone.

According to Tagliabue, the branching columns and supports of these arches were designed to evoke the feeling of “walking through a forest”, while referencing the arched roofs of historic railway stations.

“We wanted the everyday experience of catching a train to feel more natural and welcoming, giving people a sense of being at home in the middle of the city,” she told Dezeen.

“We were also interested in what wood could bring to this particular neighbourhood. There is so much glass and concrete here; introducing timber was a way of bringing back a connection with nature, through its warmth and texture,” she added.

“The roof follows the geometry of the tracks, so something that is normally hidden underground becomes visible in the shape of the building.”

The vaulted canopy not only shelters the station’s ticket barriers and access routes, but extends towards the new surrounding paved plaza on either side, which faces a church to the north and incorporates new planting to the south.

Below ground, the station platforms are lined entirely with striped panels in bold shades of red, orange and yellow informed by Pompeian frescoes. This theme is continued above ground in the form of colourful silhouettes.

“Our station is not directly above archaeological remains, so we introduced our own references to that history, with a little humour. The reds and yellows recall Pompeii, and figures appear and disappear across the surfaces,” Tagliabue explained.

“I like the idea that a station can offer these unexpected encounters: along the way, you discover something about the city and feel more connected to it,” she added.

Elsewhere in Naples, sculptor Anish Kapoor and architecture studio AL_A previously designed two sculptural station entrances – one in weathered steel and the other in aluminium – as part of a project to connect the underground to the city’s Traiano district.

Other metro station architecture recently featured on Dezeen includes the mirrored Qasr AlHokm Metro Station in Saudi Arabia by Snøhetta and the series of buildings created for Melbourne’s Metro Tunnel by studios RSHP, Hassell and WW+P Architects.

Aer Lingus Pilots Wanted A Safer Place To Land After Bomb Threat, Now Face Criminal Investigation

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Aer Lingus pilots responding to a bomb threat were directed to land at a French military airfield. According to their unions, they lacked the charts and operational information needed to land safely and requested another airport. They ultimately landed at the base, but then faced questioning and now a criminal investigation over their initial response. I find that troubling, particularly if their supposed failure to cooperate was an attempt to protect the people onboard.

As One Mile At A Time highlights, the handling of a recent Aer Lingus diversion has prompted a pointed warning from European pilot unions.

The incident occurred on November 5, 2026, aboard Aer Lingus flight EI402 from Dublin (DUB) to Rome (FCO). Following a bomb threat, the Airbus A320 diverted to Évreux-Fauville Air Base in Normandy under a French fighter escort. No explosives were found, and the aircraft eventually returned to Dublin.

But the dispute over how it reached that airfield deserves a closer look.

In a November 10 joint statement, the European Cockpit Association, Irish Air Line Pilots’ Association, and French pilot union SNPL said the crew did not have the necessary aeronautical charts and operational information for the military airfield. According to the unions, the pilots requested a more suitable alternative, but that request was refused.

The organizations also expressed concern that the pilots subsequently faced possible criminal prosecution over decisions they say were intended to protect those onboard. ECA Vice-President Captain Paul Reuter put it succinctly:

“The pilots must not find themselves treated as a threat by the very authorities whose assistance they require.”

That strikes me as a very reasonable starting point.

French authorities described the initial exchange differently. The prime minister’s office said fighter aircraft were dispatched because the pilot had not cooperated as expected.

Évreux public prosecutor Rémi Coutin told The Irish Times that investigators were examining whether the pilot initially refused the landing instruction and whether that conduct could constitute a criminal offense. Both pilots were questioned and permitted to leave France. The prosecutor said on November 6 that the investigation remained ongoing.

Aer Lingus has defended its crew, saying they “acted impeccably and were thoroughly professional.”

So we have competing descriptions of the same exchange. An investigation has been opened, but no criminal charges have been filed. I would be interested in what the cockpit recordings and communications with authorities actually reveal.

It is understandable why authorities might want an aircraft carrying a suspected explosive brought to a military installation rather than a busy passenger airport. There may have been very good reasons to choose Évreux.

But those reasons need to account for whether the crew can safely get the aircraft onto the ground there.

The objection described by the unions concerns missing operational information. That is a substantial concern, even if the runway itself can easily accommodate an A320. Pilots still need to understand the approach and assess the conditions for their particular aircraft (I realized this myself when trying to land at Madeira Airport in the United Airlines 737 flight simulator).

A fighter escort cannot make those calculations for them.

I would not expect a captain to continue toward Rome simply because diverting was inconvenient. But requesting an alternative airport because you lack information needed for a safe landing sounds like precisely the sort of judgment I want the person flying my aircraft to exercise!

If the authorities had a compelling reason why no alternative was acceptable, that should have been explained to the pilots and more precise guidance given for landing at the diversion airport. I have trouble seeing how the prospect of prosecution helps anyone land more safely.

I recently wrote about European pilots reporting fatigue and fearing the consequences of speaking up. This is a different situation, but it raises a similar concern: what happens when exercising professional judgment appears personally riskier than going along?

Of course pilots are accountable for their decisions. Their position does not place them above the law, and authorities should examine a disputed response during a security emergency. But there needs to be room for a captain to say that an instruction creates a safety problem. Otherwise, we tell pilots they bear responsibility for everyone onboard while discouraging them from exercising the judgment that responsibility requires.

I also would not dismiss the original threat simply because the search found nothing. I made the same point after the recent Austrian Airlines diversion under a MiG-29 escort. Crews and authorities must respond to the information available at the time and hindsight does not make a precaution foolish.

But taking a bomb threat seriously should include taking the pilots’ landing concerns seriously too.

I cannot resolve this dispute without knowing what was actually communicated between the aircraft and French authorities. Reviewing those communications is appropriate and I hope they are released soon.

Why Liga MX Club CD Guadalajara Are Called 'Chivas'

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Club Deportivo Guadalajara are Mexico's most decorated football club, but the story behind their popularised alternative name is steeped in history.

For supporters, broadcasters and rivals alike, they are known as 'Chivas'. It is a name that has become synonymous with their commercial identity, plastered across the Estadio Akron and shouted from Los Angeles to Monterrey.

However, 'Chivas' began as an insult. Rival fans and journalists voiced it so much that it stuck, with Guadalajara eventually adopting it. Here's where the nickname came from, and the other titles the club has picked up along the way.

Chiva is Spanish for a goat. The plural, chivas, carries connotations of skittishnes in Mexican usage. For example, animals that bolt or scatter without direction.

The related phrases attached to Guadalajara in the late 1940s, chivas locas (crazy goats) and chivas brinconas (jumping goats), were mockery of a team perceived to be running around in a random and unlogical fashion.

The top-flight in Mexico has a deep and rich history across its division, with nicknames steeped in heritage - here's all of them in one place.

The origin of this nickname dates back to September 30, 1948. On that day, the club posted a scrappy one-nil victory over Tampico.

A newspaper columnist — one that was sympathetic to city rivals Atlas — reportedly described the Guadalajara players as charging around the pitch like chivas locas — a phrase that translates roughly into 'crazy things' or 'crazy goats'.

Supporters of Atlas jumped on the name and immediately relayed it as a terrace taunt, but Guadalajara's response was the making of the nickname. Instead of pushing back or protesting, they simply leaned into it.

Incredibly, during their next city derby against Atlas, they emerged with a live goat dressed in the club's red-and-white stripes. It instantly turned it from a taunt to a beloved name, and the goat also became a permanent piece of matchday iconography.

Four other nicknames have been popularised.

El Rebaño Sagrado (The Sacred Flock) — the most-used alternative. It stems from the club's first professional title in the 1956/57 season. The story is that Cardinal José Garibi Rivera received the squad and revealed a Guadalajara shirt beneath his cassock. Then, a papal blessing from Pius XII gave journalists the religious framing that turned a herd of goats into a sacred flock.

Campeonísimo — this was reserved for the 1956-62 side that won seven league titles in a nine-year span, including four in succession, and remains the benchmark for Mexican domestic football.

Ya Merito (Almost There) — seen as the more affectionate one, applied in the early 1950s during a run of near misses before the 1957 breakthrough.

Rojiblancos — the most stereotypical, which is derived from the colour of thei kit: the red-and-white stripes.

The Witcher 3: Songs Of The Past Uses AI To Help Geralt’s Polish Voice Actor

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When gamers hear that AI has been used during a game’s development, many of them will immediately see red and vow to never play that game ever again. There are ways AI can be used for good in gaming, though, as CD Projekt has demonstrated by using AI tools to ensure Geralt’s Polish voice actor, Jacek Rozenek, can return for The Witcher 3’s Songs of the Past DLC.

The Witcher 3 will get a big new expansion in 2027 titled Songs of the Past, 12 years on from the base game’s original release. For the various Geralt actors around the world who speak for the Witcher in different languages, that meant returning to the recording booth to record more lines.

As Stephen Totilo explains for Game File, it wasn’t that simple for Rozenek, the iconic actor behind the Polish version of Geralt, who many place on the same pedestal as the lead’s English actor, Doug Cockle.

Rozenek suffered a stroke in 2019. Even though he was unable to speak for months afterwards, he returned to the world of The Witcher in 2021 to record more Geralt lines. Just a few for the next-gen update that released in 2022.

The Witcher series is getting its first release since CDPR stopped working with Russia, meaning the DLC is dropping Russian dubbing.

“I was so exhausted after this recording,” Rozenek told Totilo. However, it wasn’t just the effort needed to record new dialogue for Geralt after recovering from a stroke that posed a problem. The stroke, and subsequent recovery, means Rozenek sounds different now. Not ideal when Geralt, in all languages, needs to still sound the same as he did in the base game.

CD Projekt invited Rozenek to the studio to record some lines so developers could assess how much his voice had changed. They discovered that, although Rozenek’s voice had indeed recovered after the stroke, it was lower and gruffer than it was when he originally read for Geralt over a decade ago.

That’s when CDPR floated the idea of using Respeecher, a Ukrainian company that specializes in using machine learning to alter voices in video games. The studio had turned to Respeecher for help before. It wanted to bring back Viktor Vektor for Cyberpunk 2077’s expansion, but his Polish voice actor, Milogost Reczek, had passed away. With his son’s blessing, CDPR recruited Respeecher to help recreate his voice for the expansion.

The process was significantly different for Rozenek. Thankfully, Rozenek is still with us, and he recorded all of the Polish dialogue for Geralt in Songs of the Past. Respeecher has then taken those recordings and was tasked with making it sound like Rozenek’s original Geralt voice so it wouldn’t sound different and be potentially jarring for those playing in Polish when the DLC drops next year.

The process took dozens of hours, with Respeecher developers noting that really nailing Rozenek’s emotional tone in the tweaked recordings was the biggest challenge.

The most important part of all this, of course, is that Rozenek is on board, and it sounds like he most certainly is. “The point is that this voice should be as close as possible to my voice from 12 years ago,” the actor explained to Totilo. “When it comes to the dynamics of the voice, when it comes to the acting aspects, I feel very good, really very good,” he added, quite rightly pointing out that his voice is only part of what he brings to the table. It’s his acting ability, the life he brings to Geralt, his inflections, his emotions. It’s a whole package, and it’s why CDPR felt it needed Rozenek before it could really move forward with Songs of the Past.

It’s a heartwarming story about AI use in video games where there are proverbial fires caused by that very topic everywhere you look. Whether it be the admission by House House that AI was used sparingly during Big Walk’s development, or untalented people using Claude to rip off other people’s games. It’s refreshing to see that there are ways AI can be used positively to aid game development.
