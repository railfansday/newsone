Movement Mortgage wants two former loan officers to pay back a total of more than $1.2 million in signing bonuses after they left the company about a year into their three-year agreements.

According to court and arbitration documents, Movement paid a total of $1,235,000 in “start-up payments” to loan officers Victor Shkolnikov and Igor Velyunskiy when they joined the company in March 2022. Shkolnikov received $835,000 and Velyunskiy received $400,000.

The contracts said these payments were advances on future wages and had to be paid back in full if the loan officers left before working 36 consecutive months at the company. If they left early, the money was due within 30 days.

Both Shkolnikov and Velyunskiy left Movement around March 2023, roughly one year into their three-year commitment. Public records show Shkolnikov now works as a loan originator with United Lending Team in Illinois, while Velyunskiy is with Smart Rate Lending LLC.

Movement and the two former loan officers did not immediately respond to HousingWire’s requests for comment. National Mortgage News first reported the dispute.

Movement started arbitration in February 2024, and the cases were combined before arbitrator H. William Constangy. The LOs pushed back, filing counterclaims that accuse Movement of breach of contract, unjust enrichment, fraudulent inducement and violations of the Truth in Lending Act.

In July, the arbitrator ruled in favor of Movement. The decision said the employment agreements were valid contracts and that the two breached the contracts when they did not repay the start-up payments after leaving before the 36-month mark. All of their counterclaims were dismissed.

The award says the repayment amount will also accrue interest at North Carolina’s legal rate of 8% per year, starting in April 2023, which the arbitrator treated as the date of the breach. The arbitrator did not order either side to pay the other’s legal fees, so each party must cover its own attorneys’ costs.

In early November, Movement asked the U.S. District Court for the Western District of North Carolina, Charlotte Division, to confirm the arbitration award and turn it into a court judgment. That would make it easier for the lender to collect the money if it isn’t paid voluntarily.

Ardent Health is acquiring Boone Health, a mid-sized nonprofit system based in Columbia, Missouri, for $47 million in cash, the organizations announced Thursday. It marks Ardent's first entry into the Missouri healthcare market.

The deal, expected to close in the first quarter of 2027, includes a 10-year, $200 million capital commitment on nonprofit Ardent’s part. That money will be invested in Boone’s facilities, workforce and, in the near term, an expansion of its emergency department, according to the announcement.

Ardent has agreed to maintain Boone’s essential hospital services while developing its 392-bed regional referral center, Boone Hospital Center. It also plans to maintain employment for existing Boone employees and providers, continue its charity care policy and establish a local advisory board.

“We are acquiring a strong asset in an attractive and growing midsize market with significant opportunity to drive operating and financial improvement, all while delivering high patient quality care and safety,” Ardent Health President and CEO Dave Caspers said during a Thursday afternoon investor call discussing the deal. “It also represents an effective use of capital at a compelling valuation that we expect will create long-term shareholder value.”

Alongside its main hospital, Ardent would acquire Boone’s medical group, 19 care sites including an outpatient campus and five medical office buildings, related ancillary services and real estate. The system is the second-largest provider in its region with about 30% market share and half a billion dollars of annual revenue, Caspers said, with a local population that is well insured (93% coverage) and growing well ahead of the national average (3.4% in the next five years).

“Boone expands our footprint into a new attractive market,” Caspers explained. “It diversifies our business and enhances scale. Further, adding Boone's regional system to our portfolio is consistent with our strategy of building leading healthcare platforms in growing mid-sized communities. We're highly aligned with Boone Health on culture and mission, including putting the patient at the center of everything we do, with an aim toward best-in-class clinical care, patient quality, safety and access to services.”

The executive noted to investors that Boone’s facilities are relatively modern and well maintained, allowing Ardent to direct more capital toward its growth. The company has also identified “meaningful opportunities to optimize financial performance.” By leveraging its larger scale and expertise to tackle costs (supply chain procurement, vendor consolidation) and revenue cycle processes, Ardent executives said they expect to generate about $5 million to $10 million additional EBITDA within the deal’s first year, and at least $30 million of annual synergies three years after the deal’s close.

Boone Health has been independent since 2021, following over three decades during which it operated as part of the BJC HealthCare system. In 2025, its leadership kicked off a search for potential strategic partners “that could provide additional capital and resources to support the system's continued growth. Ardent was selected after a comprehensive evaluation process led by an independent advisory firm,” according to Thursday’s announcement.

“In Ardent, we identified a partner that supports our mission, respects our culture and can accelerate our vision without compromising what makes Boone Health special,” Jerry Kennett, M.D., chair of Boone’s Board of Trustees, said in the announcement. "Following a thoughtful review, the Board is confident Ardent is the right long-term partner for Boone Health. This agreement protects what our community has built while giving Boone Health the resources it needs to grow."

Ardent Health’s current operations include 30 acute care hospitals and over 280 care sites, several of which are run as part of a joint venture structure with other health system partners. In 2025, the company increased its revenues by 6% to $6.3 billion and grew its adjusted EBITDA to $545 million.

The company recently logged a strong third quarter off the back of solid demand for emergent care and progress on its ongoing performance improvement initiative. In September, it also officially divested from a joint venture with the University of Kansas Health System, a decision that leadership said is part of an ongoing review of its portfolio to ensure long-term strategic fit among its hospitals and partnerships.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/industry-voices-pledges-progress-kill-clipboard-keep-eye-consistency" hreflang="en">Industry Voices—From pledges to progress: Kill the clipboard, keep an eye on consistency</a>

📅 2026-11-13  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Industry Voices—From pledges to progress: Kill the clipboard, keep an eye on consistency" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

For many patients, the clipboard remains one of healthcare’s most familiar frustrations.

At nearly every visit, patients are asked to provide information they have already supplied elsewhere, including their insurance coverage, medications, family history and previous surgeries. This repetitious hassle—even if managed on tablets as in recent years—frustrates patients, increases the risk of incomplete or inaccurate information entering the record, and takes unnecessary staff time to reconcile.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ Health Technology Ecosystem initiative offers a compelling alternative through the Kill the Clipboard pledge. The effort is focused on moving technology and care practices forward so that patients can more easily retrieve and share their health information through personal health records or other health apps, removing the need for their data to be reconstructed at every encounter.

That shift could conclusively improve the patient experience. Reaching that goal, however, will require more than voluntary commitments from individual technology developers. This multi-stakeholder initiative needs work based on consensus-based standards, reliable infrastructure, clear data governance and careful attention to how new capabilities affect clinical work.

Patients often assume that health data interoperability is happening more than it is, and certainly more easily. In some cases, a person opens an app and shares their information with a provider themselves; in others, the exchange is initiated by their clinical care team. Behind that process, though, is a complex chain of care organizations, electronic health records (EHRs), identity verification services, intermediaries, standards development organizations, application developers and networks.

Consider, for example, real-time prescription benefit information. Giving patients cost and coverage options during an encounter—and ensuring that the information is accurate all the way to the point of sale—could help increase affordability and medication adherence. Yet the experience depends on pharmacy networks, price comparison apps and other third parties delivering complete, timely information into the clinician’s prescribing workflow, all while the patient is there in the exam room or awaiting discharge from the hospital. Every participant in the chain is fundamental to the increasingly complex web of health information exchange.

A 2025 study of more than 8,100 family physicians published in JAMA Network Open underscores just how much work around data-sharing remains. Depending on the type of clinical information, only 8% to 19% of survey respondents reported that outside data were routinely available, easy to find and easy to use within their EHRs.

Pledges like Kill the Clipboard can establish a valuable direction for the industry and encourage momentum. They will face challenges with adoption and impact, however, if sufficient effort doesn’t go into resolving dependencies across the country’s health tech ecosystem. Clear technical standards, realistic implementation timelines and ways to verify that promised capabilities work in practice are essential.

While the goals of increasing patient control over their own data, tackling inefficiencies, and increasing affordable care delivery are laudable and things no one would argue with, it is also true that moving quickly without those foundations could create expensive rework down the road or inadvertently widen the digital divide. If any or all pledge criteria eventually become certification requirements, as Dr. Tom Keane, the National Coordinator for Health IT, has intimated could be a possibility, the variability in readiness among different companies could become a problematic gap.

Expanding digital access also raises important questions about how patient data will be used once it leaves a regulated clinical environment.

There has been a huge proliferation of health apps in recent years intended to help patients more actively manage their own health while also better managing their medical records. Many can deliver meaningful value, but patients who are considering use of such an app need clearer information about whether an application may retain, combine, share or sell their data. Consent language should be understandable, with governance expectations following the information across the digital ecosystem, but the current legal and regulatory system needs modernization to ensure sufficient patient awareness.

Patient matching presents another safety concern. A larger volume of exchanged data is useful only when organizations can confidently connect each record to the correct person. Striking the right balance between the stringency of matching criteria and the goal of increasing data exchange is critical, as a clinician could make a prescribing or treatment decision based on another person’s medications, allergies, diagnoses or laboratory results with damaging consequences. Strong identity verification and thoughtful matching practices therefore belong at the center of any effort to make health data more portable, ensuring that patient safety protections are inherent to the matching process.

The potential consequences have recently been measured. A 2026 BMJ Quality & Safety study found that hospitalized patients with duplicate charts had 3.5 times the odds of requiring intensive care, 4.7 times the odds of inpatient death and hospital stays that were 32% longer than those of matched patients without duplicate charts. Although the observational study does not establish causation, it underscores the clinical risks associated with fragmented patient information.

For virtually any initiative focused on the patient, program vision and software design must also account for the people delivering care.

Primary care clinicians often have only a few minutes to review a patient’s record, conduct their initial assessment of the problem, complete documentation of the encounter, place orders and determine any necessary follow-up. While unquestionably helpful, adding review of new extensive patient data that has been sent from a patient’s medical record aggregation app adds another step that takes time. Then, needing to build in time to consider prescription cost comparisons, scheduling stemming from referrals, discussions of patient consent preferences, or record reconciliation further consumes part of a tightly constrained visit.

The impact on the provider and staff workflows in no way diminishes the value to the patient. These changes benefit all of us as patients and/or caregivers ourselves—but the implications for that workflow cannot be an afterthought.

The strongest implementations will embed new capabilities directly into existing workflows and surface information exactly when it is most useful. Software developers should also involve practicing clinicians in design, testing and governance so that any improvements support better care without introducing avoidable friction. In fact, prioritizing the efficient implementation of these new functionalities will increase adoption and user satisfaction, moving us all closer to our collective goals.

Eliminating the clipboard is the correct ambition, as are the goals of the other Health Tech Ecosystem pledges. They would serve patients nationwide and represent broader industry change in how we move and manage information. Patients should not have to recount their histories every time they seek care, and clinicians should have dependable data available whenever decisions must be made.

With mature standards, real-time connectivity, responsible governance, reliable patient matching and thoughtful workflow design, we can collectively turn the pledge’s aims into a new reality: more trusted digital experiences that give patients greater control and help everyone involved in their care act with greater confidence.

Nvidia not only supplies its graphics processing units to hyperscalers and tech giants, it also provides financing to other companies to help the industry expand. That could all change.

"Although we expect the company to fulfill all of its previously announced commitments, we believe that going forward, it may be more difficult for the company to negotiate new multi-party investments among ecosystem partners at the same scale it has done up to this point," Goldman Sachs analyst James Schneider said in a note Thursday.

The news of Huang's resignation comes after investors were already concerned about the massive amount of debt being taken on to fund AI infrastructure.

Tech bond yields have risen and spreads have widened on credit default swaps, most notably in lower-rated names. Credit default swaps are contracts in which investors pay for protection in case the borrower can't repay its debt. Wider spreads indicate investors view the bonds as riskier.

On Friday, the yield on Nvidia's 4.5% bond due in 2031 sat at 5.717%, nearly 5% higher week to date. Bond yields move inversely to prices.

Tech stocks slumped Friday with the Nasdaq 100 closing down 1.2% as markets continued to digest the Nvidia news. Yields on hyperscaler bonds ticked slightly higher on Friday, signaling the broader concern about AI debt lingers.

It's the smaller AI companies that could ultimately see an impact if Nvidia makes any changes to its AI financing. The company had $36 billion in off-balance-sheet AI cloud agreements, or backstops, for neoclouds, according to its last quarterly financial filing in July.

CoreWeave, which has become the poster child for AI debt fears, saw its bond yields retreat after its quarterly earnings on Tuesday eased investor concerns. The yield on its 9.75% bond due in 2031 was 14.79% on Friday after climbing to 16.7% last week. The spread on its five-year CDS also dropped more than 17% week to date, although it remained above 1,000 — which is generally considered a sign of distress.

It remains unclear how Nvidia's support for those companies may change, said CreditSights analyst Andy Li.

"We don't know what the ultimate strategy for Nvidia is going to be coming out the other side," he said.

I missed a deadline last week. The piece you’re reading was supposed to be filed before I headed out on a long weekend to Italy for my wife’s birthday.

Switching off, particularly from work, is hard for most of us, and especially in an age of ubiquitous screens and wall-to-wall information competing for our attention. For me, that always-on reality plays a major role in the difficulties I have disconnecting from the daily grind.

I’m fortunate to love my job, and if I’m candid, I probably take a little too much of my identity from it. I hold on tight, and the phone in my pocket is an ever-present enabler.

A meme that has stuck with me over the years goes something along the lines of being an adult is staring at medium screens all day only to come home and stare at a little screen while a big screen is on in the background. The irony is not lost on me that I came across this rather withering take on modern life while scrolling on said little screen.

Regardless of the role the screen plays, psychologists who study employee well-being describe what I was missing for the early part of the weekend as “psychological detachment.” A state where you are genuinely able to not think about work while away from it. The science suggests it’s an important thing to work on.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 32 studies found that vacationing has a strong effect on well-being, and that the effect lasts longer than earlier research suggested. A second, smaller analysis of eight studies points to detaching from work, along with physical activity, as among the most helpful things to do while you’re off. In another study that tracked teachers during a two-week break, exhaustion, anxiety, and low mood all declined, while those who kept ruminating about work recovered less.

Whatever the science says, I know that for me the act of stepping away from work and being disciplined about not checking back in while I’m away is critical for my mental health and for staving off burnout. Getting out in nature and hiking or cycling or swimming keeps me present. It resets and recharges my batteries, and it tops up my creativity in a way that I find benefits what I am then able to bring back to the office.

Sure enough, as we drove deeper into the mountains on a crisp, blue-sky day, I could feel the golden autumnal hues and majestic rock formations drawing me in, closer to an elemental feeling of calm and happiness. That sense of equilibrium stayed with me all weekend, and while I’d be lying if I said my mind didn’t occasionally drift to my desk, it was fleeting, with the knowledge that all the things would be waiting for me when I got back. Just like this piece was.

With Thanksgiving on the horizon, and the holidays coming thick and fast thereafter, I’ll leave you with this bit of hard-earned advice: if your job is deeply entwined with your life and part of who you are, as mine is, time away can sometimes feel like a small act of disloyalty. It isn’t. In fact, it’s probably the most important thing you can do to remain faithful to your work ethic, and to yourself.

And if you’ll be working over the holidays, or you’re not working right now, or you don’t have the benefit of someone on hand to carry some of the load for you (shout out to all the Grannies out there), or your vacation looks very different from mine, that’s all okay. Find the pockets where and when you can: an evening, a walk, an hour in front of a screen, or better yet a book, and let work wait. Above all else, be kind to yourself. That’s how you really give yourself a break.

How Kelly Rowland Keeps Her Eczema Flare-Ups Under Control During Winter

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Wintertime can bring everyone down with its colder weather and less sunlight. But for those with eczema, this time of year can wreak havoc on their skin, too.

That’s the case for Grammy-winning singer, actress, and Destiny’s Child alum Kelly Rowland, who has been living with eczema since her 20s. During the winter months, she often dreads the cold.

“[You] don’t know what the environment is going to give you—because mine is also environmental—or what the weather is going to give you,” Rowland told Healthline.

Cold weather and indoor heating can reduce humidity, drying out the skin and triggering or worsening eczema flare-ups.

“[What we know] about the skin barrier is that it really is like a gate or wall against the outside world,” Camille Howard-Verovic, MD, a dermatologist at Active Dermatology, told Healthline.

In healthy skin, the barrier controls what enters and leaves the skin. However, for people with eczema, Howard-Verovic said they have skin barrier dysfunction, which allows substances such as allergens and irritants to penetrate the skin.

Cold weather can increase transepidermal water loss, a process in which moisture escapes through the skin’s outer barrier and evaporates into the surrounding air.

“People with eczema have a tremendous amount of transepidermal water loss, so you’re just drier,” said Howard-Verovic. “[You’re] not holding onto the water as well, so that causes the itching and the rash that we feel when we have eczema.”

Rowland first noticed eczema flares behind her ears as well as on her elbows, arms, and the back of her knees.

“[It] looked like a rash, so that’s what I thought it was all this time, and I dealt with it for so long until after I had my son and then it started to get worse on my face,” she said.

She eventually went to a dermatologist who diagnosed her with moderate-to-severe eczema, a chronic inflammatory skin condition that can cause intense itching, painful skin cracking, thickened skin, and redness or swelling.

When Rowland learned that eczema can be genetic, it made sense to her.

“I remember seeing big rashes on [my mom’s] neck and her arms…so she had it, and I guess I was the lucky one,” said Rowland.

At first, she tried eliminating foods that she thought might be triggers. She also bathed in oatmeal.

“[I] literally have had a tub full of oatmeal, like literally went to the store to get store-bought fresh oats,” she said. “I tried everything, and it doesn’t help until you go talk to a doctor about it.”

At first, she tried prescription topical medications but found them cumbersome.

“I don’t have the time, if I’m being honest, and we live in a world that is moving so fast…I started buying like three tubes of whatever it was I was using and losing it; it was just too much for me,” Rowland said.

Rowland said she found relief with Ebglyss, a prescription injectable medication for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis. She has also partnered with Eli Lilly and Company, its manufacturer, to raise awareness about eczema.

Rowland said she’s sharing her experience to help others living with eczema, including people she’s met who have experienced greater challenges managing the condition.

“One lady was telling me about how she’s having a tough time [looking] in the mirror cause she doesn’t know who’s looking back at her, and she saw her skin before eczema and after,” said Rowland.

When she first had symptoms, she said she felt embarrassed, too.

“[Because] it was just in the middle of my arm space and…[if I] put out my hand to shake someone’s hand, you don’t know if somebody’s judgy,” she said.

Rowland hopes being open about her condition will encourage others to advocate for themselves and talk with their doctor about treatment options.

Because eczema can look different on deeper skin tones, Howard-Verovic noted that a dermatologist can help make the correct diagnosis.

“Some people may not even know that they have eczema,” she said. “We know that eczema is a rash; it can appear red on certain skin tones, and it can be grey or white on certain skin tones, so it can look a little different.”

In addition to medication, Rowland said managing stress helps reduce her eczema flare-ups.

“Before [when] I went on tour, because I was under so much stress, it flared up like crazy, like you wouldn’t believe,” she said. “We were in the back trying to figure out how to cover certain things up, and we figured some of it out, and some of it was really tricky, but what helped was when I actually just calmed down…not that it helped overnight, but it did just kind of bring the flare down.”

She said red light therapy, feeling grounded outside without her shoes on, getting vitamin D from natural sunlight, and cooking are ways she reduces ongoing stress.

“Cooking calms me when my kids aren’t here, and I have a really good [Zen] playlist on,” said Rowland. “[If] you ask me a question [in those moments], I might give you my heart; I’m just so happy.”

She also tries to cut back on sugar, which she believes triggers flares.

Another way to reduce flares, according to Howard-Verovic, is to avoid hot showers, especially in the winter.

“[We] know eczematous skin likes lukewarm water,” she said.

Because Howard-Verovic and her daughter both live with eczema, she set a rule about when it’s time to get out of the shower.

“[If] you see condensation on the bathroom mirror, you’ve spent too much time in the shower,” said Howard-Verovic. “[That] really hot, hot water feels really good in that moment…but when you get out all that water evaporates, and there’s no moisture in the atmosphere, and it dries your skin out, and now your skin is unhappy again.”

If you prefer to take baths, she said use gentle cleansers.

“[There] are bleach baths for really, really bad flare-ups,” she noted.

Diluted bleach baths are sometimes recommended for people with eczema, but they should only be used according to a healthcare professional’s instructions.
