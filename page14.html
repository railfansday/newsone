Ukraine warns of fresh Starshield disruptions as U.S. envoys arrive in Moscow for talks with Putin

📅 2026-11-12  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine was investigating what he described as fresh restrictions on SpaceX's Starshield service over the country, after similar speed limitations were temporarily imposed last month.

"Our units have been recording significant disruptions to Starshield's operations over the territory of Ukraine for several days now – along the entire front line and in the temporarily occupied territories of Ukraine," he wrote on X early Thursday.

Starshield is SpaceX's satellite network for government and national-security use, built on Starlink technology.

CNBC has reached out to SpaceX and the White House for comment.

"The Ukrainian military have already investigated the circumstances, and these are not random errors but systematic speed restrictions," he said, adding that they are trying to establish the circumstances with the U.S.

Zelenskyy said the restrictions imposed an effective speed limit on terminals moving at speeds above 161 kilometers per hour (100 miles per hour), disconnecting anything that was moving faster and making it harder for Ukraine to intercept Russian jet-powered drones.

"There have already been strikes that got through because our interception systems cannot reach sufficient speeds and are unable to intercept in time."

On Oct. 14, Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of undermining the country's ability to defend itself by cutting connectivity speeds and thereby disrupting military operations. On October 24, Zelenskyy confirmed that systems had returned to their previous operating speeds.

According to The Wall Street Journal, SpaceX CEO Elon Musk ordered those restrictions, rendering some Ukrainian strike and interceptor drones virtually unusable. The newspaper later reported that U.S. President Donald Trump had asked Musk to reverse the move. Trump has denied that the White House ordered Starlink restrictions in Ukraine.

Musk initially appeared to pin responsibility for any restrictions on the U.S. government, writing on X that "Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government." He later claimed Ukraine had violated Starlink's terms of service, prompting the company to restrict its access to the high-speed satellite technology, something that Ukrainian officials have strongly contested.

Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense tech advisor to Zelenskyy, said at the time that Ukraine cannot use commercial Starlink for medium- or long-range drone strikes because the service does not operate over Russian territory and had imposed drone speed limits of 70–90 kilometers per hour for months.

On Thursday, Beskrestnov confirmed that "the speed of Starlink operation during movement was again limited."

"We did not rejoice for long," he said, adding that "any terms can be changed and discussed, instead of leaving our country without sky protection."

The comments came as U.S.-brokered peace talks wrapped up in Abu Dhabi on Wednesday and U.S. special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner arrived in Moscow for further talks with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

Witkoff and Kushner were met by Russian presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev as they arrived at Vnukovo Airport on Thursday morning, according to Russian state news agency Tass. The pair were expected to discuss a lasting resolution to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which has now raged for more than four-and-a-half years.

Later on Thursday, Witkoff and Kushner are poised to meet with Zelenskyy on the sidelines of a political summit held in Ireland.

Trilateral talks in the United Arab Emirates between Ukrainian, Russian and American negotiators ended earlier this week without a major diplomatic breakthrough on a ceasefire or territorial settlement.

Nvidia stock falls 7% as investors await Jensen Huang’s fate

📅 2026-11-12  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia shares fell on Thursday after the company's board met to decide whether to remove Jensen Huang as CEO following reports that he has had two extramarital affairs.

On Sunday, Huang admitted to having an affair. The CEO was romantically involved with Alice Huang, an executive at cloud company Megaspeed, according to The Financial Times, who U.S. intelligence officials believe is a spy for China.

The New York Times on Wednesday reported that Huang had another extramarital relationship with Kimberly Powell, vice president of health care at Nvidia. Powell reports to Huang, according to the Times.

CNBC confirmed that the Nvidia board met on Wednesday to discuss whether to oust Huang as CEO.

Nvidia's stock was down 6.9% in premarket trading as of 6:23 a.m. ET.

Shares of Nvidia rivals AMD and Broadcom were up 3.9% and 3%, respectively, in premarket trading. Meanwhile, shares of chip stocks Intel, Marvell, Arm and Qualcomm were all trading lower.

Several Wall Street analysts told CNBC that if Huang is removed as CEO, this could lead the stock to fall 20%, given how critical he has been to Nvidia's success. Nvidia's stock is already down more than 20% over the last month.

Bernstein on Thursday slashed its price target on Nvidia's stock by half from $400 to $200.

Stacy Rasgon, a senior analyst at Bernstein, told CNBC's "Squawk Box Asia" on Thursday that Huang's alleged affair with a direct report means it is "much tougher for the board to leave him in position."

Huang has been central to Nvidia's rise since he co-founded the company in 1993 and was pivotal in transforming Nvidia from a gaming hardware player to the company at the center of the artificial intelligence boom.

Rasgon said it is unclear who could be the "visionary" to lead Nvidia into the future as new CEO.

"Jensen is Nvidia, right? He is the visionary. The reason they're in the position that they're in today is because he made decisions 10 years ago," Rasgon said. "Who is the right person now to make the decisions for the next 10 years? I don't intuitively know who that person would be."

U.S. lawmakers have called for Huang's resignation, adding further pressure to the CEO and the company. Richard Reyle, chief investment officer at Questar Capital Partners, said Nvidia's lack of a succession plan makes the stock "uninvestable."

"Nvidia doesn't have that person in place, and so it's going to be either a choice of keeping Jensen and being faced with months and months of being hauled in front of probably congressional boards and things to explain themselves and all kinds of interrogations," Reyle told CNBC's "Access Middle East."

"Or they're going to have to move on from him, and that's just going to make the stock uninvestable. That doesn't make the AI trade uninvestable, but it certainly makes Nvidia uninvestable."

Mondrio Raises $3.75 Million Seed Round to Connect AI Pricing Strategy With Deal Execution

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mondrio has raised $3.75 million in seed funding to connect two activities that often happen in separate systems: deciding how a business should price its products and carrying those decisions into individual sales deals. The Amsterdam- and San Francisco-based company is building an agentic pricing platform that turns customer conversations, product changes, and deal outcomes into recommendations—and helps sales teams put approved pricing into practice.

The round was led by Heartcore Capital, with participation from Pretiosum VC, Hersir Ventures, Cambrena Capital, Momo VC, and angel investors with backgrounds in pricing, configure-price-quote software, AI, and payments. Mondrio plans to expand its AI and engineering teams, add pricing experts, and strengthen its commercial presence in the United States and Europe.

A pricing strategy can look convincing in a spreadsheet and still break down during negotiations. Salespeople encounter objections the original research missed. Customers value features differently from what a product team expected. Discounts become routine, while the reasons behind them remain scattered across calls, CRM records, and approval requests.

Mondrio’s approach is to make those signals part of an ongoing process. Its platform analyzes quantitative data alongside qualitative information, including customer conversations and market feedback, to identify opportunities and recommend changes. Lessons from quotes, negotiations, and renewals then feed back into the next pricing decision.

That connection matters as AI changes the economics of software. A feature that invokes models or performs automated work can create variable costs each time a customer uses it. Packaging, usage allowances, and discounts therefore affect both the attractiveness of an offer and the cost of delivering it. Faster product releases also give companies more frequent reasons to revisit what they charge.

CEO and co-founder Gregor Biljardt said the opportunity lies in agents combining quantitative information with insights from conversations and markets. Mondrio is applying that combination to a commercial workflow in which an apparently small pricing decision can carry consequences across many subsequent deals.

The company’s Pricing Studio handles pricing design: packaging, pricing models, and price levels. Its published workflow moves through research, design, simulation, decision-making, and rollout, giving teams a way to test an offer before introducing it to customers.

Research can draw on customer surveys, willingness-to-pay information, and AI-generated personas. The platform also lists features for benchmarking, pricing simulations, experiments, and customer migrations. These functions address different questions: which features belong together, which customer groups value them, and how a proposed change could affect an existing customer base.

AI personas provide a way to explore hypotheses, but their responses are simulated evidence rather than observed customer behavior. The useful feedback loop comes from comparing those hypotheses with research and actual commercial outcomes as offers reach the market.

Once a team approves a pricing decision, it can flow into Mondrio’s Deal Engine. That handoff is central to the product’s design: a new price list should influence the quotes customers receive, instead of remaining an analysis that sales teams must translate into their own tools.

Deal Engine covers execution, including quote creation, discount guidance, approvals, and the information collected as a deal progresses. Mondrio describes a workflow in which an agent builds a quote using the current price list, evaluates it against policy, and routes exceptions for approval.

The distinction between a recommendation and an authorized action is important. Quotes that meet a company’s rules can move through automated approvals; exceptions can be escalated with supporting context, such as the effect of a discount on margins. The platform’s listed capabilities also include a customer portal, quote engagement tracking, deal insights, and electronic signatures.

This gives the system a potential role beyond reducing the effort of producing a sales document. Repeated objections, unsuccessful offers, or discount patterns can become inputs to pricing design. A company could use that evidence to distinguish a weak package from an isolated negotiation, then adjust its strategy while keeping deal-level controls in place.

Mondrio’s integration catalog shows how it intends to assemble that context. Listed connections include Salesforce and HubSpot for CRM information, Gong for customer conversations, Amplitude and Mixpanel for product usage, and billing systems such as Chargebee, Zuora, and Stripe Billing. DocuSign is listed for Deal Engine’s signing workflow.

These sources represent different views of the same commercial relationship. A CRM records the opportunity, a conversation platform captures what a buyer said, product analytics show usage, and billing records what the customer pays. Bringing those signals into pricing work can help teams assess whether an offer reflects customer value and actual delivery economics.

The catalog assigns integrations to different modules, so the presence of a connection does not mean every system supports identical functions. The broader technical proposition is to connect pricing decisions to the operational records that can explain their results.

Biljardt founded Mondrio in 2026 with Joep van den Nieuwenhoff, its chief commercial officer. The company offers two ways to use the platform: existing pricing teams can work with its AI capabilities, or customers can bring in Mondrio’s pricing experts for additional support.

The funding will support further work on agents, synthetic customer capabilities, and the connection between pricing strategy and deal execution. Mondrio also plans to add pricing architects, develop relationships with B2B technology companies and private equity firms, and lay the groundwork for expansion into manufacturing and professional services.

Mondrio’s bet is that pricing becomes more valuable when it operates continuously. The challenge will be turning fragmented commercial evidence into changes that teams can understand, approve, and execute—and then using the results to improve the next decision.

Swimlane Named Visionary Leader in Frost & Sullivan AI SOC Radar

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Swimlane announced on November 12, 2026, that Frost & Sullivan had named it a Visionary Leader in the 2026 Frost Radar for AI security operations center (AI SOC) platforms, with innovation and growth scores of 4.45 and 4.30, respectively, out of five.

Frost & Sullivan independently evaluated more than 60 AI SOC vendors and selected 24 growth and innovation leaders, according to Swimlane’s company-hosted report summary. The broader market contains more than 80 vendors, and the Visionary Leader category recognizes companies that combine innovation, growth and industry influence.

Lucas Ferreyra, an industry principal at Frost & Sullivan, said the placement reflected Swimlane’s vendor-neutral design, broad coverage, auditability and pairing of agents with automation. The evaluation comes as security teams facing skills shortages and alert fatigue look beyond alert triage toward coordinated detection, investigation and response.

Swimlane says its AI SOC, powered by Hero AI, can autonomously investigate and resolve known and novel incidents, subject to approval gates configured by each customer. Its coverage spans 16 security and IT domains, including endpoint, cloud, identity, email, threat intelligence, operational technology and connected devices, security information and event management (SIEM), and vulnerability management.

In the described workflow, agents query data in SIEM platforms, cloud services, on-premises data lakes and operational stores rather than requiring customers to duplicate those records. Relevant evidence is pulled into the investigation record, while customers decide what Swimlane stores and for how long. Hero AI maps those sources and enriches alerts with threat intelligence, organizational context, investigation history, and the MITRE ATT&CK and D3FEND security frameworks.

Within the platform, deep agents perform complex reasoning while expert agents handle specialized tasks, according to Swimlane. Customers choose model providers and deployment locations to match task complexity and cost. The Investigation & Response Agent produces plans analysts can review or override, and Hero AI can execute those plans or turn them into deterministic playbooks, which are fixed automation sequences for repeatable work. The division reserves adaptive reasoning for novel threats and uses predictable, lower-cost execution for recurring activity.

The company reports that one healthcare customer handling about 180 threats a day reduced investigation costs by up to 90% with this hybrid approach, a result limited to that customer rather than a platform-wide average.

Swimlane says every large-language-model interaction and agent action is auditable. Agents carry governed identities, role-based access controls and lifecycle controls, with tenant-isolated data and connectors. Customers set the boundaries for model providers, deployment locations, autonomy, knowledge access and deterministic execution, placing limits around what the system can do without approval.

The report’s recommended practices cover full-lifecycle security operations, embedded governance and human oversight, and open integration with measurable outcomes; it also describes third-party integration as central to Swimlane. Customer-built agents and natural-language automation let customers extend the platform, while multitenancy and flexible deployment adapt it to different operating environments. The approach is designed to complement existing security investments instead of requiring wholesale platform replacement.

Swimlane says its customers report a 240% return on investment in the first year and a 75% reduction in mean time to respond, meaning the average time taken to respond to an incident.

Swimlane says roughly half of its business runs through channel partners and one-third through managed security service providers (MSSPs). A July 2026 offering for MSSPs added cross-tenant threat intelligence, a central command center, standardized client onboarding, and portfolio analytics with ServiceNow and Jira integration. Frost & Sullivan describes the model as leaving the end-customer relationship, data and service delivery with the MSSP, positioning Swimlane to serve providers rather than compete with them.

The wider significance extends beyond model selection. Applying agent reasoning across distributed tools requires enforceable limits on data retention, access and response authority. Swimlane’s reported cost and response-time results supply the operational case for that reach, while Frost & Sullivan’s recommended practices make governance and human oversight essential as AI SOC platforms become more autonomous.

Rafay Adds Confidential AI Orchestration for Kubernetes Pods

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Rafay Systems and Fortanix announced on November 11, 2026 that the Rafay Platform now orchestrates Fortanix Confidential AI workloads running on CNCF Confidential Containers (CoCo), extending their existing confidential virtual machine integration to Kubernetes pods. The joint offering is available now for neoclouds, sovereign AI clouds and enterprises.

The service addresses a gap in conventional encryption. Data can be protected at rest and in transit, but without confidential computing, data and model weights must be decrypted for processing in CPU and GPU memory. That creates an exposure on infrastructure operated by someone else, where people with privileged access could reach customer records, patient information, financial transactions or proprietary models.

CoCo, a CNCF Incubating project built on Kata Containers, runs each Kubernetes pod in its own lightweight confidential virtual machine. The CPU encrypts memory and, on supported hardware, GPUs can also be included in the trust boundary. Developers continue using standard Kubernetes workflows while workloads run inside hardware-based Trusted Execution Environments, which isolate code and data during processing.

Fortanix Confidential Computing Manager performs composite attestation, checking the integrity of CPU and GPU trusted execution environments, firmware and software before authorizing key release. Fortanix Data Security Manager, which the announcement describes as backed by FIPS 140-3 Level 3 hardware security module certification, releases encryption keys only to attested environments. If attestation fails, the keys remain protected, preventing the workload from accessing sensitive data or executing successfully.

Rafay supplies the operating layer around those security controls. Its blueprints install the Fortanix CoCo add-ons and keep them synchronized across confidential clusters. Operators onboard GPU infrastructure once and assign each customer an isolated tenant, while policies, access controls and quotas apply across tenants and clusters.

Administrators can package a confidential workload as a catalog item, called a SKU, in Rafay SKU Studio. End users then launch it on demand through Rafay Developer Hub without filing a support ticket. The workflow combines tenant-level service management with Fortanix’s verification of each protected environment before sensitive information is unlocked.

“Model owners want their weights hidden from operators and customers; enterprises want their data hidden from model owners and operators; and operators must prove they can see neither, even via another tenant or compromised software,” said Anuj Jaiswal, chief product officer at Fortanix.

The companies see several operating models for the service. Neoclouds and GPU cloud providers can offer confidential AI as a premium option backed by evidence that tenant workloads run in trusted environments and keys go only to verified workloads. Sovereign AI clouds can retain keys in an operator-controlled hardware security module, on-premises or as SaaS. Regulated enterprises can process sensitive data without exposing it to the infrastructure operator, while model providers can keep model weights encrypted until a customer environment passes attestation.

A Rafay technical post dated March 16, 2026 documented the companies’ earlier confidential virtual machine integration. In that workflow, Fortanix validated the environment and supplied keys after attestation, while Rafay provided repeatable service definitions, centralized multi-tenancy and self-service deployment. The new launch extends that division of responsibilities to CoCo-based Kubernetes pods.

Fortanix had also already announced Confidential AI support for CoCo in October 2026. That release described work on open-source plugins connecting its attestation and key-release products to CoCo, with SaaS, on-premises and fully air-gapped deployment options. The new development is Rafay’s available orchestration of those deployments alongside the existing virtual machine integration.

The launch therefore separates service operation from data access: Rafay handles the catalog, tenant isolation and governance, while Fortanix’s attestation determines whether decryption keys are released. For shared GPU infrastructure, that allows an operator to run and sell AI capacity without customers having to treat its administrative access as implicit permission to read their data or model weights.

The AI Industry Wants Our Trust, But It Needs Better Controls First

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In security, we don’t get to discover whether our controls work after something goes wrong. We test them. We challenge them. We assume someone will eventually find the weakness we missed.

So why should frontier AI get a different standard?

As AI systems become more autonomous and gain greater access to real-world systems, we’re seeing something that should concern anyone who has spent time on the defensive side of cybersecurity: capability is moving faster than control.

And speed is not an excuse for weaker security. It is the reason we need stronger security.

A recent assessment by nonprofit Guidelight AI looked at publicly available evidence of AI control practices across Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and xAI. None scored above a C+ overall.

That doesn’t mean these companies are reckless. It doesn’t mean additional controls aren’t operating behind closed doors.

But security doesn’t run on assumptions. If we’re being asked to trust increasingly powerful systems, we should expect evidence that the controls surrounding them can withstand the same scrutiny we would demand anywhere else.

When an AI agent behaves unexpectedly, gains unintended access or exposes a weakness, the story tends to focus on what the technology did.

A security practitioner wants to know: Was the activity logged? Did anyone see it happening? Were dangerous actions gated? Could someone stop the system? Was there a containment plan, and had anyone tested it?

Those questions aren’t unique to AI. They’re the questions we’ve been asking in cybersecurity for years.

Yet prevention and containment appear to remain weak spots in the emerging frontier AI control environment. Public evidence around gated actions, circuit breakers and emergency containment remains limited.

That should matter more than the novelty of the incident. When something fails, I want to know what failed, what stopped it from getting worse and what changed afterward.

Transparency is useful. Learning is better.

I don’t think the biggest near-term threat is AI suddenly inventing an entirely new category of cyberattack. The bigger problem is much more practical. AI can make the attacks we already understand happen much faster.

Research that once took hours can happen in minutes. Attack techniques can be chained together faster. Vulnerabilities can be identified and acted upon before a human-centered security process has made it through its first escalation call.

That’s a problem because much of today’s security infrastructure still assumes there will be time to respond.

If an attack unfolds rapidly, a response process built around tickets, meetings, approvals and manual escalation can become obsolete before anyone reaches step three.

And that’s where the AI industry has some work to do.

If frontier labs expect enterprises to rethink visibility, vulnerability management, architecture and incident response for the AI era, they should show us that they’ve done the same thing themselves.

I’ve spent my career around security testing, and one principle holds up remarkably well: intentions don’t prove security. Evidence does.

You find out what happens when the controls don’t behave the way the architecture diagram says they should. And you make sure someone has the authority to stop the system when necessary. Frontier AI should be held to that same standard.

That means adversarial testing of controls, measurable evidence that those controls work and clear authority for shutting systems down when something goes wrong.

Frontier labs cannot improvise containment after an AI agent acts outside its intended boundaries. As they train and test increasingly capable models, they must take responsibility for the actions those systems can take. That requires rigorous adversarial testing, pre-authorized safeguards, clear limits on agent authority and proven ways to intervene or shut a system down before a failure spreads.

Human judgment still matters. But the labs developing these systems must establish the boundaries, test whether they hold and remain accountable when they do not.

Their responsibility is not simply to demonstrate what an agent can do. It is to show that the agent can operate safely, that its behavior can be controlled and that new capabilities make the world more secure, not less.

I’m not arguing that we slow AI innovation to a crawl. I’m arguing that security has to keep pace with it. If we invest enormous resources in making AI more capable, we need comparable urgency around making it defensible.

But as frontier labs accelerate the training and testing of new AI models with cyber capabilities, they must keep defenders in mind. First, by helping them detect and respond to threats faster. More importantly, by keeping those systems from becoming threats themselves. That responsibility also requires accepting outside scrutiny.

Independent review can’t become something companies embrace only when it supports the story they want to tell. If frontier AI systems are powerful enough to change the threat landscape, their controls should be strong enough to withstand meaningful independent testing.

As a security practitioner, I’m willing to give it. But first, show me the controls.

James Webb’s Mysteriously Bright Galaxies May Finally Have an Explanation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Calculations suggest powerful ultraviolet light from young stars could sweep gas out of early galaxies while leaving dense star-forming regions intact, offering a clue to their mysterious brightness.

The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed something unexpected about the early universe: bright galaxies were far more common just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang than astronomers thought possible. A new analysis suggests these galaxies may not necessarily be producing more light than expected. Instead, powerful ultraviolet radiation from young stars could be clearing away gas and dust, allowing more of their light to reach us.

Researchers at the Simons Foundation’s Flatiron Institute in New York City and the University of Texas at Dallas investigated this possibility by examining how ultraviolet light called Lyman-alpha radiation interacts with material surrounding young stars. Their findings, published November 4 in The Astrophysical Journal, suggest that this radiation can drive gas and dust out of less dense regions of galaxies, potentially explaining why some of the earliest galaxies appear unusually bright.

“Our results show Lyman-alpha radiation can push low-density gas and dust away really, really fast,” says Shyam Menon, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute’s Center for Computational Astrophysics and the study’s lead author. “It can sort of clear the ‘fog,’ allowing us to see stars in the early universe much more clearly than we would otherwise.”

Lyman-alpha radiation forms when an electron collides with a hydrogen atom or joins a hydrogen ion, and it can be especially abundant in regions where young stars are forming. When this light encounters gas or dust, it transfers momentum to the material, creating pressure that can push it outward.

What makes Lyman-alpha radiation particularly powerful is how hydrogen responds to it. While most absorbed light is released at a different wavelength, hydrogen atoms almost immediately re-emit Lyman-alpha radiation at the same wavelength, allowing nearby atoms to absorb and release it repeatedly. These repeated interactions increase the force exerted by the light, potentially pushing surrounding material away from regions where stars are forming.

Astronomers have long suspected that this pressure could reshape stellar nurseries by removing gas needed for star formation, possibly even stopping the process under extreme conditions. However, the repeated interactions between radiation and hydrogen are difficult to reproduce in computer simulations, making it challenging to determine how much influence the radiation actually has.

“We’ve known for a very long time that Lyman-alpha radiation could be important because it carries a lot of force,” Menon says. “But we’ve never actually been able to estimate how important it is in a realistic environment because of how challenging it is to model.”

To address this problem, Menon and collaborator Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas analyzed snapshots from an existing simulation of a star-forming region in the early universe. Rather than tracking every interaction between light and matter, they calculated how much pressure Lyman-alpha radiation would exert on the surrounding gas and dust.

Their calculations revealed that the radiation behaves differently depending on the density of the surrounding material. In dense star-forming regions, gravity remained strong enough to hold gas and dust in place despite the outward pressure, suggesting that Lyman-alpha radiation had little effect on star formation there. In less crowded regions between stars, however, the radiation could overcome gravity and drive material outward at high speeds.

Multiple views of a 2.5-million-year simulation visualizing a gas cloud as it is cleared away and the formation of stars. From left to right, the views show the gas density, ionization, and temperature. Credit: Shyam Menon (Flatiron Institute) and Aaron Smith (UT Dallas)

“When the density is low enough, the Lyman-alpha radiation can push the gas to really high speeds, launching explosive winds that can shoot material clear out of the galaxy,” Menon says.

These winds could help explain the unexpectedly bright galaxies detected by the James Webb Space Telescope. By removing loosely distributed gas and dust from between stars, the radiation may create clearer paths for starlight to escape, making distant galaxies appear brighter without requiring them to produce unusually large amounts of light.

Important questions remain, particularly about the properties of dust in the early universe and how radiation interacts with moving gas as stars form. Researchers will need more comprehensive simulations that account for these processes together to better understand their influence on early galaxies.

Recent work by Olof Nebrin of Stockholm University and study co-author Smith has demonstrated that these interactions can be modeled in two dimensions, although more realistic three-dimensional simulations remain a challenge.

“But the hope is that in the coming years, with improvements to computing power and the development of more effective algorithms, we can eventually do these simulations in three dimensions.”

Reference: “Lyα Radiation Pressure in Dense Star Clusters: Implications for Star Formation and Winds at Cosmic Dawn” by Shyam H. Menon and Aaron Smith, 4 November 2026, The Astrophysical Journal. DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/aeaed6
