Report: Apple saved over $5 billion in taxes through Singapore and Ireland in 2026

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new Financial Times report reveals how Apple saved billions in taxes in 2026 by shifting profits through Singapore and Ireland. Here are the details.

A few days ago, Apple published its annual Form 10-K, as required by the SEC, containing detailed information about its financial performance and operations for its 2026 fiscal year.

Included in the form, as the FT noted, is information regarding how Apple reduced its tax bill by billions of dollars through its operations in Singapore and Ireland.

For years, Apple’s tax arrangements in Ireland were the subject of scrutiny, culminating in a 2024 ruling by the EU’s top court that required the company to pay €13 billion in back taxes. Apple completed the payment in 2025.

That came a few years after Apple announced plans to bring most of its $252 billion in overseas cash back to the US, following tax reforms introduced during Donald Trump’s first administration.

Since then, to discourage companies from shifting profits to countries with lower tax rates, more than 130 countries have agreed to introduce a minimum corporate tax rate of 15% for large multinational companies, as part of a 2021 initiative coordinated by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).

Under the agreement, companies that pay less than 15% in a particular country may face additional taxes to make up the difference. The new rules also resulted in an additional $2.5 billion in taxes for Apple in Singapore in 2026, according to the company’s SEC filing.

Ireland adopted the new rules in 2024, followed by Singapore in 2025.

Back to the FT’s report, it notes that new US accounting rules now require publicly traded companies to disclose when their taxes on overseas profits differ significantly from the 21% US federal corporate tax rate.

These new requirements revealed that Apple saved $2.7 billion in taxes through Singapore in 2026, surpassing the $2.6 billion it saved through Ireland.

If Apple had been taxed on all of its global profits in 2026 at the US federal rate, it would have incurred a total bill of $33bn — demonstrating the scale of the $5bn tax reduction it received from booking profits in Ireland and Singapore. The company incurred corporation taxes totalling $27bn this year.

The FT also noted that Apple’s effective tax rate in Singapore fluctuated around 10% between 2023 and 2025, but is expected to approach 15% under the new rules. Singapore’s standard corporate tax rate is 17%.

Marine Atlan’s coming-of-age drama “La Gradiva” earned the Critics Award for best film, presented in association with the Los Angeles Film Critics Association. Written by Atlan and Anne Brouillet, the film marks the directorial debut of Atlan, who is also an acclaimed cinematographer. U.S. distribution will be handled by 1-2 Special.

It stars Antonia Buresi alongside newcomers Colas Quignard, Suzanne Gerin and Mitia Capellier-Audat.

The Documentary Film Award, meanwhile, went to “Whispers in the Woods,” the latest film from acclaimed wildlife photographer and filmmaker Vincent Munier, whose previous credits include “The Velvet Queen.”

“The Green Eyes,” directed by Fanny Liatard and Jérémy Trouilh, won the American Students Award. On the television side, “Alice: Pioneer of Cinema,” starring Bérénice Bejo as pioneering French filmmaker Alice Guy-Blaché, won the Audience Series Award. The series, directed by Virginie Verrier and Guillaume Lonergan, has also sold to major territories with a U.S. deal in the pipeline.

The Jury Series Award went to “Swapped,” directed by Vania Leturcq and Savina Dellicour.

In the short film competition, Melissa Silveira’s “Diego” won the Audience Short Film Award, while Jocelyn Charles’ animated film “God Is Shy,” featuring the voices of Danièle Evenou, Alba Gaïa Bellugi and Anthony Bajon, earned the Jury Short Film Award.

The awards were announced at the festival’s closing ceremony on Sunday, Nov. 8, following a week of screenings and industry events celebrating French cinema and television.

Druski’s ‘SNL’ Gig Marks The Start Of The Creator Economy’s Franchise Era – Guest Column

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editors note: Adam Goodman is the founder and CEO of Invisible Narratives, a creator-first entertainment studio focused on developing and scaling multi-platform franchises. He previously served as President of Paramount Pictures’ Film Group and President of Production at DreamWorks SKG.

On Saturday, Druski became the first host in Saturday Night Live history to have built his career as a social media creator. On December 1, his semi-scripted comedy Collect & Praise premieres on Netflix.

A decade ago, an Instagram sketch comedian headlining one of American comedy’s most storied institutions would have sounded like a punchline. Today, it’s a programming decision. Watch on Deadline

But the bigger story isn’t that Hollywood is embracing creators. It’s that creators are fundamentally changing how entertainment gets made.

The entertainment industry has repeatedly reinvented itself over the course of my career, from working for John Hughes while in high school in the late ’80s to my time overseeing the Motion Picture Group at Paramount Pictures and DreamWorks. From VHS to DVD to streaming, each transformation changed how content is distributed and monetized. But the fundamental process of developing content has remained largely the same.

We develop an idea, hire writers and directors, assemble a cast, finance production, and spend millions marketing the finished product. Only then do we really discover whether an audience wants it. We test and research, but by the time meaningful feedback arrives, much of the money has already been spent.

It’s an extraordinarily expensive way to find out whether something works.

When we released Paranormal Activity, we tried something different. We let audiences demand the film by city before expanding its release. Rather than simply telling audiences what to watch, we let them tell us what they wanted.

Creators increasingly operate with that kind of audience feedback built into everything they do. They start with an audience and build programming around it. They introduce characters, test formats, and learn in days what resonates, what gets shared, and what brings people back — all with a sliver of a studio’s infrastructure.

That doesn’t mean every creator can make a great movie, or that 30 million followers translates into 30 million ticket buyers. They don’t. But creators have what Hollywood has historically spent fortunes trying to acquire: a direct, ongoing relationship with an audience backed by insights and data. That audience is now watching creator content on the household’s primary screen, not just iPads and cell phones. According to Nielsen, YouTube accounted for a record 15.8% of U.S. television viewing in August, more than double Netflix’s share.

Creators are extraordinarily good at building audiences. But Hollywood has spent a century learning to build franchises.

A successful channel can reach hundreds of millions of people, but its value can depend on the creator’s ability to keep producing and satisfying algorithms they don’t control. A franchise is intellectual property that can outlive its original format, its platform, and even its creator. It’s a world audiences return to through film, television, games, consumer products, and experiences that haven’t been invented yet.

When we launched Transformers, we weren’t just making a movie about robots. We were reintroducing a world to a generation that had never played with the toys, and building something that could keep generating stories, products and audiences for decades. That’s the discipline Hollywood brings. What the creator economy has introduced is the ability to spot signals of franchise potential long before anyone commits hundreds of millions of dollars.

Five Nights at Freddy’s proves the point. The independent video game franchise spent nearly a decade building a fan community through sequels, fan theories and YouTube playthroughs before reaching theaters. By the time the movie opened, the audience wasn’t a hope. It was a fact, and the film became one of the biggest horror hits of its year. The Amazing Digital Circus offers another example: an animated YouTube series that built a global fan base entirely outside of the traditional studio system.

Creators have built something extraordinarily valuable: audiences with a genuine emotional investment in their success. Their fans aren’t just watching. They’re rooting for them. But building an audience isn’t the same as building intellectual property. Many creators remain caught on a treadmill of producing the next video, chasing trends, and figuring out what to post next week. They have loyal followers, but not necessarily repeatable formats, enduring characters, or a programming strategy. The opportunity is to turn that audience loyalty into intellectual property they can own, expand, and build into lasting franchises.

That changes the relationship between Hollywood and creators. Creators don’t need Hollywood to become famous; many already have audiences larger than television networks. What they need, if they want to build something that outlasts the next upload, is a way to turn that audience relationship into intellectual property they can own, develop and expand. Hollywood can provide the creative expertise, financing, production infrastructure, distribution and franchise-building discipline to make that happen.

Creators are extraordinarily good at building audiences. But Hollywood has spent a century learning to build franchises.

But Hollywood has to learn from creators, too. Creators have mastered continuous programming, community engagement, rapid experimentation and immediate feedback. The studios that define the next decade won’t simply adopt the creator economy’s talent. They’ll adopt its instincts while bringing the creative ambition and franchise-building expertise that Hollywood has spent a century developing.

The first chapter of the creator economy was about individuals building audiences. The second was about monetizing those audiences through advertising, sponsorships, and commerce. The next chapter will be about ownership: turning channels into programming brands and original characters and story worlds into enduring franchises.

Great stories and compelling characters have always been the foundation of this industry, and Hollywood has never had a monopoly on either. A new generation of storytellers has direct access to global audiences without waiting for Hollywood’s permission. That’s not a threat. It’s the opportunity of the decade.

The next generation of franchises won’t all come from Hollywood. But Hollywood can help make them last for generations.

Update Your iPhone to Prevent People From Seeing iMessages They Shouldn't

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Has this ever happened to you? You add someone new to an iMessage group chat, and wonder, "Huh, can they see any of the messages from before they were added?" The answer to that question is supposed to be "no." By design, new iMessage group chat members are only privy to texts that arrive after they were added. When they join, it's essentially a blank slate, as if you just created the group for the first time. At least, that's how it's intended to work.

As it turns out, there's a bug with iMessage that may allow new group chat members to see texts from before they were added to the chat. That's a problem, and not just for the iPhone users out there talking smack about people before inviting them to join the group chat. It means iMessage, and Apple's platforms in general, aren't working like they're supposed to be. You don't have to be talking behind people's backs to not want new members seeing existing chats. Maybe you're planning a surprise party for someone, and add them to the group once everyone's in on the plan; maybe the texts in the chat aren't relevant to the current group, and the person joining simply does not need to access them.

That's not all: There exists another security flaw that could allow remote hackers to join an iMessage group and receive new messages. You might add a new user to a group chat and inadvertently invite them to read all existing messages, while at the same time being attacked by a bad actor who can then intercept all future messages. Not good.

Luckily, there's currently a fix for these problems. Apple patched (and revealed) the bugs, along with seven other security flaws, with a series of software updates on Monday for iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple Watch, and Vision Pro. Not all of the updates include patches for these two iMessage flaws—specifically, some of the updates for older Apple devices. But the rest, including iOS 27.2.1, macOS 27.2.1, macOS 26.7.3, macOS 15.8.3, watchOS 27.2.1, and visionOS 27.2.1, include the iMessage patches.

Each update includes its own list of patches, but, for this article, I'll take a look at the nine patches in iOS 27.2.1. You'll find the two iMessage bugs, plus seven other patches, in the list below:

AirPlay: Impact: An attacker in Bluetooth range may be able to bypass the rate limit on pairing code attempts. Description: A logic issue was addressed with improved state management. CVE-2026-113968: Apple

App Intents: Impact: An app may be able to access sensitive user data. Description: An authorization issue was addressed with improved state management. CVE-2026-113963: László Várady

Bluetooth: Impact: An attacker in Bluetooth range may be able to corrupt process memory. Description: A use-after-free issue was addressed with improved memory management. CVE-2026-113970: Apple

brotli: Impact: Processing maliciously crafted input may lead to memory corruption. Description: An out-of-bounds write issue was addressed with improved input validation. CVE-2026-111497

CoreUtils: Impact: A malicious Bluetooth device may be able to establish a pairing without user interaction. Description: This issue was addressed with improved checks. CVE-2026-113965: Apple

CoreUtils: Impact: An attacker in Bluetooth range may be able to bypass the rate limit on pairing code attempts and pair with a device without user interaction. Description: A logic issue was addressed with improved state management. CVE-2026-113964: an anonymous researcher

Messages: Impact: A remote attacker may be able to join an iMessage group chat and receive its messages. Description: An authorization issue was addressed with improved validation. CVE-2026-113961: an anonymous researcher

Messages: Impact: A user added to an iMessage group may be able to access messages sent before they joined. Description: This issue was addressed by removing the vulnerable code. CVE-2026-113960: an anonymous researcher

Rapportd: Impact: An attacker on the same network may be able to bypass the rate limit on AirPlay pairing code attempts. Description: A logic issue was addressed with improved state management. CVE-2026-113969: Apple

For a full accounting of the patches included in the other security updates, check out Apple's official security releases site.

To install Apple's latest security updates, you'll simply need to update your device as you usually do. On iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, and Vision Pro, head to Settings > General > Software Update; on Mac, go to System Settings > General > Software Update. Here, follow the on-screen instructions to download and install the latest version of your device's software.
