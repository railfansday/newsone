On June 18, 2024, it overtook Microsoft $MSFT as the most valuable listed company. Company rankings by market value change daily. That makes the June 2024 milestone a dated one, not a permanent title. The two dates sit 31 years apart.

The link between those two dates is the graphics chip itself. Hardware designed to draw 3D scenes for games turned out to suit other kinds of computing work. Nvidia's CUDA software, introduced in 2006, let developers use its GPUs for general-purpose programs. In 2012, AlexNet showed that the same chips could speed up deep learning. Those two steps pointed the company toward AI systems for data centers. After OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022, demand for Nvidia's data center chips rose.

Not every step moved the company forward. Its first product, released in 1995, did not succeed commercially. The company changed its approach to graphics afterward. A planned acquisition was abandoned after regulators raised objections. Its purchase of Mellanox, by contrast, added data center networking technology. Those setbacks and deals sit in the timeline alongside product launches and market value figures.

This Nvidia timeline covers 10 milestones, from the 1993 founding to the June 2024 day the chipmaker passed Microsoft in market value. It is a selection, not a complete history of the company. The entries run in chronological order and are not ranked. The early entries cover graphics chips for PCs and a game console. The middle entries cover the software and research that opened GPUs to new uses. The later entries cover data center hardware, acquisitions, export restrictions and the market value steps that followed. Market value claims in the timeline are tied to the dates they occurred. Each entry stands on its own, with the date, product, deal or figure that defined it.

Nvidia $NVDA was founded in April 1993 by three co-founders: Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. The company was based in Santa Clara, California.

Its early work centered on 3D graphics for personal computers.

The NV1 was Nvidia $NVDA's first product, and it reached the market in 1995. It did not succeed commercially.

Nvidia $NVDA went public on Nasdaq $NDAQ on January 22, 1999, at $12 a share. In October of that year, it launched the GeForce 256 and marketed the chip as the first GPU, or graphics processing unit.

The listing came less than six years after the company's April 1993 founding. Once its shares began trading on Nasdaq, Nvidia became a publicly traded company.

The product milestone followed about nine months after the listing. In October 1999, Nvidia released the GeForce 256, a graphics chip for PCs.

Nvidia promoted the GeForce 256 as the first GPU. That label was the company's own marketing claim. Nvidia used the term to present the chip as a new kind of graphics product.

The two 1999 events differed in kind. The IPO changed how Nvidia was owned and financed. The GeForce 256 gave the company a new chip and a name for the category it claimed that chip started.

Nvidia $NVDA supplied the graphics processor for Microsoft $MSFT's original Xbox, the game console that launched in 2001. The deal added a console business to the chipmaker's existing sales of graphics cards for PCs.

With the Xbox, Nvidia's graphics hardware reached players through two kinds of products. One was a graphics card installed in a personal computer. The other was a home console sold under Microsoft's brand.

Nvidia $NVDA announced CUDA in November 2006. The platform lets developers run general-purpose programs on Nvidia GPUs, not only graphics code.

The announcement came alongside the GeForce 8800 GTX, a graphics card built on Nvidia's G80 chip. Nvidia introduced the software platform and the new card at the same time.

A GPU had been designed to render images for games. CUDA gave programmers a way to write software that put an Nvidia GPU to work on tasks other than drawing a picture on a screen. That widened the range of jobs the company's hardware could take on.

The GeForce 8800 GTX was a graphics card for PC gaming. Paired with CUDA, the launch addressed two audiences. One was buyers looking for a graphics card. The other was developers who wanted to use a GPU for their own programs.

CUDA's role grew in the years after 2006. It later became the software base for much of AI computing.

AlexNet was a deep learning model. Its ImageNet result showed that GPUs could speed up that kind of work.

The 2012 win linked Nvidia's graphics processors to deep learning research. The link came through the work of Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, who chose a pair of the chipmaker's GPUs to train their network.

In April 2016, Nvidia $NVDA announced the Tesla $TSLA P100, a GPU built on its Pascal architecture. That same month, it introduced the DGX-1, an AI system. Together, the two launches marked the chipmaker's turn toward the data center.

The two products sat at different levels. The Tesla P100 was a single chip. The DGX-1 was a complete system built for AI work. With it, Nvidia offered a finished machine as well as the processors that go inside one.

In August 2016, Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang delivered a DGX-1 to OpenAI. The handover came four months after the system's introduction. It put one of the new machines in the hands of an organization working on artificial intelligence.

Pascal did not stay Nvidia's newest design for long. In 2017, the GPU maker followed it with Volta, an architecture that added tensor cores.

Nvidia $NVDA agreed in March 2019 to buy Mellanox for $6.9 billion. The purchase closed in April 2020 and gave the chipmaker data center networking technology.

About 13 months passed between the announcement and the close. The deal took a business built around graphics processors into the networking side of the data center.

A much larger bid followed. In September 2020, five months after the Mellanox purchase closed, Nvidia announced a $40 billion deal to buy Arm.

That agreement never closed. It stayed pending for about 17 months. In February 2022, the companies abandoned it after regulators raised objections.

The two deals ended differently. Mellanox became part of Nvidia, and its networking technology stayed with the chipmaker. The Arm bid ended without Nvidia acquiring the company.

Hopper is the name of the architecture. H100 is the name of the data center GPU built on it. Nvidia announced the chip in March, before ChatGPT reached the public that November.

ChatGPT was an OpenAI product, not an Nvidia one. The demand that rose after its release was for Nvidia's data center chips. The H100 belongs to that category.

U.S. export rules also limited where those chips could be sold. In October 2022, the U.S. imposed restrictions on exports of advanced chips to China. The rules covered the A100 and the H100, two of Nvidia's data center GPUs.

The H100 therefore came under U.S. export restrictions for China in the same year Nvidia announced it.

Nvidia followed Hopper with another architecture. In March 2024, the company announced Blackwell.

This stretch of Nvidia history runs from the H100 announcement in March 2022 to the Blackwell announcement in March 2024. The October 2022 export restrictions and the November 2022 release of ChatGPT fall between those two dates.

Nvidia $NVDA's market value first passed $1 trillion in May 2023. It crossed $2 trillion in February 2024 and $3 trillion in June 2024. On June 18, 2024, the chipmaker overtook Microsoft $MSFT as the most valuable listed company.

Market value, also called market capitalization, is a company's share price multiplied by its number of shares outstanding. It moves with the stock on every trading day. Each threshold records how investors priced Nvidia on a given date. It is not a fixed measure of the business.

Market value is also separate from a company's sales or profit. A higher market value does not mean a company sold more or earned more than another. It reflects the price investors paid for its shares, multiplied across all of them. When Nvidia passed Microsoft, the comparison was between two stock market valuations on the same day.

The word "listed" matters in that comparison. The ranking covers companies whose shares trade on public stock exchanges. A privately held company has no daily share price. It cannot be measured on the same daily basis.

The two trillion-dollar steps took different lengths of time. Nvidia needed about nine months to go from $1 trillion in May 2023 to $2 trillion in February 2024. The climb from $2 trillion to $3 trillion took about four months and ended in June 2024. Each step added the same $1 trillion in market value. The second step took less than half as long as the first.

The Microsoft milestone came in the same month as the $3 trillion crossing. The lead was a position held on a particular trading day. A company that ranks first on one day can fall behind on the next as share prices move.

An S&P 500 index fund looks like a vote for 500 companies at once. The money inside it is not split evenly, though. Each dollar is divided according to company size. The largest firms take a far bigger slice than the smallest ones. Nvidia $NVDA shows how that design plays out at the top of the market.

The split is set by float-adjusted market capitalization, meaning the value of the shares available to public investors. A stock that outpaces the rest of the market gains weight automatically. No committee vote or rebalancing is needed to make that happen.

The company first passed a $4 trillion market value on July 9, 2025. On Oct. 29, 2025, it became the first public company to reach $5 trillion. A market value that large translates directly into a heavy presence in any cap-weighted index.

So how much of the S&P 500 is Nvidia? The answer changes every trading day. Its weight rises and falls with its share price relative to the rest of the roughly 500 companies in the index. Any precise percentage is a snapshot tied to a specific date. Index providers and fund companies publish dated holdings that show the current figure.

Concentration is the reason that weight matters. When a few stocks make up a large part of an index, the index's return depends heavily on how those few perform. A drop in one top holding moves the whole benchmark more than a drop in a smaller company would. Anyone who owns an S&P 500 fund holds those stocks automatically, whether or not they chose to.

The S&P 500 assigns each company a weight based on its float-adjusted market capitalization. Companies with more tradable market value take bigger slices of the index, and smaller companies take thinner ones.

Market capitalization is a company's share price multiplied by its shares outstanding. The float adjustment trims that share count to the stock available for public trading. Shares held by insiders, governments or other long-term strategic holders are generally excluded. S&P Dow Jones Indices, the company that maintains the index, applies this adjustment so each weight reflects stock that investors can actually buy.

The index did not always work this way. The S&P 500 launched in 1957 and was weighted by full market capitalization for decades. It switched to float adjustment in 2005, phasing the change in over that year.

Every constituent's weight is a share of one combined total. All the weights together add up to 100%. When one company gains weight, others must lose some.

Weights shift whenever prices move. A company whose stock rises faster than the rest of the index gains weight. A company whose shares lag loses weight in the same way. Neither change requires any trading by the index.

This self-adjusting design matters for index funds. A fund that holds each stock in proportion to its market value stays aligned with the index as prices change. It does not need to buy or sell shares just because one stock has outpaced another. Trading is mostly needed when companies join or leave the index, or when share counts change.

The S&P 500 is usually described as 500 companies, and it holds roughly that many. It lists slightly more than 500 tickers, though. Some companies have more than one share class in the index. Alphabet $GOOGL, for example, has both its Class A and Class C shares included.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average works on a different principle. It is price-weighted, so a stock with a higher share price counts for more, regardless of the company's total value. In the S&P 500, share price alone does not set a company's influence.

Stock splits show the difference. A split lowers a company's share price and raises its share count by the same factor. Its market value stays the same, so its S&P 500 weight does not change. In the Dow, the lower share price shrinks the stock's pull on the average.

A committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices decides which companies belong in the S&P 500. It applies eligibility rules covering U.S. domicile, liquidity and a record of positive earnings. Once a company is in, the market-cap formula sets its weight.

Nvidia $NVDA's weight in the S&P 500 has no fixed value. It equals the chipmaker's float-adjusted market value divided by the combined float-adjusted value of every company in the index, and both numbers shift with every trade. The result is a share that changes throughout every trading session.

That makes any answer to how much of the S&P 500 is Nvidia a reading from one particular moment. A figure quoted in a news story last month describes the index as it stood on that day. Prices may have moved enough since then to make it stale.

The weight responds to relative performance, not absolute performance. If Nvidia's stock climbs while most of the index stays flat, its slice grows. If Nvidia and the rest of the market rise by the same proportion, its weight stays where it was. A sharp single-day drop in Nvidia shrinks its share even if nothing else moves.

Moves in other large companies matter too. The index total includes every constituent, so a rally in other megacaps enlarges the denominator. Nvidia's weight can slip on a day when its own stock does nothing at all.

The most reliable way to check a current figure is a published holdings list with a date on it. S&P Dow Jones Indices, which runs the index, publishes fact sheets that list the largest constituents by weight. Exchange-traded funds that track the S&P 500 also post their holdings online. Examples include the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF and the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF. Many ETFs update those lists daily.

A fund's reported weight can differ slightly from the index's own figure. Funds hold small amounts of cash, and their records may reflect a different pricing time. Traditional mutual funds that track the index usually disclose holdings less often, and typically with a lag.

Reading the figure correctly also means checking its date and its source. Two articles citing different numbers for Nvidia's weight are not necessarily in conflict. They may simply describe the index on different days.

Nvidia's position in the index is an output of market prices, not a fixed allocation. It is recalculated continuously during trading hours as those prices change.

Nvidia's total market value stayed the same at the moment of the split. The S&P 500 weights companies by market value rather than share price. The split therefore left Nvidia's index weight untouched. That weight still moves only as Nvidia's value changes relative to the other constituents.

The S&P 500 is top-heavy. A small group of very large companies, Nvidia $NVDA among them, accounts for an outsized share of the index's total value. Several hundred other members split the rest.

The gap between the top and the bottom of the index is wide. The smallest constituents are worth a tiny fraction of the largest. A single megacap can carry more weight than dozens of the index's smaller companies combined. On many days, the index's move tracks a handful of giant stocks more closely than it tracks the typical member.

Megacap is an informal label for companies valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars or more. The information technology sector's weight alone therefore leaves out several of the index's biggest technology-driven companies.

The combined share held by the largest names changes every trading day. S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes a fact sheet listing the index's top 10 constituents by weight, along with the date those figures apply to.

The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index holds the same companies as the standard S&P 500, but it gives each one an identical slice. Comparing the two versions shows how much the familiar index depends on its largest members, including Nvidia $NVDA.

In the standard index, a company's influence tracks its float-adjusted market value. The biggest firms carry far more weight than the smallest ones. A move in a single megacap stock can outweigh the combined moves of dozens of smaller constituents.

The equal-weight version removes that hierarchy. At each rebalance, every constituent receives the same allocation. Nvidia then counts for no more than the smallest company in the index. A gain of a given size in any one stock has about the same effect on the equal-weight index, whichever company posts it.

S&P Dow Jones Indices rebalances the equal-weight index quarterly. Between those dates, winners drift above the target and laggards drift below it. At each rebalance, the index trims the stocks that rose and adds to those that fell. Every holding returns to an equal share.

That process requires more trading than the cap-weighted version, which adjusts on its own as prices move. Funds that track the equal-weight index generally have higher turnover as a result.

The gap between the two indexes is a readout of market breadth. When the largest stocks outperform the rest, the cap-weighted S&P 500 pulls ahead. Those stocks drive most of its return. When the largest names lag, the equal-weight version tends to come out ahead. In that version, the typical company matters as much as the giants.

A wide, persistent gap in favor of the cap-weighted index points to a market led by a narrow group. A narrowing gap, or one that flips, indicates that gains are spreading to more of the index's members. Neither version is a forecast. Each measures the same market from a different angle.

The equal-weight index also tilts toward smaller companies by design. Relative to the standard index, it gives more room to sectors such as industrials, utilities and real estate. It gives less room to technology. That sector mix is one reason the two versions can diverge even when no single stock dominates.

For a reader trying to gauge Nvidia's footprint in the index, the comparison isolates the effect of company size. In the equal-weight index, Nvidia's share returns to the same level as every other constituent each quarter. In the cap-weighted index, its share is set by its market value. The distance between those two treatments is one measure of how concentrated the standard index has become.

Nvidia $NVDA is not the first company to carry outsized weight in the S&P 500. IBM $IBM and AT&T $T each held unusually large shares of the index in earlier decades. Technology stocks as a group made up a very large slice of it around the 2000 dot-com peak.

The S&P 500 in its current form launched in 1957. It has weighted companies by market value from the start. That design means the most valuable companies of any era have had the most influence on how the index moves.

IBM was a leading example for much of the postwar period. Its mainframe computers dominated corporate data processing in the 1960s and 1970s. As investors rewarded that position, IBM's market value grew large enough that its share price could shift the broader index on its own.

AT&T held a similar position as the parent of the Bell System, the regulated telephone network that served most of the U.S. Its weight was reshaped by antitrust law rather than by share-price moves. An antitrust settlement broke up the Bell System, effective Jan. 1, 1984. AT&T's local phone operations were split into seven regional companies. The value once held in a single stock was spread across several separate ones.

The 1960s and early 1970s also produced the "Nifty Fifty." This was a group of large, fast-growing companies that many investors treated as stocks to buy and hold at almost any price. Many of those shares fell sharply in the 1973-74 bear market.

The late-1990s run-up created a different kind of concentration. Instead of one or two companies, an entire sector swelled. Microsoft $MSFT, Cisco $CSCO Systems, Intel $INTC and Oracle $ORCL were among the largest constituents as software, chip and networking stocks climbed. After the market peaked in March 2000, the sector's slide pulled the whole index down with it. The S&P 500 did not reach its low until October 2002.

Each episode unwound in its own way — a court-supervised breakup for AT&T, a prolonged market decline for the dot-com leaders. Intel, one of the largest companies of that era, was later replaced by Nvidia in the Dow Jones Industrial Average in November 2024. In the S&P 500, the mechanism has stayed the same throughout. The companies the market values most at a given moment carry the most weight, and the index rises and falls with them.

Concentration risk means an S&P 500 index fund's return depends heavily on a small number of very large stocks, including Nvidia $NVDA. A drop in one of those top holdings pulls the fund down further than it would if every company carried equal weight.

The cause is built into the fund's design. A fund that tracks the S&P 500 copies the index's weights, and those weights follow company size. A shareholder in that fund does not choose how much Nvidia to own. The index sets that amount, and the fund follows it.

This works in both directions. A rally in the largest stocks can lift the whole index even when many of its members are flat or falling. A selloff in the same names can pull the index down while many smaller companies hold steady. In either case, the headline index return reflects a handful of giants more than it reflects the typical company in the index.

Holders can also own more of a single company than they realize. An S&P 500 fund already includes Nvidia at its full index weight. An investor who also buys Nvidia shares directly adds to that position on top of the fund's holding.

The same overlap appears in other common funds. Nvidia is classified in the information technology sector. A fund tracking that sector therefore holds it too. Nvidia trades on the Nasdaq $NDAQ and is part of the Nasdaq-100, so funds tracking that index carry it as well. Nvidia has also been a member of the Dow Jones Industrial Average since November 2024, when it replaced Intel $INTC. Funds that track the Dow hold it for that reason.

Each of these holdings can look separate on an account statement. Underneath, several of them rest on the same company's share price.

Concentration can also tie the index to a single business trend. Nvidia sells chips used in data centers for artificial intelligence. Other large constituents, such as Microsoft $MSFT, Alphabet $GOOGL and Amazon $AMZN, run cloud computing businesses that operate those data centers. A shift in spending on that technology can move several of the biggest index members at once.

Concentration risk does not mean an index fund holds fewer companies. The fund still owns roughly 500 businesses across every sector of the index. What changes is how much each one counts toward the fund's daily gain or loss.

Common ways to reduce that concentration include equal-weight index funds and spreading money across more sectors, company sizes and countries.

The holdings disclosure is the starting point. Most exchange-traded funds post their full portfolios on their sponsors' websites every trading day. Mutual funds report holdings less often. Their published weights can therefore lag the market.

A fund's holdings page lists Nvidia as a percentage of the fund's assets. Applying that percentage to the dollar value of the position gives the amount of Nvidia inside that fund. Repeating the step for every fund and adding directly owned shares produces a total. Dividing that total by the whole portfolio's value shows Nvidia's share of it.

Overlap is where totals often grow. Nvidia appears in S&P 500 funds, total U.S. stock market funds, Nasdaq $NDAQ-100 funds, large-cap growth funds, technology sector funds and semiconductor funds. It has also been part of the Dow Jones Industrial Average since November 2024, when it replaced Intel $INTC. An investor holding several of these products can own the same company many times over without buying it by name.

Many brokerages and fund research platforms offer portfolio analysis tools. These tools combine holdings across accounts and display the largest underlying positions in a single view.

On the reduction side, a fund tracking the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index gives Nvidia the same slice as every other constituent at each rebalance. That index rebalances quarterly. Between those dates, weights drift as prices move.

The equal-weight approach carries its own trade-offs. It leans toward smaller companies relative to the standard index. It also trades more often to restore equal weights.

Broader diversification is the other common method. Small-cap and mid-cap funds, international stock funds and sector funds outside technology hold little or no Nvidia. Adding them lowers the chip designer's share of a total portfolio without selling anything.

Selling existing positions is a further option, and it has consequences. In a taxable account, selling shares that have gained value can trigger capital gains tax. Shifting money inside tax-advantaged retirement accounts generally does not create an immediate tax bill.

Four forces move Nvidia $NVDA's weight in the S&P 500: its stock's performance against the rest of the market, the value of every other constituent, its share count and the portion of its shares counted as float. Each one acts on either Nvidia's own value or the index total.

Relative performance is the most direct driver. If Nvidia's stock gains more than the index as a whole, its slice grows. If it lags, its slice shrinks. That can happen even when the stock rises in absolute terms. A day when Nvidia climbs a little while other large stocks climb more can leave its weight lower than before.

The rest of the index matters too. The S&P 500 includes other megacap companies, among them Apple $AAPL, Microsoft $MSFT, Alphabet $GOOGL and Amazon $AMZN. A strong run in those stocks enlarges the index's total value. That pulls down Nvidia's share without any change in Nvidia's own market value. A broad rally across smaller constituents works the same way. It takes many more of them moving together to shift the total by a comparable amount.

Membership changes also alter the total. When the index committee replaces one company with another, the incoming firm's value takes the place of the outgoing firm's. Every other weight, including Nvidia's, adjusts slightly as a result.

Share count is another lever. Market capitalization equals the share price multiplied by the number of shares. Buybacks reduce that number, and new stock issuance increases it. Either one can move a company's value and its weight.

The float adjustment adds a further layer. S&P Dow Jones Indices, which maintains the S&P 500, counts only the shares available to public investors. Stock held by insiders or strategic holders can be excluded. A change in how many shares qualify as float can nudge a weight up or down.

A stock split is not one of these levers. It multiplies the share count and divides the price by the same factor, leaving market value and weight unchanged.

The S&P 500 also sets no ceiling on a single company's weight. Some capped indexes limit any one stock's share, but the flagship index lets weights follow market value.

The committee that oversees the S&P 500 chooses which companies belong, using criteria such as size, liquidity and profitability. It does not decide how much of the index each member gets. Once a company is in, its weight is mechanical. A change in Nvidia's weight reflects how investors value the company against roughly 500 others.

From NDAs to exit interviews: 10 ways companies protect trade secrets

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Trade secret protection against insider threats depends on layered controls, and these 10 measures, from access limits and NDAs to exit interviews, show how companies keep secrets in-house

In court, the value of a formula or a customer list is only half the test of a trade secret. The other half is whether its owner worked to keep it secret. Under U.S. law, information keeps trade secret status only if its owner takes reasonable measures to protect that secrecy. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 and the Uniform Trade Secrets Act, adopted in some form by most states, both contain that requirement. Source code or a pricing model left open to anyone can lose that protection.

Insiders are the hardest case for trade secret protection. Employees, contractors and vendors already hold legitimate access to the information a company most wants to keep. They do not need to break into anything. They need only copy a file, forward an email or carry knowledge out the door when they leave.

A 2018 federal case shows how that risk can surface. Prosecutors charged former Apple $AAPL engineer Xiaolang Zhang with stealing trade secrets related to the company's self-driving car work. The case involved an employee, not an outside intruder.

No single control closes the gap. Companies tend to layer legal, technical and human measures so that a failure in one can be caught by another. Contracts set expectations and give an employer a route to court. Software limits what a person can reach and records what they do. Training and offboarding practices shape whether people understand the rules and treat them as binding.

Those layers also serve a second purpose. If a dispute reaches court, a record of classification policies, access reviews and signed agreements helps show that the company took reasonable measures. Without that record, the legal claim itself can weaken.

This list covers 10 measures companies commonly use, from the first day of employment to the last and in some cases beyond it. It is a selection, not a complete catalog, and the order is not a ranking. Each slide explains one method, how it works in practice and where its limits lie. Security, legal and human resources teams can use it as a starting framework for an insider threat program. Other readers can use it to understand how organizations approach the risk of someone with a badge and a login walking away with what they know.

Companies start guarding trade secrets by deciding, in writing, which information qualifies. A firm cannot easily show a court that it protected a secret it never identified. It also cannot direct staff or software to protect information that no one has labeled.

The legal definition explains why this step comes first. Under the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016, information must draw independent economic value from not being generally known. A written inventory shows which information the company believes meets that test. It also gives the company a dated record to point to if it must show that it took reasonable measures.

An inventory typically begins with the categories that give a business its competitive edge. These can include product formulas, manufacturing processes, source code, algorithms, pricing models, supplier terms and customer lists. People from engineering, sales and research usually take part. They know which details would help a rival most. The inventory records where each item is stored, who is responsible for it and which teams use it.

Classification comes next. Many companies use tiered labels, such as public, internal, confidential and restricted. The tier determines how the information is stored, shared and eventually destroyed. A formula for a flagship product might sit in the highest tier. A general employee handbook might sit in the lowest.

Confidentiality markings put those labels into practice. A header or watermark on a document, a banner on a database login screen or a tag in file metadata tells a reader the material is protected. Markings also matter in litigation. A departing employee has a harder time claiming ignorance about a file stamped "confidential."

The approach has a known weakness. If a company marks nearly everything as confidential, the label stops signaling anything. Employees may learn to ignore it. In a dispute, an opposing lawyer can argue that the company never separated real secrets from routine paperwork.

Inventories also go stale. New products, acquisitions and reorganizations change what a company holds and what it values. For that reason, many firms review their lists on a regular schedule. The goal is a record that matches what the business actually guards.

Least privilege means each employee, contractor or system account gets only the access its job requires, and nothing more. Need-to-know applies the same logic to the information itself: a person sees a specific secret only when their work depends on it.

The idea has deep roots in computer security. Jerome Saltzer and Michael Schroeder listed least privilege among their design principles in a 1975 paper, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems." Need-to-know comes from government handling of classified material. In that setting, a security clearance alone does not open every file.

Companies usually put these rules into practice through role-based access control, often shortened to RBAC. Administrators attach permissions to job roles, such as sales engineer or formulation chemist, rather than granting them person by person. A new hire inherits the rights of the assigned role. Researchers at the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology helped formalize the RBAC model in the 1990s.

Segmentation adds a second layer. A manufacturer might keep its process recipes in a repository separate from general engineering files. A software company might split its source code so that most developers can reach only the modules they maintain. An employee who copies everything within reach then leaves with a fragment rather than the whole.

Permissions tend to pile up over time. A worker who moves from research to marketing may keep the old research access unless someone removes it. Security teams call this privilege creep. Periodic access reviews, run on a fixed schedule or triggered by a role change, ask managers to confirm that each permission still matches the person's current duties.

Orphaned accounts create a related gap. Logins left behind by finished projects or reassigned staff give an insider a path that no one is watching.

The cost is friction. Tight controls can slow collaboration, and engineers locked out of a needed file may look for workarounds. Many firms answer this with a documented request process. Temporary access is granted, logged and set to expire on a fixed date.

A nondisclosure agreement signed at hiring defines what an employee must keep confidential. It also gives the company a contractual basis to sue if that information leaves.

Most employee confidentiality agreements follow a similar structure. They define confidential information, often by category, such as source code, manufacturing processes, pricing data and customer lists. They limit how the employee may use that information, usually to work performed for the employer. They also list exclusions, such as information that is already public or that the employee knew before joining.

Timing matters. Companies typically present the agreement before a new hire receives system credentials or sees sensitive material. A signature collected after months of access leaves a gap in the record for that period.

Duration is a separate drafting choice. Many agreements keep obligations for trade secrets in force for as long as the information stays secret. Less sensitive business information often carries a fixed term instead.

U.S. federal law adds a specific requirement. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 grants immunity to employees who disclose trade secrets in confidence to a federal, state or local government official, or to an attorney. That protection applies when the disclosure is made solely to report or investigate a suspected violation of law. It also covers disclosures filed under seal in a lawsuit.

Employers must give notice of that immunity in any agreement governing the use of trade secrets or confidential information. The rule applies to contracts entered into or updated after the law took effect in May 2016. The notice can appear in the agreement itself or through a cross-reference to a company policy document.

Leaving the notice out has a defined cost. The employer cannot recover exemplary damages or attorney fees from that employee in a later action under the federal statute. The company can still sue, but it gives up part of the available remedy.

An agreement has limits. A signature does not block a file transfer or a printout. Its value comes after a breach, when it supports a claim for an injunction or damages.

Noncompete and nonsolicitation clauses limit what a former employee can do after leaving, not just what they can disclose. Companies use them as a second layer behind confidentiality agreements. Whether a court will enforce them depends heavily on where the employee works.

A noncompete agreement bars a worker from joining a competitor or starting a rival business for a set period after departure. Its value for trade secret protection is indirect. If an engineer cannot work for a direct competitor for a year, a formula or product roadmap is less likely to travel with them. A nondisclosure agreement works differently. It lets the person work anywhere but forbids sharing protected information.

Enforceability is the central constraint. California voids most employee noncompete agreements under Business and Professions Code section 16600. Employers there cannot rely on them to keep departing staff away from rivals. A handful of other states also restrict them sharply.

Where noncompetes are allowed, courts commonly ask whether the restriction is reasonable. They look at its length, its geographic reach and the type of work it covers. A clause written too broadly risks being narrowed or thrown out entirely.

Rules also differ across borders. A multinational employer may face one standard in Texas, another in Germany and another in the U.K. Legal teams often draft jurisdiction-specific versions rather than relying on a single global template.

Nonsolicitation clauses are narrower. They typically bar a former employee from recruiting ex-colleagues or pursuing the company's clients for a defined period. They do not block someone from taking a new job. For that reason, courts in many jurisdictions treat them more favorably than noncompetes. California courts have limited many of these clauses as well.

Garden leave is another option, common in the U.K. financial sector. The departing employee stays on the payroll during a notice period but stops working and loses access to company systems. Their knowledge of current projects ages while they remain bound by their employment contract.

Federal trade secret law draws its own line. The Defend Trade Secrets Act bars courts from issuing an injunction that prevents a person from entering an employment relationship. Any conditions placed on new employment must rest on evidence of threatened misappropriation. What the person merely knows is not enough. The law also says such injunctions cannot conflict with state laws that prohibit restraints on a lawful profession, trade or business.

Data loss prevention software, usually called DLP, tracks where sensitive files go and can stop a transfer before the data leaves the company. It checks email, web uploads, USB drives and print jobs against rules that define what information must stay inside.

DLP tools generally operate in three places. Endpoint agents run on laptops and desktops. There they can see files copied to removable drives, pasted into other apps or sent to a printer. Network tools inspect traffic leaving the corporate network, including outgoing email and web uploads. Cloud tools connect to file-sharing and collaboration services, where much company data is stored.

The software decides what counts as sensitive by inspecting content. Simple rules match fixed patterns, such as the format of a credit card or Social Security number. Trade secrets rarely follow a fixed pattern. Companies therefore rely on other detection methods. Document fingerprinting lets a tool recognize a protected file, or a large excerpt from it, even after someone renames it. Exact data matching compares outgoing content against a registered set of records, such as a customer database.

Classification labels make the job easier. When a file carries a tag such as "confidential" or "restricted," a DLP policy can act on that tag instead of guessing from the text. Microsoft $MSFT Purview, for example, lets companies tie data loss prevention policies to sensitivity labels applied to documents and email.

Policies can respond in several ways. A tool may block a transfer outright. It may warn the user and ask for a business justification. It may encrypt the file or log the event for later review. Companies often start in monitoring mode to learn how data normally moves before they turn on blocking.

DLP has clear limits. Rules that are too broad generate false positives. Those alerts interrupt legitimate work and bury security teams in noise. Rules that are too narrow miss compressed, encrypted or heavily edited files. Inspecting encrypted web traffic requires the company to decrypt it first, which raises its own technical and privacy questions.

Some channels sit outside the software's view entirely. An employee can photograph a screen with a personal phone, copy details by hand or remember a process. DLP cannot stop what a person knows. Companies treat it as one layer that narrows the easiest exit routes, not as a guarantee that nothing leaves.

User and entity behavior analytics, or UEBA, spots insider risk by learning what normal activity looks like for each person and system. It then alerts security teams when behavior departs from that baseline, catching patterns that fixed rules can miss.

The approach starts with data. UEBA platforms take in logs from identity systems, file servers, cloud applications, VPNs and endpoint devices. Over time, they model typical behavior for each individual and for peers in the same role. An engineer who regularly opens design files has a different baseline from a sales manager who works mostly in a customer database.

Alerts come from deviations. One common trigger is a sudden spike in downloads. Others include access to repositories outside a person's usual projects and logins at odd hours or from new locations. Bulk activity shortly before a resignation is another pattern security teams watch for.

Many systems combine several weak signals into a single risk score. A late-night login on its own means little. A late-night login followed by a large export to a personal cloud account carries more weight.

Logging and audit trails sit underneath all of this. A record of who opened which file, when and from where serves two purposes. It feeds the analytics as events happen. It also becomes evidence if the company later needs to show what an insider took. Retention policies matter here. Logs deleted after a short window cannot support an investigation that begins months later.

The tools have limits. Baselines take time to form, so new hires and people changing roles can generate noise. An anomaly is not proof of wrongdoing. A flagged spike may reflect a legitimate deadline or a new assignment. Human analysts still need to review alerts before anyone acts on them.

Law shapes how far monitoring can go, and the rules differ by region. In the EU, the General Data Protection Regulation requires a lawful basis and transparency when employers process employee data. In Germany, works councils have co-determination rights over technical systems capable of monitoring staff. In the U.S., federal law sets fewer limits on workplace monitoring, but some states add their own. New York and Connecticut require employers to notify workers about electronic monitoring.

Companies using UEBA commonly set out their monitoring rules in a written policy that employees receive before the monitoring applies to them. Access to the behavioral data is usually limited to a small group of analysts. Those steps address the transparency duties in Europe and the notice laws in U.S. states such as New York and Connecticut.

Employee training turns a company's secrecy rules into daily habits. Staff who know what counts as confidential, how it is labeled and how to respond to suspicious requests are better placed to avoid leaking information by accident. They also cannot easily claim they never knew the rules.

Programs typically start at onboarding. New hires learn which categories of information the company treats as confidential, such as product formulas, manufacturing processes, unreleased designs, source code, pricing models and customer lists. They also learn where that material may be stored and who may see it.

Recurring sessions keep the message from fading after the first week. Companies often pair annual refreshers with shorter reminders tied to specific events. Examples include a product launch, a merger or a move to new collaboration software.

Much of the content is practical. Employees learn what a marking like "Confidential" or "Restricted" requires of them. That can mean not forwarding a document to a personal email account and not saving files to unapproved cloud services. It can also mean not discussing a project in public places such as airport lounges or trade show floors.

Training also covers approaches from outside the company. Social engineering attempts, including phishing emails and phone calls from people posing as colleagues or IT staff, aim to trick employees into handing over access or documents. Engineers and researchers may also hear from recruiters, consultants or expert network firms seeking details about current work. Training explains how to decline those requests and where to report them.

Another topic is what new employees bring with them. Companies commonly instruct hires not to bring files or confidential information from former employers. That instruction reduces the risk that the new employer gets drawn into another company's trade secret dispute.

Training matters in court as well as in the office. U.S. trade secret law protects information only when its owner takes reasonable measures to keep it secret. Records showing who attended which session, and when, can help a company show a court that it took those measures.

Training has limits. It cannot stop someone who has already decided to take information. It is aimed mainly at preventing careless mistakes and at making clear, in writing, what the rules required.

Companies extend trade secret protection beyond their own payroll in two ways. They bind contractors, vendors and partners to written confidentiality terms, and they share only the information each outside party needs. Third parties often hold access similar to what employees have. The controls have to follow the data outside the company.

Outside parties sit in a gap that internal policies can miss. A software contractor may work inside a company's code repository. A contract manufacturer may hold full production specifications. A marketing agency may receive customer lists or pricing plans. None of these people signed the employee handbook, and many work for firms that also serve competitors.

The legal stakes are direct. Handing confidential material to an outside firm with no confidentiality obligation can undercut a company's claim that it kept the information secret. A nondisclosure agreement, or NDA, signed before any disclosure gives the company evidence that it treated the information as confidential.

Vendor contracts often go further than a basic NDA. They can define what counts as confidential information and limit its use to a specific project. They can require the vendor to bind its own staff and subcontractors to the same terms. Many also require the return or destruction of materials when the work ends, with written certification.

Audit rights add a check on those promises. A contract can allow the company to inspect how the vendor stores and handles its data, or to request security documentation. Without such a clause, a company may have little visibility into what happens after data leaves its systems.

Technical controls mirror the contract terms. Contractor accounts can be set to expire on the contract's end date. That removes the need for someone to remember to close them. Access can also be confined to a single project folder, a separate environment or a virtual desktop that keeps files off the contractor's own machines.

Joint ventures and research partnerships raise a different problem. Each side wants to collaborate without handing over everything it knows. Partners often disclose information in stages, starting with nonconfidential summaries before sharing detailed specifications. Some set up clean teams — small groups walled off from the rest of the business — to review sensitive material. Clean teams are common in merger due diligence between competitors, where antitrust rules also limit what can be shared.

Records carry weight throughout the relationship. A log of what was disclosed, to whom and when lets a company show which secrets a partner actually received. That record can be central to a misappropriation claim if the relationship ends in a dispute.

The weeks around an employee's departure are a high-risk window for trade secret theft. Companies reduce that risk with a structured offboarding process. It covers recent account activity, an exit interview, returned devices, signed acknowledgments and same-day credential cutoff.

The review often starts before the person walks out. Once someone gives notice, security and legal teams may examine that employee's recent account activity. They look for large downloads, files sent to personal email addresses or cloud accounts, and access to projects outside the person's current role. A finding at this stage gives the company time to act before the employee starts a new job.

The exit interview serves a legal purpose as well as a human resources one. A manager or HR representative reminds the departing worker which categories of information remain confidential after the job ends. The employee is asked to confirm that company documents, code and customer lists have been returned or deleted from personal devices. Many companies ask for that confirmation in writing. A signed certification restates obligations the employee already accepted. It also creates a record if a dispute follows.

Physical and digital property comes back at the same time. Laptops, phones, security badges, hardware tokens and external drives are collected and logged. Returned devices are often preserved rather than wiped right away. That way, their contents can still be examined if questions arise later.

Access revocation belongs on the last day of employment. In tense departures, it can happen at the moment the person is notified. The cutoff covers network logins, email, VPN connections, cloud applications, shared drives and building entry. Accounts in third-party tools, such as code repositories or customer relationship management systems, are easy to miss. A single forgotten account can leave a door open after the person is gone.

The case of Anthony Levandowski shows how much scrutiny one departure can draw. Levandowski was a Google $GOOGL engineer who left to found a self-driving truck startup that Uber $UBER acquired. Waymo, Alphabet's self-driving car company, then sued Uber over trade secrets tied to his departure. The companies settled in February 2018. Levandowski later pleaded guilty to one count of trade secret theft. He received a presidential pardon in January 2021.

An insider threat program gives a company one team and one plan for spotting, investigating and answering suspected trade secret theft. Pairing it with a prepared legal response lets the company act quickly once evidence points to a specific person.

These programs commonly bring together three groups. Security staff understand the systems and the logs. Lawyers know what evidence a court will accept and what employment law allows. Human resources sees performance disputes, resignations and complaints that may give context to a technical alert. When these groups work separately, warning signs can sit in three different places without anyone connecting them.

The CERT National Insider Threat Center at Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute publishes guidance that companies can use as a reference when building such a program. Its material treats insider risk as an organizational problem, not only a technical one. It addresses governance, policy and coordination between departments.

A written charter usually defines who sits on the team and who can authorize an investigation. It also sets out how employee privacy is protected along the way. An investigation launched without clear authority can expose the company to claims of its own. It can also weaken the case it was meant to build.

The response plan covers what happens after a credible alert. The first step is usually evidence preservation. That means securing laptops, phones, email accounts and access logs before anything is wiped or overwritten. Forensic examiners can then reconstruct which files were opened, copied or sent, and when. A documented chain of custody helps that evidence hold up later.

The legal options depend on what the review finds. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 lets a trade secret owner sue in federal civil court. In extraordinary circumstances, a court can order an ex parte seizure of property to keep a secret from spreading. Ex parte means the order is issued without advance notice to the other side. The law also allows courts to grant injunctions and award damages.

Some cases move beyond civil court. The Economic Espionage Act of 1996 makes trade secret theft a federal crime. Companies can refer matters to federal prosecutors. A referral puts the criminal case in the government's hands. Legal teams weigh that step against the company's own goals, such as speed or confidentiality.

Teams test the plan through tabletop exercises, which walk members through a simulated incident step by step. They also keep contact lists current and revise the plan when systems or laws change. Those exercises show whether the team can secure devices and logs fast enough to support a request for emergency court relief.

How boards investigate a CEO: 10 parts of an internal probe

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn how boards investigate a CEO, from special committees and outside counsel to interviews, disclosure rules and the outcomes directors can choose when an internal probe ends

Every company has one executive whom the board alone hires, pays, evaluates and can remove: the chief executive. That makes an inquiry into the CEO different from any other internal investigation. The person under review often controls the budget, the legal department and much of the information directors depend on. Corporate governance practice has developed a set of safeguards to work around that conflict.

Understanding how boards investigate a CEO starts with a familiar sequence. Independent directors take charge. They retain outside lawyers who report to them instead of to management. They write down what the inquiry will examine, lock down relevant records, interview witnesses and follow the money. At each stage, they decide what the company owes regulators, investors and the public.

Two Delaware court decisions define the legal stakes. Delaware is the state of incorporation for many large U.S. companies. Its courts expect directors to make a good-faith effort to oversee major risks. The Delaware Court of Chancery set out that oversight duty in its 1996 Caremark decision. In 2019, the Delaware Supreme Court applied it in Marchand v. Barnhill, a case arising from food safety problems at Blue Bell Creameries. Directors who overlook warning signs about a chief executive can face shareholder claims. A poorly run inquiry carries its own costs. It can put attorney-client privilege at risk, cause a company to miss a filing deadline or invite closer scrutiny from regulators.

This slideshow breaks the process into 10 parts. The first four cover who leads the probe, which lawyers do the work, how the scope is defined and how evidence is preserved. The next four explain witness interviews, forensic accounting, whistleblower complaints and the disclosure rules that apply to public companies. The final two describe how the CEO is kept out of the board's deliberations and what directors can do once the findings arrive.

The descriptions reflect common U.S. practice and established law. Bylaws, corporate charters and executive employment contracts differ from company to company. Practice varies from one investigation to the next.

Each slide is written to stand on its own. A reader interested only in Upjohn warnings, clawbacks or Form 8-K deadlines can skip directly to that entry.

When allegations reach the chief executive, a board usually assigns the inquiry to a special committee made up only of independent directors. These directors have no business, family or financial ties to the CEO. That lets them weigh the facts without a conflict of interest.

The committee can be created for the occasion or drawn from a body that already exists. The audit committee is a frequent choice when the allegations concern financial reporting. It already oversees the outside auditor and the company's internal controls. Claims about workplace conduct or personal behavior may instead go to a purpose-built committee. They can also go to the full board, meeting without the CEO.

Independence is judged by actual relationships, not only by a director's formal status. A director whom the CEO recruited may be left off the committee. The same goes for a director who serves alongside the CEO on another company's board, or one with a long personal friendship with the executive. Boards commonly ask counsel to review each prospective member's ties before the work begins.

Delaware case law gives directors reason to take care. Under the oversight duty recognized in In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation, directors must make a good-faith effort to ensure the company has information and reporting systems. The Delaware Supreme Court applied that duty in Marchand v. Barnhill, a case involving Blue Bell Creameries. The court stressed that boards must make this effort for the risks most central to the business. Shareholders can bring derivative claims against directors who fail to meet that duty.

A committee needs formal authority as well as independent members. A board resolution, often called the committee's charter, gives it the power to hire its own advisers. The same document lets it demand company records and speak with any employee. It also sets the reporting line, which in most cases runs to the full board minus the CEO.

The choice of committee leader carries weight. A lead independent director or a non-executive chair often acts as the main contact for the outside lawyers. That arrangement keeps the CEO and other senior managers out of the chain of communication. It also helps shield the inquiry from accusations of interference by the executive under review.

A special committee investigating a CEO hires its own outside law firm. That firm takes instructions from the committee rather than from the chief executive or anyone in management. The separate reporting line gives regulators, auditors and shareholders a reason to trust what the investigation finds.

Boards generally avoid the company's regular outside law firm for this work. A firm that earns large fees from routine corporate matters can look like it has a stake in the result. So can a firm whose partners deal with the CEO regularly. Committees tend to pick a firm with no history of advising the executive under review. They also run a conflicts check on the firm's other clients before signing.

The engagement letter names the committee as the party the lawyers answer to. That wording helps support attorney-client privilege. The privilege can shield confidential communications between a lawyer and a client from disclosure in later lawsuits. It belongs to the company itself. In this setting, the committee typically decides on the company's behalf whether to keep it or give it up.

The protection can be lost. Handing findings to a government agency or another outside party may lead a court to rule that privilege has been waived for that material. Some committees ask for oral briefings instead of a long written report for that reason. Others want a document they can share with regulators and accept the waiver risk that comes with it.

Investigation teams at these firms often include former federal prosecutors. Those lawyers know how the Justice Department and the Securities and Exchange Commission judge whether a company's internal inquiry was independent and thorough. The firm can also bring in specialists, such as e-discovery vendors, and supervise their work.

The company pays the legal bills. That puts the CEO's own organization in the position of funding an inquiry into its leader. Committees handle the problem by approving the fee terms themselves and having invoices sent straight to them. Management has no role in setting the budget or signing off on the bills.

Counsel also guides the committee's process from the first meeting. The lawyers advise on the order of steps, how to document decisions and when to brief the outside auditor or regulators. That record can matter later if a court or agency examines how the board handled the matter.

Before any witness is interviewed, the special committee puts the scope of a CEO investigation in writing. That mandate names the allegations under review, the time period covered, the people and records involved and the authority the investigators hold.

The document is usually a committee resolution, an engagement letter with outside counsel or both. Its terms need to be precise. An allegation that the CEO misused a company aircraft calls for different records and witnesses than an allegation of harassment. Pressure on the finance team to hit a quarterly target would call for yet another set.

A defined scope also keeps the inquiry from drifting. A review of expense reports can surface questions about related-party deals or a vendor contract. Each new thread adds cost and time. With a written mandate in hand, the committee can choose to widen the inquiry, keep it narrow or open a separate review with its own terms.

The mandate states who receives the findings. In most cases that is the special committee, which then decides what to pass to the full board. The document can bar the CEO from receiving drafts. Counsel still reviews the executive's employment agreement and the company's bylaws. Either one may grant the CEO a right to information or a chance to respond.

Access is another core term. A typical mandate gives the investigators authority to reach any employee, any company system and any document the business holds. It also directs management to cooperate. Corporate codes of conduct and employment contracts often require staff to assist internal inquiries, and the mandate can cite those obligations.

The document also covers unexpected discoveries. Investigators may find signs of a separate problem, such as possible bribery or an accounting error. The mandate can require them to alert the committee at once. The committee then decides whether to bring in other advisers or notify the outside auditor.

A mandate is not fixed. When the facts change, the committee amends the scope in writing. Each expansion or narrowing then appears in the record as a deliberate decision rather than an accident.

That record has a later use. The company may face shareholder litigation or questions from regulators. A written mandate and dated committee minutes show what the committee asked for, when it asked and why.

Locking down evidence is the first concrete step in a CEO investigation. Early in the process, the company sends a legal hold notice telling employees to keep relevant records intact. Its IT team also switches off the routine deletion of email, files and chat history.

The chief executive's own data comes first. Investigators move early to secure the CEO's company email, calendar, phone, laptop and cloud accounts. A common method is a forensic image, which is a complete copy of a device's contents made in a way that can be verified later. A copy taken at the outset makes it harder for anyone to argue afterward that material was altered or removed.

Personal phones and messaging apps are harder to reach. Executives may conduct business over text messages, WhatsApp or Signal. Some of these apps can be set to erase messages after a period of time. The U.S. Department of Justice addressed this problem in March 2023, when it updated its Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs. The revision covers how companies govern personal devices, communication platforms and messaging applications, including those that make messages ephemeral.

Losing data carries legal consequences. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) deals with electronically stored information that should have been kept but was lost. If a party failed to take reasonable steps to preserve it, a court can order measures to cure the resulting harm. Where the loss was intended to deprive another party of the information, the court can impose harsher sanctions. Those include instructing a jury that the lost information was unfavorable to the party that lost it, or dismissing the case.

The hold notice goes to a defined group known as custodians — the people likely to hold relevant records. In a CEO probe, that list usually starts with the executive, direct reports and assistants. It then extends to finance staff and others involved in the matters under review. Counsel adds names as interviews and documents point to other people who handled the material.

Records generated by company systems matter as much as messages. Access logs, badge swipes and expense approval trails show who acted and when. Investigators use them to test what witnesses say against what the systems recorded. Some systems overwrite their logs on a rotating schedule. The hold covers them for that reason.

Collection follows preservation. E-discovery vendors process the gathered data into a searchable form. Lawyers then search it using keywords, names and date ranges drawn from the scope of the investigation.

Outside counsel typically opens each witness interview in a CEO investigation with an Upjohn warning. The lawyer tells the employee that counsel represents the company, not the individual, and that the company controls the privilege over the conversation.

The warning is named for Upjohn Co. v. United States, a 1981 U.S. Supreme Court decision. The Court held that attorney-client privilege can protect conversations between a company's lawyers and its employees. The protection applies when those conversations help the lawyers give legal advice to the company. The Court rejected a narrower "control group" test, which had limited the privilege to senior managers. That ruling lets investigators question staff well below the executive suite and still treat the exchanges as privileged.

The warning is sometimes nicknamed a "corporate Miranda," although it is not a constitutional right. Its job is to prevent a misunderstanding. An employee who assumes the company's lawyer is also a personal lawyer may speak more freely than intended. A later claim that a personal attorney-client relationship existed can cloud the company's control over the interview.

A standard warning covers a handful of points. The lawyer works for the company. The conversation is confidential. The company can waive the privilege and share the employee's statements with regulators or prosecutors without the employee's consent. Counsel usually confirms that the employee understands before asking about the facts.

An employee who wants personal advice can hire a separate lawyer. Some companies pay for that representation under their bylaws or an indemnification agreement.

The order of interviews follows a pattern. Counsel generally reviews documents first and then speaks with people on the edges of the events. Witnesses closer to the conduct come next. The CEO is often interviewed near the end. By then, the lawyers can test the executive's account against emails, records and other testimony.

Interviews are usually not recorded or transcribed. Lawyers take notes and write memoranda that reflect their own impressions and analysis. That approach can strengthen work-product protection for the materials. The memoranda summarize rather than quote, and witnesses do not usually sign them.

Counsel often reminds employees that company policy forbids retaliation for taking part. Witnesses are also asked not to discuss the interview with colleagues. That request helps keep later accounts independent. It cannot stop an employee from reporting possible violations to the Securities and Exchange Commission, which bars companies from impeding such communications.

The CEO occupies a different position as the subject of the probe. The executive can bring a personal lawyer and may negotiate the timing and format of the interview. The special committee decides whether to insist on a face-to-face session or accept a written response.

Forensic accountants trace where company money went in a CEO investigation. They examine expense reports, wire transfers, vendor payments and sign-off records. The goal is to test whether spending followed company policy and whether the required approvals were actually given.

Their work starts with specific questions. Were the chief executive's travel and entertainment claims backed by receipts? Did company money reach vendors connected to the executive or to family members? Were contracts broken into smaller pieces to slip under approval limits? Did anyone override a control, and who was aware of it?

To answer those questions, the accountants line up ledger entries against bank statements, invoices and email. A payment recorded in the books with no matching invoice becomes a lead for the lawyers. So does a spending approval that cannot be traced to the person who was supposed to give it.

Data analytics widens the search. Software can sort through large volumes of transactions to flag duplicate payments, round-number entries, payments made at unusual times and money sent to new vendors. None of those patterns proves misconduct by itself. Each one points investigators toward documents to pull and witnesses to question.

Related-party transactions draw close scrutiny in an inquiry focused on a chief executive. These are deals between the company and an insider, or someone close to an insider. U.S. public companies must disclose some of these arrangements in their securities filings. A deal that was left out or described inaccurately can become a reporting problem separate from the original allegation.

The accounting firm is usually hired by outside counsel rather than by the company directly. That structure draws on United States v. Kovel, a 1961 decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. The court recognized that attorney-client privilege can extend to an accountant who helps a lawyer understand financial information in order to give legal advice. Whether the protection holds in a given case depends on how the engagement is set up and used.

The company's outside auditor plays a different part. The auditor stays independent of the investigation. It may still ask what the board's special committee has found. Alleged executive misconduct can affect the auditor's view of management's integrity and of the financial statements. The committee and its lawyers decide when and how the auditor is briefed.

The accountants' conclusions flow into the committee's final report. They put a dollar figure on the amounts at issue. They also help directors distinguish a policy breach from an accounting error or an intentional scheme.

Many CEO investigations begin with a tip, such as a hotline report, an employee complaint or a letter sent directly to a director. U.S. law requires listed companies to keep a channel open for these reports, and it protects the people who use it.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 gave that job to the audit committee. Audit committees of listed companies must set procedures for receiving, keeping and handling complaints about accounting, internal controls and auditing. Employees must be able to raise those concerns confidentially and anonymously. Many companies hire an outside vendor to run the hotline.

Section 806 of the same law addresses retaliation. It protects public-company employees who report conduct they reasonably believe is fraud or a securities law violation. A worker who is fired or demoted after reporting can file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Safety and Health Administration within 180 days. Boards that receive a complaint typically document how the complainant is treated from the first day.

The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 opened a second path. Its whistleblower program lets the Securities and Exchange Commission pay tipsters a share of what it recovers. Awards run from 10 percent to 30 percent of money collected in actions with sanctions above $1 million. An employee can go to the agency at the same time as the board, or without telling the board at all.

The agency's rules reward internal reporting without requiring it. A whistleblower who reports inside the company and then contacts the agency within 120 days can be treated as having reported on the date of the internal complaint. The agency's Rule 21F-17 bars anyone from taking steps to impede a person from communicating directly with agency staff about a possible securities law violation. That ban covers enforcing or threatening to enforce a confidentiality agreement.

The practical result is that a board does not fully control the timeline. A committee that receives a credible tip moves quickly and records when the information arrived. It avoids any step that could look like an effort to unmask or silence the source.

Routing is the first decision. A complaint that names the CEO or the general counsel should bypass both and go straight to independent directors, usually starting with the audit committee chair. That director judges whether the allegation, if true, is serious enough to warrant an independent probe. Investigators typically speak with the complainant early. They protect the person's identity as far as the law and the facts permit.

A public company does not have to announce an internal investigation of its CEO when the inquiry begins. Certain outcomes do trigger fast reporting. If the chief executive leaves, the company generally must file a Form 8-K with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) within four business days.

Form 8-K is the SEC's current report for major corporate events. The governing provision for a CEO exit is Item 5.02. It covers the departure of directors and certain senior officers, including the principal executive officer. The filing must state that the executive resigned, retired or was terminated, along with the date. A reason is not required. If the company chooses to give one, that description has to be accurate and not misleading.

The same item covers the other side of a leadership change. When the board appoints a new principal executive officer, Item 5.02 calls for a filing on that appointment too.

Financial findings carry a deadline of their own. The board or audit committee may conclude that previously issued financial statements should no longer be relied on. Item 4.02 of Form 8-K then requires a filing within four business days. A probe into executive spending or revenue practices can lead to that conclusion.

Staying quiet about an inquiry still carries legal risk. Rule 10b-5, adopted under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, bars materially false statements. It also bars omitting facts needed to keep other statements from being misleading. A company that praises its leadership while aware of serious allegations can expose itself to liability. Counsel reviews earnings releases, filings and executive remarks with that risk in view.

Executive certifications add pressure. Sections 302 and 906 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 require the CEO and chief financial officer to certify the company's periodic reports. When an investigation casts doubt on those reports, the committee, the outside auditor and disclosure counsel assess whether the earlier statements still hold.

Reporting to authorities is a separate decision from public disclosure. A company may choose to tell the SEC or the U.S. Department of Justice about possible violations. The Justice Department has published corporate enforcement policies describing credit for voluntary self-disclosure, cooperation and remediation. The board weighs that option with its lawyers.

Stock exchange rules also apply. The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ expect listed companies to release material news promptly. A CEO exit or a restatement can meet that standard.

A small group controls the message. The committee, outside counsel and the corporate secretary agree on what is said to employees and the public. Conflicting accounts can create legal problems of their own.

During an internal investigation, the chief executive is cut out of every decision about the probe. The CEO does not attend special committee meetings, does not see the committee's papers and has no say over the investigating lawyers. In many cases, the executive continues to run the business day to day.

The arrangement is harder to manage when one person is both CEO and board chair. In that case, the lead independent director or another independent director chosen by the board presides over any discussion of the inquiry. The chair's usual control over meeting agendas and board materials does not extend to the investigation.

Listed companies already have a forum for these conversations. New York Stock Exchange rules require non-management directors to meet in regularly scheduled executive sessions without management present. Nasdaq $NDAQ rules require regularly scheduled executive sessions of independent directors. Those meetings give directors a routine place to discuss the probe without the executive in the room.

Recusal covers votes as well as attendance. The chief executive abstains from any board decision about the investigation, including its budget and its conclusions. The general counsel may also have a conflict. That officer usually reports to the CEO. Committees often limit the general counsel's role to logistics, or keep the general counsel out of the inquiry entirely.

Directors also decide how the executive will operate while the work continues. One choice is a leave of absence. Another is to remove the CEO's authority over particular areas, such as the finance function or the employees being interviewed. A third is to make no change when the allegations are narrow. The committee weighs the risk of interference against disruption to the company.

The executive keeps rights during the process. A CEO under investigation can hire a personal lawyer and is normally given a chance to answer the allegations. Company bylaws, indemnification agreements and employment contracts can determine whether the company covers legal costs as they arise. Section 145 of the Delaware General Corporation Law permits companies to advance those expenses to officers. The officer typically must promise to repay the money if a court later finds the officer is not entitled to indemnification.

Succession planning runs on a separate track. Directors identify who could serve as interim CEO and how operations would stay stable if the executive leaves. That discussion is kept apart from the fact-finding. The separation keeps choices about a possible replacement from shaping the investigation's conclusions.

The rest of the board stays informed. The committee briefs the other independent directors at intervals. Those updates let the full board carry out its own oversight duties.

At the end of a CEO investigation, the special committee presents its findings and the independent directors decide how to respond. The options range from clearing the executive entirely to firing the CEO for cause. Reprimands, pay cuts and compensation recovery fall in between.

The CEO's employment agreement usually sets the boundaries of that decision. Many executive contracts define cause to include conduct such as fraud, willful misconduct or serious breaches of company policy. A dismissal for cause can cost the executive severance and unvested equity. Directors and counsel therefore test the facts against the contract's exact wording. Labeling a departure as for cause when the evidence does not meet the definition can lead to a dispute with the former executive.

Recovering pay is a separate track. The Securities and Exchange Commission adopted Rule 10D-1 under the Dodd-Frank Act. It requires listed companies to keep policies for clawing back incentive-based compensation that executives received in error because of an accounting restatement. The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ put the rule into effect through their listing standards. Companies had to adopt compliant policies by December 2023.

Rule 10D-1 has a narrow trigger. A restatement sets it off, not misconduct in general. Recovery applies whether or not the executive was at fault. It covers current and former executive officers. It reaches incentive pay received during the three completed fiscal years before the restatement was required. A CEO who broke conduct rules without affecting the financial statements falls outside it.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 contains an older recovery provision. Section 304 covers the CEO and the chief financial officer when misconduct leads to a restatement. It reaches bonuses, incentive pay and profits from selling company stock in the 12 months after the misstated filing. The SEC enforces it, not the board.

Company-written clawback clauses can go further. Depending on how they are drafted and what the executive signed, they may reach compensation tied to misconduct. Collecting the money can still require negotiation or a lawsuit.

The committee's conclusions go to the independent directors, who weigh them before voting on any action. Their reasoning belongs in the board minutes. Those minutes become the formal record of how the company handled the allegations.

Remediation follows the decision about the CEO. Committees often order changes to the controls, approval limits or reporting lines that allowed the problem. A documented record of those fixes can matter if regulators later examine how the board responded.

Not every probe ends with a finding against the executive. When the evidence does not support the allegations, the committee says so in its conclusions. The board then records that it has cleared the CEO. That written record shows directors examined the allegations and acted on what the evidence showed.

Some cancer cells survive chemotherapy by turning into giants

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Cancer cells that survive chemo swell into giant senescent cells that summon immune repair, and drugs that block their IAP proteins kill them.

Chemotherapy can kill a cancer cell, but some survivors enter an unusual state. After a near-lethal dose, they stop dividing and swell to many times their normal size.

Then they start pumping out the chemical signals a wound uses to call in the immune system’s repair crew. These surviving cells may help tumors return.

A team in Dublin has identified a mechanism that keeps them alive after the damage, along with experimental drugs that kill them in laboratory tests.

Seamus Martin, the Smurfit Professor of Medical Genetics at Trinity College Dublin, led the work from his lab with postdoctoral fellow Gokhan Gunay at the university’s Smurfit Institute of Genetics.

Collaborators at the University of Cologne and the Olivia Newton-John Cancer Research Institute in Melbourne took part.

Nearly all of it was done in human cancer cells grown in the lab, with some experiments in mice, and none of it involved patients.

In an interview with Earth.com, Martin was asked what surprised him most. He pointed to the cells themselves.

“These are really bizarre-looking cells,” he said.

“They are truly gigantic when compared with normal cells (approx. 10X normal size) and this is highly unusual behavior for cells, even cancer cells.”

The team gave cervical, bowel and lung cancer cells a three-hour pulse of cisplatin or etoposide, two common chemotherapy drugs that work by damaging DNA.

The dose killed about 40% of the cells within two days. The rest stopped dividing and were still alive a week later, flattened and enormous.

Biologists call this state senescence, and cells in it are often nicknamed zombie cells. They stop dividing but remain alive, secreting a stream of inflammatory proteins.

In a tumor, those signals can attract immune cells that promote new blood vessel growth and help create conditions that support tumor growth.

Martin’s lab studies the machinery that makes cells self-destruct, and the team wanted to know why these cells hadn’t.

The chemotherapy had switched on the cells’ own self-destruct receptors.

Two of them, known as Fas and DR5, rose sharply on the surface of the damaged cells, in every cell type tested and in mouse tumors treated with cisplatin.

Inside the cells, those receptors had assembled the protein complex that normally triggers death. It was fully assembled, and something was holding it back.

One of the restraints turned out to be a family of proteins called inhibitors of apoptosis, or IAPs. The senescent cells were making more of one of them than untreated cells do.

“IAPs act as the antidote to the killing effects of Death Receptors that enable the senescent cells to survive,” Martin told Earth.com.

“However, this now leaves the senescent cells in a state of addiction to the IAPs for their continued survival, which now makes them extremely vulnerable to IAP antagonists.”

Drugs that destroy IAPs already exist, and several have entered clinical trials. The team tested two of them, BV6 and birinapant, which wiped out the IAP proteins inside cells within eight hours.

Given alongside chemotherapy, the drugs killed more cancer cells outright and left far fewer survivors to enter the zombie state.

Given afterward, to cells that had already turned senescent, they killed those cells at doses that did little to normal cells.

Cells engineered to lack the self-destruct receptors, or the enzyme they activate, were largely protected from the drugs, which is how the team knew the receptors were doing the killing.

A widely studied senolytic drug, navitoclax, kills senescent cells by a different route, and it worked just as well without those receptors.

The team also went the other way. Mice bred without caspase-8, the enzyme the receptors activate, built up senescent cells in the lining of the small intestine and showed more inflammatory signaling in the liver.

In cells, deleting the same enzyme let more survivors enter senescence and kept them alive longer.

Old mice told a similar story. In lung, kidney and heart tissue from 24-month-old animals, the two death receptors were higher than in three-month-olds.

There was one living test of the drugs. Mice with an inherited retinal disease, which produces leaky new blood vessels and senescent cells in the retina, got injections of BV6 into the eye.

Their lesions shrank, and the count of senescent cells in the retina fell.

Professor Martin described the strategy as a one-two punch.

The first punch is a standard DNA-damaging drug such as cisplatin, and the second, delivered days later, is a drug that kills the senescent cells the first punch created.

In the paper, the team suggested giving an IAP-blocking drug 5 to 7 days after each round of chemotherapy.

Given ahead of chemotherapy instead, it would push more cells toward death in the first place.

The timing is the hard part, Martin noted. The second punch has to land while the tumor is vulnerable, and he said several trials of IAP-blocking drugs have been run or are under way.

The one-two punch has not been tested against a tumor in an animal in this study.

The chemotherapy-plus-drug experiments were in dishes, and the tumor experiments in mice measured the receptors rather than survival.

The one animal test of the drugs’ zombie-killing power was in a diseased eye, not a cancer.

Whether a human tumor’s survivors depend on IAPs the way these lab cells did is the open question.

Answering it means the trial the team proposes: chemotherapy paired with an IAP-blocking drug, timed to the days when the giant cells appear.

Martin said getting that timing right may decide whether the second punch connects.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

5 Near-Perfect Fantasy Books, Recommended by Brandon Sanderson

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brandon Sanderson is one of the most popular authors in the fantasy genre right now, yet he never misses a chance to pay his success forward. Sanderson has made headlines for steering the publishing industry itself and advocating for other writers along the way. His story of single-minded focus on becoming a professional author is inspiring to many readers, and he often encourages aspiring young writers to read as much as possible. However, Sanderson qualifies all of his book recommendations with the caveat that not every book will resonate with every reader. He encourages fans to read widely and seek out what interests them most.

That said, fans are often interested in what their favorite authors are inspired by, and Sanderson has not been stingy about sharing. The Stormlight Archive scribe has a post on his website's FAQ page specifically dedicated to book recommendations, with a particular eye towards students interested in writing. He even noted a portion of this list as "clean" fantasy, since Sanderson himself avoids explicit language and sexual content in his books. That's a perfect example of the personal preferences Sanderson mentioned at the top of his post. Read on for five of the best novels and series Sanderson recommends to his fans.

In his post, Sanderson recommends the works of Tad Williams in general, but Williams is best known for his series Memory, Sorrow, & Thorn. That's also his longest series — nine novels counting the prequels, the interlude, and the sequel series The Last King of Osten Ard. This series was a massive influence on George R.R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire, and likely many others. Williams has written three other fantasy series as well as stand-alone novels, short stories, screenplays, and comic books.

Sanderson warns that Williams' writing can be "very long-winded," adding that he personally likes this style, but it might put others off in the early chapters. He also listed Williams among the "clean" authors on his list, though there are some extremely dark themes in Memory, Sorrow, and Thorn. The series follows a young man named Simon thrust into an apocalyptic struggle against ancient magical forces. He and his allies are on the back foot throughout the entire story, and they take plenty of losses before the climactic battle.

Sanderson has mentioned Dragonsbane by Barbara Hambly many times in posts, interviews, and lectures, saying it's one of the main reasons he became interested in fantasy. He also used it as an example of following your own interests as a reader, pointing out that no one would have predicted a teenage boy would be interested the esoteric adventures of a middle-aged woman. Years after its publication, Hambly began writing sequels to Dragonsbane, with new installments still coming out today.

The story is about a witch named Jenny Waynest who lives a harsh, relatively isolated life in the Northlands along with the lord John Aversin. They are asked to come south to help fend of an ancient, powerful dragon that is encroaching on the more populous city. They do so in exchange for aid for their Northlands, but they find the situation in the south more complicated than they expected. Not only is the dragon Morkeleb more powerful than they expected, but the king is under the influence of another witch who did not want Jenny to make the journey. The book was lauded by fiction readers when it was published in 1985, and Sanderson's recommendation has brought it back into the conversation in recent years.

Again, Sanderson recommends "Anything by Daniel Abraham under any of his various pen names," but The Dagger and the Coin series is a great place to start. It's one of the most popular series Abraham has published under his own name, though he is best known to many fans for co-writing The Expanse series under the pen name James S.A. Corey. The name refers to both Abraham and Ty Franck, who are currently using the Corey moniker once again for a sci-fi series called The Captive's War.

The Dagger and the Coin consists of five books published from 2011 to 2016. It's an epic fantasy series with a broad scope and many point of view characters, often earning it comparisons to A Song of Ice and Fire. Abraham is a friend and frequent collaborator of Martin, so this should come as no surprise. The series is named for two very different forces that can both lead to calamity for humanity — the dagger representing violence and war, and the coin representing economic instability.

The Hundred Thousand Kingdoms is the first book in N.K. Jemisin's The Inheritance Trilogy, followed by The Broken Kingdoms and The Kingdom of the Gods. The series is set in a unique fantasy world drawing inspiration from many cultures and regions — not just medieval Europe. Jemisin employs hard magic systems in her writing much like Sanderson, though the comparison is more often made with Jemisin's next series, The Broken Earth Trilogy. In those books, magic-users called Orogenes have the ability to control seismic energy, and through that affect the climate indirectly. Both of Jemisin's trilogies are beautifully written and incredibly compelling, as are her stand-alone novels. They are definitely worth checking out for Sanderson fans — or any fantasy fans for that matter.

Finally, Sanderson recommends "Anything by Robin Hobb, but particularly the Fitz books," referring to Hobb's 16-book series The Realm of the Elderlings. This series is broken up into five parts, three of which follow the character Fitzchivalry Farseer and are written in the first person. It's a spectacular case of grounded, character-driven stories somehow building to an epic, fantastical saga. The series has a unique focus on magical ecology, leading to some truly unique takes on fantasy tropes like dragons and prophecies.

Sanderson's recommendations are all excellent, especially when taken alongside his encouragement to explore the genre for yourself. It's a great time to be a fantasy fan right now, and it's great to see authors like Sanderson encouraging their readers to branch out. All of the books mentioned here are available now in print, digital, and audiobook formats.

The Packers’ Defense Is Living on Takeaways

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nine games into 2026, the Green Bay Packers' defense has 19 takeaways: 13 interceptions and 6 fumble recoveries. Last season, they had 14 in 17 games. Sunday's 45-27 win in Foxborough made it six straight games with multiple takeaways. Green Bay took the ball away four times and turned those turnovers into 21 points.

What stands out is that it isn't coming from blitzing. Against New England, Green Bay never rushed more than four, and all three interceptions came with Drake Maye in a clean pocket. The Packers are winning after the snap, with disguise, rotation and safeties who read the quarterback.

Safeties Xavier McKinney and Evan Williams have nine takeaways between them, almost half the team's total.

McKinney leads the team with five interceptions, including two-pick games against Chicago and New England. On Sunday, he picked off Maye's first pass of the game and returned it to the New England 14. Later he read Maye's eyes from a deep-quarter alignment and drove on a backside dig.

Williams has three interceptions and a fumble recovery. His pick against the Patriots came off a Brandon Cisse deflection on a deep shot, with Williams rotating over the top while McKinney was used as a rusher.

Nine different defenders have a takeaway. Six of them have an interception. Isaiah McDuffie had the best all-around day in Week 9. He stepped up when Ty'Ron Hopper left with a chest injury and became the first Packer since 2000 with multiple tackles for loss, a sack, a forced fumble and a fumble recovery in one game. His strip-and-recover of Rhamondre Stevenson in the third quarter ended any thought of a Patriots comeback.

McDuffie also has two forced fumbles. Javon Bullard, Zaire Franklin, Devonte Wyatt and Anthony Campbell have one each.

Green Bay has done this without its biggest name. The defense has played all nine games without All-Pro edge rusher Micah Parsons. It has also lost linebackers Edgerrin Cooper and Zaire Franklin, and three defensive tackles, to injured reserve. A defense that can't count on pressure has had to take the ball away with coverage instead. So far it has worked: the Packers are 6-3 and have won five of their last six.

Takeaway rates swing a lot from year to year, and some regression should be expected. Still, a defense whose turnovers come from disguise and safety play has a sturdier base than one living on tipped balls.
