NFL Slams Mike Florio & Chris Simms' Hitler Comments About Mike Vrabel

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The NFL admonished football commentators Mike Florio and Chris Simms after the podcast hosts described New England Patriots head coach Mike Vrabel's facial hair as a "German mustache."

The NFL is addressing new inflammatory comments.

After sports commentators Mike Florio and Chris Simms mocked coach Mike Vrabel's appearance on their Pro Football Talk Live podcast, making allusions to Nazi Germany and Adolf Hitler's mustache, the league condemned the comments.

The NFL's executive vice president Jeff Miller told Front Office Sports in a Nov. 18 statement, "We found the remarks inappropriate and offensive."

While discussing the New England Patriots' Nov. 15 loss to the Detroit Lions in Munich, Germany, Simms and Florio—who did not explicitly name Hitler—made remarks about the Patriots head coach's looks.

"He got the German mustache to go along with that right now, too," Simms—a former Denver Broncos quarterback—said during their Nov. 17 episode. "So, he was hitting all themes there over in Germany."

Meanwhile, Florio, 61, joined in, adding, "If you're in Germany, you gotta be careful trimming the sides of that thing."

As he laughed, Simms, 46, noted that Vrabel's appearance in Germany could make some pedestrians do a double-take. He continued, "Who's that on the street over there? He put on a few pounds. Hold on, he's big, holy crap."

But others on the PFL team weren't impressed with the jokes, with Florio noting that their show producer Gary Carter was heard groaning during the segment.

"It's funny, Gary," he said. "It was 80 years ago. Let us have some fun, for crying out loud."

Florio—who began the radio show in 2015—and Simms addressed the interaction following the incident.

"On yesterday's (Nov. 17) PFT Live show," they said in a joint statement on NBC Sports' website, "we made comments that some found offensive and we apologize."

NBC Sports—which distributes the podcast, though Florio retains 100 percent equity—also admonished the hosts for the comments.

"On yesterday's PFT Live show, Mike and Chris made statements that were inappropriate," a spokesperson for the sports news company told The Athletic. "They have apologized."

E! News has reached out to Vrabel's reps for comment and has not yet heard back.

What Does Trail Magic Look Like Around the World? These 5 Stories Show Us

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The snacks at the hut made Matt's day, despite the mucky conditions (Photo: Matt Jordan)

On the 2024 Highlander UAE, a multi-day 40-mile hike through the Hajar Mountains, I struggled far more than I had expected. The terrain was brutal, with steep descents over sharp, rocky granite tracks along the 6,345-foot Jebel Jais summit. My heavy pack weighed me down, and I could feel every stone beneath my boots. By day three, my two big toes hurt so much that on one challenging descent, I collapsed and couldn’t take another step.

That’s when a local Emirati man came walking up the trail behind me. He saw that I was struggling and stopped to pull out a roll of tape from his bag. Then, he peeled off my very stinky hiking socks without a second thought, taped up my toes, and bandaged my knees. He even carried my pack for several miles, guiding me down the remaining steep sections.

This stranger had no reason to go out of his way to help me, and I know I could not have finished that day without him. Hikers call this kind of experience “trail magic.” The Appalachian Trail Conservancy defines the term as any instance of finding what you need most when you least expect it; experiencing something rare, extraordinary, or inspiring in nature, or encountering unexpected acts of generosity that restore one’s faith in humanity.

Trail magic is often associated with Appalachian Trail history from the early 1980s. However, after my experience in the UAE, I started wondering what it looks like elsewhere. So, here are five stories from other hikers who have experienced trail magic in one form or another around the world.

Matt Jordan, a hiking and backpacking guide with Intrepid Travel, was walking New Zealand’s 1,881-mile Te Araroa Trail southbound in 2023 when he hit the muddy Longwood Forest in Fiordland. After a miserable day of rain and bog, he pushed on toward Turnbull’s Hut, one of the last backcountry huts on that segment. He’d heard that sometimes, free supplies were stored there for hikers passing through. As he approached, he prayed it hadn’t already all been taken by the time he got there.

“Thankfully it hadn’t,” he says. When he opened the store area, he found a cache of goodies.

“A local man who hikes in supplies, hauls out trash, and maintains the hut about twice a month leaves coolers stocked with beer, soda, and candy bars,” Jordan says. Excitedly, he grabbed a drink, then promptly slipped in the mud and fell on his backside. After days of awful weather conditions, though, that didn’t stop him from laughing out loud.

“For me, the gesture was about more than a cold drink. It was a reminder of the generosity of strangers and the sense of community that makes long-distance hikes unforgettable worldwide.”

Meeta Singh Rao, a hiking guide in India, says some of her best trail magic came from shepherds who spend months in the high mountain pastures of the Indian Himalayas. In October 2022, she was leading a trek to Nawali Bugyal in Uttarakhand when the weather turned. Heavy rain began to fall, and strong winds blew. The mules carrying their gear were also lost, and camp was miles away.

The group came across a shepherd with his flock. He heard where they were headed, and offered them shelter in his channi, a mountain hut made of stone, mud, and plastic sheets. “When rainwater leaked through the roof, he walked out into the downpour to rearrange the plastic so we stayed dry,” says Rao.

When Rao offered to pay, the shepherd gently refused, saying they were his guests. “A couple with so little of their own opened their home to us, giving us a feeling of belonging in the mountains,” she adds.

Kaitlyn Bussell has walked four Caminos. But her second, the 62-mile Camino Inglés in September 2023 through Galicia, was particularly challenging. The rain was constant, few hostels and cafés were open in the fall, and she walked long stretches without seeing another pilgrim.

On the second day, wet and ready for a break, she ducked into the first bar she found for a café con leche and to charge her phone. When she went to pay, the young woman behind the counter told her the coffee was free and handed her some water, prayer cards and a small Camino keepsake.

“It was a small thing, but it came at exactly the right moment,” Bussell recalls. “After days of rain, it lifted my spirits and made me realize that this pilgrimage is as much about the people you meet as it is the miles you walk.”

On Japan’s Shikoku 88 Temples Pilgrimage route, Lauren Roerick and her brother were leaving one of the temples when she spotted a sign offering matcha tea ceremonies for pilgrims. They initially walked away unsure whether they would be intruding but returned and decided to try it anyway.

“A Japanese man welcomed us inside where two women in kimonos were preparing and serving matcha under his instruction,” adds Roerick. “To make us feel welcome, he called a friend and had her on speakerphone, translating the whole ceremony from Japanese into English.” Lauren and her brother learned about the tradition, tried making tea themselves, and ate small Japanese sweets.

“It was proof that trail magic isn’t always a bottle of water or a helping hand,” says Roerick. “Sometimes a stranger opens his door, works around a language barrier, and shares a bit of culture with people passing through.”

On a windy hike to Matterhorn Base Camp, Libera Pagano, tour director with EF Adventures, noticed an older woman struggling. The altitude made her dizzy, her legs were shaking, and her friend had walked far ahead. “I offered to sit with the woman while she rested behind a large rock and we started chatting,” says Pagano.

That’s when Pagano learned it was the woman’s 86th birthday, and the hike was a present she’d given herself. They stayed together, and when the woman felt ready, they walked slowly until they caught up with her companion. When the group met back at basecamp, the woman told Pagano that without her help, she would never have made it to the summit.

“I thought nothing of what I had done,” says Pagano. “But for that woman, it was the difference between giving up and fulfilling her birthday wish.”

I never learned the name of the man who bandaged my toes in the Hajar Mountains, and I probably never will. But I think about him whenever I struggle on a trail or see another hiker limping.

All these stories have one thing in common—human compassion. Sometimes that is stopping to help a stranger in need, sharing what little you have, or leaving a cache of supplies for a hiker you’ll probably never meet. These gestures don’t have to be grand or cost much, but they can make an enormous difference and are an easy thing to pay forward so everyone experiences a little magic outdoors.

Scientists Uncover Why a Common Nutrient Can Be Dangerous for Blood Vessels

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that dietary phosphate may influence blood vessel damage and lifespan, with important implications for people with chronic kidney disease.

A newly identified role for a protein in the kidneys could help explain why excess phosphate in the blood contributes to hardened arteries and shorter lifespans. Researchers at the University of Colorado Anschutz School of Medicine found that animals lacking this protein developed more calcium deposits in their blood vessels and died earlier, particularly when consuming a diet high in phosphate.

The study, published in October 2026, identified TMEM174 as an important regulator of blood phosphate levels. Reducing dietary phosphate lessened some of the harmful effects in animals deficient in the protein. The findings point to a potential treatment target for chronic kidney disease (CKD), a condition that makes it difficult for the body to eliminate excess phosphate.

The kidneys continuously filter phosphate from the bloodstream, removing excess amounts through urine while retaining enough to support essential functions such as bone formation. A protein called NPT2A helps maintain this balance by reabsorbing approximately three-quarters of the filtered phosphate and returning it to the blood.

The researchers discovered that TMEM174 acts as a counterbalance to NPT2A. By binding to NPT2A and reducing its activity, TMEM174 helps prevent excessive phosphate reabsorption. In animal models lacking TMEM174, phosphate regulation became disrupted, vascular calcification increased, and lifespan was significantly reduced.

“We don’t fully understand how yet, but somehow TMEM174 is destabilizing NPT2A,” says Makoto Miyazaki, PhD, a research professor in the Division of Renal Diseases and Hypertension and senior author of the study. “And without enough TMEM174, homeostasis is disrupted.”

Phosphorus is an essential nutrient found in meat, fish, dairy products, nuts, legumes, and whole grains. It is also commonly added to processed foods and beverages to improve flavor and extend shelf life. Unlike many naturally occurring phosphorus compounds, phosphate additives are generally absorbed more efficiently by the digestive system.

Although the body needs phosphorus, persistently elevated blood phosphate (hyperphosphatemia) can damage the cardiovascular system. Excess phosphate can cause smooth muscle cells in blood vessel walls to develop characteristics of bone-forming cells. These altered cells promote the accumulation of calcium phosphate crystals, causing blood vessels to stiffen (or calcify).

Healthy arteries expand and contract in response to changing blood flow. Calcification reduces that flexibility, contributing to vascular aging and increasing cardiovascular risks. In the new study, the combination of TMEM174 deficiency and a phosphate-rich diet accelerated this process in animals.

“Phosphate is really underestimated in terms of longevity in all mammals. The higher the concentration of phosphate in the blood, the shorter the lifespan,” says Miyazaki.

Elevated blood phosphate is relatively uncommon in the general population because healthy kidneys can usually remove the excess. In people with CKD, however, declining kidney function reduces phosphate excretion, allowing the mineral to accumulate in the bloodstream.

This impaired phosphate regulation can contribute to vascular calcification, which is particularly common in people with kidney disease. The newly discovered role of TMEM174 suggests another potential source of vulnerability. Although TMEM174 deficiency appears to be primarily genetic rather than caused by CKD, researchers suspect that people affected by both conditions could face additional cardiovascular risks.

The experiments established a connection between TMEM174 deficiency, abnormal phosphate regulation, vascular calcification, and reduced survival in animal models. Whether differences in TMEM174 activity have comparable effects on cardiovascular health or lifespan in humans remains to be determined.

The findings suggest that treatments affecting TMEM174 or its interaction with NPT2A might help the kidneys eliminate more phosphate instead of returning it to the bloodstream. Such an approach could eventually provide another way to manage phosphate levels in people with CKD.

Dietary changes may also help. The researchers found that reducing phosphate intake partially protected animals lacking TMEM174 from harmful effects. Miyazaki notes that limiting phosphate-rich processed foods, including fast food, deli meats, and sodas, could be beneficial. However, phosphate occurs naturally in so many foods that dietary restrictions alone may be insufficient, particularly for people with impaired kidney function.

Future research will examine how TMEM174 interacts with other proteins and hormones involved in phosphate regulation. Miyazaki also hopes to investigate why calcium deposits are so prevalent in the blood vessels of people with CKD.

“Scientists have been studying phosphate homeostasis for over 100 years, so to still find a new protein that regulates phosphate is kind of unique,” says Miyazaki.

Reference: “TMEM174 deficiency reduces longevity by promoting phosphate-driven vascular calcification” by Jose G. Miranda, Judith Blaine and Makoto Miyazaki, 2 October 2026, Scientific Reports. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-74353-2

Price tag for US war in Iran barrels past $48 billion

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A Pentagon budget officer has said the price of the Iran war surpassed $48.3 billion last week. That does not include costs sustained in Iranian attacks on US bases — lawmakers want details the Pentagon is loath to give.

The cost of the US war in Iran, which Donald Trump launched without Congressional approval on February 28, blew past $48.3 billion (€44 billion) on November 13, according to Pentagon budgetary official Arthur Miller.

Miller laid out the figures during Thursday testimony before the US Senate Armed Services Committee as his nomination to become Navy comptroller is considered.

Miller said the costs cover US military operations, equipment and munitions but not damage sustained at US military installations across the Middle East.

According to news agency AP, damage to hundreds of aircraft hangars, barracks, radar systems, warehouses and other US military infrastructure has run up into the billions of dollars, however, Miller failed to provide an estimate of the total cost when pressed during the hearing.

Committee Chairman Roger Wicker, a Republican from Mississippi, called on Miller to provide the figures as soon as possible.

In October, the Wall Street Journal cited Pentagon sources who said damage to US facilities in the region could range from $5 billion to as much as $15 billion.

The Washington-based think tank Center for Strategic and International Studies put the cost for repairing buildings alone — excluding equipment and systems inside — at more than $4 billion.

Merchants who use AI report a chargeback dispute median win rate of 38%, up from 27.75%

NEW YORK, Nov. 17, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Chargeflow, the AI chargeback platform used by more than 20,000 merchants, today published "The State of Chargeback Management: How AI Is Reshaping Performance," a survey of 121 enterprise merchants in the U.S. and U.K. conducted by Juniper Research. Merchants that use AI win a median 38% of the chargebacks they dispute, against 27.75% for those that don't. Yet the median merchant in both groups disputes and wins the same share of its eligible chargebacks: 6.65%.

AI users also contest the same median share of their eligible chargebacks as non-users. Juniper Research points to a gap between adopting AI and changing how the work gets done: 80% of respondents use AI in chargeback management, but only 11% have mostly or fully automated it.

The report's findings come as pressure on merchants grows. Chargebacks rose over the past 18 months for 65% of respondents, and nine in 10 of those saw growth of 10% or more. Almost half put the annual cost of chargebacks at 0.5% to 1% of revenue. Friendly fraud is a large part of the problem: 43% believe it accounts for more than a quarter of their chargebacks.

"Most merchants have added AI to a manual process," said Dan Moshkovich, VP of Go-to-Market at Chargeflow. "It helps them win more of the disputes they file, but people still decide which ones to file, so they fight the same small share. That's the next step for AI in chargebacks: knowing which disputes are worth fighting and building a case for each one."

Merchants want evidence of higher win rates to justify AI investment

Asked what would justify spending on AI for chargebacks, 39% of merchants chose a higher win rate and 34% chose more revenue recovered. Fewer than one in five chose time saved or lower costs. Most want a big gain before they buy: 69% said a tool would need to lift their win rate by at least 11 percentage points, taking a merchant that wins 30% of its disputes to 41% or more. AI hasn't cleared that bar yet: AI users report a median win rate 10.25 percentage points higher than non-users, just short of the 11-point mark. Juniper Research concludes that "while AI has delivered incremental improvements, it is not yet enough to meet enterprise expectations." When choosing a provider, 72% rated the quality of its data as very important and 70% said the same of security and compliance, against 31% for total cost.

Of respondents, 77% said chargebacks rise over the December holidays. Most are still optimistic: 58% expect lower chargeback losses in the 2026 season than in 2025. Chargeflow's own data shows what changes over that peak. The Chargeflow Data Lab, launched Oct. 22, studied about 3 million disputes across the last three peak seasons. Over the 2025 Black Friday to Cyber Monday weekend, 34.7% of chargebacks said the order never arrived, up from 19.9% in a normal week. Fraud claims fell to 27.2% from 37.8%, and the same shift appeared in 2023, 2024 and 2025.

As AI matures, chargeback tools will be judged on whether they turn intelligence into better decisions and measurable financial gains," said Nick Maynard, VP of Research, Juniper Research. "The opportunity is not simply to automate chargeback management. It is to make chargeback management materially more effective."

San Francisco Proclaims Fromm Day as the Fromm Institute Turns 50

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

More than 960 students enrolled at the Fromm Institute this fall. In a student survey, 63 percent of respondents said they felt less isolated.

SAN FRANCISCO, Nov. 19, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- On her lunchtime walks through the University of San Francisco in 2012, Max Ferman kept seeing the same groups of older adults talking and laughing as they walked from Fromm Hall to the University Center. They disappeared in the summer and came back in the fall. "Who were these happy old people!?" she recalled wondering.

Ferman became a Fromm student in 2022 and shared that story on behalf of her fellow students at the Fromm Institute for Lifelong Learning's 50th anniversary gala on Nov. 14, 2026. Students, faculty, university leaders and supporters gathered at the McLaren Center, where the Institute received a proclamation from Mayor Daniel Lurie Office declaring Nov. 14 Fromm Day in San Francisco.

San Francisco proclaimed Nov. 14 Fromm Day as the Fromm Institute marked 50 years of lifelong learning.

Fern Warner attended the Institute's first session in 1976 and still takes classes today. Betty Dvorson, who is about to turn 101, keeps coming to class because learning makes her happy. In a Fromm Institute student survey, 82 percent of respondents reported greater life satisfaction, 63 percent said they felt less isolated and 52 percent reported forming new social networks.

Executive Director Derek Leighnor described how those relationships take shape. A conversation that starts in class can turn into a standing lunch date or a friendship that lasts for years. When the pandemic closed classrooms in 2020, Fromm students learned to meet online so they could keep studying together and stay connected.

University of San Francisco President Salvador D. Aceves invited guests to picture an 18-year-old undergraduate heading to a first-year seminar while a 90-year-old Fromm student walked to class. Hanna and Alfred Fromm's founding vision, he said, broadened whom the university could serve and gave younger and older students a chance to learn from one another.

Rabbi Brian Lurie, chair of the Friends of the Fromm Institute Board of Directors, thanked university leaders, the board and Leighnor for sustaining the Institute.

Hanna Fromm expected about 50 people at the first open house. About 150 enrolled in the first year. This fall, more than 960 enrolled. Fifty years after that first open house, Fromm students are introducing more San Franciscans to the program at neighborhood farmers markets.

Enrollment is now open for the Fromm Institute's Winter session, which begins Jan. 11, 2027. Adults 50 and older can explore courses and enroll.

About the Fromm Institute for Lifelong Learning

Founded in 1976 at the University of San Francisco, the Fromm Institute offers daytime courses for adults 50 and older, on campus and online. Students explore art, history, literature, science and other subjects without grades, exams or homework. fromminstitute.org

New Ukraine talks including Europe to be held 'soon' — Merz

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Germany's Chancellor Friedrich Merz says European leaders could take part in fresh Ukraine talks on "neutral ground." The UK and EU countries have urged a seat at the table in meetings between the US, Russia and Ukraine.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said on Friday he believes new talks regarding Russia's invasion of Ukraine with European leaders taking part will occur "soon."

"Of course we are prepared to hold talks with Russia, we have always said this. These talks are now set to take place soon," Merz said after a meeting of Ukraine's allies in Berlin. He did not give a concrete date for the next talks, but said Europe's interests would be represented by Germany, France, the UK, Poland and Italy.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy was present at the Coalition of the Willing summit in the German capital, along with British Prime Minister Andy Burnham, French President Emmanuel Macron, US President Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner and US Special Envoy Steve Witkoff.

Merz said the talks including Europe would take place on "neutral ground," such as the United Arab Emirates.

Merz along with other European leaders have been critical of meetings between US, Ukrainian and Russian representatives regarding the conflict, arguing that Europe needs a direct seat at the negotiating table.

Russia's invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022, with the Trump administration prioritizing an end to the war.

Trump took part in a bilateral meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, China on Friday, with Trump saying the two leaders would discuss the war in closed-door talks. Putin acknowledged that Europeans want a seat at the table, and said Russia invited European leaders to Moscow.

"We understand that discussing issues linked to security in Europe is pointless without Europeans," Putin said, while rejecting any military cooperation between Europe and Ukraine.

Trump also took part in a historic trilateral meeting with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping.

The Trump-Putin-Xi meeting raised fears that the US, Russia and China are looking to carve up the world into spheres of influence. It drew comparisons with the February 1945 Yalta Conference, where US President Franklin D. Roosevelt, UK Prime Minister Winston Churchill and Soviet Union leader Joseph Stalin discussed Europe's future as Nazi Germany's defeat was imminent in World War II.

Like other installments in the long-running and beloved series, "Grand Theft Auto 6" is as much about the autos as it is about the grand theft. But for some people, the biggest draw has always been Rockstar's sweet, sweet, non-licensed but clearly real-world-inspired cars (and planes, bikes, and boats, of course). Throughout the game, you'll find and unlock car customization options of all types, from decals to lighting and beyond.

If you want one of the coolest rides in all of gaming, though, you don't have to work very hard. You just have to shell out the extra $20 to get GTA 6 Ultimate Edition. Doing so gets you early-game access to the coveted and beautiful Ganado Retro Build. This is an exclusive customization of player character Jason's personal '70 Ganado, a Ford Ranchero-like pickup truck that's become an instant classic. Of all the cars of GTA 6, the Ganado Retro Build is especially noteworthy because of what it replaces. It's a major glow up from the well-loved, drab-colored Ganado Jason starts the game with.

While Ultimate Edition owners are spoiled with perhaps the best-looking car in the game, they still have to work for it just a bit. No matter which version of the game you buy, you start out with Jason's base Vapid Ganado, which isn't in the best condition. To upgrade to the iconic Retro Build, you'll have to drive the Ganado to a location called Coastal Auto Care, found in Key Lento. Just don't forget to fill up on the way, because cars in "Grand Theft Auto 6" don't have infinite fuel. The player is introduced to Coastal Auto Care early on via a mission, and you can find it on your map by filtering for "Mod Shops."

You can upgrade your Ganado during the "Just a Scratch" mission, or you can visit Coastal Auto Care any time after completing the mission to apply the upgrade. Once you've driven into the garage's entrance, you get the car customization screen. This screen offers you a choice of different customization options, like cosmetics, bodywork, performance, and interior. Instead of choosing any of those, select the "Pro Builds" tab, which offers you the choice between full kits and wheel kits. For Ultimate Edition players, the Ganado Retro Build kit is found under "Full Kits," and it's free to apply. Unfortunately, this classic design and slick color palette is specific to Jason's Ganado, so you can't bring in other cars and apply the Retro Build kit to them.

Once you're cruising around in the sickest car in the game, you'll want to practice defensive driving. GTA 6 has an advanced physics engine for more realistic car crashes, and nobody wants to see such a beautiful car smashed to bits in a fender-bender.

If you're the type of person who likes to try out every car possible, you'll have your work cut out for you in "GTA 6." There are over 300 different rides you can find throughout the course of the game. Some, like the Declasse Akane (inspired by the Chevy Sonic) are pretty unexciting, albeit still fun to commit major crimes in. Others are breath-takers that can help under-thrilled enthusiasts live their exotic and classic driving dreams.

Take the '95 Grotti Cheetah, inspired by the Ferrari Testarossa. This is another Ultimate Edition-only vehicle, but boy is it sick. If you figure you'll probably never drive a real Testarossa without renting one in Vegas, driving the Cheetah around is a fun second-best. Another eye-grabbing option is the obnoxious green Canis Kamacho, inspired by the Jeep Crew Chief 715 concept. It's a solid in-game off-roader that's available in the base game.

For players with aspirations that extend beyond the asphalt, the Shitzu Squalo is a special boat available to owners of the Ultimate Edition of the game. It's based on the Christ-Craft Stinger 390x and can be found at the docks of Washington Beach. It has a striking pink and blue gradient color scheme, and despite its name, it's not squalid at all. You'll also get access to biplanes, jets, helicopters, and even airboats as you play the game, allowing you to explore the world in whatever style fits your fancy.

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

Poison Ivy may be the only Batman villain whose cause many readers quietly agree with.

In her origin story from Batman comics, Dr. Pamela Isley (Poison Ivy) was once a passionate scientist. However, she was injected with toxins that merged her with plant life. She can control vegetation and feel the pain of plants directly.1 Driven by empathic distress, she fights environmental abuse and political negligence. Archetypally, she embodies both the grief and the fury of a wounded Earth.

Her grief reflects emotions many of us share; the research literature captures how people commonly feel sadness, fear, anger, helplessness, and profound grief in response to ecological loss.2 This is closely tied to the experience of solastalgia, or the anguish of watching a cherished home environment deteriorate until it no longer offers the solace it once did.

Environmental grief and climate-related trauma are natural and instinctive responses to climate breakdown.3 They arise from tangible losses of ecosystems, wildlife, homes, jobs, and communities. They also arise from losses that are harder to see, such as diminished trust, a loss of faith in the future, and a deeper psycho-spiritual disconnection from Earth itself.

Poison Ivy is also a figure of political grief. She is driven by moral outrage at governance that permits deforestation, pollution, and the exploitation of the natural world. Environmental grief often intersects with political grief, especially as climate change and environmental breakdown places a far greater burden on disadvantaged communities and developing countries.4 Subsequently, growing numbers of people are questioning values that give personhood to corporations but not to the natural world.

Poison Ivy accurately shows how these losses can be felt on an intensely personal level, one that penetrates us to the core of our being.

Grief, hopelessness, and despair can push us into survival states marked by defensiveness, psychological inflexibility, and absolutist thinking.5 Over time, these experiences can lead people toward extremist positions, social alienation, and political polarization.6 Poison Ivy’s impulsive, destructive choices do not create meaningful collective change. Her over-identification with plants causes her to lose touch with her own humanity.

In troubling times, it can be tempting to imagine that we need a figure like Poison Ivy. This supervillain fantasy will not solve the ecological crisis, though, or meaningfully comfort our grieving hearts.

The world has already experienced enough destructive authoritarianism that violates all traditional cultural wisdom. Violence will only make problems worse, and coercion entrenches polarization and resistance.6 Tragically, supervillains embody the same cycles of harm they claim to oppose. Without an ethical framework, supervillains impose rigid ideologies that oppose cooperation, respect, dignity, and mutual understanding.

The environmental crisis and the ongoing tear in our political systems evoke a great deal of fear, which is exactly why we need superheroes. The Justice League from DC Comics is a diverse group of superheroes who unite against threats too large for any one of them to handle. Despite differing ideologies, they preserve a sense of common humanity.

Adopting a stance of common humanity does not require compromising deeply held values or excusing abuses of power.6 Instead, it means focusing on our intention to connect rather than our need to defend. Many Indigenous worldviews express this through four guiding values: relationship, responsibility, reciprocity, and redistribution.7

Over 40 years ago, Roger Walsh urged us to face collective existential threats from a place of psychological self-awareness rather than fear or shadow.8 External change is most effective when it is fuelled by inner transformation and by tending to our own unresolved losses. Furthermore, crises benefit from level-headed individuals who are respectful, strive to minimize harm, utilize ethical frameworks, and maintain unwavering integrity. In other words, people who can embody and psychologically integrate the qualities of a superhero are what the world needs.

When we can metabolize our widespread environmental and political grief, we create inner space and presence. That space awakens us to life, love, and interdependence, and to the ability to re-imagine the future. No one is also meant to face this grief alone. Our collective moment of reckoning has arrived, and we need all the superheroes we can muster.

This piece draws on my book chapter, “From Poison Ivy to the Justice League: A Psychological Approach to Environmental and Political Grief,” in Superhero Grief and the Multiverse of Loss: Considerations and Case Studies, edited by Jill Harrington and Darcy Harris (Routledge, 2026). This new collection uses superheroes and the multiverse to explore how non-death loss impacts our psychology.9
