JetBlue's CEO has pushed back on creditor fears and is betting on premium to return to profitability

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

JetBlue Airways executives have sought to calm investors after shares in the airline hit a record low following a report that bondholders had contracted restructuring advisors. "We have no intention, no plans to file for bankruptcy. We are not engaged with any restructuring company," CEO Joanna Geraghty told CNBC in an interview last month.

Shares have bounced back and are up 40% so far this month as of Tuesday's close.

Geraghty has said the company is focused on its turnaround plan. A major part of that strategy is the airline betting on the premium travel boom to make it profitable again. It will start the next chapter of the effort on Wednesday, when it unveils its new domestic first-class seats at its home hub at John F. Kennedy International Airport.

Tune in at 10:50 a.m. ET as JetBlue CEO Joanna Geraghty joins CNBC TV to discuss the company's target of getting back to profitability. Watch in real time on CNBC+ or the CNBC Pro stream.

JetBlue announced it would offer a domestic first class — BlueFirst — in December 2024 and plans to start flying aircraft with the new seats and full on-board product next month. Retrofits will continue during 2027, Geraghty told CNBC late last month.

BlueFirst is different from the lie-flat, longer-haul business class, Mint, that JetBlue offers on transcontinental and Europe routes. The carrier plans to offer BlueFirst on flights on popular leisure routes throughout Florida and the Caribbean.

The New York airline last posted a profit in 2019, before the pandemic. JetBlue is betting that resilient travel demand, especially for roomier seats, will help fuel its turnaround plan, which aims to get the carrier to an operating profit next year and $1 in adjusted earnings per share in 2028.

The company reported it ended the third quarter with $2.2 billion in liquidity and that its unencumbered asset base is now $7 billion, including $2.8 billion in aircraft and engines.

JetBlue had total debt of $8.4 billion as of Sept. 30, the carrier said in its earnings filing.

Geraghty told CNBC last month there is a "fundamental disconnect between the narrative in the bond market and what our financial results actually demonstrate."

When asked last month about whether JetBlue's eye on keeping costs down includes furloughs, layoffs or buyouts, Geraghty said "absolutely not."

JetBlue shares are still down 12% so far this year. Deutsche Bank upgraded the airline last week, citing airlines' conservative growth and a focus on yields versus filling planes.

Airlines large and small are racing to outfit their cabins with roomier, more expensive seating options to help cover ever-higher costs, from jet fuel to labor to airport operation fees.

For example, a ticket in the new JetBlue recliner first-class seats between Kennedy Airport and Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport in Florida is going for $847 roundtrip in the first week of March, while standard economy is $446.

On Tuesday, budget carrier Frontier Airlines also unveiled its new first class seats, which will take up the first two rows, and said it would outfit its Airbus fleet with premium seats that have extra legroom and in-seat power through row eight. The airline will take out a row of seating on its planes to make the pricier ones fit, Chief Marketing Officer Bobby Schroeter told CNBC in an interview.

World of Warcraft: Forever is a love letter to why we all fell for it in 2005. It still has a familiar charm but it adds on extra areas, extra quests, and a plethora of fantastic user interface improvements which makes it feel far more modern than before. It's everything that veteran players have longed for.

As I created a Hunter in World of Warcraft: Forever for what must be the 100th time by now, the saying 'the definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results' rolled around my head a lot. I've played this game before. A lot. Is Forever truly sufficiently different to be so obsessed again? Well, yes. It's this lovely, warm, flawed, wonderfully infuriating dose of nostalgia. It's simultaneously far improved from the original World of Warcraft and, more recently, World of Warcraft Classic (we'll get into the differences shortly), while leaving you with questions of 'why would you leave that in?'.

Will it convert those who never liked WoW in the first place? Of course not. I don't think that's the point of World of Warcraft: Forever. Instead, it brings home all those slightly lost souls from 20+ years ago (AKA millennials like me who want to return to familiar comfort once more), but with some core improvements. It does that incredibly well and given it has brought back a record number of lapsed players, so far it's succeeding.

I've been playing World of Warcraft since the closed beta days in 2004. I've reviewed many World of Warcraft expansion packs (including my WoW: Midnight review this year), played Classic substantially, and have now skipped meals and sleep in favor of playing Forever. I've played seemingly every other mainstream MMO you can think of too, and should probably learn to step away from the PC more often but the allure of WoW remains too strong. – Reviewer Jen Allen

Before I dive too deep, let's sum up the differences between World of Warcraft, World of Warcraft Classic, and World of Warcraft Forever so you actually know why the game's important. Retail is best seen as an entirely different beast to Forever. It's very fast-paced, throws nearly as many unlocks at you as a standard game of Fortnite, and is all about leveling up as fast as possible to get to the next part of the story. Getting from level 1 to the level cap of 80 can take just 10 hours if you know what you're doing. It has its place for sure and I really like it, but it's far removed from World of Warcraft's launch years, which is where Classic comes in.

Classic took the game right back to the start and has gradually introduced expansion packs in chronological order. It has a linear progression path, while its leveling process is slow-paced. Each level is richly earned and you'll spend around 200 hours or more playing to reach the level cap of 70. Crucially, not much has been done to spruce anything up. There are some odd UI choices, it looks increasingly dated, and it feels old. It's familiar and fun but also fairly rough around the edges once you start looking.

World of Warcraft: Forever takes what many loved about Classic, but with some key quality-of-life improvements. Player level is permanently capped at 60 with horizontal rather than vertical progression. Oh, and there's a new race called the Skyborne and a bunch of new race/class combinations. You'd be forgiven for thinking Forever still sounds a lot like Classic from this brief overview. It kind of is. But, it's better. This feels like a take on World of Warcraft Classic fans like me wanted, truer to the original experience but willing to remix its approach to deliver new sensations as well.

I spent dozens of hours playing the World of Warcraft: Forever beta, and I've already put dozens more into the full release. I'm not alone either. Friends are excited about it again and the servers are packed. I've yet to see them prohibitively packed though. Forever uses a new layering system whereby players pick how they want to play the game (Normal, PvP, or Roleplay) and that's it. There are no pesky issues with 'oh no, my friend chose a different server to me and now we can't play' with the game shrewdly keeping an eye on its layers and moving people around as and when that layer gets full to the point it would degrade the play experience.

It mostly works. Early on, friends and I found that we had to manually reset the layer by logging out and back into the game, but after the first day, it worked seamlessly. Such a move is an early example of how Forever is designed to be far more accessible for all.

Right from the start, you're actually guided through the game with a helpful early tutorial teaching you how to move, attack, and what to do when you die for the first time. As a veteran player, I didn't need it, but I could see the substantial benefit. Retail has a tutorial, but Classic never bothered with one. Another point in favor of this iteration.

Diving in is a beautiful experience once more, especially if you roll one of the new Skyborne race characters. Affectionately referred to as blue Smurfs, these elves are wondrous. They look great amongst a game with improved looking models but none that can rival this new race. You can play either as Alliance or Horde with the Skyborne, with Druid, Hunter, Rogue, and Warrior available for all, while Horde and Alliance have Mage and Shaman classes at their disposal respectively.

There are specific Skyborne racial abilities which are useful too. Walk on Air allows you to glide in the air for 10 seconds which I found a great timesaver when wanting to fall from high distances. There's also Skysight for Horde which boosts run speed by 10%, while Alliance can activate a ley line and gain 100% increased health and mana regeneration for a time. Throughout my time playing WoW, I tend to forget to use my racial abilities – but these are the kind of skills I didn't want to miss out on.

Skyborne also has some delightful new animations such as a unique dance, a cute cheer, and a more appealing jump than most. Druids get new forms as well. It all feels very fresh once more. After extensively leveling with Skyborne, I didn't want to go back to the legacy races. The Skyborne starter zone of Zephras Isle is luscious. It has that typical World of Warcraft feel to it but it's sprawling, colorful, and a gorgeous reflection of how far games have come.

The water effects across the game are more realistic with waves moving naturally, and the lighting reflecting how it should be compared to real life. Even the audio is slightly muffled when you're underwater, although I found I needed good headphones to absorb this fully. There are some great looking weather effects too. Seeing The Barrens on a hot summer's day then the next day encountering bad storms and fog is special.

None of this would matter much if Forever wasn't fun but it is so much fun. Right now, everyone in-game is as welcoming as they were during the original days. I easily found pickup groups but also I'd have random players resurrect me just because they could, or toss me a heal at a key moment. Another time, I was boarding a zeppelin outside of Orgrimmar and the ship was full of shamans in wolf form dancing and encouraging everyone else to join in.

After years of World of Warcraft feeling lonely and more of a solo MMO, Forever has united players again. At points, I'd be in a silent group, but at others, we'd chat and share our experiences. My Hunter teamed up with a Druid for one quest, and even though neither of us spoke the same language so we communicated through emotes. It was a beautiful moment when we succeeded.

There are new dungeons too covering all level ranges. Hall of Thanes is an early addition for levels 13-18 and it isn't just a matter of 'run and smash' with a bit more strategy easing you into the dungeon mentality, before hitting Ruins of Lordaeron at 15-20, Excavation Site: Wetlands in the 20s, and more beyond that. There's a rich variety here with experience bonuses varying a little while Blizzard balances things. I don't live in dungeons but I've enjoyed diving in to break up the exploration side of the game.

Forever also encourages community spirit with its campfire system. Feeling delightfully RPGish, you can plonk down a campfire and share some buffs with other players. It ties into cooking well, but ultimately it gives you a moment to reflect. While exploring, I've come across plenty of players just camping and chilling. It's a nice reminder that you're playing an MMO, an active world full of players making their own way.

There are still predictable moments of tedium, of course. While Forever has much faster respawns than other versions of the game (sometimes too fast to keep up with in quieter areas), quest item drops can be very hit and miss. At one point, I spent a disproportionate amount of time trying to collect satyr horns from mobs. I needed 16. They only seemed to drop about every 10 mobs despite me being able to see the horns on the satyrs. A boring reminder that sometimes, you'll still be simply grinding away in-game rather than absorbing those beautiful moments of unity.

After 22 years, World of Warcraft: Forever has finally sold me on Blizzard's undying MMO – now I never want to leave

Combat is mostly much how it's always been. There's autoattack via one key press (or button on the action bar) and then you choose other actions such as casting a particular spell, buff, or special attack.

There's now gamepad support which has opened the game up to unofficial Steam Deck support via a few tweaks. I found this worked well enough. Much like Final Fantasy 14's implementation of gamepad support on console, I wouldn't fancy my chances using a gamepad during more intense moments such as dungeons or raids. It's just not quite as precise or as speedy as a traditional mouse and keyboard setup. It is handy for casual play though, and for those times you don't want to be hunched over your keyboard. Ultimately, more choice is only ever a good thing.

Curiously, while Forever has made the game smaller by not implementing expansion packs, it also feels much bigger as exploration is mostly conducted on foot until you can afford a ground mount (no flying mounts here) but it means everything you achieve feels richly earned.

For the most part, Forever is a wondrous success. The game I'm itching to play more of even though it's all I've done for days. There are so many good tweaks that make it better than Classic. A recent ally alert pops up next to players you've recently grouped with, there's Discord integration which was previously only on Retail, and the quest limit is now up to 40 from 20 (incredibly convenient if you're like me and stockpile quest hand-ins).

The promised 1,000 or so new quests are interspersed nicely with many of the early ones linking together zones more cohesively, and introducing professions and other valuable skills well. Some simply provide you with good gear like bags or a weapon that helps you feel strong for a few levels. All the new quests I've experienced feel like they deserved to be there rather than feeling like filler to pad out the experience.

I'm curious about what will happen further down the line for the game. There's a roadmap lined up for new raids in December before we get even more new raids, dungeons, and quests in 2027, so the next 12 months should be pretty exciting. From there, who knows? WoW: Forever focuses on horizontal progression so we won't see a level cap increase but that makes sense for a game that's all about the journey rather than the destination.

As in life, it's a smart move to enjoy the ride rather than think too far ahead and I haven't had this much fun with World of Warcraft in decades. Sometimes that's all you need. Now, if you'll excuse me, I have a Skyborne Hunter seeking my attention. I want to make some new gear for my guild and get raid ready. The good times are finally back.

The Hubble Space Telescope has captured a breathtaking view of thousands of stars glittering like cosmic jewels in a dazzling display of blue, gold and orange.

But this spectacular scene is far more than just a pretty picture. Some of these stars are almost as old as the universe itself and could hold clues to how our Milky Way formed.

The exquisite image released by ESA on Nov. 5 shows Messier 30 (M30), a densely packed collection of stars located approximately 27,500 light-years from Earth in the constellation Capricornus.

M30 is a globular cluster, a roughly spherical collection of tens of thousands to millions of stars held together by gravity.

Our Milky Way is home to at least 150 of these ancient stellar gatherings, some of which are estimated to be between 11.5 billion and 13.5 billion years old. For comparison, the universe itself is approximately 13.8 billion years old!

Besides looking like a vast cosmic treasure trove glittering across the universe, M30 also has a fascinating story to tell.

Globular clusters are essentially cosmic time capsules. Their ancient stars preserve chemical clues about the conditions that existed when they formed, helping astronomers piece together the Milky Way's early history.

Astronomers have also discovered that M30, along with several other globular clusters, traverses the Milky Way in the opposite direction to the galaxy's rotation. This unusual motion raises the intriguing possibility that M30 may not have formed in our galaxy at all. It could have simply been captured by the Milky Way billions of years ago.

Hanifa Is Relaunching After an 8-Month Hiatus

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hanifa is back. A little over eight months after founder Anifa Mvuemba paused operations for her label, which had been in business for 15 years, it’s relaunching with a new collection and updated business model.

The collection features the colorful party clothes the brand is known for, like a red evening gown with a bright blue feathered brooch and a blue liquid organza dress (a 2022 revival bound to become the season’s made-to-order showstopper). There are also more practical pieces, like Hanifa’s take on the track pant (barrel-legged with a silk stripe) and bomber jacket. The Harper dress, a green and black-dotted hit from last year, is now available in a deep blue.

Mvuemba hopes the new Hanifa feels true to the brand’s original ethos, just a little more mature: the designer was 21 when she started the brand in 2011, and now that she’s about to turn 36, her style and taste has evolved, she says, alongside her customers’. Mvuemba spent months reworking the brand’s fabrications, cuts and patterns, especially for knitwear, its biggest category. She went through dozens of iterations to find the right cashmere blend, for example, that will hold its shape and have cashmere’s soft handfeel, while keeping the brand in the price range its customers have come to expect.

The brand worked to improve its fabric quality without compromising on price.

The relaunch will be a test in regaining consumer trust. Things began to unwind last December, when purchases made during her brand’s annual Hanifa Friday sale in November failed to ship, thanks to production delays and supply chain issues. For some customers, months went by without any communication — or delivery — from the brand. Disgruntled customers took to social media to complain, not just about delayed orders (all of which Hanifa said it was eventually able to fulfill, or issued refunds for), but about the brand’s general direction, which they believed went downhill. In a statement posted online in March, Mvuemba apologized, and announced the brand would go on hiatus.

“Mentally, I wasn’t prepared to continue,” Mvuemba says. “I wasn’t in the right headspace. And in order for me to be the best version of myself, the best leader, mother, wife, I could be to my family, my team, my business — I needed that mental clarity to just take a step back, rest, recalibrate, to get a clear view on what we needed to do for the business as a whole. I needed a break,” Mvuemba says. “I’m just not the type of founder or person that performs for the optics. It was extremely hard to do, but it was necessary, because in this space, it’s not something that we see often. But it’s something that we as founders need to be able to do.”

Hanifa’s new collection comes after the brand paused orders in March.

In the end, Mvuemba only took about two weeks off from work, she says. She found a therapist and spent time with her baby, who was born in December and whose maternity care was interrupted by crisis management in the fallout. Then, she turned her attention to Hanifa 2.0.

“I love this business so much. I love this brand so much. I had to get back,” she says. “We uprooted everything. We looked at our communications, our branding, our sizing, our website. Now, everything — our processes, our manufacturers — has shifted, and it’s nice to have had this time to sit through everything, go back, revise, go back, revise, and then say, ‘OK, we’re ready.’”

Now the brand is ready to relaunch, Hanifa is returning to market on different terms.

Mvuemba is eyeing outerwear as an expansion opportunity for her collection.

The biggest change to Hanifa’s operations is switching to a ready-to-ship model, versus a past pre-order model. With no pre-order period, where the brand could produce only a committed number of SKUs, the company has fronted the entire new collection (which Mvuemba says is “expensive but necessary”) and plans to use real-time performance data to determine production cadence and inventory investments. Having inventory overhead also shortens lead times for purchases to be fulfilled, and gives the brand space to ship items by sea rather than air, which cuts costs.

“We thought it was really important to come out the door swinging,” Mvuemba says. “People want their things when they want their things.” It’s also invested in new customer service and logistics partners, with brand plans to build data analysis into its production cycle to ensure that it’s responding quicker to what’s selling. Mvuemba hired a brand strategist, an art director and a PR firm to help steer the comeback story. The brand has always been bootstrapped, but she says she’s open to outside funding if the right partner comes along. She’s also open to the right retail partners, but that right now, the direct-to-consumer model is the best fit thanks to production and shipping timing.

Her focus above all, she says, is her customer. Mvuemba comes back time and again to the importance of data, and how much she wants to spend the rollout of this collection listening to feedback. She hopes that the brand’s core customer — the company has a legion of longtime loyalists who were sad to see it go — returns, and that the brand can grow its reach. By category, she’s excited about expanding the brand’s shoes and outerwear, both of which are steps in building a more complete ready-to-wear label, which is her main goal for next year. Scaling, Mvuemba says, will come at the right pace.

“I used to say I want it to be as big as LVMH. And with this year, I just had so much time to reflect and I’m OK with the pace that we’re going,” she says. “I just want to have a globally recognized brand that a lot of women can identify with and that we can continue to create these beautiful, amazing pieces. I’m so excited to show them this new era of the brand. We’re sharper, we’re clearer, we understand who our girl is, who our customer is, and I’m excited to give them more.”

‘Dare Him to Veto It’: Democrats Are Ready to Push for Real Reform

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In virtually every poll conducted this year and at ballot boxes across the nation last week, Americans were abundantly clear what they want from their government. They are worried about affordability, they are tired of the open corruption and profiteering by politicians and corporations, and they want their taxes to be spent on them — not on foreign wars.

In the midterm elections, voters dealt Donald Trump and the Republican Party a punishing blow just two years after elevating the MAGA movement in 2024. The GOP lost control of the House and Senate, five gubernatorial seats — including in Texas, Georgia, and Ohio — and shed swaths of downballot offices.

The party, fresh off monumental midterm victories that exceeded even their most feverish daydreams, are already planning a wave of oversight actions and accountability measures against members of the Trump administration. House Democrats on the Oversight Committee previously outlined to Rolling Stone their investigative priorities for Trump’s lame duck years — including, as enumerated by incoming Oversight Chair Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.), “Trump family corruption, DHS and ICE, corporate corruption, and a refocus on the administration’s handling of the Epstein Scandal.” The power to subpoena witnesses and shape the federal budget are difficult for Trump to undermine.

Passing legislation is another story, as Democrats will be forced to deal with the power of the presidential veto. But that doesn’t mean they shouldn’t force Trump to use it.

“For me, there’s still nothing more exciting in music than discovering a great song from someone you’ve never heard of,” Bergman says in a press release. “That’s always been at the core of what makes music publishing a great business. Meet the young architects behind your favorite movies, series, streams and stars. THR’s 33rd annual power list of Hollywood’s top players, 35 and under.

By Winston Cho, Aaron Couch, Caitlin Huston, Mia Galuppo, Ryan Gajewski, Katie Kilkenny, Borys Kit, Lacey Rose November 11, 2026

Nobody ever said that Hollywood careers come easy. But right now, with consolidation and layoffs decimating the middle rungs of entertainment’s steep corporate ladder and studio remits changing with each earnings report, dream jobs have never been more difficult to land, much less retain.

Such trends aren’t slowing the ascent of THR’s Next Gen class of 2026, who join such illustrious alumni as Donna Langley, Ari Emanuel, Kevin Feige and Peter Rice. In fact, this group is too busy forging new paths through the chaos — whether that’s bringing Gen Z back to cinemas (as indie producer Christian Vazquez did with both Primetime and You Can See Everything) or fortifying the streaming ecosystem amid endless churn (see Claudia Shin and Louie Hayes, behind the Apple TV Emmy darling Widow’s Bay and the Prime Video breakout Sterling Point, respectively.)

Meet the 35 executives and reps, each of them 35 years old or younger, who should be atop your call list, if they aren’t already.

LOGLINE Championing rom-coms like Anyone But You and Netflix’s upcoming 13 Going on 30 update.

THE ARC Altman got a unique view of the industry when she would join her father, Steve, who rented out lighting, camera and grip equipment: “I grew up doing my homework in grip trucks, and I just loved being on set.” Her dad’s rom-com adoration rubbed off during their weekly trips to the movies, where films like Love Actually made an early impression. Altman studied journalism at Emerson but caught the producing bug while moonlighting on college TV projects. After time at Gersh, she became then-Universal exec Jeff Kirschenbaum’s assistant and followed him to RK Films. Up next: Amazon MGM’s tennis rom-com Love Love and Netflix’s Millie Bobby Brown-led Just Picture It.

Researchers at Google DeepMind have unveiled a new AI model designed to unravel how changes in our DNA affect different cells, giving scientists a more detailed view of a notoriously difficult problem that could hold important clues to the causes of disease — and point the way to new treatments.

AlphaGenome 2 is the successor to DeepMind’s genome-predicting AI model AlphaGenome, which was released last year. While nearly every cell in the human body carries the same DNA, different types of cells — from heart muscle cells and skin cells to immune cells fighting an infection — switch different genes on and off, and those patterns can change depending on what a cell is doing. As a result, the same genetic variant can have very different effects depending on the type and state of the cell.

The new model is designed to capture those differences, predicting the effects of genetic variants on individual cell types and states. Google said in a blog post published Wednesday that it offers a much finer-grained view than broader models like AlphaGenome, potentially revealing disease-relevant effects in specific cells that might otherwise go unnoticed.

AlphaGenome 2 was trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles across 68 tissues to give it that more detailed view. DeepMind says the model effectively turns static cell atlases — huge data repositories that map and catalog individual cells in detail — “into a predictive engine for human genetics.” According to the company, AlphaGenome 2 identifies nearly twice as many variants that can change gene expression in cells than its previous state-of-the-art model.

Notably, the researchers found that AlphaGenome 2 could also make predictions about new types of cells and biological states without needing to be retrained. It could also identify gaps in experimental data, potentially helping scientists decide what to investigate next in the lab.

For Adam Gayoso, a research scientist at Google DeepMind involved in the project, the big breakthrough is “tying human genetics to cell biology.” AlphaGenome 2 gives researchers a way to drill down on genetic variants identified in large populations, to see what effects they may be having on a cellular level, and potentially connect those effects to disease, he told The Verge.

Google DeepMind’s genomics lead Žiga Avsec told The Verge that this specificity is important if you have a goal like figuring out how to treat a particular disease. “When you develop drugs, you’re targeting specific proteins and specific cell types,” he explained, which makes having this kind of granularity vital.

Building a model like AlphaGenome 2 has only recently become possible thanks to new technologies that can capture individual cells and their states at high resolution and create the vast datasets needed to train it, Avsec said. DeepMind is now working to improve its genetics models by generating more data, including for cell states that aren’t represented well today. Avsec said future versions could also potentially be trained on data “across multiple species,” giving the models a broader view of biology. Longer term, he said models like AlphaGenome 2 could feed into efforts to create “virtual cells,” digital simulations designed to mimic biological systems.

Although Avsec said he views AlphaGenome 2 as a successor to AlphaGenome, it isn’t replacing it any time soon. He said its more detailed predictions come at a computational cost and are “more expensive” to produce than those of its predecessor, he said. Those demands would have made it impractical to use AlphaGenome 2 to produce the genome-wide Atlas covering “every possible DNA letter change in the human genome” that DeepMind released in September.

Google says it is making AlphaGenome 2 available for non-commercial research via the AlphaGenome API from today, with a limited “private preview” for commercial research.

The model is the latest in a growing array of efforts by Google and rival AI labs to use the technology to tackle major problems in science and medicine. Pushmeet Kohli, who heads DeepMind’s AI for science research, has hinted that more impressive scientific results are on the way, and the organization is expected to open an automated materials science lab in the UK soon. Anthropic, meanwhile, has been experimenting with drug design and given Claude a wet lab, while OpenAI has been mowing down an impressive amount of longstanding problems in mathematics and related fields.
