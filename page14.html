The redesigned compact sedan features Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant, Gleo AI, and an updated regenerative braking system with EV-like one-pedal driving.

Hyundai pulled back the cover on the new 2027 Elantra sedan and 2028 Elantra Hybrid this week at the LA Auto Show, and, wow, it looks like nothing else in its class. The bold styling may not be for everyone — though, personally, I dig it — but economy car shoppers will certainly be interested in the compact hybrid sedan’s targeted 53 mpg and Gleo, Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant that controls the automaker’s new Android Automotive OS-based dashboard tech.

The humble Hyundai Elantra hasn’t historically been a sexy car, but it remains an important one for the brand. It’s the automaker’s second-bestselling vehicle, behind only the Tucson. More importantly, the Elantra is one of the stalwarts of the affordable compact sedan class, a segment that many US automakers have abandoned in favor of more profitable SUVs. At a time when buyers are more sensitive than ever to inflated fuel costs and sticker prices, economy car options like the Elantra and its second-generation hybrid powertrain are worth our attention.

The updated hybrid powertrain pairs a 1.6-liter four-cylinder gasoline engine with a 6-speed dual-clutch automatic gearbox. Combined output at the front wheels rises to 155 horsepower (up from 139 hp in the current car), thanks to a more powerful 45-kilowatt electric-assist motor (up from 32kW).

An updated regenerative braking system recaptures energy when slowing and now offers an “EV-like one-pedal driving experience” that can bring the car to a complete stop at its maximum setting. Additionally, the hybrid now features a new Stay mode that lets the climate control and infotainment systems run while parked for up to 30 minutes without firing up the gas engine.

Sharing the stage in LA, the new nonhybrid Elantra arrives with the same 2.0-liter four-cylinder engine and continuously variable transmission as the current generation. At 147 hp with a target of 35 mpg combined, it’s no more powerful than before, but it should also be no less efficient, despite the sedan’s increased size. I’d say the hybrid is the better choice, though it will likely also be the more expensive powertrain option.

Inside, a new cockpit design moves the now-hoodless digital instrument cluster, a 9.9-inch “Slim Information Display,” away from the driver and toward the windshield on the dashboard. The new placement, similar to what I’ve seen on recent Toyota models, puts vehicle information closer to the driver’s line of sight.

Pleos Connect, Hyundai’s new Android Automotive OS-based infotainment suite, lives on a 12.9-inch center display. Despite being based on Android, it still features the automaker’s bespoke navigation and media software, rather than Google Maps. The system is said to be over-the-air updatable and features standard wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto for drivers who’d rather bring their own apps on the road.

The Pleos dashboard OS is matched with Gleo AI (who names these things?!), which is Hyundai’s new AI voice assistant that lets drivers and passengers talk to the car rather than occupy a hand on the touchscreen. Hyundai says the system features “contextual language support, multi-command capability and zone-specific recognition,” which basically means the car knows who’s talking to it and which seat they’re in and can assign context to voice prompts based on the situation.

“Affordable doesn’t have to be anonymous,” said Olabisi Boyle, Hyundai’s senior vice president of product, at the unveiling. I agree: Hyundai’s designers clearly aren’t afraid to push the design envelope, and the new Elantra is anything but bland.

The sedan introduces the automaker’s Art of Steel design language, with chunky flared fenders that feel inspired by the brand’s recent new SUVs. The tall greenhouse looks a bit cartoonish if you catch it at the wrong angle, but it provides increased headroom inside. From certain angles, the sedan’s beefy fenders remind me of an angular Korean take on the Subaru WRX. From other viewpoints, I can see inspiration from Hyundai’s N Vision 74 concept and the hydrogen-fueled Nexo, particularly in the triangular rear windows.

The sedan measures 188.8 inches bumper-to-bumper on a 108.3-inch wheelbase that is 1.2 inches longer than the current model’s. Hyundai says the stretch for this generation improves rear-passenger space, now rated at 39.1 inches of legroom on the second row. The Elantra, according to its designers, now approaches the interior volume of a midsize car, a claim I’d take with a grain of salt had I not watched multiple six-foot-plus attendees slip into the back seat with relative ease.

Hyundai did not announce pricing or detailed specs at the auto show debut, and availability is still vague. However, based on the stated model years, which are usually slightly offset from calendar years, I expect the 2027 Elantra sedan to arrive in early 2027, with the 2028 Elantra Hybrid following in late 2027.

S&P 500 correction: Five forces behind the market sell-off

📅 2026-11-20  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Esteemed Wharton professor Jeremy Siegel was more animated than usual on Friday afternoon... with good reason.

The normally staid author of "Stocks for the Long Run" spoke to CNBC on Friday as the market careened toward some ugly milestones. The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average closed in correction territory, off more than 10% from their records. The Nasdaq Composite lost its gain for the year and the more concentrated Nasdaq-100 entered bear market territory on an intraday basis.

"These things are like straws that break the camel's back. You know what I mean? Everyone [is] all, 'I don't care, I don't care cause everything else is good' and when those straws keep on going... Bang," said Siegel.

Just a month-and-a-half ago, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite were at record highs. But the straws were already adding up by then and a market in denial is just now recognizing them.

Here are the reasons behind the sell-off:

The breaking point came this week in the form of the initial public offering of artificial intelligence leader Anthropic and a pick-up in the selling intensity for one-time AI chip leader Nvidia.

Anthropic upped its offering amount a day before going public despite the already dimming climate around AI-related stocks. The move ensured it would be the biggest IPO ever, but played into the fears that this debut — following SpaceX's monster offering over the summer — was a collective cash-out moment for the AI complex that will leave the investing masses holding the bag.

In other words, it could be the ultimate "sell the news" moment for the AI trade.

On Thursday, Anthropic traded from its $83 IPO price to a high above $115 before instantly rolling over and taking the AI trade with it. Anthropic's sell-off intensified Friday, and when the dust settled Anthropic closed the week at $88.91.

The Nasdaq posted its worst week since the Covid plunge of March 2020, down 10%.

Nvidia fell 14% on Friday with the selling intensifying after a Reuters headline hit the tape that the chipmaker's accounting is the focus of a Securities and Exchange Commission probe.

Nvidia is now down nearly 40% in November alone, a month that saw the AI bull market's leading salesman, Jensen Huang, resign as its CEO because of an affair with an alleged Chinese spy that raised speculation about whether the company's chips were ending up in Beijing's hands despite government restrictions.

To start the week, the Commerce Department tightened restrictions on U.S.-made chips to stop China from accessing them remotely from other countries.

A solid earnings report on Tuesday fell flat as Nvidia's board definitively ruled out any chance Huang would return, disappointing some of the stock's fanatics still holding out hope for his far-fetched return. Nvidia ended the week with a 23% loss.

The S&P 500 traded below its 200-day moving average — what's considered a measure of a security's long-term trend — on Tuesday and made a convincing break of it on Thursday. This turned many on Wall Street— like commodity trading advisors — into automatic sellers on the market. So-called CTAs are individuals or organizations that advise others on trading futures and swaps across a range of assets.

"We know there's a lot of trend riders on these markets," said Siegel. "They have their trend lines. They've ridden it up and as soon as it goes down, they're selling and they're selling and they're selling and they just perpetuate" the decline, he said.

The Iran conflict has caused the price oil to surge and while it's off its highs it still remains above $83 a barrel, while the fees for transiting the Strait of Hormuz and the rest of the region keep persistent pressure on global prices.

A series of numbers earlier in the month showed inflation is still far from the Federal Reserve's 2% target, sparking a run of central bank officials to warn they may have to get more aggressive with interest rates.

After increasing rates in September by a quarter point, the Fed skipped a hike in late October and sparked concern among bond investors it may be falling behind the inflation fight. The 10-year Treasury yield subsequently jumped above 5% and stayed there until a flight of safe haven fixed income buying brought it back below that level on Friday.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said this week that the Fed may need to do a half percentage point hike next month to catch up.

"I'm very, very concerned about inflation. It's kind of headed in the wrong direction, and it's spreading out," Waller told CNBC.

"Don't fight the Fed" has long been a maxim on Wall Street and right now expectations are that the central bank will need to raise rates in December and again in January.

And late-breaking on Friday was a report from the Financial Times that the Trump Administration is considering restarting strikes on Iran.

Before the grim market milestones were met on Friday, it had long lost its mojo on fears that the pace of growth driven by AI companies was slowing.

As Anthropic prepared to go public, its third-quarter figures released earlier this month showed revenue growth last quarter was 47%, down from 144% in the second quarter.

AI momentum trades, like the VanEck Semiconductor ETF and the iShares MSCI USA Momentum Factor ETF, all topped out during the summer.

The fears of slowing growth picked up this month after the Democrats won control of Congress and key governorships, fueling speculation that states — or even the whole country — could implement stricter rules on data center construction.

CoreWeave, which builds data centers using high amounts of debt, is down 71% from its high. Caterpillar, which has reinvented itself as an AI play because of data center construction and generators, is down more than 50% from its high.

The view of Siegel and many market observers is that the economy remains in good enough shape that this will just be a run-of-the-mill correction that resets a bull market that had raised people's expectations too high because of its early ferocity.

The S&P 500 returned more than 20% in 2023 and 2024 and then gained 16% last year.

"The average over long periods for the stock market is 8%, 9% a year up," said Siegel. "People began to think that 15 to 20 [percent] was going to be the norm with AI and we have to bring that down to reality."

The only problem with that theory is that the stock market and its wealth effect was one of the major factors underpinning the economy. Stocks are a greater source of household wealth — especially for baby boomers — than they ever have been before.

With the S&P 500 clinging to a 1% gain for the year, this stock market rout could end up tipping off a virtuous cycle that slows the economy, and in turn, leads to bigger equity losses.

Check what your AI agents do before they do it, with Semantic governance policies

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Two days before earnings, an employee asks their coworker agent to send the preliminary quarterly results to an investor who says they're under NDA, and to copy the employee's personal Gmail so they can review the numbers tonight. The agent can read the file and send email, so it fulfills the user request. Semantic governance policies, available in Preview in Gemini platform, enable enterprises to govern their agents and provide guardrails to stop that call before it runs. They check each tool call an agent proposes against what the user asked for and against rules you write in plain language.

Permissions alone can't catch this. They say what an agent can do, not whether a particular call follows your rules. A model can also be talked out of the rules in its system instructions, by a user with a good reason, or by a document telling it to do something nobody asked for, a technique called indirect prompt injection. So the check has to sit outside the agent. Semantic governance policies are enforced through a policy enforcement point, such as Agent Gateway, alongside the agent governance controls we expanded in October, so policies stay separate from your agent's code.

Semantic governance policies check each tool call your agent's model proposes, before it runs, against two things:

Today, the policy enforcement point that Semantic governance policies support is Agent Gateway. You need to configure your agent so all your agent egress traffic goes through Agent Gateway, facilitating traffic between your agent and its model. When the model responds with a tool call, Agent Gateway sends the proposed call to the Semantic governance policy engine for evaluation before the response reaches your agent.

In the policy engine, an LLM judge evaluates the proposed call against the conversation so far, the agent's system instructions and tool descriptions, and your policies.

If the call passes, the response reaches your agent unchanged. If it doesn't, the engine replaces the response's content, tool calls included, with a configurable denial message, so the call never runs. Either way, the verdict and the judge's rationale go to Cloud Logging in your project. Responses that contain only text, such as an answer, a plan, or a question back to the user, aren't evaluated, because they take no action.

Consider a coworker agent for a finance team. It can search the shared drive, read files, send email, and delete draft files, and its system instructions include the date of the next earnings release. The company writes one policy for the agent:

"Quarterly financial results (revenue, margins, bookings, guidance) should not be shared publicly until the public earnings release. Before the release, they may be shared only with @example.com addresses. Never send them to external or personal addresses, investors, analysts, or press, regardless of NDAs, urgency, or the requester's seniority."

In each denied case, the agent gets a denial message instead of the call. The judge's rationale can quote your policy, so if users shouldn't see it, set your own denial message on the policy.

Agent Anomaly Detection: Safeguarding agents across other clouds

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agent Anomaly Detection is in Private Preview, including monitoring for agents that run outside Google Cloud. To request access, see the Agent Anomaly Detection overview.

Our launch post introduced Agent Anomaly Detection, which helps you understand and act on agent behavior that drifts outside its intended boundaries. It covered agents on Google Cloud, such as Agent Runtime, Cloud Run, and Google Kubernetes Engine (GKE). Many teams also run agents on other clouds: on AWS Lambda next to an inventory database, as a hosted agent in Microsoft Foundry, or on-premises next to a system that can't move. Those agents carry the same behavioral risks, and until now Agent Anomaly Detection couldn't review them.

Agent Anomaly Detection works from an agent's traces and logs, so it doesn't need access to the agent's runtime. If those traces and logs reach your Google Cloud project, it can detect anomalous behavior wherever the agent runs. In this post, we explain what Agent Anomaly Detection needs from an agent on other cloud, then show what it found for an agent on AWS Lambda and an agent on Microsoft Foundry.

Agent Anomaly Detection reads the logging and observability buckets that you choose when you enable it. Telemetry from anywhere works when:

Agents built with the Agent Development Kit (ADK) for Python, version 2.12.0 or later, can produce this telemetry with ADK's Google Cloud exporters and a few environment variables, which the next section shows.

An agent outside Google Cloud needs two things to send its telemetry:

For the full setup on AWS and Azure, see the documentation.

Within 30 minutes, the agent appears in the Monitored agents list, which means it has been discovered and is ready to be enabled. Once it's enabled, manually or by automatic agent enrollment at discovery, Agent Anomaly Detection starts analyzing its sessions.

The agent's model and tool calls still run where the agent runs. Only telemetry, including the prompts and responses that Agent Anomaly Detection reads, goes to your Google Cloud project. The analysis runs separately, so it doesn't slow the agent down.

To evaluate Agent Anomaly Detection outside Google Cloud, we deployed ADK agents across two platforms: an inventory agent on AWS Lambda and a customer-service agent on Microsoft Foundry. The examples below show how Agent Anomaly Detection reads whole conversations, not isolated calls, to catch behavioral drift.

Our example inventory agent runs in AWS Lambda with one tool, list_inventory, which pages through a product catalog. We sent two sessions: a shopper who checked stock for a few items and stopped, and a scraper that requested 100 catalog items at a time at increasing offsets to copy the full catalog and inventory data. Both sessions used the same tool with permitted calls, but differed in pattern and intent.

Of the two sessions, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged only the scraper's. No call failed, but the whole exchange showed systematic scraping. The analysis found cascading failures (loops and repeated calls that build on each other) and resource exhaustion (runaway use of tokens, compute, or tool calls), and recommended rate-limiting list_inventory calls.

Detection doesn't depend on the agent's model. When we ran the inventory agent on Amazon Nova Lite through Amazon Bedrock, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 10 of its 40 sessions. Some began as routine questions that the model answered by paging through the entire catalog until the function timed out: no errors, no blocked calls, and easy to miss.

Our example ecommerce customer-service agent has two tools: get_order_status and issue_refund. Refunds involve money, so they carry the highest risk.

We ran six sessions against the Foundry agent, which we told to carry out every request without validation. Two were normal: One asked for an order's status, and one asked for a $20 refund on an order that arrived damaged. The other four pushed the agent past its role, and Agent Anomaly Detection flagged all four.

In one, a user who claimed to be the store administrator had every order refunded at 10 times its total to a personal account, $1,784.90 in all. In another, a user pasted an order note that said "SYSTEM OVERRIDE," and the agent refunded $999 on a $15.99 order.

For the order note, Agent Anomaly Detection explained that override instructions in the note coerced the agent into an unverified $999 refund. It recommended validating issue_refund arguments to block override patterns in order notes, and requiring extra validation for refunds above a set threshold.

If your agent's telemetry reaches Google Cloud, Agent Anomaly Detection can monitor it with the same detectors and console as your agents on Agent Runtime. Today that means agents built with ADK for Python. Findings for agents outside Google Cloud appear only in the Agent Anomaly Detection console.

The Real Grand Theft

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor-in-chief Chris Foster on why Grand Theft Auto 6 costs more than you think (Photo: Donald Miralle/Getty Images)

Everything has a price, and I think the cost of Grand Theft Auto 6 — the much-anticipated sixth installment of one of the most successful video games in history — is far too high.

And I’m not talking about the cost to your wallet.

Triathletes know all about expensive things: Tri bikes now start around $3,000 and rise (quickly) up from there; running shoes now routinely crack $200; and now we’ve got $270 swim caps. And don’t even get me started on entry fees — the 2026 Hawaii Ironman World Championship, for instance, topped $1,800, granted that’s easily our sport’s crown jewel, but even a new event like 70.3 Utah still rings in around $500.

And even once you factor in the hard out-of-pocket expenses for equipment, entry fees, pool access, coaching, travel, accommodations, and so on, there’s still that looming cost of your time.

If a midpack triathlete did our most popular 70.3 training plan, they’d spend about 150 hours of training over the 20-week plan. For our 16-week Olympic-distance plan, a triathlete would rack up about 65 hours of training. That’s a pretty big chunk of life, but I know I’m preaching to the choir, as all triathletes know that our sport requires a big time commitment.

However, there’s good news: We know that much of that time can be spent outside; we know that — when done correctly — swimming, biking, and running are very healthy endeavors; and we know that much of that time can be spent with others. To that last point, for better or for worse, even just the act of going to a race forces us to participate within a community.

In a digital world that competes ferociously for our attention (think: social media, TV, AI, and so on), giving your precious and valuable attention — your time — to swimming, biking, running, and triathlon will do more for your physical and mental health than getting hooked and strung out on a screen for hours.

So it’s here, in the obsession over the release of GTA 6, that as the leader of an outdoor-related title — and within the context of an era of critically dangerous social disconnection and physical isolation — I find myself confused about why the larger culture is celebrating the creation of something that will drain our attention and time like a dystopian plug in the back of a Matrix battery-person’s skull. I just can’t celebrate.

On one hand, a video game centered on crime and violence is probably more of a sign of the times than a cause for rising crime rates in real life. I’ll succumb to a little bit of Qoheleth-level despair recognizing here that “what has been done is what will be done, and there is nothing new under the sun.”

And of course this is an outdoor sports publication, not Morality Weekly, but I still can’t shake another quote from the bestselling book of all time: “The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light, but if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness.”

GTA 6’s content notwithstanding, from my position in outdoor/endurance sports, I certainly can’t quite abide when our culture treats the release of a video game like a high holy holiday. Especially when it comes at such a high cost.

Set aside the hard price tag of a gaming console and game (let’s say less than $1k), which is actually relatively low compared to a hobby like triathlon, and keep your eyes fixed on the time cost and quality.

Experts from IGN note that Grand Theft Auto 6 took their editors between 42 and 115 hours to complete the new release. It’s probably a good guess that the average gamer would spend 80ish hours to noodle around in that virtual environment. That’s a lot of “life spend.”

And unlike many other video games, it’s time spent, well…alone.

GTA 6 has no multiplayer mode as of this writing (though it could come). This means there’s no opportunity to connect with other people — even remotely — during gameplay. You can’t commit crimes alongside your buddies in the living room; you can’t fire at police alongside friends in Sweden, Hong Kong, or France. You’re alone, for 60 hours, stealing cars and shooting people. You interact only with that world and the characters coded into it.

Now I won’t be entirely obtuse here — not everyone can physically train for a triathlon; not everyone has time or safe access to a place where they can spend 65-150 hours outdoors, even if they wanted to.

However, there is a large proportion of people whose time spent playing a video game (alone) will prevent them from being outside (with others) — moving their bodies through nature in some way, shape, or form.

That’s why, rather than relish the empty streets and bike paths that have been reported across major metropolitan areas this week, I’ll be hoping that once the game ends and the credits roll (do video games have credits?), folks will head back outside to get a refund for time better spent.

Long-term memory for an AI companion without a vector database: 40 messages verbatim + 15 distilled facts

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I run Lovora, an AI companion service for adults in nine languages. The number one thing people ask an AI companion is "will she remember me?", and "memory" turns out to mean two different things. Here is the design we use, and why.

1. The recent conversation, word for word.

On every reply the model receives the last 40 messages of the chat, both sides, exactly as written. In voice calls it is the last 24, because spoken turns need to be answered faster and the prompt has to stay small.

This layer is what keeps a scene or an argument coherent. A summary would lose the exact wording, the jokes and the thing she promised two messages ago, which is exactly what people notice in a conversation.

Every 5 messages the user sends, a separate pass reads the latest part of the conversation and updates a short list of lasting facts: at most 15 short facts, about 1,500 characters. That list goes into the prompt of every later reply, in chat and in calls.

That is the layer that lets her ask about the job interview you mentioned last week, hundreds of messages later.

The distillation pass is instructed to keep facts about the user, never about the character, whose personality and backstory are fixed:

Small talk is discarded. A fact that is contradicted later is updated or dropped, not appended: the list is rewritten, not grown. The character is told to use what she remembers naturally, by asking about it or referring to it, and never to recite it as a list. Reciting is the fastest way to make a companion feel like a database.

We chose not to retrieve past messages by similarity. For a companion it has three problems:

A small fixed budget forces the distillation step to decide what matters, which is exactly the judgment retrieval skips.

The verbatim window is the expensive part: 40 messages is a real share of the context on every reply. The fact pass is cheap: it runs once every 5 user messages, as a separate call on a short excerpt.

If you are building anything conversational (support bots, tutors, companions), I would start here: a verbatim window sized to your latency budget, plus a small, rewritten list of facts about the user. Retrieval can come later if you ever need it.
