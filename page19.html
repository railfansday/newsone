How <i><em>Marty Supreme</em></i> Costume Designer Miyako Bellizzi Really Shops

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The consummate downtown girl joins Bazaar’s podcast to talk about collecting vintage skirt suits and Supreme hoodies

The Good Buy is a podcast from Harper’s Bazaar in which editors Leah Chernikoff and Lynette Nylander invite celebrities, designers, models, and tastemakers to talk shop: what they buy, where they got it, and why it matters. Come down the fashion rabbit hole and take a peek inside the closets and shopping carts of the world’s most stylish people. Learn how they use style to tell their stories.

From Uncut Gems to Marty Supreme, Oscar-nominated costume designer Miyako Bellizzi is responsible for dressing some of the most memorable films of the past decade. A longtime collaborator with the Safdie brothers—Joshua and Benjamin—she’s brought the filmmakers’ stories to life with detail-oriented styling that feels lived-in and real. Whether capturing the ostentatious wardrobe of a 2012 Diamond District hustler or crafting 1950s-accurate ensembles for over 3,000 background actors in the Timothée Chalamet–led ping-pong drama, she’s proven time and time again she has a knack for storytelling.

In her personal life, this expertise has translated into an eclectic closet full of vintage finds. Known for mixing high-end designer pieces with streetwear pulls and 1940s workwear, she dresses herself with as much intention as she does the characters you see on screen. Showing up to film this week’s installment of Harper’s Bazaar’s The Good Buy, she paired a fitted blazer from her collection of 1940s and ’50s skirt suits with shorts she made herself, Phoebe Philo flats, and sculptural silver statement earrings.

“I love costume design, I love fashion, I love film, I love it all,” she said. “I love people, so I think that’s why.”

In the episode, Bellizzi dives into everything from costuming the screen to shopping for herself. Read on for highlights and watch the show in full above.

“I’ve always loved vintage, I think my parents put me onto it. My grandma had all these clothes from New York from the ’50s and ’60s that she didn’t want, and my dad is a big vintage buyer. Even my Barbies were vintage Barbies. But I also just appreciate well-made pieces, I appreciate good fabrics, things that you can’t find today. As a designer, it’s fascinating to see how things were made back in different eras.

“I collect vintage 1940s and ’50s skirt suits. It’s always been my favorite era, which was why Marty Supreme was a dream film for me. I think I started collecting in high school—we used to have vintage fairs in San Francisco, where I’m from. I like the shape and silhouette. This is what Mugler referenced. I’m a historical person and I love the shapes and silhouettes of Mugler, and I have a lot of Mugler, but also I know where it comes from, so I like to go to the source.”

“I don’t personally buy a lot of contemporary brands for myself, other than Phoebe Philo and the Row. I [shop there for] very classic basics, because I find all the freaky weird things vintage.”

“When I was a kid, I was like 5’9” in the fifth grade. I was really tall. And we’d always have to shop in the boy’s section. When I first started, I was at Details Magazine. It was my first internship; that’s why I moved to New York. But I always felt out of place. I never felt like I fit in with the fashion girlies of that time. And so I remember enjoying menswear and learning about menswear and tailoring, and I found that was more inspiring to me than being in the women’s fashion closet.”

“I was at Vice and I was doing all the fashion stories, and there was a mass exodus and it was the group that left that asked if I would be interested in doing a film. I had never done one before, and I was like, ‘Sure, I’ll try.’ Then, [with the Safdie brothers,] we had mutual friends. The film industry is very small. Everything is timing. I never thought I wanted to be a costume designer; this was not the goal when I first moved to New York. But I love it because it’s this family—you travel with your crew and you’re doing these jobs together and you live together. You’re living and breathing this alternate reality. All you’re thinking about are these characters. It’s almost like how an actor has to get into character. You have to become the character you’re creating.”

“It was tough, honestly. You have to think about yourself being presented in that way. Traditionally, most costume designers aren’t fashion girlies. I was excited for it but it was also very stressful. I remember I was working in Paris at the time and I was staying with [designer] Gabe [Semeraro]. I was freaking out. I went to a few vintage archives there, being like, ‘At least I’m in Paris. I can find a dress.’ And I did find a really beautiful red Galliano dress that I loved that I was going to rent, but I was like, ‘I don’t know, it’s so expensive.’ One night, I was home with Gabe, and he was like, ‘Babe, I’m just gonna make one for you.’ It was one of my favorite dresses I’ve ever worn or seen.”

“Every time I wrap a film, it feels like an achievement. I remember I was doing a film in Hawaii, and when I was there, I loved that the women wore their wealth on their wrists. They stack solid gold bangles [on their wrists], and most of times they have names of people in their families [engraved]. And they’re the most beautiful things. I figured out where to find them—at a flea market—and I got one and had it engraved. Now I’ve turned it into a thing where I buy myself a piece of jewelry or something really nice after I wrap a film."

“I am very thoughtful about what I buy for myself. I never want to spend too much money on myself. But I have made a folder on my phone of pieces I regret not buying. We were in Paris and went to Alaïa and I wanted this pink set that I didn’t buy and I still think about it.”

“Now that we are approaching fall, I live in a long sleeve. That’s my Bay area [upbringing] coming out of me. I need to have a XXXL long-sleeve T-shirt, heavy cotton. That is my comfort layer. I bring one everywhere. That and a hoodie. Those are my two hometown pieces. And when I get obsessed with something, I find a lot of them. I have a lot of Supreme hoodies, although my new favorites are 6397, because they have fun colors.”

“I was thinking about what I would buy if I had all the money in the world. It’s a pair of cuffs—or, actually, anything from this jewelry designer Art Smith. It’s kind of modernist jewelry from a different era, like 50 years ago. So something like that. Sophie Buhai is kind of the modern-day equivalent. Pieces that could be in museums.”

“I’m going to be realistic—I did buy four pairs of shoes this week: Chanel flats [from Matthieu Blazy’s first collection] on the RealReal, a pair of [Michael Rider’s] Celine jazz shoes, two pairs of Bottega woven flats.”

Judicial Watch founder Larry Klayman disbarred by Supreme Court

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Supreme Court disbarred conservative watchdog group Judicial Watch founder and activist Larry Klayman on Monday following his 18-month suspension issued last year by the District of Columbia Court of Appeals. (Image from Shutterstock)

The U.S. Supreme Court disbarred conservative watchdog group Judicial Watch founder and activist Larry Klayman on Monday following his 18-month suspension issued last year by the District of Columbia Court of Appeals.

Klayman’s disbarment follows a suspension for violating numerous ethical rules in connection to representing rancher Cliven Bundy in Nevada federal court, Law360 reports.

Bundy had been facing a criminal case over his 2014 standoff with federal agents when Klayman, a Florida attorney, sought pro hac vice status to join Bundy’s legal team in 2016. The District of Columbia Office of Disciplinary Counsel filed a complaint against Klayman in 2018, claiming that he misled the U.S. District Court for the District of Nevada about his disciplinary status when he tried to represent Bundy. A D.C appellate court ruling adopted the District of Columbia Bar’s findings and suspension.

The disciplinary office alleged that Klayman also made baseless claims against the judge who denied his admission to the Nevada court.

Klayman, in a statement to Law360 on Monday said, “The order contains no factual or legal reasoning, is subject to reconsideration and is not final. I made a compelling submission to the Supreme Court recently, and apparently, it was not considered. This will be a basis for reconsideration.”

Amazon has greenlit a new period romance piece for TV.

The Gilded Age meets Bridgerton in a new period romance drama from Amazon, who've revealed new details about the upcoming show. TV period dramas have become popular across various streaming platforms in recent years. From the romantic overtones of Bridgerton to the dramatic tension in The Gilded Age, period dramas have plenty to say across various settings. This includes both in a romantic context, as well as the general tribulations and difficulties of the time for people in different ways.

Now, Amazon has confirmed that it will be developing a new period romance drama titled The American Heiress for MGM+. The new series stars Emmy-winning actor Julianna Margulies, pulled from the pages of Daisy Goodwin's bestselling novel of the same name. The eight-episode series sees Magulies take on the role of Isabella Cash. The story focuses on her daughter, Cora Cash, a young princess brought to England against her will despite being in love with American artist Teddy. However, upon visiting the 12th Duke of Wareham's castle, things begin to change.

The plot synopsis says that Cora is the main protagonist, focusing on her interactions with her new, English environment as she wrestles with what she truly wants in life. In the meantime, Isabella finds a challenge in the Double Duchess, a woman who has connections to many different dukes. The story will see the pair adjusting to their new life, all while dealing with the expected challenges women in the 1890s faced.

See what MGM+ global head Michael Wright had to say about the show, and the praise he afforded all those involved in bringing it to life:

The American Heiress is a seductive mix of captivating characters, old world class politics, and romantic ambition. Daisy Goodwin is a master at crafting entertaining and smart stories full of fascinating people and high stakes, and this show is Daisy at her best. Having Julianna Margulies lead this amazing cast with her unique strength, intelligence and charm is ideal. Together with producer Stacey Snider, this team makes The American Heiress a perfect complement to the MGM+ brand of classic Hollywood storytelling for a modern audience.

Goodwin serves as showrunner on the series, alongside executive producer status with Stacey Snider, Chief Creative Officer of FilmNation Entertainment. Julianna Margulies, Damian Keogh, and Stella Merz, alongside FilmNation creative director Kirstie Macdonald serve as executive producers as well. The series is co-produced by MGM+ Studios and Fifth Season, the latter of whom will focus on global distribution. Joanna Crow is a producer, and Gemma Wright a co-producer. The show will be directed by Catherine Morshead, Sandra Goldbacher, and Edward Hall.

In addition to Wright, Fifth Season President of Television Peter Traugott also released a statement, talking about the success of other MGM+ productions like American Classic and The Westies. It helped to inform a decision about The American Heiress, and how well it will likely perform once it arrives on the platform:

We’re thrilled to work with Daisy, Stacey, and the MGM+ team on this deeply compelling story of love warped by power, which we look forward to bringing to life for audiences around the world. Following the success of projects such as American Classic and The Westies, The American Heiress marks an exciting milestone in the continued growth of our partnership with MGM+.

Goodwin herself also spoke about the forthcoming TV series, promising a mother-daughter story that she hopes will captivate a large audience and deliver a historical yet relevant tale to a wider audience:

The American Heiress is a story about mothers and daughters, old society obligation and new world entitlement, and the search for meaning in a world distorted by money. I hope it will make viewers laugh, think, and swoon. I am a history nerd, but I am also a fan of the Hollywood golden age—think Consuelo Vanderbilt meets Rebecca! I am beyond excited to be working with Julianna Margulies who embodies the majesty and frustration of Isabella Cash.

The American Heiress already has all the makings of another stellar period drama in the age of streaming. Although there's no production start date as of writing, the series' development means it'll fit right in with established series like Bridgerton and The Gilded Age. Since Goodwin herself has proven to be a TV champion, having been showrunner of Victoria, Amazon is hoping to capture that same level of intrigue as the upcoming adaptation gets underway.

How Michael Carrick's Man United fell apart: Players' bust-up on flight back from Madrid, weakness in training and on touchline - and why dressing-room sources believe divided board 'hung him out to dry'

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By CHRIS WHEELER, NORTHERN SPORTS WRITER and NATHAN SALT - FOOTBALL REPORTER

Published: 10:30 EST, 11 November 2026 | Updated: 06:44 EST, 12 November 2026

For the last two days, Michael Carrick has followed his familiar route towards Manchester United’s training base, nudging his black Land Rover past the security barrier at Carrington before 8am, and making his way into work just as he has for much of the last two decades as a player, coach and head coach at the club.

While Carrick's fate has been decided by men in suits in Monaco and Manchester, he has put on his training gear and gone through the motions of coaching whichever players are left during the international break.

It has become increasingly apparent that Carrick was a dead man walking in the two weeks since the United board gave him a stay of execution and bought themselves time after the 3-0 defeat by Bournemouth last month.

Having effectively been brought in as a caretaker when United plucked him off the beach in Barbados in mid-January, he left much the same way: merely keeping the seat warm for Mauricio Pochettino in the chaotic days that have led to his sacking.

Sources close to the United dressing room believe Carrick has been ‘hung out to dry’ as United dithered over his future and then began a futile attempt to appoint Pochettino as his replacement in secret.

This is the story of how Carrick's United fell apart. It is the story of how disgruntled players aired their dirty laundry in public and clashed in private. It is the story of how the club's boardroom became the backdrop for an executive power struggle. And it is the story of how the magic touch that saw Carrick land the job permanently just five months ago deserted him...

Michael Carrick is out as Manchester United manager - just ten league games after guiding United to third and being handed a two-year contract

Carrick has had to turn up as normal at Carrington this week even as the world knew he was for the chop. Some dressing-room sources say he has been 'hung out to dry'

Carrick would have seen the writing on the wall when he got up to give an impressive speech at a dinner in the Heritage Suite at Old Trafford on Friday night to celebrate the 40th anniversary of Sir Alex Ferguson’s appointment.

The significance of the occasion was not lost on Carrick. Ferguson was brought in to succeed Ron Atkinson on the same date in November 1986 with United one place above the relegation zone. Having boxed himself into an identical corner, Carrick too was on borrowed time.

Two days later, he stood helplessly on the touchline at Old Trafford as United played out a ridiculous 4-4 draw with Aston Villa. Twice his team fought back from two goals down, but it was the final proof for United bosses that change was unavoidable.

The biggest club in English football having to endure the humiliation of at least two weeks in the relegation zone was too much for them to stomach.

His body language after the Bournemouth game when a tearful and crestfallen Carrick offered what looked like a farewell wave to the Stretford End – four days after he approached fans in Como to apologise for an equally painful 4-1 Champions League surrender – suggested he was already done.

The 45-year-old limped on to another defeat at Chelsea, then presided over a barmy 3-2 comeback win over Roma and the bonkers draw with Villa. A consummate and composed midfielder for United, Carrick of all people knew this wasn’t the way football should be played.

All the while, he spoke like a man who could turn things around. They always do. But the performances and results told a different story.

His critics would argue that someone whose only previous job in management was at Middlesbrough should never have been put in charge of United in the first place.

Others would say the bravest move by the club would have been to thank Carrick for his admirable efforts in securing third place and Champions League qualification last season, and move on in the summer when Pochettino would have been available for free before signing a new contract as US coach with a £7million release clause.

The 45-year-old's body language after an earlier loss to Bournemouth had suggested he was done back then, with Carrick appearing to wave farewell to the Stretford End

Instead, Carrick was rewarded with a two-year deal in May and the wheels fell off in spectacular fashion. Rather like his friend and former team-mate Ole Gunnar Solskjaer, the honeymoon period gave way to something altogether less appealing.

At least Solskjaer stuck around for three more years. Having beaten the Norwegian to the job in January, Carrick’s reign lasted less than 10 months.

The similarities between the two men are striking. Ex-players who basked in the glory days under Ferguson and know what it takes to succeed at Old Trafford, but were incapable of lifting the modern-day Manchester United to similar heights.

Having replaced Ruben Amorim and inspired a remarkable turnaround in the second half of last season, Carrick must have thought he had a chance.

His calm demeanour and experience of life in the Old Trafford pressure cooker that had played a part in his appointment was in contrast to the emotional incontinence of Amorim. How strange then that it has given way to such calamity and chaos.

United got off to the worst possible start by losing at newly-promoted Hull City in August. Tigers boss Sergej Jakirovic belittled them by saying his goalkeeper Konstantinos Tzolakis might as well have been sipping a cup of tea, and the hits have just kept on coming for Carrick.

Aside from Bournemouth, there were the late equalisers scored by Ainsley Maitland-Niles and Dominic Solanke for Everton and Tottenham, the controversial winner conceded to 10-man Manchester City, crashing out of the Carabao Cup to Brighton after giving up a two-goal lead, and the defeats in Europe by Atletico Madrid and Como after going behind in the opening seconds.

By the time Alysson scored in the first minute for Villa on Sunday, United’s defence had become as leaky as the Old Trafford roof. The four goals Carrick’s side conceded took it to 15 in his final four games.

United's defence has been leaky all season long, with Ainsley Maitland-Niles' stoppage-time equaliser at Hill Dickinson Stadium among the goals to cost them valuable points

So why the alarming decline from last season? The United players haven’t looked sharp after a disjointed World Cup summer that involved a more relaxed one-week training camp in Ireland. There have been too many injuries.

Some players have spoken of a drop-off in training under Carrick and his backroom staff. Others feel that the switch to a more possession-based 4-2-4 system was asking too much of a group who aren’t suited to it.

There was surprise that a man so invested in the club's academy used so few young players. Harry Amass was left kicking his heels despite the chronic shortage of left backs, JJ Gabriel's shock exit was an unwelcome distraction, and Shea Lacey's re-emergence came too late.

It would be grossly unfair to pin this all on Carrick though. He is just the latest unfortunate to pay the price for the shambles at United which runs from the boardroom to the dressing room.

Amid ongoing uncertainty over his future, there has been a power struggle behind the scenes between director of football Jason Wilcox and director of recruitment Christopher Vivell over who to replace him, while sources close to the Glazer family have revealed a growing dissatisfaction at how minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe and Ineos are running the club.

United failed Carrick in the transfer market last summer with a futile pursuit of his No 1 midfield target Elliot Anderson, and instead delivered Youri Tielemans and Andrey Santos.

Carrick could see trouble on the horizon when he spoke to the media at Carton House in Maynooth during that pre-season camp in early August, but tried not to rock the boat.

It was the day his daughter Louise was getting her A-level results. ‘You know, we would like more, we need to keep pushing for more,’ he said, using the word ‘push’ another nine times.

Carlos Baleba followed but an ankle injury meant that he couldn’t play until October, and the £70m signing then suffered a hamstring injury.

United blundered catastrophically by focusing on midfield and delaying improvements to other areas of the team until next summer. In particular, the decision not to sign cover for Luke Shaw and Benjamin Sesko backfired spectacularly as both players succumbed to injury.

Lisandro Martinez called out United’s underwhelming recruitment in Madrid by saying he expected more of Wilcox, and he isn’t the only one who feels that way.

Tielemans gave a terse ‘no comment’ when he was asked on camera about his team-mate’s remarks, but it’s understood the two players clashed on the team flight back to Manchester as cracks began to show in an increasingly unhappy camp.

The camaraderie fostered by rounds of golf involving Carrick and the players during the break in Ireland was beginning to fall apart.

It came to a head when captain Bruno Fernandes became the second member of the leadership group to speak out of turn, questioning Carrick’s tactics after the loss to Como.

Having continuously defended his players in the face of criticism from some of his former United team-mates over their lack of effort, Carrick’s spiky response that ‘if you don't run forward and you don't run back, you aren’t going to win’ perhaps revealed his true feelings about this group.

He and Fernandes settled their differences in a clear-the-air meeting, but the questions didn’t go away. When he was asked about their relationship again at the end of what proved to be his final pre-match press conference last Friday, Carrick tapped his inquisitor on the shoulder as he left the room at Carrington and said with a smile: ‘Nice try!’

It was typical of Carrick, who often met questions with the knowing grin of a man who couldn’t say what he really thought.

It got to the point where he was encouraged by the club to be a little more forthcoming in press conferences. There have been some barbed post-match exchanges in TV interviews with Sky Sports and Viaplay this season, and the five-minute monologue before the Bournemouth game was pure theatre.

But on the whole, Carrick’s ability to play a straight bat in front of the cameras was a welcome change for the Old Trafford hierarchy compared to the loose-lipped Amorim – particularly with the Amazon Prime documentary-makers following their every move.

‘He talks a lot but doesn't say much,’ said Roy Keane, never Carrick’s biggest fan, on Sunday. ‘I don't know if that's a tactic, like a politician.’

More of an issue was Carrick’s rather passionless appearance on the touchline, which seemed to be reflected in his team at times.

Always dressed in black, a hand placed studiously on his chin, it has contrasted with more animated adversaries such as Unai Emery, Roberto De Zerbi and Diego Simeone in recent weeks.

‘He doesn't upset anyone, but doesn't inspire anyone either,’ remarked one dressing room source.

The fans were always behind him though, even the ones who may have accepted some time ago that a change was needed. Support and sympathy were never in short supply for a coach who has been let down by the men in the directors’ box and the players on the pitch.

That much has been abundantly clear by the abuse meted out to Ratcliffe and Co this season, and the unprecedented scenes when the fans turned on the players after Bournemouth.

Carrick looks set to be replaced by former Tottenham and Chelsea boss Mauricio Pochettino (right)

Carrick has been known to stand alongside them in the past, and it would be no surprise to see him there again in future.

‘Oh, it’s Carrick you know…’ echoed his tribute song from the Stretford End as he disappeared down the tunnel for the last time on Sunday evening.

So another bites the dust. A good man. A United man. The right man for the job? Maybe not, as it turns out. That poisoned chalice now passes to Mauricio Pochettino.

That update also has some serious implications for the timeline of Avatar as a whole, in addition to Seven Havens' timeline more specifically.

Bringing Pavi and Nisha closer to Aang also seems like a bit of a no-brainer. Avatar: The Last Airbender is widely regarded as one of the best animated shows ever made. The Legend of Korra has aged well, but it was far less universally acclaimed. Seven Havens should take as much inspiration from Aang and Team Avatar as possible. Luckily, the show's creators have announced that they're going to do exactly that.

A popular fan theory about Avatar: Seven Havens asserted that the show's second season could feature a time jump. Prediction: After Losing Over $1.7 Trillion in Market Cap This Year, Nvidia Stock Will Rebound in Epic Fashion. Here's Why.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia stock has crashed 27% over the last month.

For much of 2026, Nvidia (NVDA +2.44%) looked unstoppable. The artificial intelligence (AI) chipmaker delivered strong financial results, while Wall Street rewarded its dominance with a lofty valuation. In early October, Nvidia's market capitalization peaked around $5.8 trillion, making it the world's most valuable company.

Unfortunately, the momentum didn't last. As of this writing (Nov. 10), Nvidia's market cap has fallen to $4.1 trillion. That's a decline of 29%, representing roughly $1.7 trillion in lost value.

The sell-off reflects growing uncertainty surrounding AI infrastructure spending, political developments, and a new investigation involving CEO Jensen Huang. Nevertheless, I think investors are overlooking something important.

Nvidia's stock price has changed dramatically, but its underlying competitive advantages haven't disappeared. Here's why I think the semiconductor behemoth could stage a comeback in 2027.

Nvidia's meteoric rise isn't simply the result of investors being excited about AI. It was actually supported by unprecedented growth in revenue and profit. During the company's fiscal second quarter, Nvidia generated $96.2 billion in revenue, representing a 106% increase year over year. The data center business contributed $89 billion, growing 117% annually, while earnings per share (EPS) surged 128% to $2.46.

Nvidia's growth is an extension of the capital expenditure (capex) commitments from hyperscalers including Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta Platforms, and Oracle.

As big tech expands its computing infrastructure to train and operate AI models, companies are purchasing Nvidia's GPUs, networking equipment, and integrated computing systems. The company's Blackwell architecture has been instrumental in meeting this demand, while the newer Vera Rubin platform is entering full-scale production.

Nvidia is also expanding into specialized inference accelerators and agentic AI. In other words, Nvidia isn't simply selling individual chips. It's building a comprehensive stack of hardware and software for the next generation of AI. This explains why investors were willing to assign the company such a premium valuation.

Nvidia's valuation increasingly depends on the assumption that AI infrastructure spending will continue expanding at an unprecedented pace. However, investors are beginning to question how quickly the hyperscalers can generate enough returns on their data center investments.

The midterm elections introduced another layer of uncertainty. With Democrats gaining control of Congress, investors are reassessing the possibility of additional scrutiny surrounding data center construction, energy consumption, and electricity prices. While this doesn't mean AI spending will suddenly collapse, tighter regulations or slower project approvals could affect the timing of infrastructure investments.

More recently, Nvidia has encountered an entirely different challenge. Reports concerning CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with a business executive suspected of ties to Chinese intelligence prompted Nvidia's board of directors to launch an investigation.

While the board says it has no reason to believe company secrets or national security information were compromised, the situation introduces governance and potential succession risks. If Huang is eventually replaced or steps down as CEO, investors would understandably question how the transition might affect Nvidia's strategy, customer relationships, and execution. For now, the uncertainty itself is contributing to outsize pressure on Nvidia shares.

Despite these challenges, I think Nvidia remains exceptionally well positioned for long-term growth. Consider the company's competitive position. Advanced Micro Devices is expanding its AI accelerator business, while Broadcom is helping companies develop custom chips.

However, neither competitor has replicated Nvidia's ecosystem of GPUs, networking technology, and the CUDA software system. This ecosystem matters because it makes switching suppliers considerably time-consuming and expensive, creating meaningful barriers to competition.

Even if data center construction slows, that doesn't necessarily mean Nvidia's existing customers will stop purchasing the company's products. Cloud providers and frontier labs will still need to continue upgrading infrastructure, replacing older systems, and supporting growing demand for AI inference.

Even if Nvidia eventually operates under a different CEO, the company's technology, intellectual property (IP), customer relationships, and engineering talent aren't going to vanish overnight. Likewise, slower growth in AI infrastructure spending doesn't automatically eliminate Nvidia's profitability.

Nvidia could look different a year from now. Its leadership might change, its customers could become more selective, and its growth rate might moderate. Nevertheless, I expect Nvidia to remain one of the most important and profitable companies in the AI industry.

In the short term, stock prices are often driven by sentiment, uncertainty, and headlines. Over the long term, earnings growth and valuation tend to matter far more. That's why I think Nvidia's recent sell-off could look like a compelling buying opportunity, and why I'm predicting a meaningful recovery in 2027.

The stock market is more concentrated than ever before, with America's top five tech giants (Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet) representing an eye-popping 30% of the entire S&P 500. This situation is dangerous because it means the index no longer offers a high level of diversification, and any decline in these stocks will have a huge impact on the index's overall return. China’s Nvidia Honeypot Isn’t Surprising. Jensen Huang Fell for It Anyway

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There’s a specific mantra drilled into government workers, military officers, and intelligence personnel during counterintelligence briefings: “If she’s a 10 and you’re a 5, she’s a spy!” Or, as the US Army’s Counterintelligence Command posted on Valentine’s Day: “It doesn’t take a mathematician to figure out ‘10 + 5 = honeytrap.’” The very beautiful woman who is suddenly interested in your very ordinary existence probably has ulterior motives, is the message.

Jensen Huang surely doesn’t live a typical life—he’s a centibillionaire, the swashbuckling CEO of Nvidia, one of the hottest and most geopolitically consequential companies of the 21st century. Surely he’s aware of the possibility that he’d cross paths with intelligence assets. In fact, it’s hard to think of many companies in the world that would be a higher priority target for foreign intelligence agencies.

All of that makes the stock-shaking news this week all the more surprising. How did Huang get embroiled in a spy scandal that mixes infidelity, international espionage, the Trump administration, and the economic behemoth of the multitrillion-dollar AI industry?

The story kicked off just before Halloween, with a six-byline story in The New York Times reporting that Huang was the target of a Chinese spy plot. Starting somewhere between 2024 and 2025, Huang allegedly had an affair with Alice Huang (no relation), a one-time executive at a Singapore-based cloud provider known as Megaspeed. US intelligence agencies warned top US officials that they believed she was a Chinese intelligence asset. So far, Nvidia’s board, which has launched its own investigation, says it has “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised,” but that’s far from the only concern.

As one would expect, China has denied any nefarious plot centered on Jensen Huang. A Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson on Monday said, “The narrative about so-called Chinese spies is entirely based on groundless speculation—it’s nothing more than slander and smearing.”

But according to reporting by The New York Times and the Financial Times, US intelligence officials also claim that Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, was reporting to China’s Ministry of State Security intelligence agency. On Monday, Megaspeed said on LinkedIn that US authorities found “no evidence of any violations concerning the illicit transfer of chips or any illegal activities." (There’s no indication so far that Alice Huang, a one-time Chinese state journalist, is an employee or “officer” of Chinese intelligence; she’s believed to be an “asset,” an outsider who aids an intelligence agency, sometimes for pay, sometimes not.)

China, though, has elevated economic espionage to a core pillar of its economic and military development, stealing the designs for everything from US fighter jets to solar panels and commercial paint recipes. “Honeypots” or “honey traps”—positioning an alluring operative to seduce or develop a relationship with a key target—are one of intelligence’s most infamous and well-established strategies. French officials have warned specifically about Chinese honeypots since at least 2011, when intelligence reports cited a pharmaceutical researcher who was seduced and then blackmailed by a Chinese spy operation.

Over the past quarter of a century, as Silicon Valley emerged as a center of political and economic power, it has become a key focus of Chinese intelligence. In one plot, a Chinese national named Fang Fang spent years trying to cozy up to Bay Area politicians, including then US Representative Eric Swalwell. (While intelligence officials at the time reportedly believed Fang to be an operative, law enforcement officials never brought any charges against her). When traveling to China, US business executives have become accustomed to leaving their regular phones and computers at home and using burner devices to prevent the installation of spyware or malware (as I did, when I traveled to China for WIRED in 2019 to report on Huawei). In 2012, when Leon Panetta was making his first trip to China as defense secretary, he and his team were advised to bathe and shower in the dark in the event his hotel room was bugged. Last year, the threat of honeypots prompted the US government to explicitly ban personnel working in China and Hong Kong from engaging in romantic or sexual relationships with Chinese citizens. More recently, US officials joined with allies to warn about China’s increasingly sophisticated “LinkedIn espionage”: using the business-focused social networking site to recruit and build relationships with unwitting executives.

Alice Huang’s Megaspeed, for its part, is a major Pacific region player in cloud services focused on the advanced computer chips that are the heart of Nvidia’s business. The Biden administration had sharply limited the export of cutting-edge chips globally to slow China’s development of AI, but Jensen Huang has successfully lobbied the Trump administration to loosen those restrictions.

Speaking at a press conference on Monday, Donald Trump denied having prior knowledge of Alice Huang’s potential espionage. “I didn’t hear about it then, but a lot of people have heard about it, and Jensen totally denies that,” Trump said. “I asked him that question: ‘Did you have any idea that she was a spy?’ He said, ‘I had no idea that she was a spy.’” On Tuesday, the Times reported that Marco Rubio had briefed Trump in May, right before Jensen Huang joined the President on Air Force One for a trip to China.

The veracity of Trump's claims will surely be investigated, especially in the context of the famous Watergate maxim: “What did the president know and when did he know it?” Nvidia’s CEO has been publicly linked to the Megaspeed executive since at least 2024 and Alice’s husband, Kang Weigh, has reportedly known Jensen since the 2010s. WIRED’s Lauren Goode reported on Monday that Jensen Huang, starting in 2025, hired Risa Heller’s crisis communications firm, which is well-versed in handling public sex scandals. This spring, secretary of state Marco Rubio evidently helped craft a classified memo inside the administration warning officials about Jensen Huang’s potentially compromising relationship.

Jensen Huang—who has developed an important reputation as AI’s #1 cheerleader at the frontier of AI labs—is now stuck in an awkward situation. Is he an idiot, unaware that his paramour might have been a foreign intelligence asset? Merely reckless with his own reputation, fiduciary responsibility to Nvidia, and the technological security of the United States? Or a witting accomplice to undermining US national security?

Regardless of the truth, Nvidia loses. Shareholders have ridden a roller-coaster that has seen Nvidia’s stock rise by about 600 percent over the past five years, becoming the world’s second-most valuable company. In the past month, the stock has plummeted by a quarter. The disaster for Nvidia is just beginning, it seems, as lawsuits loom, including by its own shareholders.

Similarly, the Trump administration is likely to face real questions from the newly Democratic congress: When were the president and other top officials briefed about this intelligence threat? What communication, if any, did they make to Nvidia and to whom? Should more have been done by US intelligence to block Alice Huang from having access to Nvidia and its CEO?

The fact that Megaspeed is a major customer of Nvidia makes everything more complicated. Indeed, reports have circulated for at least a year that the US was concerned that Megaspeed was diverting chips to Chinese intelligence agencies, an allegation that could have massive consequences for the global AI race. Bloomberg this week reported that Megaspeed's subsidiaries have purchased billions of dollars worth of Nvidia chips and that an audit requested by the US government last year found that Megaspeed couldn’t initially account for some 50,000 chips it had purchased.

Meanwhile, Nvidia’s Huang and President Trump developed a uniquely close professional relationship. They’ve met multiple times as Huang has pushed to loosen Biden-era export controls, which Trump has repeatedly relaxed; Huang has donated $25 million to Trump’s ballroom project; while en route to Beijing earlier this year with a delegation of tech leaders, including Elon Musk and Tim Cook, Trump’s Air Force One actually made a special stop in Alaska to pick up Huang; Huang also attended a White House state dinner for Chinese president Xi Jinping and Trump, who in turn, awarded him a National Medal of Science earlier this fall.

The implication that Trump knew and did nothing for a year would make this one of the greatest counterintelligence failures of the past decade. There will be consequences: Across history, many have gone to prison for honeypot scandals; a former adviser was arrested just last year in a similar plot; though he was acquitted of committing economic espionage, he was convicted for making false statements to investigators.

Even in normal circumstances, corporate America’s tolerance of CEO misbehavior has shrunken dramatically. In recent years, CEOs at companies as varied as Nestlé, Kohl’s, BP, NBCUniversal, and the railroad Norfolk Southern have all resigned or been forced out after inappropriate relationships that violated company standards.

If Nvidia were any other company and Jensen Huang any other CEO, the facts of the scandal currently known would be enough to topple some heads. But Trump is still president, and November is only a third over. It’s not even clear that this will rank as the biggest scandal of the month—but it should.
