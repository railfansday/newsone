England left sweating as Harry Kane faces months out with ankle injury

England captain Harry Kane is facing three months out with an ankle injury, Bayern Munich boss Vincent Kompany has confirmed.

The Ballon d’Or winner scored against both Croatia and Spain in the international break and was seeking a first goal in five matches for the Bundesliga leaders in all competitions at the Allianz Arena.

However, as he looked to latch on to Michael Olise’s deflected low cross at the back post, Cologne full-back Joel Schmied got to the ball first and clattered into Kane’s left leg in his follow-through.

Kane immediately clutched his lower leg and writhed in pain for a few moments before gingerly getting back to his feet, but he was a virtual bystander for the next couple of minutes.

The 33-year-old gestured to the dugout, sunk to the turf and then received treatment on his ankle before making way for Tom Bischof, limping off the field alongside two physios.

Kane had a quick exchange with Kompany, back in the Bayern hot-seat for the first time in six matches after being diagnosed with meningeal irritation last month, before heading down the tunnel.

Speaking at full-time, Kompany confirmed Kane was facing months on the sidelines with what Max Eberl described as a tear of the syndesmotic ligament.

Sky Germany has reported he can expect to spend three months on the sidelines, meaning he will hope return before England face Norway in the Nations League quarter-finals in March.

Matters only grew worse for Bayern thereafter, as Olise was also forced off shortly after Kane with an ankle injury of his own. He was able to walk off the pitch before treatment, though.

Tottenham loanee Mikey Moore opened the scoring for Cologne after just 12 minutes, but the match finished 1-1 as Nathaniel Brown equalised for the hosts before the break.

What can AI learn from a thousand days of hearing what a baby hears?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Princeton recorded eight babies for their first 1,000 days, and a model trained on one child's audio learned language on the same schedule.

A toddler can pick up hundreds of words just by hearing people talk. Could a computer start from scratch and recognize many of those same words on roughly the same timetable?

Researchers at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute wired up 15 homes with cameras and microphones and recorded babies for most of their waking hours across the first 1,000 days of life.

The researchers turned speech recorded around eight children into clean audio using synthetic voices.

They trained a separate model on each child’s audio in the order the speech was originally recorded.

The models identified recurring sound patterns that corresponded to words.

On average, each model reached the halfway point on a vocabulary checklist within 22 days of the child it was paired with.

“We wanted to turn the usual logic of developmental research around,” said Uri Hasson, a professor of psychology and neuroscience at Princeton who led the study.

“Instead of observing a child for an hour in the laboratory, we asked whether we could build a model capable of learning and immerse it in the child’s actual experience, day after day.”

The families were in New Jersey and spanned a range of incomes and backgrounds. Each home got four to 14 recording devices spread across two to five rooms.

The devices ran 12 to 14 hours a day, from February 2022 to May 2025.

The project gathered roughly 1.2 million hours of audio and video, of which the eight homes analyzed in this paper supplied about 600,000. An automated pipeline worked out where the child was in the house and pulled out the speech happening nearby.

Each child ended up with between 1,900 and 6,600 hours of usable speech, three to seven and a half hours a day. That adds up to 6 to 27 million words per child, over a median of 960 recording days.

Every month from eight months on, caregivers filled out a standard checklist of which words their child understood and said. Those forms are how the researchers knew what each child knew, and when.

Children reached half of the 649-word checklist anywhere from about 12 to 22 months old. That ten-month spread between children is the thing most studies average away.

The model started from random numbers. It had no dictionary, no phonetics, no notion of where one word ends and the next begins, and no text.

The program’s first part chops the incoming sound into 20-millisecond pieces and sorts them into categories it invents for itself. Its second part tries to guess the next piece from what came before.

Whenever a run of pieces becomes reliably predictable, the program files it away as a unit, which is the closest thing it has to a word.

Nothing about that design is specific to English or even to speech. What makes it unusual is the schedule.

The audio arrives in the order it was recorded, one day at a time, rather than shuffled into batches the way an AI language model is normally trained.

At the end of each simulated day, the program gets a sample of speech from earlier days, weighted toward recent ones. The researchers call it replay.

English has 39 phonemes, the sound units that distinguish bat from pat. Children spend their first year tuning in to the ones their own language uses.

Within roughly 12 to 15 months of simulated time, the program had carved out dedicated categories for nearly all of them.

Across the eight children, the models averaged 37.5 phonemes each. The lowest count came from the household with the least recording.

The order it happened in, sounds before words, is the order infants follow.

Words came after. For the child the paper calls Coral, the checklist said she understood 97 words at 12 months, and her model had 91.

Across all eight children, the models and the children hit the same vocabulary milestones within an average of 22 days of each other, with the worst case 56 days.

The models also reproduced the differences between children, landing close to each child’s own halfway age.

They did not match perfectly. The models tended to start learning words a little late and then catch up faster, so the shape of the curve was only partly right.

The surprise in the paper is what happened when the researchers took replay away.

Coral’s household produced something like 10 million words a year, which is a rich environment by any measure. Hearing all of it once, in order, wasn’t enough. With a single pass the program barely learned words at all.

Around 25 to 30 passes over each day’s material was enough, and more than that added very little.

The researchers link this to what sleep is thought to do for memory. A program replaying audio files is not a sleeping infant, and the paper is careful about that.

The amount of recording mattered just as much. Fed only a tenth of Coral’s daily speech, under an hour a day, the program learned none of the checklist words by 24 months.

At 30% of the day, about two and a half hours, it learned 80 of the 649 checklist words.

At 70% it got about nine-tenths of the way to the full result. The rarer words, it seems, only turn up for a listener who is there most of the day.

One limitation shapes everything else. Household recordings are full of clattering dishes, television, and two people talking at once, and the program was not asked to cope with any of that.

Instead the researchers transcribed all the speech around each child, then had a text-to-speech system read the transcripts back as clean audio.

Each household got a mix of eight adult female voices, with 30% of segments read in a voice matched to the child’s mother.

So the word order, the timing and the vocabulary are real, and the sound isn’t. The acoustic problem a baby actually solves, picking one voice out of a noisy room, was left for later work.

Three of the eight children also had shorter recordings, 14 to 20 months of usable input, so their later trajectories are extrapolated rather than modeled.

The program also sees nothing: no faces, no pointing, no object being handed over, which is a large part of how toddlers work out what a word refers to.

The program learned sounds and words. It did not learn grammar, and whether the same machinery would arrive at sentence structure is the open question the authors name.

The team wants to use the setup for questions nobody can put to a real baby. What would less sleep do, or screen speech instead of a parent’s?

Future studies could also add eye contact and the rest of the social world the cameras captured but the model never saw.

Hadas Raviv, a computational cognitive scientist in Princeton’s psychology department who built the analysis pipeline, framed the goal as the opposite of the usual one in artificial intelligence.

“Unlike much of modern AI, which aims for superhuman performance, we’re trying to understand how humans learn and how that learning gives rise to intelligence,” Raviv said.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

Chelsea were exposed by a traditional No9 in their last outing at Sunderland and Dominic Calvert-Lewin could do the same

Chelsea have a No 9 problem at both ends of the pitch. Up top, Xabi Alonso is struggling to find a solution in the absence of Joao Pedro, who has still not returned to training after weeks out with a thigh problem. And at the back, Alonso’s porous defence appears fallible in the face of the old-school striker’s revival.

Defeat at Sunderland brought accusations that Chelsea’s defence was susceptible to being “bullied”. The primary bully, in this case, was frontman Brian Brobbey, one of the Premier League’s most imposing units. His overpowering of Levi Colwill led to Sunderland’s controversial penalty that proved the difference two weeks ago.

Alonso didn’t agree with such a critique at the time. "Not bullied, I would say that it was a situation that we could have defended better,” he said after the match at the Stadium of Light. But amid Chelsea’s frantic form, questions are being asked whether Alonso’s back line can handle the Premier League at its most physical.

In this regard, Saturday’s test of Leeds provides the ultimate test. There are few teams in the top-flight as physical as Leeds, even if Daniel Farke may sometimes opt to play down his players as “butterflies”, like he did for Jayden Bogle after the right-back avoided a red card for a challenge on Tottenham’s Mathys Tel.

Leeds have won the most duels in the Premier League this season; Farke’s players are not scared of going in for a challenge. That has made them a particularly difficult team to score against, conceding a mere eight goals so far. Chelsea have let in double that tally, with only Arsenal and Brighton conceding fewer than the Whites.

Alonso isn’t only learning of English football’s physical requirement now; his five seasons at Liverpool would have taught him as such. And he knows that if his team continue to be beaten in this department, it will repeatedly cost them in this league regardless of the opposition. “It’s the Premier League. It’s not new,” Alonso said. “It’s not just against Leeds. It will happen next week against Forest, and the next one against Palace. This is the level of the competition.”

Ever the tactician, Alonso does not put full emphasis on brute physicality – even if it is a necessary step to unlocking quality. He continued: “It’s not just the duels – it’s about a football thing or tactical thing. When you have good control, normally you are in better positions to press and duel. When you don’t have that much control, you aren’t in that good position. It’s not just ‘one v one, let’s see what happens’. No, you can help situations to be stronger in duels. After, it’s the individual qualities that help.”

Leeds nevertheless appeared to lean into the criticism in the build-up to their trip down south. They posted a compilation of their best moments against the Blues from over the years, which included its fair share of crunching tackles and Chelsea mistakes. Whether a statement of intent or merely mind games, Alonso said his side was only focusing on themselves. “We focus on not being soft tomorrow and being competitive,” he said. “That’s our goal.”

Like Sunderland, Leeds’ main threat comes in the form of a traditional No 9. Dominic Calvert-Lewin may have been studying tape of Chelsea’s last outing and licking his lips this week, knowing that he has the capacity to cause the same sort of problems as Brobbey.

“We prepare knowing that we have prepared because it’s a player with different qualities but the same kind of profile,” Alonso said. “It’s nothing new. It’s quite common in the Premier League that they have a good No 9.

“Calvert-Lewin is doing well. He played with England. But it is up to us, how we defend, how we compete in those situations, how we attack the last line, how we attack their defenders. The preparation has, as always, we think been good. We are ready for tomorrow.”

Back in fashion, the target man profile is giving Chelsea’s young defence trouble. While Alonso’s arrival at Chelsea coincided with the move away from their chaos of youth, his back line remains fresh-faced. His five first-team centre-backs have an average age of 23, with Maxence Lacroix the oldest at 26. They all came into senior football with the old-school No 9 being just that, and the leap back in time has forced them to adapt.

So far, the learning curve has been apparent. But Alonso doesn’t believe his players lack the tools to deal with what used to be the norm up top.

Asked if centre-backs have forgotten how to defend the traditional No 9, Alonso replied: “No, I don’t think forgotten. But it’s demanding to play against No 9s. They have good movement in the box, out the box. But this is the demand. You can ask the full-backs, they have to play against great wingers in one v one situations. But I don’t think it’s been forgotten.”

Alonso is personally happy to see the revival of the No 9. “It’s good they are back, these type of players,” he said. Whether he still feels the same after the visit of Calvert-Lewin and the rest of Leeds’ hardmen may depend on his players toughening up to the task.

The Couple Created a Giant Sunflower Walkway For Their Multicultural Sun Valley Wedding

Parker Selman and Karan Rai’s love story was born from a sunset. “Our paths crossed a few times before, but the first time we remember speaking was on a roof as we watched the sun go down our senior year at Tufts University,” Parker recalls. A few weeks later, they found themselves alone on a dance floor. “I looked down at my phone to see it was midnight and, therefore, officially my birthday,” Parker says. “I told him he should give me a kiss because it was my birthday, and he did. We were both smitten, and constantly in one another's orbit.” Despite the two lining up jobs on opposite coasts, they quickly realized being practical was not an option, and Karan managed to convince his company to transfer him to New York.

The couple got engaged on a crisp autumn walk around Governors Island—Parker’s favorite spot in the city. She owns and operates a wedding photography business, and as they strolled along, she asked to take a picture of Karan on her medium-format camera with their apartment building and skyline in the background. “While I was snapping the photo, he got down on one knee,” she says, “and I had the privilege of capturing my own proposal.” They rode the ferry back to Manhattan and went to Della’s, a local wine bar, where their families and friends were waiting to celebrate.

Two months later, she proposed back. “While we were walking in Sun Valley, I requested he take a picture of me sitting in the snow with our dog Grumpy,” she says. "I proposed the same way he proposed to me, the moment captured on my Rolleiflex.” Parker used diamonds and emeralds from her late great-grandmother’s engagement ring and gold from Karan’s late grandmother’s earrings to create his signet ring.

Their multi-day wedding unfolded in early September 2026 near Sun Valley, Idaho (where Parker’s family lives). They chose Central Idaho 4-H Camp, located 30 minutes outside of town, for its series of cabins and a large field up in the mountains. “The space was a blank canvas with no sound ordinance,” she says. “Studio Sully transformed the campsite into something magical.”

Going into the planning process, the couple knew they wanted the entire event to feel like a fusion of their cultures. In the days leading up to the Sun Valley celebration, Karan’s parents threw a series of traditional Indian wedding ceremonies and celebrations in his hometown of Pleasanton, California. Once guests arrived in Idaho, they all checked in at The Sun Valley Lodge, where each day the couple had organized group activities.

Festivities kicked off on Friday with a Western-themed welcome party at their favorite après-ski bar, Grumpy’s. Parker wore a tweed corset and skirt by Wiederhoeft accessorized with brown leather Manolo Blahniks and a wooden horse purse by Vin. When the weather cooled down, she tossed on a vintage suede fringe RRL jacket. Karan followed a similar script and wore a Bode suede fringe jacket and One of These Days pants with a painted horse and cowboy detail on the knees. His bandana was made by his mother. “She created a piece inspired by Marie Lichtenberg’s jeweled bandana, utilizing earrings and jewelry of Karan’s grandmothers, mother, and sister,” Parker says.

Guests were encouraged to wear textiles, paisley, and eclectic patterns to evoke a Wild West meets India atmosphere. In one corner, Boise Thread Studio set up shop to embroider initials onto trucker hats, shirts, and bandanas. Meanwhile, guests drank schooners (the name for Grumpy’s beer chalices) on the patio and ate burgers and hot dogs. “Karan and I named our beloved miniature dachshund Grumpy after the bar’s corn dogs, so it is only fitting that we ordered them until they ran out,” Parker says. Her mother welcomed the group with a sunset toast before moving inside. “We took over the electronic jukebox and closed down the restaurant until Karan had to stand up on the booth to usher everyone out the door.”

The next afternoon they hosted a sangeet by the Big Wood River. “The estate has a series of winding paths that lead to the riverside, and we wanted the afternoon portion to feel like a mountain garden party with activities and new discoveries tucked into each corner,” Parker says. The flowing river and live music by Boise-based Ashley Rose Band served as the soundtrack as guests played croquet, horseshoes, and badminton. Near the band, Henna by Sanjana created patterns on guests' hands.

Parker wore Danielle Frankel’s cream Mariela dress featuring two layers of hand-painted wildflowers. “I wanted a long braid with flowers woven in, and Jasmine Burnside helped bring the vision to life,” she says. Karan wore a light blue Jatin Malik sherwani with juttis from his personal collection.

As the sun set, guests were escorted to a clearing where a buffet was set up for dinner, and the dance floor was built around a large willow tree. The vignette was inspired by a 1905 Impressionist painting called Nighttime Festivities that depicts a turn-of-the-century party beneath the trees of a French palace. “I told Melissa [Sully] I wanted the sangeet to be based on this and we became obsessed with bringing the painting to life,” Parker explains. Lights and flowers were strung into the branches that draped overhead. Karan’s parents and Parker’s best friend, Ana, gave speeches before a series of traditional dances were performed by various family members and friends, followed by a lively dance floor courtesy of DJ Anupya.

The morning of the wedding, Parker and Karan got ready with their families and friends. “We are terrible at surprises, and he had seen my dress many times before, so I saw no purpose in a first look,” she says. The couple set off in a convertible (Parker’s hair protected in a vintage scarf from the year she was born), stopping at a vista for photos before arriving at the venue.

For her wedding look, Parker worked with Patricia Voto of One/Of to create a custom gown inspired by Christian Dior’s 1955 Soirée de Lahore. Early on, she knew she wanted something that felt like a combination of Karan and herself. “The original gown was made by the designer two years before his death and was based on a sari with the one-shoulder design and gold embroidery,” she explains. All of the embroidery was done in India, and Parker paired the gown with a custom One/Of veil featuring gold embroidery along the edge, as well as light green shoes by Paula Nadal.

Karan wore a custom Bode tuxedo with a golden sunflower detail, and as a boutonniere, he wore his own emerald kalgi (a traditional Indian headpiece for turbans). “His cufflinks and studs were my late grandfather’s, his cummerbund was unbranded vintage ’70s made in India with embellished sunflowers, and his shoes were suede Hermès.”

Guests arrived to the ceremony dressed in jewel tones and metallics, walking down a pathway with giant sunflowers on either side. Parker and her mother, an amateur florist, had collaborated with Madison of Hart Floral to create the dramatic entrance. “When I spoke of our love for sunflowers and our desire to have them serve as a recurring visual throughout the day, Madison and Melissa joined forces,” Parker explains.

For the ceremony, they stood within a chuppah structure with draped gold chainmail and burgundy sunflower motifs. Karan’s best friend Drew officiated the ceremony, and the fathers of her late friends Alex and Josh read an excerpt by Ovid of the Greek myth of Baucis and Philemon. “Karan’s vows and my own were mirrors of each other. Unknowingly, we made many of the same references, and we each ended by quoting the story of Baucis and Philemon, who, when they died, received the honor of becoming intertwined as trees so they could live on forever.”

Coming from mixed religious backgrounds (Karan’s parents are Sikh and Hindu and Parker’s are Episcopalian and Jewish), the couple chose to honor both. “Karan arrived on horseback as a nod to traditional Punjabi weddings. St. Christopher medallions were placed on each program, and my sister, Alison, shared a short speech about the patron saint of travelers, the namesake of the church we attended as children, and spoke about the new journey Karan and I were embarking upon,” Parker says. “My sister-in-law, Kareena, shared a speech about the jaimala ceremony as a nod to the Hindu wedding tradition, and spoke about the importance of seeing each other as equals.” Karan’s maternal uncle led the group in a Sikh Ardas prayer, and the ceremony ended with the Jewish tradition of the breaking of the glass. As the couple exited, guests threw dried bougainvillea from Karan’s family garden in Chandigarh.

Cocktail hour kicked off with oysters shipped from Island Creek Oyster Co. (the site of the couple’s first two dates). “We really wanted textiles to be an important decor motif throughout the celebration,” Parker says, noting that there was leveled seating overlaid with rugs, a rug-covered bar, and a rug-covered makeshift bandstand. Later on, their sisters shared speeches, and the couple danced to “Can’t Take My Eyes Off You,” then launching into “Dancing in the Moonlight” as tambourines were passed to guests. “Guests followed Karan and I through the field to reveal the tent, set among the cabins.”

The band continued to play as guests entered the tent and danced. At each seat, Parker and Karan placed a letter explaining what their friendship meant to the couple. After the buzz died down, Parker’s father opened the evening with a speech. “We then had a family-style dinner of our favorite bites: kale salad, focaccia, Idaho trout, pesto gnocchi, and vodka meatballs,” she says.

The couple snuck away to cut the cake—a 30-inch round flourless chocolate confection covered in raspberries—which was passed out on the outskirts of the dance floor. Indian sweets called ladoos were also passed around.

After a full week of wedding events between California for the traditional Indian celebration and four days in Sun Valley, the two were understandably exhausted. “It took weeks to recover, but the whole time all we could talk about was how deeply happy we were.”

10 Best Easter Eggs in GTA 6

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Aden Carter is a Contributor at DualShockers who has been covering games professionally since 2020. He specializes in lists and guides, with a focus on RPGs, horror games, survival horror, Soulslikes, cozy/sim games, and action-adventure titles. Before joining DualShockers, Aden contributed to GameRant, XDA, Gamepur, I

The most common thing for any game developer to do is to make nods to different franchises in their game. Affectionately called easter eggs, these little details are meant to be surprises for players who love various other franchises.

Whether you are a fan of games, television shows, or movies, there’s plenty to discover in Grand Theft Auto 6. Rockstar has taken the time to hide trophies all around Leonida for those who are diligent enough to go searching for them. Even 'Weird Al' Yankovic is hiding in this game somewhere.

Have you found Dexter’s apartment? Maybe you have gone searching for the literal easter egg. Either way, these are amazing nods to other franchises in the mighty GTA 6.

Dexter is a very popular television series about a vigilante serial killer. This serial killer just so happens to live in Miami, which just so happens to be the city that Vice City is based on. What a coincidence!

It just so happens that Rockstar noticed this connection and decided to add a few nods to the series in GTA 6, with the first of them being Dexter’s apartment, which is located on Washington Island.

Of course, you won’t be able to reach this area right away, but once you get access to the map, you can head straight there to check it out and pay tribute to the television series. You can also find some tools on the roof.

Bigfoot has decided to make his way out of the northern forests and headed south to the marshlands of Leonida. It’s a bit weird that he decided to make this island paradise his home, but I won’t question that.

If you progress through the story of GTA 6, there is a mission that will set you on the path to discovering Bigfoot, but he is easy to overlook, as he runs away rather quickly once you get near him.

I barely caught a glimpse of him, but chased him down and caught a photo before he mysteriously disappeared into the marsh. I have no idea if I will see him again, but it was cool to catch a glimpse of this mysterious figure.

After Grand Theft Auto 5's release, the Mount Chiliad mystery was one of the most talked about easter eggs out there. Here's what we know today.

Some developers make nods to different games or pop culture, but Rockstar takes the time to put a literal easter egg in its game. That’s right, if you go to the correct spot on the map, you can find a nest with literal easter eggs in it.

The location in question is a radio tower in South Vice Dale County. The radio tower takes multiple minutes to climb, but the payoff is definitely worth it, as you find a nest full of easter eggs at the top.

While I won’t spoil what happens when you throw one, it does involve a cutscene, and you should definitely take the time to visit the location and watch it unfold.

One of the most memorable missions in GTA 5 is when Michael’s boat gets stolen and you need to chase down the people responsible. Unfortunately, Michael’s boat doesn’t get returned, but we now know the whereabouts of it, and it turns out that it landed in Jason’s yard.

Next to Jason’s house, there is a run-down boat that looks similar to Michael’s. People have pointed this out for a while, but there was no confirmation until now.

Since the game is officially out, you can check out the boat, and you can even climb on top of it. Once there, you can use your phone’s camera to check out the display. Doing so will allow you to see a display that shows part of the GTA 5 map, seemingly confirming that this is Michael’s boat.

Have you ever heard of a movie called Island Claws? If not, you aren’t the only one, but there is a nod to this film in GTA 6. The movie is from the 1980s, and it is about a scientist who genetically modifies crabs to make them grow larger to solve world hunger.

Unfortunately, this doesn’t work out very well, and a similar thing has transpired in Leonida. In Pelican Valley, you can find a trail of blood that leads to a shack. Inside the shack, you can find information about failed experiments on crabs.

Along with this, Jason will get attacked by a giant crab that jumps down on him from the ceiling. It is a rather large easter egg that is meant to pay homage to the classic independent film. Good job doing your research Rockstar.

One of the things that people loved to do in Grand Theft Auto 5 was break into the military base, and the go-to means of breaking into the base was via the hill jump just outside the fence along the highway.

Well, there is another military base in GTA 6 and it is located near where you start the game. It is called Mile Zero Air Station, and it has a convenient hill just outside the fence that you can use to jump into the base just like in GTA 5.

There is no way that this is a coincidence because the hill is an obvious jumping point. Thank you Rockstar for including an obvious homage to the previous game and the silly antics players got into.

From drawings in a mental hospital to TV and radio commercials, these are nine Rockstar Games Easter Eggs that hint at a shared universe.

The most common meme that has ever been spread about a video game is probably the, “We got [insert item here] before GTA 6.” It popped up every single time something major happened before the release of the game. Well it can’t be said anymore since the game is out.

With that being said, the meme will probably become about either Elder Scrolls 6 or GTA 7, or maybe even RAC 7. You might be wondering what that is. Well, if you scroll through your phone’s Snapmatic app, you can find a post about a game called Barnyard Blue: Hot Pursuit.

The post reads, “Barnyard Blue video game before Race and Chase 7 is crazy.” There is even a comment that reads, “We really got Barnyard Blue games before RAC 7.” This is, of course, a jab at the classic meme that appeared for so many years.

The X-Men actually exist in the Grand Theft Auto universe, except they don’t exist in the same way as you know them. Instead of being called the X-Men, they are referred to as the RX-Men. A strange choice, but one that makes it obvious that it is a reference to the classic hero team.

There are various magazines that you can find in the different convenience stores around Leonida, but you can reliably find this magazine in the 24-7 supermarket in northern Key Lento.

In this story, you can find a copy of RX-Men issue 13 titled, “Paging Dr. Placebo.” It features GTA renditions of different heroes and villains. There’s also collectible cards for these characters sprinkled around Leonida.

Speaking of trading cards, you can find various wrestling trading cards across Leonida. This is an obvious easter egg to the various wrestling trading cards that already exist. The wrestling company is called B.A.M, and it stands for Backyard American Mania.

Florida has a rather large wrestling scene, with the WWE training facility being in Orlando. On top of this, various famous wrestlers live or lived in Florida.

To go even further, the acronym of B.A.M. can also be related to Bird Azarian Memorial Training Center, which is located in Illinois, but is a wrestling training center. Odds are, it is just a reference to WWE wrestling.

Early in the game, Jason and Cal sit down to play some games on the JoyStation, with the system itself being an obvious reference to the PlayStation 5. However, that isn’t the whole easter egg, as the game that is played is called Tenebrae Lux.

Tenebrae Lux is a game themed after the movie called The Lighthouse, which features both Robert Pattinson and Willem Dafoe. This is awesome, but there is yet one more easter egg hidden here.

The name Tenebrae Lux is Latin for the term “light-dark.” This happens to be the exact translation of the French term Clair Obscur, which is one of the greatest games to have been released in recent years and a game that took the world by storm.

AI adoption could disproportionately harm Black and Latino workers

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It has become increasingly clear that the AI revolution is not just poised to upend white collar jobs, despite what public discourse might suggest. While entry level roles are, in fact, on the decline in tech and finance—and employers continue to use AI as a rationale for layoffs—there is ample evidence that rapid AI adoption could interrupt pathways for workers without college degrees. 

The effects of this upheaval will be uneven, which means certain demographic groups are more likely to be impacted. Some of the jobs that have been deemed “highly exposed” to AI, including clerical and administrative roles, are largely dominated by women, and a new report from the McKinsey Institute for Economic Mobility posits that Black and Latino workers could also face major consequences.

The report finds that Black and Latino workers are overrepresented in the sectors where automation and AI adoption will supplant work altogether, rather than augment work. While the overall rate of AI adoption is relatively stable across different demographics, Black and Latino workers will be affected more acutely by job displacement. Across working hours that are impacted by AI adoption, 40% of hours performed by Black workers and 41% by Latino workers will be displaced (as compared to 37% among white workers.) 

That may not seem like a huge gap. But as the report points out, many of the jobs that are most exposed to AI have historically been pathways to the middle class, making them crucial drivers of social mobility across communities that have faced systemic barriers to entry. Black workers, in particular, dominate six jobs that are likely to see the biggest decline in labor demand, according to McKinsey, including postal mail carriers and customer service representatives; Latino workers are also especially likely to hold some of those jobs, namely dishwashers and hand packers. 

Black and Latino workers—along with those in rural regions—are also much more likely to be in jobs that involve physical labor, which are more vulnerable to being automated through robotics and other AI-enabled systems. On the whole, lower wage workers will face the greatest impact and are 7.6 times as likely to need to transition into a new job when compared to higher wage workers; people without a college degree are also 1.8 times more likely to need to do the same. 

Meanwhile, white workers are better positioned to both avoid this level of AI disruption and actually benefit from AI adoption in a number of ways. It’s not just that they’re more likely to hold jobs where AI will likely enhance their work, boosting productivity rather than replacing employees outright. When compared to their Black and Latino counterparts, white workers are also 30-50% more likely to work in the industries where private capital will be deployed, yielding more jobs and wealth creation, the McKinsey report states. 

That means white workers already have more of a foothold in jobs involving robotics or semiconductors or biotech, setting them up for success as hundreds of billions of dollars pour into those industries.(Look no further than the Anthropic IPO this week, which has created thousands of millionaires.)  In other words: Without broader policy changes or reskilling initiatives, many Black and Latino workers may be both forced out of their jobs and locked out of opportunities to cash in on the AI boom.
