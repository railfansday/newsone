Amazon surges launches with rival SpaceX after ULA fails to deliver

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon has hundreds of satellites in storage waiting for rides to space.

A European Ariane 6 launcher propelled 36 satellites into orbit for the Amazon Leo broadband constellation Tuesday, continuing the network’s build-out after 40 satellites were lost earlier this month during a failure of United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket.

The launch was an important rebound for Amazon, bringing the Amazon Leo fleet to 432 satellites as the company seeks to fill the void left by the grounding of ULA’s Vulcan rocket, Amazon’s primary launch provider. Now, an Amazon official tells Ars it is accelerating several Falcon 9 launches the company has purchased from SpaceX, which also owns the Starlink satellite network that Amazon seeks to dethrone.

The next Falcon 9 launch for Amazon Leo is now planned for December, Amazon said, moved forward from its previous launch date next year. Several more Falcon 9s are scheduled to launch Amazon Leo satellites in rapid succession after the December flight.

Amazon cast a wide net when it started signing deals for launch services five years ago, securing rides on 108 flights with every available medium- or heavy-lift rocket in the West to place a fleet of 3,232 satellites into low-Earth orbit. As of Tuesday, Amazon and its launch partners have completed 17 of those flights, with one mission failure.

Executives from Amazon initially bought up capacity from US-based United Launch Alliance, Europe’s Arianespace, and Blue Origin. Nearly all of those launch contracts depended on rockets still in development: ULA’s Vulcan, Europe’s Ariane 6, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn.

Each of the launch vehicles faced development delays, as all new rockets do. New Glenn has been grounded since May after an explosion on Blue Origin’s launch pad in Florida. ULA’s Vulcan will be sidelined for months after misfiring during a mission with Amazon satellites November 15.

Amazon started booking launches in 2023 with SpaceX. Falcon 9 successfully launched its first three missions for Amazon last year, each with 24 satellites. Amazon soon reserved 10 more flights on SpaceX’s Falcon 9 rocket as delays mounted with Amazon Leo’s other launch providers, especially ULA and Blue Origin.

The relationship between Amazon and SpaceX is a peculiar one. Amazon aims to catch up to SpaceX’s Starlink network, now boasting more than 11,000 satellites, to bring competition to the satellite broadband market. Federal regulators approved Amazon Leo’s second-generation constellation earlier this year, an addition that will bring the fleet to more than 7,000 satellites in the 2030s. Amazon also plans a direct-to-device service, joining SpaceX and others in the pursuit of consumer demand for mobile connectivity.

But Starlink is far ahead. SpaceX has repurposed the Starlink satellite design for use on several US military programs, and Starlink launches on Falcon 9 are winding down as SpaceX’s Starship enters service. Starship is capable of launching heavier, more powerful Starlink satellites, a culmination that will only widen the gap with Amazon and other competitors in the satellite Internet business.

Starlink’s move to Starship opens up launch slots on Falcon 9, allowing Amazon to accelerate its launches with SpaceX in the near term. But SpaceX doesn’t plan to keep Falcon 9 around for long. The company has reportedly stopped accepting commercial Falcon 9 bookings after 2028.

That means Amazon Leo needs its other launch providers to step up. Arianespace is doing so, with four successful Ariane 6 flights for Amazon this year. Amazon reserved six additional flights with Arianespace in September as Ariane 6 proved its mettle.

But there are questions about Europe’s ability to ramp up production of Ariane 6 rockets, which are single-use and not reusable like Falcon 9 and New Glenn. Europe’s rocket factories are sized to support a launch cadence of up to nine or 10 Ariane 6 flights per year. Arianespace will likely reach six Ariane 6 flights this year, with one more remaining before the end of December.

European taxpayers paid for development of the Ariane 6 and the industrial capacity to build the rockets, and they continue to subsidize each Ariane 6 flight, whether it carries a European government payload or commercial satellites for a large American corporation like Amazon.

European government ministers are mulling proposals to increase Ariane 6 production to meet surging demand from commercial users and Europe’s internal space programs. A decision could come next month, but a ramp-up in launch cadence won’t happen overnight.

Amazon paid United Launch Alliance hundreds of millions of dollars for expansion at Cape Canaveral, Florida, to prepare for as many as 25 Vulcan launches per year. Such a launch cadence is a long way off for ULA, which has now launched Vulcan five times (with one failure) in its three years of service.

Blue Origin, owned by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos, could resume New Glenn flights early next year after completing a launch pad rebuild. It is not clear when Amazon might begin launching on New Glenn, but finding slots in the rocket’s launch schedule could be difficult as Blue Origin prioritizes work for NASA’s Artemis lunar program.

In the meantime, SpaceX will offer Amazon its most assured rides to space. Falcon 9s can launch from Cape Canaveral every few days, and Amazon is ready to tap into that capability with hundreds of satellites in storage waiting to go to orbit.

Hang most of your laundry on a folding rack or a line, since an electric dryer costs about 40 cents a load at average U.S. electricity prices. A $30 rack pays for itself after about 75 loads you hang instead, and you can still keep the dryer for towels, sheets, and rainy weeks.

The average electric dryer uses just over 690 kilowatt-hours of electricity a year, a field study the Northwest Energy Efficiency Alliance published in 2026 found. At the average U.S. residential rate of 18.16 cents per kilowatt-hour in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, that comes to about $125 a year.

The same study found the average household runs 307 full drying cycles a year, so each load costs about 40 cents to dry, and a load you hang costs nothing. A gas dryer costs less to run than an electric one, so hanging its loads saves less.

At 40 cents a load, a couple who wash seven loads a week and hang five of them save $2 a week, or $104 a year, and cut their drying bill by more than 70%. Their $30 folding rack pays for itself in 15 weeks.

You don’t need a yard. A folding rack stands in a corner, a wall-mounted rack folds flat when it’s empty, and shirts dry on hangers hooked over the shower rod. A retractable line can stretch across a tub or a balcony, but check your lease or homeowners association’s rules for a ban on outdoor clotheslines before you hang one outside.

If you hang towels too, take them down while they’re still a little damp, then soften them with 10 minutes in the dryer. Air-drying also spares your clothes the dryer’s heat, which helps them last longer.

For the loads you still dry, clean the lint screen every time and use the moisture-sensor or auto-dry setting instead of a timer, so the dryer stops once the clothes are dry. Run those loads back to back, too, while the drum still holds the last load’s heat.

Hang workout clothes and other lightweight synthetics first, since they dry faster than towels or jeans. At 40 cents a load, every weekly load you hang instead of drying saves about $21 a year.

Wildfires Closed 189 Miles of the Pacific Crest Trail in 2026: What 2027 Hikers Need To Know

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

According to the end-of-year wildfire recap from the Pacific Crest Trail Association (PCTA), seven fires closed 189 miles of the trail in 2026, burning directly across six miles of the trail itself.

California experienced a below-average fire year, while Oregon suffered a record-setting fire year. However, the fires in Oregon largely avoided the PCT corridor. In California, 329,000 acres burned, with the PCT seeing brief closures from the Corral and Talbot fires totaling 32.5 miles of trail.

In Oregon, over 2 million acres burned, but only 6.1 miles of trail were closed from the Badger Butte and Lemiti fires in Mount Hood National Forest.

The Badger Butte Fire in Oregon. Photo via PCTA

Washington, in contrast, experienced the brunt of this year’s closures, especially later into the summer. Trail crews are still working to evaluate the extent of the damage along the trail.

Statewide, over 650,000 acres burned, leading to 150 miles of closed trail from the Ptarmigan, Miners, Three Queens, King, and Sisi fires. Most importantly, the Ptarmigan Fire led to the closure of the northernmost 50 miles of trail, including the Northern Terminus.

Fire has occurred across landscapes along the PCT for thousands of years, often burning at low intensity every few years to a few decades. While low-intensity burns clear brush, replenish soil nutrients, and support new growth, burned trail corridors take years to recover.

Impacts from the older Dixie and Bear fires continue to drop dead timber and sprout heavy brush each year. Across 2025 and 2026, PCTA staff and volunteers cut 3,100 fallen logs in the Bear Fire scar alone, part of more than 23,000 downed trees cleared across the entire PCT since 2024.

Anyone mapping out a 2027 hike on the PCT should prepare for hazards that last well beyond the timeline of an active fire. Closure orders sometimes extend through winter and into the next season, so hikers need to check the PCTA closures page when making plans. Inside these scars, burned timber can topple without warning, especially from wind, making it unsafe to camp or take long breaks under dead trees.

Burned timber is prone to toppling. Photo via Katie Jackson

Winter weather can also reshape recent burn scars before summer arrives. Scorched slopes are unstable, making them much more prone to landslides. Heavy winter rains could still wash out additional sections before the start of the 2027 hiking season.

Hikers should expect downed trees, overgrown brush, and missing or rough tread. Lost forest canopy also brings more sun exposure and can change water sources, so carrying extra water and checking updated water reports will still be very important.

Even with the ill-timed, unfortunately placed closures, a whole group of 2026 hikers still put together rewarding seasons by staying flexible, taking detours, or planning return trips for the missing miles.

“We got off trail at Snoqualmie in August due to the fires. Our only free weekend in October had snow in the forecast, but we went for it! Feeling incredibly lucky to get to the border this year. 2426 miles, what a journey!”  Photo and quote via Brisket & Lamb

Bringing that same adaptability to the Pacific Crest Trail in 2027 will make navigating the post-fire terrain much easier.
