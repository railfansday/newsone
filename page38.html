Despite posting a solid earnings beat, Elastic sees its stock slide on rough guidance

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Elastic N.V.’s stock was trending lower in late trading today after the enterprise search software firm reported earnings and revenue that beat Wall Street’s expectations but disappointed with guidance that came in below forecasts.

The company reported second-quarter earnings before certain costs such as stock compensation of 94 cents per share, easily beating the analyst consensus estimate of 81 cents. Revenue rose 17%, to $496 million, surpassing the Street’s target of $487 million.

The results helped Elastic bounce back into profitability. Its net income for the quarter came to $171 million, rebounding from a loss of $51.3 million in the same period one year ago.

However, investors were less than impressed with the company’s guidance. For the quarter in progress, Elastic said it’s looking for earnings of between 90 cents and 92 cents per share on sales of $507.5 million to $508.5 million. That’s some way below the Street’s targets of 95 cents in earnings and $517.6 million in revenue. The company is not helped by the weak Euro, with currency conversions likely to cause a $4.5 million headwind on revenue in the quarter.

The full-year picture is not much better, as Elastic forecast annual sales of between $2 billion and $2.01 billion compared with the Street’s model of $2.01 billion.

Netherlands-based Elastic is one of Europe’s most successful software companies. It’s the developer of the popular open-source enterprise search platform Elasticsearch, which is used by thousands of businesses all over the world. The base platform is free to download and use, with Elastic making money by selling a special cloud-hosted version that ships with premium features not available in the open edition. The company also sells application observability and security tools.

In the last couple of years, Elastic has tried to cash in on the artificial intelligence boom by positioning Elasticsearch as a useful cog in the wheel that feeds real-time data into large language models to provide them with business context. A key capability is Elastic Context Engine, which gathers, structures, and serves business-specific context to AI agents so they can make accurate decisions with fewer tokens and lower latency.

More recently, the company has built AI-native products including AlertZero, which enables enterprises to orchestrate teams of autonomous security agents that automate tasks around data protection and threat hunting.

Elastic Chief Executive Ash Kulkarni (pictured) said he’s excited about the potential of those new capabilities to drive significant revenue over time. “Elastic delivered another strong quarter, beating guidance across all key metrics,” he said. “Our strong results reflect expanding customer adoption and position us well to continue our strong execution in the back half of fiscal 2027.”

Unfortunately for Kulkarni, investors didn’t seem to share his enthusiasm. The market was clearly expecting trouble on the horizon in the hours before the company published its report, as its stock declined 8% during regular trading. Once the results were out, it fell another 8% after-hours. That means Elastic’s stock is now up just over 1% in the year to date.

Elastic’s stock had rallied more than 150% between April and October, but virtually all of those gains have now been erased. Prior to today, the shares had already lost more than 20% this month amid broader concerns about the AI industry. Some investors continue to worry about the potential of AI technology to challenge the business models of software companies.

Marvel Shares First Image of Jessica Jones 'Jungle Costume' For New Appearance

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A new series will send Jessica Jones on a tour of the Marvel Universe, distancing the acerbic private eye-cum-superhero from her traditional stomping ground of New York City. Now, Marvel has revealed Jess' new costume for the upcoming adventure.

Jessica Jones turns twenty-five this year, first debuting in 2001's Alias #1 by Brian Michael Bendis and Michael Gaydos. Played by Krysten Ritter in the Jessica Jones TV show, the character typically stalks around New York in a leather jacket, jeans and boots.

However, to celebrate Jessica Jones' anniversary, Marvel's upcoming Jessica Jones: Alias will deliberately hurl her out of her comfort zone, forcing the urban hero to survive in some of its most inhospitable locations...

Coming December 2026, Jessica Jones: Alias #1 reunites Bendis and Gaydos for a new adventure with their iconic creation. Now, in a new interview with Marvel, Bendis reveals the plan to force Jessica out of her comfort zone, theming the series on the idea that "Jessica can't get home." The hero's newest adventure will see her stranded in locations including the Savage Land, space and Doctor Doom's Latveria.

Michael Gaydos' cover for Jessica Jones: Alias #3 shows Jessica Jones in a leopard-print costume, standing beside a roaring tiger. In Marvel lore, the Savage Land is a hidden jungle in the depths of Antarctica, where dinosaurs rule and heroes including Kevin Plunder's Ka-Zar and Shanna the She-Devil hold sway.

The series is likely to push Jessica to the limit, as her adventures prevent her from seeing her family - husband Luke Cage, daughter Danielle and recently adopted son Joe (the biological son of longtime enemy the Purple Man.)

The family are shown together in a preview of the issue (below), with Jessica also shown remonstrating with a Doombot and crashing a meeting of New York's crime bosses, including Mr. Negative, the Owl, the White Rabbit and the Rose - a major figure in Bendis' beloved Daredevil run.

The preview also shows Jessica being approached by a new client, likely beginning her voyage across Marvel lore. Speaking to Marvel, Bendis says that Jessica's journey will allow him to visit past stories and characters:

I got excited about that idea because, yes, it's a struggle for the character, but it also allows me to look around the Marvel Universe, which I’ve been a part of, on and off, for many years. I kind of used the character to look around and observe, or revisit certain places that I had not been able to visit in a while.

Bendis also wants the anniversary return to be a celebration of the character and her journey so far, praising what other creative teams have done with the character in the years since her debut.

What I wanted to do was not only celebrate everything we did when we started, but to my complete delight, Jessica has been in the hands of some of my absolute favorite writers over the years: Kelly Thompson, Gail Simone, Sam Humphries. It has just been a delight to watch her being shepherded so carefully. When she debuted, she was reflecting a unique flavor, and now, she has evolved with the times.

What I wanted to do was tell a story that wasn't like, "Hey, let's flash back to that story we never told you from 25 years ago," but be very forward in momentum about who Jessica is today, what she wants, what she's fearful of losing, and what she can do. This is a Jessica whose husband was just Mayor of New York. They're now parents with very unique children, but they also want to continue their journey as heroes and investigators. I was eager to revisit the characters, having gone from where Luke and Jessica were emotionally in the first issue of Alias to now, and it just reflects how much they've grown and how much they've grown together.

The idea of characters getting specific costumes for adventures set in the Savage Land is an old one, applied nowhere more famously than Rogue. Debuting in 1990's Uncanny X-Men #269, Rogue's costume was shredded, giving her what many fans consider one of her most iconic looks.

Marvel has revisited the look multiple times - most recently in 2025's Rogue: The Savage Land - with variant covers and statues frequently celebrating the design. However, the costume is controversial with many fans, acting as a locus for debates around objectification in superhero media.

While Jessica Jones has been a sexual character since Alias - which was published under Marvel's mature reader MAX imprint - she's also a character defined by agency and significant trauma around the subject. Her costume design has been a major aspect of her characterization since the very beginning, whether as a PI or during her flashback appearances as Jewel and Knightress.

It's likely that Jessica Jones' new look will be used to critique or parody how Marvel has handled Rogue's Savage Land redesign, with Rogue as a character who quickly became acclimatized to the Savage Land, while Bendis states, "I don't think there's any place on the planet that Jessica wants to be in less."

Coming later this year, Marvel will celebrate Jessica Jones' anniversary with a new series designed to push her as far outside her comfort zone as possible.

The 2025 tax law that made opportunity zones permanent set those terms, and they start on Jan. 1, 2027, when the Treasury’s new zones take effect.

After you sell stock, a business, or property at a gain, you have 180 days to put that gain into a qualified opportunity fund, which invests in designated low-income areas called opportunity zones. What Does Trail Magic Look Like Around the World? These 5 Stories Show Us

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The snacks at the hut made Matt's day, despite the mucky conditions (Photo: Matt Jordan)

On the 2024 Highlander UAE, a multi-day 40-mile hike through the Hajar Mountains, I struggled far more than I had expected. The terrain was brutal, with steep descents over sharp, rocky granite tracks along the 6,345-foot Jebel Jais summit. My heavy pack weighed me down, and I could feel every stone beneath my boots. By day three, my two big toes hurt so much that on one challenging descent, I collapsed and couldn’t take another step.

That’s when a local Emirati man came walking up the trail behind me. He saw that I was struggling and stopped to pull out a roll of tape from his bag. Then, he peeled off my very stinky hiking socks without a second thought, taped up my toes, and bandaged my knees. He even carried my pack for several miles, guiding me down the remaining steep sections.

This stranger had no reason to go out of his way to help me, and I know I could not have finished that day without him. Hikers call this kind of experience “trail magic.” The Appalachian Trail Conservancy defines the term as any instance of finding what you need most when you least expect it; experiencing something rare, extraordinary, or inspiring in nature, or encountering unexpected acts of generosity that restore one’s faith in humanity.

Trail magic is often associated with Appalachian Trail history from the early 1980s. However, after my experience in the UAE, I started wondering what it looks like elsewhere. So, here are five stories from other hikers who have experienced trail magic in one form or another around the world.

Matt Jordan, a hiking and backpacking guide with Intrepid Travel, was walking New Zealand’s 1,881-mile Te Araroa Trail southbound in 2023 when he hit the muddy Longwood Forest in Fiordland. After a miserable day of rain and bog, he pushed on toward Turnbull’s Hut, one of the last backcountry huts on that segment. He’d heard that sometimes, free supplies were stored there for hikers passing through. As he approached, he prayed it hadn’t already all been taken by the time he got there.

“Thankfully it hadn’t,” he says. When he opened the store area, he found a cache of goodies.

“A local man who hikes in supplies, hauls out trash, and maintains the hut about twice a month leaves coolers stocked with beer, soda, and candy bars,” Jordan says. Excitedly, he grabbed a drink, then promptly slipped in the mud and fell on his backside. After days of awful weather conditions, though, that didn’t stop him from laughing out loud.

“For me, the gesture was about more than a cold drink. It was a reminder of the generosity of strangers and the sense of community that makes long-distance hikes unforgettable worldwide.”

Meeta Singh Rao, a hiking guide in India, says some of her best trail magic came from shepherds who spend months in the high mountain pastures of the Indian Himalayas. In October 2022, she was leading a trek to Nawali Bugyal in Uttarakhand when the weather turned. Heavy rain began to fall, and strong winds blew. The mules carrying their gear were also lost, and camp was miles away.

The group came across a shepherd with his flock. He heard where they were headed, and offered them shelter in his channi, a mountain hut made of stone, mud, and plastic sheets. “When rainwater leaked through the roof, he walked out into the downpour to rearrange the plastic so we stayed dry,” says Rao.

When Rao offered to pay, the shepherd gently refused, saying they were his guests. “A couple with so little of their own opened their home to us, giving us a feeling of belonging in the mountains,” she adds.

Kaitlyn Bussell has walked four Caminos. But her second, the 62-mile Camino Inglés in September 2023 through Galicia, was particularly challenging. The rain was constant, few hostels and cafés were open in the fall, and she walked long stretches without seeing another pilgrim.

On the second day, wet and ready for a break, she ducked into the first bar she found for a café con leche and to charge her phone. When she went to pay, the young woman behind the counter told her the coffee was free and handed her some water, prayer cards and a small Camino keepsake.

“It was a small thing, but it came at exactly the right moment,” Bussell recalls. “After days of rain, it lifted my spirits and made me realize that this pilgrimage is as much about the people you meet as it is the miles you walk.”

On Japan’s Shikoku 88 Temples Pilgrimage route, Lauren Roerick and her brother were leaving one of the temples when she spotted a sign offering matcha tea ceremonies for pilgrims. They initially walked away unsure whether they would be intruding but returned and decided to try it anyway.

“A Japanese man welcomed us inside where two women in kimonos were preparing and serving matcha under his instruction,” adds Roerick. “To make us feel welcome, he called a friend and had her on speakerphone, translating the whole ceremony from Japanese into English.” Lauren and her brother learned about the tradition, tried making tea themselves, and ate small Japanese sweets.

“It was proof that trail magic isn’t always a bottle of water or a helping hand,” says Roerick. “Sometimes a stranger opens his door, works around a language barrier, and shares a bit of culture with people passing through.”

On a windy hike to Matterhorn Base Camp, Libera Pagano, tour director with EF Adventures, noticed an older woman struggling. The altitude made her dizzy, her legs were shaking, and her friend had walked far ahead. “I offered to sit with the woman while she rested behind a large rock and we started chatting,” says Pagano.

That’s when Pagano learned it was the woman’s 86th birthday, and the hike was a present she’d given herself. They stayed together, and when the woman felt ready, they walked slowly until they caught up with her companion. When the group met back at basecamp, the woman told Pagano that without her help, she would never have made it to the summit.

“I thought nothing of what I had done,” says Pagano. “But for that woman, it was the difference between giving up and fulfilling her birthday wish.”

I never learned the name of the man who bandaged my toes in the Hajar Mountains, and I probably never will. But I think about him whenever I struggle on a trail or see another hiker limping.

All these stories have one thing in common—human compassion. Sometimes that is stopping to help a stranger in need, sharing what little you have, or leaving a cache of supplies for a hiker you’ll probably never meet. These gestures don’t have to be grand or cost much, but they can make an enormous difference and are an easy thing to pay forward so everyone experiences a little magic outdoors.

Scientists Uncover Why a Common Nutrient Can Be Dangerous for Blood Vessels

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that dietary phosphate may influence blood vessel damage and lifespan, with important implications for people with chronic kidney disease.

A newly identified role for a protein in the kidneys could help explain why excess phosphate in the blood contributes to hardened arteries and shorter lifespans. Researchers at the University of Colorado Anschutz School of Medicine found that animals lacking this protein developed more calcium deposits in their blood vessels and died earlier, particularly when consuming a diet high in phosphate.

The study, published in October 2026, identified TMEM174 as an important regulator of blood phosphate levels. Reducing dietary phosphate lessened some of the harmful effects in animals deficient in the protein. The findings point to a potential treatment target for chronic kidney disease (CKD), a condition that makes it difficult for the body to eliminate excess phosphate.

The kidneys continuously filter phosphate from the bloodstream, removing excess amounts through urine while retaining enough to support essential functions such as bone formation. A protein called NPT2A helps maintain this balance by reabsorbing approximately three-quarters of the filtered phosphate and returning it to the blood.

The researchers discovered that TMEM174 acts as a counterbalance to NPT2A. By binding to NPT2A and reducing its activity, TMEM174 helps prevent excessive phosphate reabsorption. In animal models lacking TMEM174, phosphate regulation became disrupted, vascular calcification increased, and lifespan was significantly reduced.

“We don’t fully understand how yet, but somehow TMEM174 is destabilizing NPT2A,” says Makoto Miyazaki, PhD, a research professor in the Division of Renal Diseases and Hypertension and senior author of the study. “And without enough TMEM174, homeostasis is disrupted.”

Phosphorus is an essential nutrient found in meat, fish, dairy products, nuts, legumes, and whole grains. It is also commonly added to processed foods and beverages to improve flavor and extend shelf life. Unlike many naturally occurring phosphorus compounds, phosphate additives are generally absorbed more efficiently by the digestive system.

Although the body needs phosphorus, persistently elevated blood phosphate (hyperphosphatemia) can damage the cardiovascular system. Excess phosphate can cause smooth muscle cells in blood vessel walls to develop characteristics of bone-forming cells. These altered cells promote the accumulation of calcium phosphate crystals, causing blood vessels to stiffen (or calcify).

Healthy arteries expand and contract in response to changing blood flow. Calcification reduces that flexibility, contributing to vascular aging and increasing cardiovascular risks. In the new study, the combination of TMEM174 deficiency and a phosphate-rich diet accelerated this process in animals.

“Phosphate is really underestimated in terms of longevity in all mammals. The higher the concentration of phosphate in the blood, the shorter the lifespan,” says Miyazaki.

Elevated blood phosphate is relatively uncommon in the general population because healthy kidneys can usually remove the excess. In people with CKD, however, declining kidney function reduces phosphate excretion, allowing the mineral to accumulate in the bloodstream.

This impaired phosphate regulation can contribute to vascular calcification, which is particularly common in people with kidney disease. The newly discovered role of TMEM174 suggests another potential source of vulnerability. Although TMEM174 deficiency appears to be primarily genetic rather than caused by CKD, researchers suspect that people affected by both conditions could face additional cardiovascular risks.

The experiments established a connection between TMEM174 deficiency, abnormal phosphate regulation, vascular calcification, and reduced survival in animal models. Whether differences in TMEM174 activity have comparable effects on cardiovascular health or lifespan in humans remains to be determined.

The findings suggest that treatments affecting TMEM174 or its interaction with NPT2A might help the kidneys eliminate more phosphate instead of returning it to the bloodstream. Such an approach could eventually provide another way to manage phosphate levels in people with CKD.

Dietary changes may also help. The researchers found that reducing phosphate intake partially protected animals lacking TMEM174 from harmful effects. Miyazaki notes that limiting phosphate-rich processed foods, including fast food, deli meats, and sodas, could be beneficial. However, phosphate occurs naturally in so many foods that dietary restrictions alone may be insufficient, particularly for people with impaired kidney function.

Future research will examine how TMEM174 interacts with other proteins and hormones involved in phosphate regulation. Miyazaki also hopes to investigate why calcium deposits are so prevalent in the blood vessels of people with CKD.

“Scientists have been studying phosphate homeostasis for over 100 years, so to still find a new protein that regulates phosphate is kind of unique,” says Miyazaki.
