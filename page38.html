Victor Wembanyama's Spurs teammates catching his alley-oop fever

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

PHOENIX - A feathery pass lofted toward the basket from the free-throw line followed by a perfectly timed explosion skyward for a vicious one-handed slam.

The sensational alley-oop dunk the Spurs executed late in the third quarter of their 124-109 win at Sacramento on Friday was a thing of beauty - but it came with a twist.

It was a Victor Wembanyama-to-Stephon Castle connection, not the other way around.

The Spurs entered Monday night's game against the Suns boasting the top alley-oop combination in Castle to Wembanyama with 14 such plays, but in Sacramento, it was the Wembanyama feed to the explosive third-year guard that went viral.

"It's cool," Castle said after shootaround ahead of Monday night's game against the Suns.

"To be able to have that kind of connection with the big fella, whether it's me throwing a lob or him throwing it to me and it being so seamless, it's a details thing. But I think with how talented we are, it produces highlights like that."

The play also exemplified a trend of late for the defending Western Conference champions in that it's not just Wembanyama throwing down alley-oop slams with authority.

In Saturday night's 125-103 win at Portland, Luke Kornet and Tarris Reed Jr. had slams courtesy of alley-oop feeds from Keldon Johnson.

In the 112-111 win over Dallas on Wednesday at the Frost Bank Center, Devin Vassell set up Dylan Harper for an alley-oop dunk, and Kornet got one on an assist from Julian Champagnie.

"It's fun," Wembanyama said of the Spurs' diverse alley-oop avalanche.

"We have to keep finding ways, keep experimenting," the 7-foot-4 center added. "But the lob is the best shot in the game, so if we got good positions and good passes, there's no reason that anybody shouldn't catch a lob."

"We're so talented, we have so many scorers, we have so many people that can create that when we do play the right way, when we do play with our teammates, first off it's fun and second off it looks beautiful," he said.

Spurs coach Mitch Johnson said the widespread alley-oop dunking highlight's the team's athleticism, offensive depth and unselfishness.

"We have nine guys on our team who, in my estimation, have been leading scorers, and remain (high-level) offensive players in this league," the coach said. "If we don't acknowledge and value and celebrate passing, it's going to be hard for us to have the right type of harmony on the offensive end and as a team. I know there's some mumbo and jumbo in there, but it's a real thing when you start talking about the ecosystem of a team in the locker room.

"And when we can acknowledge and value passing and understand and what that does for our team and then celebrate it so people feel a part of plays where I might have made the pass to you and you made the pass to the person making the shot, that's when you start talking about high-level talent playing that style of basketball that's really hard to guard."

Castle said all the alley-ooping also reflects team chemistry.

"It's just the way we like to play off of each other," he said. "We know how hard we are to guard when the ball's popping, with how much talent we have on the court at all times."

With so many weapons, alley-oops and lobs seem to be coming from all directions these days.

"The ability to do that makes us very unpredictable," Wembanyama said.

Of course, it all begins and ends with the 22-year-old French superstar, said De'Aaron Fox, who ranks second on the team in alley-oop feeds to Wembanyama with 11.

"Vic's probably the best roller ever to play the game, probably the best lob threat ever," Fox said. "People try to keep him away from the rim as much as possible, but when he does get behind the defense, I think everybody knows - us and them - it's hard to stop or there's nothing they can do."

Wembanyama's 40-plus dunks and 30-plus alley-oop slams leads the NBA. He entered the game against Phoenix ranked fourth in the league in scoring with a career-best-29.1 average and he finished last season averaging 25.0.

"We're doing a better job running the floor, spacing the floor, and he's just doing a better job of asserting himself in the right positions," Champagnie said of Wembanyama's increased scoring. "The intent behind how he's playing is a little different. He's going to dunk the ball a lot more. So the more and more he does that, the more and more he does his thing in space, it kind of just opens up. Teams have to either guard the three, guard the dunk."

And when it comes to dunking, especially of the alley-oop variety, so many other Spurs players are getting into the act.

CAA Wants to Create an AI-Generated Celebrity Advertising Machine

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new platform from the major talent agency and powerhouse ad agency Ogilvy allows marketers to whip up AI-generated ads featuring celebrities without requiring talent to spend hours in front of the camera.

Celebrity ads are getting an AI makeover.

Powerhouse ad agency Ogilvy announced on Monday that it’s partnering with CAA to launch ECHO, an AI platform that allows marketers to whip up AI-generated ads featuring celebrities. The collaboration opens the door for talent to take on more endorsement deals without having to spend much more time shooting commercials while safeguarding how their digital replicas are used.

The platform utilizes what CAA calls its “vault,” a database that captures, stores and licenses the digital likeness, voice and intellectual property of clients who opt in. With ECHO, brands will be able to create a portfolio of media-ready assets off of a short shoot, potentially reducing costs by as much as 75 percent and vastly reducing turnaround time, the companies said. Talent must sign off on every ad, with automated compliance engines checking AI-generated materials against contracts and brand safety guidelines. The major talent agency did not disclose which A-listers opted in to the effort.

“We built The CAA Vault to ensure that talent owns, controls, and can safely monetize their digital likeness on their own terms and only with their consent,” said CAA head of strategic development Alex Shannon. “By connecting The CAA Vault to Ogilvy’s ECHO platform, CAA is giving many of the world’s leading brands a secure, consent-first activation layer, delivering full visibility and protection for talent and high-craft, verified creative at the speed of culture for brands.”

ECHO’s workflow can be integrated directly into standard post-production tools. A traditional asset shoot of eight to 10 assets can be expanded into roughly 30 to 50 for the creation of ads taking advantage of buzzy TikTok trends, CAA and Oglivy said. All consumer-facing products will carry a visible watermark that reads, “[Talent Name] Approved | Recreated with AI”.

In a statement, Ogilvy CEO Laurent Ezekiel said the tool solves the “fundamental tension between speed, scale and trust” in the AI era. “We are giving our clients unprecedented competitive advantage – allowing them to lead culture in real time with total trust and uncompromised craft.”

The tools represents the interest in monetizing the commercial use of celebrities’ digital likenesses and, perhaps, changing views among consumers regarding the deployment of AI. Brands have largely been hesitant to wade into those waters due to pushback against deepfakes and unauthorized AI use but acceptance appears to be growing.

Last month, the likeness of 1930s movie star Greta Garbo was revived in an ad from Swedish ball-bearings market SKF. ECHO is among the first to build an infrastructure around celebrities who opt into the usage of their digital replicas in ads.

“We are witnessing the ‘NIL moment’ between advertising and entertainment,” said Ogilvy chief innovation officer Carol Reed. “Instead of fearing AI or using it to produce cheap volume, building ECHO in partnership with CAA proves that technology can protect artists while setting storytellers free. Our global research confirms that consumers don’t want AI to sideline the artist; they want explicit proof that the talent is in control.”

I’m a 70-Year-Old Woman. So What's Ahead?

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

I turned 70 in October. My best friend, who is two months older than me, kept telling me it is okay and that once I decide how I’m going to mark the day, my life will get back on track. I knew she was right, but as the weeks approached, I started to think about what 70 meant for me. None of the other decades had hit me in this way.

Is this moment a reminder? An opportunity to think about how to make meaning and how I want to live my life from now? Do I want change?

I wondered why I was reflecting on this, anyway. I figured that by thinking about it intentionally (that new overused word that I love) it must mean that there is some sort of change happening. An awareness of another passing.

I'm taking emotional stock of where I am.

I have a full life with my husband of 48 years, and we are both healthy. Big tick.

I have three children who are all partnered, settled, and have worthwhile creative lives. I have five beautiful grandchildren, from 15 to almost 3, and another due next year. They light up my life, and I love spending time with them. They are growing up quickly. Even the 3-year-old already has a full calendar.

I have friends and family in Australia and in the U.S.

So the question is, why would I want to change anything?

Then I realised that I was on the precipice of a new phase of letting go. My playbook, guided by the work of Edna Furman, to let go of my children at each stage holds, and I know at 70 I have yet a new bridge to cross. A vital one.

But what is this bridge? My children don't need me on a day-to-day basis; they are all separate, self-sufficient, and inhabit their own orbits.

Seventy is a recalibration. There is a sort of tussle with my own and my husband’s mortality. Not in a morbid way, but in a real way that I hadn’t thought about before.

It's a recognition that while we are okay now, a time will come when we go to the doctor and… I am constantly aware that many other people, including those much younger than us, must manage this reality daily.

I go back 12 years to when I single-handedly took my twin grandchildren to the park and chased them both as they went in different directions. I am very active, but could I do this now? I have trouble safely keeping up with a 3-year-old running down the street.

I need to reconsider how to find my new meaning. I turn to role models, those women who are the same stage as me. I find comfort once again in the words of my best friend, who has been a major part of my life for 58 years. She is also in the process of letting go of her children; we have talked about it incessantly for the last 10 years. While she has three daughters that she is very close to, and is available when really needed, she made the intentional decision not to be “the sleepover Nanna." Her artwork nourishes her, as does her time with her family. She refuses to rush, and she has found peace. Most of the time.

I think about how this can apply to me. I know I’d like to read more because it feeds my soul. I have a particular volunteer programme that I am going to embark on.

And I want to continue my work on letting go, mothering, and maybe some literature and film. I’m not sure yet.

The reality is my disquiet will lead me somewhere. It always does.

As always, I go to psychological theory to help me.

The words of Edna Furman come rushing back into my mind. They have guided me to let go, that others are "there to be left." I am faced with yet another new adjustment. The post I wrote last April needs an update.

Then I go to respected psychoanalyst Erik Erikson, whose developmental life stages offer guidance for psychosocial growth. He presents life clearly as a struggle between major turning points which need to be resolved before moving forward. According to this, I’m moving from middle adulthood, generativity vs. stagnation, toward later adulthood, which occurs at 65-plus, marked by integrity vs. despair.

How does this apply to me? I take this as my need to be generative, productive, and useful, while I move toward wisdom and fighting off the negativity about mortality and decline. I recall the mantras that I repeat daily: don't be a victim, no shoulds, it’s not about me, adapt and harness positive energy.

Then I imagine what journalist Abigail Shrier, in her “Tough Love” advice column in Free Press, would say to me in her loving, chiding way.

And just today I got the biggest reminder, which I’m going to bottle.

My daughter is spending the day with me as we both sit on the couch, do some writing, eat doughnuts, and laugh together.

Then my son drops in to do a call and comes into the kitchen and gives me a warm hug.

Then my other son texts me. Do you want tickets for a show tonight?

I feel so full and back on track for today.

Tomorrow I will be struggling again, but that’s okay.
