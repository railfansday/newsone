The Austrian-German drama, which follows a professional clown struggling to find hope after losing her family in a car accident, also earned best actress for Valerie Pachner and best supporting actor for Robert Stadlober. This Is What Was on the Menu at the First Thanksgiving in 1621

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Somehow, turkey has spent the last four centuries getting all the glory.

For a meal that has been discussed, depicted and reenacted for more than four centuries, the first Thanksgiving has left us surprisingly few details about what anyone actually ate. We know there were roughly 140 people, three days of feasting and exactly five deer. Beyond that, the historical record is rather unhelpful. Apparently, nobody thought to write down the menu.

The gathering took place in Plymouth in the fall of 1621, when about 50 English colonists celebrated their first successful harvest alongside 90 Wampanoag, including their leader, Ousamequin, better known to history as Massasoit. It was a meeting between two communities whose relationship was far more complicated than the cheerful scene that would eventually find its way into American classrooms. Although they had formed an alliance earlier that year, cultural differences and tensions of an English settlement on Wampanoag land were hardly going to disappear over dinner.

And while the English had much to celebrate in bringing in their first harvest, they owed a great deal of that success to Indigenous knowledge. The Wampanoag understood the region’s growing seasons, its waterways and the foods that could be grown and gathered in the surrounding landscape. Their traditions had helped sustain communities there long before the colonists arrived.

The two surviving English accounts of the gathering offer only a few details about the feast itself, but taken together with what historians know about the food traditions of both communities, they give us a rather interesting picture of what may have been on the table.

Edward Winslow, one of the English colonists who attended the gathering, recorded that the Wampanoag went hunting and returned with five deer, which they presented to the colony’s leaders. It’s one of the few specific foods mentioned in his account and an especially substantial contribution to a celebration that lasted three days.

Deer were an important source of food for the Wampanoag, who also made use of their hides, bones and other materials. In England, however, deer hunting was largely reserved for the wealthy, making venison something most ordinary people rarely got to enjoy. The meat may have been roasted over a fire or simmered in a hearty venison stew, although Winslow neglected to tell us how it was prepared. We know exactly how many deer arrived, but what became of them in the kitchen remains a mystery.

Roast turkey has become so thoroughly associated with Thanksgiving that it’s difficult to imagine the holiday without one occupying the center of the table. The trouble is, we don’t actually know whether turkey was served at the original celebration.

Winslow wrote that four Englishmen went hunting for wildfowl and returned with enough to feed the colonists for nearly a week. What he didn’t do was tell us which birds they shot. Wild turkeys were present in the region, but so were ducks, geese and other game birds, all perfectly suitable for roasting over a fire. There’s every possibility that turkey was on the menu, but nothing to suggest it enjoyed the distinction we’ve since bestowed upon it. For all we know, the centerpiece was a duck, and we’ve spent the last few centuries giving credit to the wrong bird.

Corn was one of the most important foods available to both communities, although the English were relatively new to growing it. The Wampanoag had cultivated maize for generations, developing agricultural practices suited to the region’s soil and growing seasons. Their knowledge was enormously valuable to the colonists, whose first attempts at establishing a food supply had been anything but fruitful.

One traditional Wampanoag preparation was nasaump, a thick porridge made from ground corn, sometimes cooked with other ingredients. Corn could also be ground into meal for simple breads, early relatives of the cornbread we bake today, or used to thicken stews and make other hearty dishes.

The waters around Plymouth were full of food, provided you knew where to look and how to catch it. The Wampanoag had long fished the region’s rivers and coastline, gathering shellfish and catching everything from bass and cod to eels. Clams, mussels and lobsters were plentiful too.

The English were beginning to take advantage of these resources by 1621. William Bradford, the Plymouth colony’s governor, wrote of the colonists catching substantial quantities of cod and bass during the summer. A freshly caught fish could be roasted or boiled, while clams and mussels might be cooked over a fire. Lobster was another plentiful local catch that was enjoyed regularly. The variety of ocean fare available might have made for a Thanksgiving spread that looked rather more like a seafood boil than the feast we know today.

Squash and pumpkin were important crops in Wampanoag agriculture, and their ability to keep well after harvest made them especially useful as the colder weather set in. Both could be roasted, boiled or added to stews, much like we still prepare winter squash today, though without the benefit of a balsamic glaze.

The English called pumpkins pompions, a name I think we abandoned prematurely. They were familiar with the vegetable, although the varieties growing in New England differed from those they might have seen at home. A pumpkin dish would have been entirely plausible at the harvest celebration, but anyone hoping for a slice of pumpkin pie would have to wait almost two centuries. The colonists had limited supplies of wheat flour, butter and sugar, and the familiar custard-filled pie wouldn’t appear in American cookbooks until 1796.

The Wampanoag grew beans alongside corn and squash, three crops that complemented each other remarkably well. Cornstalks supported climbing beans, while squash vines spread across the ground and helped shade the soil. The combination was as useful in the garden as it was on the plate.

A traditional Wampanoag stew called sobaheg combines ingredients like corn, beans, squash and meat or fish, depending on what’s available or in season. The combination of corn and beans also appears in succotash, another dish with Indigenous New England roots that remains an American favorite.

The forests and wetlands of New England offered plenty beyond cultivated crops. Chestnuts, walnuts, hazelnuts, acorns, wild grapes and cranberries were among the foods the Wampanoag knew how to gather and prepare, each with its own season and uses.

Nuts could be eaten plain, stored or ground into meal, while fruits could be enjoyed fresh or preserved for later. Acorns required particular attention, since their natural bitterness had to be leached out with water before they could be used in cooking. It’s one of the few ingredients we’ve more or less surrendered entirely to the squirrels.

Of all the wild fruits available, cranberries have perhaps the most recognizable place on a modern Thanksgiving table. Their natural tartness eventually found a friend in sugar, giving rise to the cranberry sauce that’s become nearly as much a part of the holiday as the turkey itself.

Gun Rights and Guilt by Association

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Some excerpts from the longish opinion today in Shilling v. County of San Diego, decided today by Ninth Circuit Judge Patrick Bumatay, joined by Judges Kenneth Lee and Jennifer Sung:

The San Diego County Sheriff revoked Kenneth Shilling's concealed-carry weapon ("CCW") license over his affiliation with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club…. [We conclude, among other things, that] the district court improperly dismissed his First Amendment [freedom of association] claim….

Kenneth Charles Shilling resides in San Diego, California. He's a member of the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which, according to Shilling, was founded in 1946 by World War II veterans. In his view, it's not like some other motorcycle clubs, which are known for criminal and gang activity. Instead, the Boozefighters focus on charitable work and the "core values of family, work, and community." Shilling's record is clean; he has no criminal history.

In June 2022, when the "good moral character" requirement [for getting a concealed caryr license] still existed [under California law], Shilling applied for and received a CCW license from then-San Diego County Sheriff Anthony Ray. This was short lived. Less than a year and a half later, in December 2023, a different San Diego County Sheriff, Kelly Martinez, used the "good moral character" requirement to revoke Shilling's CCW license. In a letter notifying Shilling of the revocation, Sheriff Martinez explained that the "San Diego Sheriff's Department['s] good moral character definition" excludes a person who:

Has a pattern of or propensity for criminal conduct, as demonstrated by anything in the application or investigation for a license. This includes, but is not limited to, evidence of criminal behavior or a close nexus to a criminal gang. In making this determination, the Sheriff's Department shall use an objective inquiry, considering the facts and circumstances known at the time of the application or through the investigation.

The letter said that Shilling's license was revoked due to his association with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club, which the Sheriff described as a "criminal street gang." Shilling was not given the opportunity to contest the allegations before his license was revoked, but he could request reconsideration of the decision. The new CCW regime with the "dangerousness" requirement took effect on January 1, 2024, shortly after Shilling's license was revoked….

Here's the court's analysis allowing Shilling's First Amendment freedom of association claim to go forward:

The First Amendment guarantees all Americans the "right to associate with others." … [T]he right protects groups of individuals "in pursuit of a wide variety of political, social, economic, educational, religious and cultural ends." …

The government may infringe the freedom of association in "varied ways," including by "punish[ing] individuals for their affiliations." The government cannot impose "criminal sanctions or deny[ ] rights and privileges solely because of a citizen's association with an unpopular organization." For example, a group of students could not be denied the "benefits" of official campus recognition at a state college merely because of its affiliation with a national organization associated with disruptive and violent campus activity…. [Likewise,] "Civil liability may not be imposed merely because an individual belonged to a group, some members of which committed acts of violence." … If the government seeks to deprive an individual of a benefit, privilege, or right based on association with an alleged criminal organization, "it is necessary to establish that the group itself possessed unlawful goals and that the individual held a specific intent to further those illegal aims." …

Given this understanding of the First Amendment, Shilling easily pleaded a violation of his freedom of association…. [His] allegations sufficiently raise an infringement of Shilling's free-association right because the Sheriff deprived him of a benefit, privilege, or right—the ability to carry a concealed weapon in public—"solely because of [his] association with an unpopular organization." Unless the Sheriff adequately meets her burden of showing the Boozefighters "possessed unlawful goals" and Shilling's "specific intent to further those illegal aims," she cannot "punish [Shilling] for [his] affiliations." This kind of "guilt by association alone, without establishing that an individual's association poses the threat feared by the Government, is an impermissible basis upon which to deny First Amendment rights."

In dismissing this claim, the district court misunderstood the nature of the free-association right. It asserted that Shilling had to show the revocation of his CCW license somehow prevented him from associating with the Boozefighters Motorcycle Club or participating in the Club's activities. While preventing an individual from participating in a group's lawful activities may be one way to violate the First Amendment right, it's infringed in "varied ways." And "denying" an individual benefits, rights, or privileges "solely because of [his] association"—as Shilling alleges—is certainly another way….

For the court's analysis of sovereign immunity and of whether Schilling's Second Amendment claim was moot, see the full opinion.

Gary W. Gorski (Veterans Law Center) represents plaintiff.

What can AI learn from a thousand days of hearing what a baby hears?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Princeton recorded eight babies for their first 1,000 days, and a model trained on one child's audio learned language on the same schedule.

A toddler can pick up hundreds of words just by hearing people talk. Could a computer start from scratch and recognize many of those same words on roughly the same timetable?

Researchers at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute wired up 15 homes with cameras and microphones and recorded babies for most of their waking hours across the first 1,000 days of life.

The researchers turned speech recorded around eight children into clean audio using synthetic voices.

They trained a separate model on each child’s audio in the order the speech was originally recorded.

The models identified recurring sound patterns that corresponded to words.

On average, each model reached the halfway point on a vocabulary checklist within 22 days of the child it was paired with.

“We wanted to turn the usual logic of developmental research around,” said Uri Hasson, a professor of psychology and neuroscience at Princeton who led the study.

“Instead of observing a child for an hour in the laboratory, we asked whether we could build a model capable of learning and immerse it in the child’s actual experience, day after day.”

The families were in New Jersey and spanned a range of incomes and backgrounds. Each home got four to 14 recording devices spread across two to five rooms.

The devices ran 12 to 14 hours a day, from February 2022 to May 2025.

The project gathered roughly 1.2 million hours of audio and video, of which the eight homes analyzed in this paper supplied about 600,000. An automated pipeline worked out where the child was in the house and pulled out the speech happening nearby.

Each child ended up with between 1,900 and 6,600 hours of usable speech, three to seven and a half hours a day. That adds up to 6 to 27 million words per child, over a median of 960 recording days.

Every month from eight months on, caregivers filled out a standard checklist of which words their child understood and said. Those forms are how the researchers knew what each child knew, and when.

Children reached half of the 649-word checklist anywhere from about 12 to 22 months old. That ten-month spread between children is the thing most studies average away.

The model started from random numbers. It had no dictionary, no phonetics, no notion of where one word ends and the next begins, and no text.

The program’s first part chops the incoming sound into 20-millisecond pieces and sorts them into categories it invents for itself. Its second part tries to guess the next piece from what came before.

Whenever a run of pieces becomes reliably predictable, the program files it away as a unit, which is the closest thing it has to a word.

Nothing about that design is specific to English or even to speech. What makes it unusual is the schedule.

The audio arrives in the order it was recorded, one day at a time, rather than shuffled into batches the way an AI language model is normally trained.

At the end of each simulated day, the program gets a sample of speech from earlier days, weighted toward recent ones. The researchers call it replay.

English has 39 phonemes, the sound units that distinguish bat from pat. Children spend their first year tuning in to the ones their own language uses.

Within roughly 12 to 15 months of simulated time, the program had carved out dedicated categories for nearly all of them.

Across the eight children, the models averaged 37.5 phonemes each. The lowest count came from the household with the least recording.

The order it happened in, sounds before words, is the order infants follow.

Words came after. For the child the paper calls Coral, the checklist said she understood 97 words at 12 months, and her model had 91.

Across all eight children, the models and the children hit the same vocabulary milestones within an average of 22 days of each other, with the worst case 56 days.

The models also reproduced the differences between children, landing close to each child’s own halfway age.

They did not match perfectly. The models tended to start learning words a little late and then catch up faster, so the shape of the curve was only partly right.

The surprise in the paper is what happened when the researchers took replay away.

Coral’s household produced something like 10 million words a year, which is a rich environment by any measure. Hearing all of it once, in order, wasn’t enough. With a single pass the program barely learned words at all.

Around 25 to 30 passes over each day’s material was enough, and more than that added very little.

The researchers link this to what sleep is thought to do for memory. A program replaying audio files is not a sleeping infant, and the paper is careful about that.

The amount of recording mattered just as much. Fed only a tenth of Coral’s daily speech, under an hour a day, the program learned none of the checklist words by 24 months.

At 30% of the day, about two and a half hours, it learned 80 of the 649 checklist words.

At 70% it got about nine-tenths of the way to the full result. The rarer words, it seems, only turn up for a listener who is there most of the day.

One limitation shapes everything else. Household recordings are full of clattering dishes, television, and two people talking at once, and the program was not asked to cope with any of that.

Instead the researchers transcribed all the speech around each child, then had a text-to-speech system read the transcripts back as clean audio.

Each household got a mix of eight adult female voices, with 30% of segments read in a voice matched to the child’s mother.

So the word order, the timing and the vocabulary are real, and the sound isn’t. The acoustic problem a baby actually solves, picking one voice out of a noisy room, was left for later work.

Three of the eight children also had shorter recordings, 14 to 20 months of usable input, so their later trajectories are extrapolated rather than modeled.

The program also sees nothing: no faces, no pointing, no object being handed over, which is a large part of how toddlers work out what a word refers to.

The program learned sounds and words. It did not learn grammar, and whether the same machinery would arrive at sentence structure is the open question the authors name.

The team wants to use the setup for questions nobody can put to a real baby. What would less sleep do, or screen speech instead of a parent’s?

Future studies could also add eye contact and the rest of the social world the cameras captured but the model never saw.

Hadas Raviv, a computational cognitive scientist in Princeton’s psychology department who built the analysis pipeline, framed the goal as the opposite of the usual one in artificial intelligence.

“Unlike much of modern AI, which aims for superhuman performance, we’re trying to understand how humans learn and how that learning gives rise to intelligence,” Raviv said.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

Chelsea have a No 9 problem at both ends of the pitch. Up top, Xabi Alonso is struggling to find a solution in the absence of Joao Pedro, who has still not returned to training after weeks out with a thigh problem. And at the back, Alonso’s porous defence appears fallible in the face of the old-school striker’s revival.

Defeat at Sunderland brought accusations that Chelsea’s defence was susceptible to being “bullied”. The primary bully, in this case, was frontman Brian Brobbey, one of the Premier League’s most imposing units. His overpowering of Levi Colwill led to Sunderland’s controversial penalty that proved the difference two weeks ago.

Alonso didn’t agree with such a critique at the time. "Not bullied, I would say that it was a situation that we could have defended better,” he said after the match at the Stadium of Light. But amid Chelsea’s frantic form, questions are being asked whether Alonso’s back line can handle the Premier League at its most physical.

In this regard, Saturday’s test of Leeds provides the ultimate test. There are few teams in the top-flight as physical as Leeds, even if Daniel Farke may sometimes opt to play down his players as “butterflies”, like he did for Jayden Bogle after the right-back avoided a red card for a challenge on Tottenham’s Mathys Tel.

Leeds have won the most duels in the Premier League this season; Farke’s players are not scared of going in for a challenge. That has made them a particularly difficult team to score against, conceding a mere eight goals so far. Chelsea have let in double that tally, with only Arsenal and Brighton conceding fewer than the Whites.

Alonso isn’t only learning of English football’s physical requirement now; his five seasons at Liverpool would have taught him as such. And he knows that if his team continue to be beaten in this department, it will repeatedly cost them in this league regardless of the opposition. “It’s the Premier League. It’s not new,” Alonso said. “It’s not just against Leeds. It will happen next week against Forest, and the next one against Palace. This is the level of the competition.”

Ever the tactician, Alonso does not put full emphasis on brute physicality – even if it is a necessary step to unlocking quality. He continued: “It’s not just the duels – it’s about a football thing or tactical thing. When you have good control, normally you are in better positions to press and duel. When you don’t have that much control, you aren’t in that good position. It’s not just ‘one v one, let’s see what happens’. No, you can help situations to be stronger in duels. After, it’s the individual qualities that help.”

Leeds nevertheless appeared to lean into the criticism in the build-up to their trip down south. They posted a compilation of their best moments against the Blues from over the years, which included its fair share of crunching tackles and Chelsea mistakes. Whether a statement of intent or merely mind games, Alonso said his side was only focusing on themselves. “We focus on not being soft tomorrow and being competitive,” he said. “That’s our goal.”

Like Sunderland, Leeds’ main threat comes in the form of a traditional No 9. Dominic Calvert-Lewin may have been studying tape of Chelsea’s last outing and licking his lips this week, knowing that he has the capacity to cause the same sort of problems as Brobbey.

“We prepare knowing that we have prepared because it’s a player with different qualities but the same kind of profile,” Alonso said. “It’s nothing new. It’s quite common in the Premier League that they have a good No 9.

“Calvert-Lewin is doing well. He played with England. But it is up to us, how we defend, how we compete in those situations, how we attack the last line, how we attack their defenders. The preparation has, as always, we think been good. We are ready for tomorrow.”

Back in fashion, the target man profile is giving Chelsea’s young defence trouble. While Alonso’s arrival at Chelsea coincided with the move away from their chaos of youth, his back line remains fresh-faced. His five first-team centre-backs have an average age of 23, with Maxence Lacroix the oldest at 26. They all came into senior football with the old-school No 9 being just that, and the leap back in time has forced them to adapt.

So far, the learning curve has been apparent. But Alonso doesn’t believe his players lack the tools to deal with what used to be the norm up top.

Asked if centre-backs have forgotten how to defend the traditional No 9, Alonso replied: “No, I don’t think forgotten. But it’s demanding to play against No 9s. They have good movement in the box, out the box. But this is the demand. You can ask the full-backs, they have to play against great wingers in one v one situations. But I don’t think it’s been forgotten.”

Alonso is personally happy to see the revival of the No 9. “It’s good they are back, these type of players,” he said. Whether he still feels the same after the visit of Calvert-Lewin and the rest of Leeds’ hardmen may depend on his players toughening up to the task.
