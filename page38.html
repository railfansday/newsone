The report put Anthropic’s debut valuation at a record $1.5 trillion. Its seven co-founders have pledged to donate 80% of their equity, while employee wealth has drawn attention from organizations working on global health, animal welfare and scientific research.

In an October 18, 2026 announcement, Anthropic said its proposed Public Benefit Fund would initially receive 5% of company shares, subject to stockholder approval, with another 2% available at the board’s discretion for every 50% increase in company value above a set baseline. The LLC structure preserves flexibility among forms of giving, with lobbying and political giving excluded from use of its Anthropic equity. Approximately half of employees participate in a separate equity donation match program.

GiveDirectly offers a concrete example of what that preparation entails. President and CEO Nick Allardice has spent months identifying barriers to expanding direct cash transfers, which send money to poor households without conditions on its use. Recipients need mobile phones for digital payments, so the nonprofit secured investments during 2026 to build a larger phone supply chain. It offers phones for about $15, generally deducting the cost from a recipient’s first payment.

A September 21, 2026 operational plan set a goal of being able to absorb $5 billion or more annually by 2028, ten times GiveDirectly’s capacity at the end of 2025. Existing operations could deliver approximately $500 million during 2026, already more than the organization had in donations to distribute. The plan does not assume that its target will be funded.

The preparation investments are funded by private donors rather than general donations and address bottlenecks that cash cannot immediately solve: regulatory approvals, strategic partnerships, technology and suppliers. GiveDirectly distributed nearly 125,000 phones in 2025 and is preparing for a possible need for 2 million to 3 million annually. Its procurement work includes direct negotiations with manufacturers, real-time inventory tracking and recruitment of a global phone procurement manager.

Remote poverty identification, identity verification and recipient communication could also expand reach without proportional staff growth. GiveDirectly’s plan cautions that reducing in-person contact can create fraud, safeguarding and exclusion risks, particularly for people without phones or experience using mobile money. The operational challenge extends beyond phone procurement to preserving verification, trust and access as enrollment becomes less labor-intensive.

65 Years of Human Spaceflight at NASA Johnson

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

For 65 years, NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston has helped shape human spaceflight, from the Gemini missions that demonstrated rendezvous and docking techniques needed for Apollo to the research conducted aboard the International Space Station, which has supported a continuous human presence for more than 26 years. Today, Johnson teams are preparing crews and systems for NASA’s next chapter: returning astronauts to the lunar surface and building the capabilities needed for a long-term human presence at the Moon.

Across those missions, generations of flight controllers, engineers, scientists, instructors, medical specialists, and other experts have helped crews live and work beyond Earth. Johnson is home to NASA’s Orion and Moon Base programs, and its teams continue to support space station operations, astronaut training, spacecraft development, mission operations, and human health.

When the Manned Spacecraft Center officially opened on Nov. 1, 1961, NASA had already flown two suborbital Mercury missions, with the first orbital flight just months away. Most Project Mercury activities remained at Cape Canaveral in Florida to maintain continuity, but the new Project Gemini took up residence in Houston, housed in temporary leased facilities across the city while construction of the new Clear Lake campus continued. The new Mission Control Center became NASA’s primary flight control center in June 1965, with the Gemini IV mission that included the first American spacewalk. Houston became the nerve center for American human spaceflight, overseeing the remaining eight Gemini missions before transitioning to the Apollo program. Following the tragic Apollo 1 fire in January 1967, managers, engineers, and astronauts at the Manned Spacecraft Center applied lessons learned to make the spacecraft safer and ensure the safety of its astronauts, resuming crewed missions in October 1968.  Managers in Houston devised a daring plan to send Apollo 8 on a circumlunar flight.

In December 1968, Apollo 8 made its historic trip around the Moon, and seven months later Apollo 11 astronauts Neil Armstrong and Edwin “Buzz” Aldrin landed on the lunar surface. Their first voice transmission back to Earth after their arrival began with the word “Houston.”  Five more Moon landings completed the Apollo program and humanity’s first phase of lunar exploration in December 1972.

Following the Moon landings, scientists at the Manned Spacecraft Center’s Lunar Receiving Laboratory conducted initial analyses of Apollo lunar samples and prepared them for researchers around the world. After the building was demolished in March 2024, the Lunar Receiving Park was dedicated to honor the laboratory’s history in 2026. Today, Johnson’s Astromaterials Research and Exploration Science (ARES) division continues to analyze and curate the Apollo collection at the Lunar Sample Laboratory Facility.

Skylab, America’s first space station, ran into trouble during its launch in May 1973, losing its thermal shield, jamming one solar array and losing the other one.  In less than 10 days, engineers in Houston designed and built a sun shield and generated procedures to free the jammed array, followed by the first extensive in-orbit repair activities by the first Skylab crew. The three missions lasting 28, 59, and 84 days accomplished more research than planned, demonstrated the value of humans on long-duration missions, and provided operators in mission control with round-the-clock experience monitoring long duration human spaceflights.

In July 1975, after several joint training sessions in Houston and Moscow, American astronauts and Soviet cosmonauts met in space for the first time during the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project. Approved as a project in 1972, five approach and landing tests of the space shuttle using orbiter Enterprise paved the way for the first orbital mission of Columbia in 1981.

Engineers in Houston began designs for a reuseable space plane in the late 1960s, with the space shuttle receiving formal approval in 1972. During the 1970s, Johnson Space Center put in place the training and simulation facilities that astronauts used for the life of the program. Upgrades to mission control enabled the use of the latest technologies to support ever more complex space shuttle missions. The April 1981 launch of space shuttle Columbia inaugurated a new era of reusable spacecraft. The program’s 135 missions flew 355 individuals from 16 nations. Missions included satellite deployments and retrievals, dedicated Department of Defense flights, a variety of science missions including deployment of planetary spacecraft, ferrying crews and cargo to two different space stations, and doing the heavy lifting in the assembly of the orbiting laboratory. The program recovered from the tragic losses of the Challenger and Columbia crews with renewed emphasis on crew safety. The final space shuttle mission, STS-135, closed out the 30-year program in July 2011.

An Earth orbiting space station, first approved by President Ronald Reagan in 1984, underwent many modifications before taking on its current form as the International Space Station in 1993. Overall management of the program resides at Johnson. The first element launch took place in November 1998, followed one month later by the first U.S. element, the Node 1 Unity module. The first expedition crew took up residence on Nov. 2, 2000, and for more than 26 years, rotating crews of astronauts and cosmonauts have lived and worked aboard the orbiting laboratory, establishing a permanent human presence in low Earth orbit.

To date, 298 individuals from 26 countries have visited the space station, conducting more than 4,000 science experiments and educational activities to enable exploration missions and improve life on Earth.

Life aboard the space station depends on a global team on the ground. Crews conduct research and maintain equipment in microgravity, while Johnson teams at the Mission Control Center coordinate the agency’s station operations with international partners. Johnson’s Space Vehicle Mockup Facility supports training for NASA astronauts and international crews on station systems, science operations, and emergency procedures. At the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory, crews rehearse spacewalks around full-scale station mockups in one of the world’s largest indoor pools. Johnson’s Human Research Program also supports health investigations aboard the station, helping researchers understand how spaceflight affects the body so crews can thrive on future deep space missions.

What began as a framework of modules has evolved into a springboard for international cooperation, advanced scientific research and technology demonstrations, the development of a low Earth orbit economy, and NASA’s next giant leaps in exploration. The space station continues to be a proving ground for NASA’s Artemis program and human exploration of Mars.

The Artemis II mission marked the first crewed flight of NASA’s Orion spacecraft. The four-person crew launched April 1, on a nearly 10-day trip around the Moon. At its farthest point, Orion traveled 252,756 miles from Earth, farther than any humans had traveled before. The mission demonstrated Orion’s capabilities with astronauts aboard and tested crew operations in deep space, setting the stage for future lunar missions.

Building on Artemis II, NASA’s Artemis III mission will test integrated operations in low Earth orbit between Orion and test versions of one or both commercial human landing systems under development by SpaceX and Blue Origin. Astronauts will demonstrate the rendezvous and docking capabilities needed to transfer crews between spacecraft for the lunar landing planned for Artemis IV.

NASA’s Moon Base Program is planning a phased effort to support a sustained human presence near the Moon’s South Pole. The first phase will focus on robotic exploration, technology demonstrations, and preparations for future surface operations. Later phases will add infrastructure to support astronauts living and working on the Moon. Lunar terrain vehicles will help crews explore farther, transport equipment, and conduct science.

At Johnson, teams are drawing on decades of experience in astronaut training, mission control, spacecraft development, and human health to prepare for these missions. From training crews for lunar operations to developing the systems that will support them, Johnson is building on 65 years of human spaceflight experience for humanity’s return to the lunar surface through the Artemis and Moon Base Programs.

GLP-1s Like Ozempic May Boost Brain Health, But Can They Lower Dementia Risk?

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

GLP-1 medications have transformed obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment. Now, researchers are investigating whether the effects of this blockbuster class of drugs extend beyond weight loss and affect brain health.

A comprehensive new review published on November 5 in the journal Science evaluated the current evidence on how GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide may affect the brain across aging, cardiometabolic disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The authors of the review suggested that GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Zepbound may offer brain health benefits. However, they were also left with the question of whether the medications act directly on the brain or whether better brain health is actually a consequence of improved cardiometabolic health.

Sheena Aurora, MD, vice president, Medical Affairs, Alzheimer’s Association, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline that more research is needed to better understand the effects of GLP-1 drugs on the brain.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

This is important for brain health because obesity, type 2 diabetes, and vascular dysfunction are all linked with cognitive impairment and dementia. Insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may also link metabolic and brain health.

Analyses of large outcome trials and observational cohorts have suggested that GLP-1 medications may be associated with a reduced risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. However, randomized trials in diseases like Alzheimer’s disease have yet to demonstrate clinical benefits.

Around 200 preclinical and mechanistic studies have shown benefits for learning, memory, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal resilience, though results are heterogeneous in design and direction, so a clear consensus on magnitude and mechanism remains lacking.

It is also unclear how well GLP-1 medications reach the brain. They appear to reach certain areas, such as the hypothalamus, brainstem, and circumventricular organs, more readily than regions protected by the blood-brain barrier.

The report authors noted that some studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in people with type 2 diabetes. This may be due to cognitive issues being linked to factors such as blood sugar, blood pressure, vascular health, and sleep.

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “These findings are interesting, but like many of the other benefits associated with GLP-1 medications, a direct causal relationship has not yet been proven.”

“Rather, the benefits appear to be more closely tied to the reduction in obesity and better control of diabetes,” he said.

Aurora noted that growing evidence supports the heart-brain connection.

She cited new findings from the Alzheimer’s Association’s landmark U.S. POINTER study presented at the Clinical Trials in Alzheimer’s Disease (CTAD) conference November 16–19 in Boston, MA.

“U.S. POINTER can change those risk factors within 24 months, and better heart health risk-factor control was linked with better cognition,” Aurora said. “We’re also interested to see whether adding a drug like a GLP-1 to a U.S. POINTER-style lifestyle program improves cognition in at-risk older adults more than the program alone.”

To find out, the Alzheimer’s Association is funding the Prevention of Risk for Cognitive Decline through Combined Therapy (PROTECT-Cog) global phase 3 clinical trial.

PROTECT-Cog is a first-of-its-kind trial designed to evaluate whether combining a proven multidomain lifestyle intervention with a metabolism-targeting drug, like a GLP-1, can further reduce the risk of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia in at-risk older adults.

Dung Trinh, MD, internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who also wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline, “I often remind my patients that what’s good for the heart is generally good for the brain.”

“The brain depends on a healthy cardiovascular system to deliver a constant supply of oxygen, glucose, and other nutrients. Conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol can damage blood vessels, promote chronic inflammation, and increase the risk of stroke and vascular cognitive impairment,” he said.

“Over time, these changes can also contribute to the development or progression of dementia.”

Trinh added that while GLP-1 medications can improve several cardiometabolic risk factors, these improvements do not automatically translate into proven dementia prevention.

“Regular physical activity, a heart-healthy diet, adequate sleep, and management of cardiovascular risk factors remain essential strategies for maintaining brain health as we age,” he said.

When the report authors turned their attention to more stringent tests on semaglutide in people already living with early Alzheimer’s disease, the picture began to change.

Novo Nordisk’s EVOKE and EVOKE + Phase 3 Trials examined the efficacy of semaglutide in people with early stage Alzheimer’s disease.

The studies showed that while semaglutide produced changes in several disease-related biomarkers, it didn’t slow the progression of Alzheimer’s disease.

This emphasizes that fixing metabolic issues might prevent cognitive decline in someone with obesity or diabetes. But it might not be enough to reverse the damage in someone with established Alzheimer’s disease, where profound synaptic loss and protein pathology have already occurred.

The review authors suggest that future research on obesity and diabetes should also include rigorous and specific cognitive testing.

"I've always wondered what a height different between a 4'10 person and a 5'7 person would look like … I found this image but idk if it's up to scale or not."

That question, posted to r/heightcomparison, is the whole genre in one sentence. People want to see a height gap, they find a picture, and then they have no way to tell whether the picture is honest. If you are building the tool that makes those pictures, "is it up to scale?" is the one question your code has to answer correctly every time.

It sounds trivial: multiply height by a constant and draw. In practice, height comparison charts break in a handful of predictable ways: an axis that does not start at zero, figures that float instead of standing on a floor, decorations that add fake height, and a sneaky unit-conversion bug that shifts some heights by a quarter of an inch and not others. This post builds a small, framework-free JavaScript module for a height comparison to scale, with a node --test suite. Along the way it explains each failure mode and the test that catches it.

The original question is old, but the pattern keeps repeating. In September 2026 a developer posted their new height comparison site to the same subreddit and asked for brutally honest feedback, specifically: "Does the visual height difference look intuitive?" and "Do you notice anything that looks inaccurate or misleading?" The replies are a free QA report, and three of them are worth turning into requirements:

On the developer side, the parsing questions never stop. A Stack Overflow question asks how to convert 5'5" to centimetres when the data sometimes says 5' 5" with a space. An r/learnjavascript beginner tried to convert feet and inches to metres and lost the values to variable scope. And in r/FanFiction, someone wanted to line up 25 characters and found that every site capped the number of people.

So the requirements write themselves: accept the formats people type, refuse the ones that are ambiguous, never let a conversion drift, draw everyone on one floor with one scale, and be honest about what a silhouette can and cannot show.

The truncated axis is the same mistake as a bar chart that does not start at zero. It is tempting because it makes small differences "readable," but in a height chart the visual ratio is the message. If you need to emphasize a 2 cm gap, add a zoomed inset or a number, not a cropped axis.

Height comparison to scale in JavaScript: storing Math.round(cm) and converting back to the nearest quarter inch shifts exactly the odd heights from 5'11" to 6'7" by 1/4 inch. Storing unrounded centimetres gives zero drift.

Let's start with the bug report, because it is the most instructive. I don't know how that site is built, but a very common pipeline reproduces the report exactly:

Run that for every inch from 5'11" to 6'7", and the heights that drift are 5'11", 6'1", 6'3", 6'5" and 6'7". Those are the odd ones, just as reported, with 6'5" going up a quarter and 6'1" going down a quarter (185.42 → 185 → 72.835 in → 6'0¾"). Even heights in that range happen to land within an eighth of an inch after rounding, so they look fine, which is exactly why this bug survives casual testing.

The inch is defined as exactly 2.54 cm, so there is no reason to lose information. The fix is a rule: pick one canonical unit, store it unrounded, and round only at the display edge. Centimetres as a JavaScript Number is fine; a double holds 180.34 to far more precision than a chart needs. If you persist to a database, an integer number of micrometres or a decimal column also works. Whole centimetres do not.

Height inputs arrive as 5'11", 5' 11, 5ft 11in, 71 in, 180 cm, 1.8 m, and the one that causes real trouble: 5.11. Is that 5.11 feet (about 155.8 cm) or 5 feet 11 inches (180.34 cm)? Users mean the second far more often, but silently guessing is how a 25 cm error ends up in a chart. My parser refuses it and offers a suggestion the UI can show as "Did you mean 5'11"?":

The 20–300 cm range is a product policy, not a fact about people. It matches the limits on Body Visualizer's height comparison tool, which also requires whole feet and inches below 12. Pick limits that suit your audience and test both edges.

Displaying feet and inches has its own classic bug: 5'12". If you compute feet with Math.floor and then round the remaining inches, 71.9 inches becomes 5 feet and 12 inches. Round the total first, then split:

denominator: 4 gives quarter inches; denominator: 1 gives whole inches. Because the input is the unrounded canonical value, formatFtIn(parseHeight("6'5\"").cm, { denominator: 4 }) returns 6'5" every time.

The difference readout should follow the same rule. I compute the percentage relative to the shorter person, which is how the Body Visualizer tool explains it too: 160 cm vs 175 cm is a 15 cm gap, and 15 / 160 = 9.375%. Whichever base you choose, state it in the UI, because "9.4% taller" and "8.6% shorter" describe the same pair.

The layout function is where "up to scale" is actually enforced. It computes a single pxPerCm from the tallest person plus some headroom, puts 0 cm on the ground line, and measures every figure from the same floor:

niceStep picks ruler intervals of 1, 2, 2.5 or 5 times a power of ten, so a chart of people gets 25 cm ticks and a chart that includes a building gets round hundreds:

The layout function is pure: give it heights and a box, get numbers back. That makes the "to scale" property directly testable, which matters more than any amount of visual review.

If you need many people, like the 25 fan-fiction characters, keep the same pxPerCm and let the chart scroll horizontally. Shrinking each slot until figures overlap, or splitting the cast into separately scaled charts, breaks comparability. If you do split, reuse one scale across every chunk.

Here is the subtle part behind the "circle" complaint. Most silhouette artwork has padding: transparent space above the head inside the image or SVG viewBox. Scale that asset to heightPx and the visible crown lands below the true height. Add a portrait circle above the head and the eye reads the circle's top as the person's top.

My silhouette path is authored in a unit box where y = 0 is the crown and y = 1 is the soles, with nothing above or below:

translate to the crown, then scale by the pixel height, and the feet land exactly on groundY. If you use richer artwork, measure its real bounding box once (for example with getBBox() in a build step) and normalize it to this convention. Keep names, portraits and badges outside the height box: below the ground line, beside the figure, or on the chest as that commenter suggested.
