Lei Lei’s Hybrid Doc ‘Vertigo Garden,’ About a Botanist’s AI-Replicated Memories, Lands at Asian Shadows Ahead of IDFA Bow (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Boutique sales company Asian Shadows has acquired “Vertigo Garden,” the new feature from Chinese filmmaker and animation artist Lei Lei, ahead of its world premiere in the Envision Competition at the International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA).

This is Lei’s second feature. IDFA has also nominated it for the Beeld en Geluid IDFA ReFrame Award, which honors creative work with archival material. “Vertigo Garden” unfolds at the Lushan Botanical Garden and follows the life of its botanist. His body is found in the snow and ice at the top of Lushan Mountain, with both body and brain intact. Scientists study the brain, use AI to reproduce his memories and set them loose in a digital world. Through conversation, the film pieces together the botanist’s storied bond with the metasequoia tree and his efforts to communicate with the planet’s memories through these ancient trees.

Lei uses the metasequoia to probe how memory, life and the planet connect, combining documentary, 3D animation and experimental techniques.

“I believe that Asian Shadows and its team truly love the film. They will treat this film like their own child and promote it to meet audiences around the world, which for me goes beyond what is called a business partnership,” Lei said.

In a director’s statement, Lei wrote that he started filming at the garden, in his hometown, with an 8mm camera in 2024, making long-term field recordings there. He credited Chris Marker’s “Sans Soleil” (1983) as the inspiration for the screenplay, and the film uses video letters to stage an imagined correspondence between the filmmaker and the botanist. The statement also set out plans to 3D-scan metasequoia fossils, trunks and the garden’s greenhouse, then move through the digital models with a virtual camera to create effects that resemble a video game. Elsewhere, Lei described training AI to generate footage of the same location again and again, cutting the results into a flickering effect he likened to the work of artist Rose Lowder.

Lei wrote and produced the film with Chai Mi. It is produced by See-Ray Studio Production, the animation and visual art company Lei founded.

Maria A. Ruggieri, head of sales and acquisitions at Asian Shadows, called Lei a singular voice among Chinese filmmakers without any doubt. “His talent as an animator, his search for new representations of China, his love and care for the recreation of lost emotions and history are perfectly assembled one more time in ‘Vertigo Garden,'” she said.

Born in Nanchang, Lei earned a master’s degree in animation from Tsinghua University. He went on to teach experimental animation at CalArts Film & Video School and has been a professor at Tsinghua’s Arts and Design Academy since 2022. His short “Missing One Player” won a Prix Ars Electronica and a Silver Dove at DOK Leipzig in 2015.

His mid-length documentary “Breathless Animals” debuted in the Berlinale Forum in 2019 and was named best mid-length documentary at Iran’s Cinéma Vérité. His first animated feature, “Silver Bird and Rainbow Fish,” premiered in the Tiger Competition at the International Film Festival Rotterdam in 2022.

Founded in 2014, Asian Shadows sells Asian films by Asian directors worldwide. Its recent titles include Eddie Cahyono’s “Ibuku,” which won the Busan Award Special Jury Prize at this year’s Busan International Film Festival, and “The Seoul Guardians,” directed by Kim Jong-woo, Kim Shin-wan and Cho Chul-young, which picked up a Netpac Award special mention in Rotterdam. The company’s catalog also includes Wang Bing’s “Man in Black,” Soda Kazuhiro’s “The Cats of Gokogu Shrine” and Mouly Surya’s “This City Is a Battlefield.”

“Vertigo Garden” is the third Lei film handled internationally by the company, after “Breathless Animals” and “Silver Bird and Rainbow Fish.”

Manchester United sack Michael Carrick with Mauricio Pochettino appointment close

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Michael Carrick has officially been sacked by Manchester United after less than six months as the club’s permanent manager, with Mauricio Pochettino expected to replace him.

In a statement released on Thursday, United said that the club “has come to the extremely difficult conclusion that a change is necessary in order to achieve our ambitions” after a “disappointing start to the season” and that they “intend to appoint a permanent successor as quickly as possible”.

United added in tribute to Carrick: “Michael is, and always will be, a Manchester United legend. Just as he did as a player, he has conducted himself with the highest standards of dedication and professionalism throughout his time back with the club.

“His leadership and achievements last season will always be fondly remembered.”

Carrick took over as United’s interim head coach in January after Ruben Amorim’s departure and was promoted to the full-time job in May after qualifying for the Champions League last season.

He has failed to carry that momentum into the new campaign, though, and departs with United mired in the Premier League relegation zone having won only one of their first 10 matches and sitting 17th in the Champions League table. They also exited the Carabao Cup at the third round stage.

A 4-1 defeat by Como in Europe last month left Carrick on the brink and a 3-0 home loss to Bournemouth then initially looked as though it would be the end of the road, but he was spared the sack and retained for the visit to Chelsea, which the Red Devils lost 2-0.

A dramatic 3-2 comeback victory over Roma in the Champions League last week looked to restore some faith - at least temporarily - but a topsy-turvy performance against Aston Villa in a chaotic 4-4 draw, only salvaged at the death by Patrick Dorgu’s last-gasp equaliser, further underlined his side’s significant issues heading into the final international break of 2026.

United parted ways with Carrick following a meeting with director of football Jason Wilcox and CEO Omar Berrada held at Carrington, where, despite the intense speculation over his position, Carrick had continued to take training this week with United’s players not currently away on international duty.

Wilcox and Berrada had been in Monaco on Wednesday for crunch talks with United co-owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe, who controls the club’s sporting policy, where it was decided that a change in the dugout was needed to turn around their fortunes.

Pochettino is expected to replace Carrick and become the new manager at Old Trafford, despite only signing a four-year contract extension to remain in charge of the United States national team following the World Cup on home soil in the summer.

An official approach is believed to have been made to US Soccer, with Pochettino’s deal containing a release clause worth $10million (£7.5m). Pochettino is keen on the move and has spoken to United about their managerial position on multiple occasions in the past, with an agreement over personal terms reported to have been reached.

Former Chelsea and Tottenham boss Pochettino - who has seen off competition for the job from the likes of ex-Newcastle manager Eddie Howe and former Germany coach Julian Nagelsmann - would become United’s eighth permanent manager since Sir Alex Ferguson retired in 2013.

Singapore Airlines Reconsiders New Business Class Seat Design After Backlash

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last week, Singapore Airlines unveiled all-new cabins, with the most exciting developments being a new first class and new business class. The new cabins look great (nothing revolutionary, but a huge improvement), with one exception — the new business class seat colors. Not only do they look cheap, but they just seem completely mismatched to the rest of the cabin.

While the airline vehemently defended the color choices for several days, it seems that they’re at least now reconsidering it, though it remains to be seen if it’s too late to make a change…

On Wednesday, November 11, 2026, Singapore Airlines CEO Goh Choon Phong had a town hall with employees, as reported by The Business Times. During this, he was asked about the negative feedback the airline has received about the new business class seat design.

In response to this, he reportedly said “we’ll look at it, we’ll see what’s feasible.” So that signals some willingness to make changes, though admittedly with how far out seats are ordered, that’s not necessarily so straightforward, so I wouldn’t count on anything happening. He did, however, emphasize that customers will come to like the seats after experiencing its comforts for themselves.

At the town hall, Singapore Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Lee Lik Hsin said this was a good learning point for the airline, but indicated that he hopes customers will mind the colors less after experiencing the seats. He also reportedly said “I suppose, you know, it’s the 80-20 rule, you can’t please them all.”

At the time of launch, Singapore Airlines VP of Product Experience, Ong Wah Li, explained the new design was in response to customer feedback, and described it as “modern, fresh, yet elegant and refined, that’s suited to business travel.”

BMW Designworks was behind the design of the cabin, and CEO Julia de Bono defended the color choices at the launch event, describing them as “more refreshing and more progressive.” As she viewed it, “what I see when I look at it, it’s definitely like a nice suit, with the white and the metal really popping out, you know, it’s more like a suit with a sneaker, so a bit more modern and fresh.”

To state the obvious, the trim and finishes on a new business class seat are hardly the most pressing issues facing the world. However, Singapore Airlines prides itself in being perhaps the most detail oriented airline in the world, because management has an obsessive attention to even the smallest things.

So I can’t help but be a bit confused by all of this:

It also just feels to me like the mandate for the design of this product was off, as shared by the CEO of BMW Designworks. Premium travel demand is through the roof, and a lot of people in premium cabins aren’t traveling for business. For that matter, these cabins will be operating the world’s longest flights, so this isn’t a case of a business traveler hopping on a plane in a suit, and going straight to a meeting (at least that doesn’t make up a meaningful percentage of the traffic here).

Is “suit with a sneaker” really the vibe you want to go for with a new business class product that’s on an airline that’s known for its incredible warmth? It’s almost like the airline views warmth and modernity as being at odds with one another, which I don’t at all agree with.

‘Second ball’ may be a Sunday league mantra but Yates showed its top-flight relevance

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Midfielder’s old-school attributes came to the fore during Nottingham Forest’s win against Manchester City

Modern football is more systematised and data-driven than ever but some things never change. Winning the first ball is important but the second ball – the Holy Grail for Sunday league footballers – is often more so.

Nottingham Forest’s 3-1 win against Manchester City last weekend featured a classic example. Forest led 2-1 inside the last 20 minutes but City, lifted by Rayan Cherki’s sweet half-volley, were pushing for an equaliser.

Another City attack comes down their right: after Jair Cunha’s clearing header from a deep Matheus Nunes cross, the ball loops up in the air before descending inside the home penalty area.

Ryan Yates, Forest’s old-fashioned midfield general, is being pressed from behind by the Argentina international Enzo Fernández as the ball drops. Yates stands his ground, using his strength to hold off his opponent’s muscular challenge. Fernández tries to impose himself, too, but ends up on the turf.

Not only does Yates win that crucial one-on-one, by cleverly improvising an overhead kick to shift the ball away from danger he inadvertently begins a counterattack that arguably changes the course of Forest’s season.

Liam Delap shepherds the ball to Morgan Gibbs-White. Enzo Maresca’s team are hopelessly exposed and Gibbs-White devours the space in front of him with a driving run.

He lays a pass off to Delap, who takes one touch before swerving a sensational shot into the far corner for 3-1. It is a memorable move that will surely compete for goal of the month. But none of it would have been possible without the dirty work of Yates: a show of strength and desire from the captain at the other end of the pitch.

Oliver Glasner understands the importance of the moment and sprints to celebrate with his players. After a barren scoring run Delap suddenly has two goals in one afternoon. The home fans are delirious. Twelve seconds after Yates won the duel, Forest have a two-goal cushion rather than a desperate fight to maintain their lead.

The second ball is a pleasingly old-school term but remains of paramount importance. Visit your local amateur club and you are likely to hear it being shouted as a motivational mantra.

At grassroots level it’s a reminder to teammates that finesse and creativity are all very well but it’s hard work, desire and a refusal to let your opponent get the better of you that win matches.

And it remains part of the elite game’s lexicon. Premier League managers sometimes point to an upcoming opponents’ second-ball prowess – a back-handed compliment if ever there was one. The hidden meaning: “They feed off scraps at set pieces. They don’t create from open play.”

Kick off your evenings with the Guardian's take on the world of football

After seeing Southampton Under-21s lose to Wealdstone in the National League Cup a couple of years ago, I mentioned to the then Saints manager, Simon Rusk, that his goalkeeper had been shouting “second ball” throughout.

“To be honest,” Rusk replied with amusing frankness, “I’m not sure some of them even know what second ball means.”

Managers like relying on data. They like predictable outcomes. They prefer to remove risk, reduce chaos and exert control over as much of the game as possible. But while the outcome of a one-on-one duel can never be certain, the consequences can often be far-reaching. That’s why second balls matter.

Against City Yates was named in Forest’s starting lineup, and given the captain’s armband, for the first time this season. Glasner, asked how he had motivated his players, revealed: “Ryan Yates spoke. I played more than 500 games as a player, I’ve managed, I don’t know, 500 games … and I’ve never heard such an inspiring speech.”

Glasner’s captain backed up stirring words with action. If there was a sense Yates’s star was fading – that his attributes of graft, tenacity and box-to-box drive were losing currency – then it was emphatically extinguished at the City Ground.

Win the first one if you can. But it’s the second ball that often makes the difference.

Mondrio Raises $3.75 Million Seed Round to Connect AI Pricing Strategy With Deal Execution

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mondrio has raised $3.75 million in seed funding to connect two activities that often happen in separate systems: deciding how a business should price its products and carrying those decisions into individual sales deals. The Amsterdam- and San Francisco-based company is building an agentic pricing platform that turns customer conversations, product changes, and deal outcomes into recommendations—and helps sales teams put approved pricing into practice.

The round was led by Heartcore Capital, with participation from Pretiosum VC, Hersir Ventures, Cambrena Capital, Momo VC, and angel investors with backgrounds in pricing, configure-price-quote software, AI, and payments. Mondrio plans to expand its AI and engineering teams, add pricing experts, and strengthen its commercial presence in the United States and Europe.

A pricing strategy can look convincing in a spreadsheet and still break down during negotiations. Salespeople encounter objections the original research missed. Customers value features differently from what a product team expected. Discounts become routine, while the reasons behind them remain scattered across calls, CRM records, and approval requests.

Mondrio’s approach is to make those signals part of an ongoing process. Its platform analyzes quantitative data alongside qualitative information, including customer conversations and market feedback, to identify opportunities and recommend changes. Lessons from quotes, negotiations, and renewals then feed back into the next pricing decision.

That connection matters as AI changes the economics of software. A feature that invokes models or performs automated work can create variable costs each time a customer uses it. Packaging, usage allowances, and discounts therefore affect both the attractiveness of an offer and the cost of delivering it. Faster product releases also give companies more frequent reasons to revisit what they charge.

CEO and co-founder Gregor Biljardt said the opportunity lies in agents combining quantitative information with insights from conversations and markets. Mondrio is applying that combination to a commercial workflow in which an apparently small pricing decision can carry consequences across many subsequent deals.

The company’s Pricing Studio handles pricing design: packaging, pricing models, and price levels. Its published workflow moves through research, design, simulation, decision-making, and rollout, giving teams a way to test an offer before introducing it to customers.

Research can draw on customer surveys, willingness-to-pay information, and AI-generated personas. The platform also lists features for benchmarking, pricing simulations, experiments, and customer migrations. These functions address different questions: which features belong together, which customer groups value them, and how a proposed change could affect an existing customer base.

AI personas provide a way to explore hypotheses, but their responses are simulated evidence rather than observed customer behavior. The useful feedback loop comes from comparing those hypotheses with research and actual commercial outcomes as offers reach the market.

Once a team approves a pricing decision, it can flow into Mondrio’s Deal Engine. That handoff is central to the product’s design: a new price list should influence the quotes customers receive, instead of remaining an analysis that sales teams must translate into their own tools.

Deal Engine covers execution, including quote creation, discount guidance, approvals, and the information collected as a deal progresses. Mondrio describes a workflow in which an agent builds a quote using the current price list, evaluates it against policy, and routes exceptions for approval.

The distinction between a recommendation and an authorized action is important. Quotes that meet a company’s rules can move through automated approvals; exceptions can be escalated with supporting context, such as the effect of a discount on margins. The platform’s listed capabilities also include a customer portal, quote engagement tracking, deal insights, and electronic signatures.

This gives the system a potential role beyond reducing the effort of producing a sales document. Repeated objections, unsuccessful offers, or discount patterns can become inputs to pricing design. A company could use that evidence to distinguish a weak package from an isolated negotiation, then adjust its strategy while keeping deal-level controls in place.

Mondrio’s integration catalog shows how it intends to assemble that context. Listed connections include Salesforce and HubSpot for CRM information, Gong for customer conversations, Amplitude and Mixpanel for product usage, and billing systems such as Chargebee, Zuora, and Stripe Billing. DocuSign is listed for Deal Engine’s signing workflow.

These sources represent different views of the same commercial relationship. A CRM records the opportunity, a conversation platform captures what a buyer said, product analytics show usage, and billing records what the customer pays. Bringing those signals into pricing work can help teams assess whether an offer reflects customer value and actual delivery economics.

The catalog assigns integrations to different modules, so the presence of a connection does not mean every system supports identical functions. The broader technical proposition is to connect pricing decisions to the operational records that can explain their results.

Biljardt founded Mondrio in 2026 with Joep van den Nieuwenhoff, its chief commercial officer. The company offers two ways to use the platform: existing pricing teams can work with its AI capabilities, or customers can bring in Mondrio’s pricing experts for additional support.

The funding will support further work on agents, synthetic customer capabilities, and the connection between pricing strategy and deal execution. Mondrio also plans to add pricing architects, develop relationships with B2B technology companies and private equity firms, and lay the groundwork for expansion into manufacturing and professional services.

Mondrio’s bet is that pricing becomes more valuable when it operates continuously. The challenge will be turning fragmented commercial evidence into changes that teams can understand, approve, and execute—and then using the results to improve the next decision.
