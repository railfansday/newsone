Before ‘Band of Brothers,’ Tom Hanks Produced a Forgotten HBO Miniseries That Aged Perfectly

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the most impressive miniseries of all time is 2001's Band of Brothers. Executive produced by Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, the series is a compelling war drama that follows Easy Company, a part of the U.S. Army's 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment during World War II. Taking home 20 Emmy nominations and seven wins (including one for Outstanding Miniseries), Band of Brothers still holds up as being an authentic and powerful depiction of extraordinary historical events. But this wasn't the first time that Hanks had a hand in producing a show revolving around a true story. Just three years before, Hanks would also produce an HBO miniseries that is a must-watch today, especially for sci-fi fans who love the great beyond.

In 1865, science fiction writer Jules Verne penned a novel called From the Earth to the Moon. More than a century later, a team of Hollywood heavyweights would borrow the title for a 12-part television series depicting some of our country's most famous astronauts. The 1998 series was produced by Hanks, Ron Howard, Brian Grazer, and Michael Bostick — figures who created some of the most influential films of the 1990s. The miniseries tells the story of the Apollo program, which NASA ran during the 1960s and 1970s.

Although the episodes are dramatized, with stunning visuals and special effects by Ernest D. Farino, each episode explores one of the risky missions these astronauts undertook. Hanks introduces each episode, and then the viewer gets to dive into each mission, which is told from different perspectives. This allows every episode to showcase a variety of themes. A particularly brilliant installment is "The Original Wives Club" in Episode 11, which was directed by Oscar winner Sally Field. This episode shows how the wives of these astronauts lived through the Apollo program, leading to a behind-the-scenes look at the stress they endured while their husbands were put in danger, as well as the pressure they experienced in such public-facing positions.

From the beginning stages of the Space Race with the Soviet Union to a pseudo-documentary-style narrative in Episode 12 of the final lunar Mission (Apollo 17), every hour is as gripping as the last. While we're able to celebrate the triumphs of these astronauts as they travel where no man has before, From the Earth to the Moon never shies away from also depicting the tragedies that befell these men. We get to witness the horrific fire onboard Apollo 1 and the infamous Apollo 13 disaster, which ensures that the series is always honest in showing how truly dangerous these missions were. Although it's horrible to think of these traumas occurring to real people, the authentic storytelling makes the series even more harrowing and fascinating to watch.

The writers of From the Earth to the Moon were also intent on not just rehashing events that had already been depicted in movies prior to its release. For example, the 1983 film The Right Stuff portrayed the events of Project Mercury, so it's just briefly summarized in Episode 1. And then there's the 1995 movie Apollo 13, which was also directed by Howard and starred Hanks.

To avoid any repetition, and to ensure viewers still felt invested in the storytelling, From the Earth to the Moon tells the story of the mission from the eyes of the media covering the event. This type of creative storytelling allows the miniseries to feel fresh. While there were a few characters invented for the series, enough famous real-life figures, including Jim Lovell (Tim Daly), Buzz Aldrin (Bryan Cranston), Neil Armstrong (Tony Goldwyn), and Alan Shepard (Ted Levine), allow the show to fall into a familiar, yet captivating authenticity.

At the time of its release, From the Earth to the Moon was a critical darling, bringing home 17 Emmy nominations and three wins, including Outstanding Miniseries (just like Band of Brothers would do a few years later). It would also cement Hanks as someone who could tell historical stories in a respectful and intriguing way (paving the way for future projects such as The Pacific and Masters of the Air).

What makes From the Earth to the Moon so compelling nearly three decades later is its ability to capture both the wonder of space exploration and the enormous personal risks behind humanity's greatest achievements.

From the Earth to the Moon is available to stream on HBO Max in the U.S.

The pros and cons of using an Amazfit smartwatch

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazfit watches are easy on your wallet, but they can’t do everything.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

Amazfit smartwatches are widely considered to be good alternatives to the bigger brands. They're generally cheaper, with some models like the well-reviewed Amazfit Bip 6 available for as little as $80. But as you would expect, there are concessions to be made to justify the lower price.

The line from the Chinese company Zepp Health includes a wide range of smartwatches to suit any lifestyle, from casual wearers to serious athletes. However, they run on a proprietary OS that limits app access, and some models have limited storage. The companion app also isn't the most intuitive.

On the plus side, Amazfit smartwatches focus on AI-powered software using its Zepp Coach and offer long battery life, with some models lasting for up to 25 days per charge. You can also find deals on Amazfit smartwatches, which are already affordable to begin with.

Before you commit, it helps to know what you're getting and what you're giving up. Here's why so many people consider Amazfit in the first place.

Amazfit smartwatches are sleek, with designs that resemble other popular models. You'll find square-shaped options like the Bip 6 and Active 2 (Square) that look like an Apple Watch. There are also round-faced ones like the Amazfit Active Max and the Amazfit T-Rex 3, which is designed for outdoor adventurers and resembles a Garmin watch in its aesthetic.

Another advantage is cross-device compatibility. You can pair an Amazfit smartwatch with either iPhone or Android since it uses its own operating system and isn't tied to either. Many trusted outlets also rank Amazfit watch models among the best budget smartwatches, citing accurate health tracking along with their stylish designs. They also note that integrations like the AI Coach, Bluetooth calling and Alexa command support are top-notch.

Amazfit smartwatches also offer over 100 sports modes, from strength training and running to gardening and Tai Chi. And they have all the important sensors you'd want for data, including heart rate, sleep, temperature, GPS and more.

Amazfit is a solid budget smartwatch brand you can continue to use even if you swap phone platforms. There's also no subscription tier for any of the data it tracks and reports, and these watches are a step up from the best cheap fitness trackers, with larger screens for at-a-glance information and control on your wrist.

Amazfit smartwatches run on the proprietary Zepp OS, so you can't access third-party apps the way you can with Wear OS watches. Instead, you're limited to a few productivity apps and games from the Zepp Store. The watches do integrate with third-party platforms like Strava, TrainingPeaks, Komoot and Runna, but you can't access Google Wallet, Google Maps, Uber, Starbucks, Gmail or banking apps. There's no Spotify app either, though you can listen on your phone and use the watch to control playback or use a remote app to mirror Spotify on the device.

Beyond apps, the software isn't as intuitive as others or as easy to navigate as those of better-known brands. You'll see unfamiliar terms like PAI, Amazfit's personal activity score and Biocharge, which is basically its equivalent of a readiness score. Some models have limited storage, which could cause issues with storing workout data or apps. The Amazfit Bip 6, for example, has 512MB of storage.

On longevity, Amazfit offers only three years of software and security support for its smartwatches. They won't stop working after that point, but it's worth factoring into the decision if you plan to hold onto the smartwatch for a while.

For a more intuitive experience with better app support, you might prefer going with affordable models from brands like Fitbit and Garmin instead, or even an older-model Samsung Galaxy Watch or Apple Watch, even if they're in their last years of software support. While Amazfit smartwatches aren't the best smartwatches you can buy, you will get reliable health tracking, long battery life and sleek designs for a great price, with a few caveats you might be able to live with.

Alabama Power spent more than $6 million on ads last year — and customers can be billed for half of them until 2029

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This article originally appeared on Inside Climate News, a nonprofit, non-partisan news organization that covers climate, energy and the environment. Sign up for their newsletter here.

On a busy election day, the Alabama Public Service Commission requested that Alabama Power voluntarily stop billing customers, effective immediately, for television commercials and other ads it buys to promote various aspects of its business.

Alabama Power is the largest electricity provider in Alabama, with territory covering about two-thirds of the state. Though it is a monopoly, the company spends millions each year on advertising, touting its reliability, customer service and other aspects of its business.

In recent months, the company has launched a series of television commercials touting a rate freeze it announced late last year amid heavy criticism over rising electricity prices.

A state law passed earlier this year will prohibit Alabama Power from including the costs of those commercials in customers’ electric bills, meaning the powerful utility will have to find all of the dollars in corporate profits. But the new law won’t take effect until 2029.

Currently, Alabama Power is allowed to bill customers for half of its non-public safety advertising costs.

During Tuesday’s meeting, PSC President Cynthia Lee Almond moved to “request” that Alabama Power voluntarily begin excluding those costs immediately.

“I don’t see any reason why we shouldn’t go ahead and take steps now to begin that process, rather than waiting until Jan. 1, 2029,” Almond said during the meeting.

Almond then made her motion, which was not on the meeting agenda, but came after the regular report from the commission’s electricity division.

“I move that the commission request that Alabama Power consent to exclude all advertising expenses from retail rate setting until after Dec. 31, 2028, when the new law takes effect,” Almond said. “Assuming Alabama Power consents, this interim measure would be reflected in its [Rate Stabilization and Equalization] filings for 2027 and 2028.”

Commissioners Jeremy Oden and Chris Beeker IV voted yes after a brief discussion.

According to the company filings with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, Alabama Power spent more than $6 million in general advertising last year, roughly $4 per customer. It’s unclear from the federal filing how much of that total was passed on to customers or how much might be impacted by the new prohibition.

Alabama Power did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Daniel Tait, executive director of Energy Alabama, said the move was a boon to Alabama Power customers, who faced some of the highest electric bills in the country in recent years.

“We are grateful for the leadership of President Almond and the full commission for acting to end customers paying for Alabama Power’s advertising,” Tait said. “Once in place, Alabama will have one of the strongest customer protections in the nation on this front.”

This will be among the last actions of the commission under its current construction before a major overhaul takes effect.

In January, the commission will expand from three members to seven. Outgoing Gov. Kay Ivey has appointed four new commissioners, who will take office in January, including the PSC’s two first-ever Black members.

Oden and Beeker will also leave the commission in January after being defeated in Republican primaries. Cullman County Sheriff Matt Gentry will take Oden’s Place 1 seat, and former state auditor and PSC Commissioner Jim Zeigler will replace Beeker in Place 2.

Zeigler, a perennial candidate in Alabama across several decades, will return to the commission 48 years after leaving the PSC. He served one term from 1975-1979, during an era of skyrocketing energy costs and protracted political battles over electric rates between Alabama Power and then-Gov. George Wallace.

Almond will be the sole remaining member of the current commission.

The new seven-member commission will be overseen by the state’s first secretary of energy, who will be selected by Gov.-elect Tommy Tuberville. The new secretary will set the commission agenda, and overruling the secretary will require a vote of five of seven commissioners.

Tuberville has not yet announced his pick for secretary of energy. The only statutory requirement is that the secretary “must have experience in utility regulation or energy policy or a related field.”

A mixed up grid and rain on the forecast has set up the prospect of a thrilling Sao Paulo Grand Prix on Sunday.

For the third year in a row, Lando Norris will start on pole position in Brazil with new F1 world champion Kimi Antonelli alongside him on the front row.

Unusually though, there will be plenty of attention further down the field as power unit changes for Red Bull's Max Verstappen and Ferrari's Lewis Hamilton mean they start at the back, while Ferrari's Charles Leclerc starts 14th for a 10-grid place penalty.

And Mercedes' George Russell, who was left frustrated to not qualify first after a strategic error from the team, starts seventh following a five-place grid penalty for a new internal combustion engine, so several of F1's biggest names will begin the Sao Paulo Grand Prix - lights out at 5pm on Sunday with build-up from 3.30pm, live on Sky Sports F1 - out of position.

"Not since the infamous debut of Daikatana has the release of a single game generated so much incensed indignation from early buyers." Our review of Pool of Radiance: Ruins of Myth Drannor didn't hold back. The 2001 dungeon crawler contained, our review said, "A multitude of outrageous bugs," including ones that corrupted saves, made quest items disappear, and most infamously deleted system files when you uninstalled the game.

SNEG's rerelease, available now on Steam and GOG, doesn't have those issues. It adds widescreen, controller support, adjustable combat speed, tooltips, an action bar, and sharpened visuals. If, like me, you skipped this one back in the day because of its reputation for being a hassle to play—even with the eventual patches and community fixes—now it's smooth and problem-free. I've played the opening hours without a single key item vanishing or save file going corrupt.

Don't get the wrong idea, though. Ruins of Myth Drannor isn't Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines. This isn't an RPG masterpiece held back by its buggy release. It's a simplistic turn-based dungeon crawl that's mostly notable for being a spiritual successor to the first Gold Box D&D game, Pool of Radiance, and for adapting the then-new third edition of D&D's rules.

The 3.0 rules were a huge step forward at the time. Any character could choose any class, and Armor Class went up instead of down—you can't imagine how much of a drag it was explaining to new players that lower AC was better than high AC, and ideally you wanted a negative number. It also formalized the central mechanic of rolling a d20 and adding a number to beat a difficulty score. If you've played 5th edition, you'll recognize a lot of its DNA here.

Which is why it's a shame Ruins of Myth Drannor isn't a complete adaptation of those rules. You can't play a wizard—sorcerer is the only option for arcane casters—or a halfling or a gnome. You choose which class to improve when you advance a level, but don't get to decide where your skill points go or what feats to take. A couple of years later, The Temple of Elemental Evil would adapt the 3.5 rules update much more fully. Ruins of Myth Drannor feels rudimentary by comparison.

It's also not much of a story. Set 10 years after the events of Pool of Radiance, a new magical pool has opened in the ruined elven city of Myth Drannor so the evil sorceress and dracolich on the box art can get up to some shenanigans, and Elminster has sent a party of adventurers in to stop them. There's one fun twist to this otherwise bog-standard plot, and it's that you're not that first party of adventurers. You're the B-team sent as backup, but a magic portal mishap means you arrive too late to save the A-team and have to take on their quest.

Which explains why four level-one nobodies now have to clear out hundreds of orcs and skeletons from samey dungeon rooms. It's a slow-paced slog, especially in Myth Drannor's main halls, earning a drip-feed of experience points and gold coins from zombies and ghouls, hoping an orc doesn't score a crit and drop one of your party members in a single blow.

I get ambushed by skeletons, then realize my cleric is perfectly positioned to hit them all with Turn Undead. I meet a weird slug monster, try talking to him and find out he's friendly. I block doors to protect my sorcerer, while rushing enemy spellcasters before they get their big area-of-effect blast off.

It's junk food D&D, but then I mostly play D&D at a table covered in pizza and beer anyway. Between more demanding games, this is an ideal low-stakes guilty pleasure and I'm glad it's finally back.
