Amy Hennig's Marvel 1943: Rise of Hydra hasn't been cancelled, Skydance reportedly says, as rumours swirl to the contrary

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

You'd be forgiven for forgetting that Amy Hennig's superhero game Marvel 1943: Rise of Hydra exists, but apparently it's still in development despite its many troubles and rumours to the contrary.

Reassurance comes from Skydance's Slack work chat where, Kotaku says, executive producer Lu Gigliotti told the team they'd spoken "directly" with Skydance leadership and could reassure staff the game wasn't going to be cancelled. "They asked me to make sure you all hear that directly," Gigliotti reportedly told staff. "There is no truth to these claims."

This follows a recent rumour that the action adventure title was potentially going to get the axe in the near future. The rumour originated from a Marvel 1943 fan account, and while the original X post has since been deleted, it didn't stop it from spreading online, including amongst the game's own development staff.

According to Kotaku's report, Rise of Hydra is close to its alpha state, despite seemingly five years of development. The game is said to have had multiple issues through its long development.

The current build is reportedly "light on gameplay", featuring only one interaction button. This is understood to be the result of Rise of Hydra having reportedly been originally envisioned as a Netflix game. According to the same report, the game is late and over budget, which resulted in cuts to its scope and scale throughout development.

Marvel 1943: Rise of Hydra was fully revealed in 2024, and is the debut title of Skydance New Media, the studio headed by veteran designer and writer Amy Hennig, who's famed for her work on the Uncharted series of games.

Though the game was originally planned for release in late 2025, it was later delayed to early 2026 and then again to "beyond early 2026". Earlier this summer, Paramount Games Studio boss Shawn Kittelsen said Rise of Hydra won't be out in 2026, but assured fans it remains in development. According to the report, Rise of Hydra was later delayed to 2027 and beyond.

Tensions are particularly high at the moment following the colossal recent Paramount and Warner Bros. merger, which has already resulted in some job losses at Paramount Games, a group that oversees the game's development, among other projects.

When (or if) Ring’s security camera drone flies into our homes in January, you won’t get the option to pilot it yourself. The Ring Drone Cam relies entirely on an “AI Pilot” system to follow pre-programmed routes and navigate autonomously around obstacles, with no manual control option available — and an embarrassing demonstration with CBS News might reveal why.

The video, broadcast on CBS Mornings and published on Threads, shows Ring founder Jamie Siminoff allowing technology reporter Jo Ling Kent to manually fly an early version of the drone via a mobile app with integrated controls.

Siminoff: “Yeah, so we can load manual controls.”

The demo then shows the drone crashing twice while being manually piloted. In the first instance, Ring reportedly told CBS News that the drone’s collision avoidance technology malfunctioned, and declined to share the video recorded by the drone prior to crashing. CBS News caught the second crash on camera, with the footage showing the drone colliding with a wall after failing to respond to manual controls. According to Siminoff, this occurred because the drone lost internet connectivity — which it also relies on to fly autonomously.

“If it loses connectivity, it’s supposed to stop and land,” Siminoff said in the video. “It did not there.”

Ring declined to show us the app in our own demonstration of the Drone Cam, and said it only works on pre-programmed flight paths. It seems that while manual flight controls were included at some stage of development, Ring won’t be making the feature available to customers when the Drone Cam launches in January.

“We explored a lot of features throughout the development, but autonomous flight to patrol points using the AI Pilot is the best customer benefit and experience,” Ring spokesperson Sam McGee said in a statement to The Verge. Ring didn’t respond to our question regarding whether the decision to not include manual controls was made following the CBS News demo.

Gut bacteria plays a huge role in vaccine side effects

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria that make a protein called flagellin keep low-grade inflammation going, and the people carrying more of them ran a fever.

For some people, a COVID shot means a night of chills and a headache. Others barely notice theirs, and the split holds, dose after dose. To some, this has always looked like luck.

Researchers have now found part of the reason, and it was in the gut before the needle went in. Certain bacteria there build whip-like tails out of a protein that the immune system reads as a warning.

People whose gut bacteria made more of that protein were the ones who ran a fever.

Kelsey Huus and her colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Biology Tübingen followed 171 healthy adults in Germany through an mRNA vaccination against SARS-CoV-2.

In people they can only show that gut microbes and fever go together, so they tested cause in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

If gut bacteria set the risk of a fever, that risk may be something a person can change, though that has not been shown in people.

In an interview with Earth.com, Ruth Ley, the study’s senior author, said the bigger idea isn’t really about vaccines at all.

“More broadly, I think the interesting idea coming from the study is that the gut microbiome helps set our baseline inflammatory ‘immune tone,'” she said. “That could potentially be relevant well beyond vaccination.”

Participants took their own temperature three times a day for two weeks, before and after their shot, and wrote every reading in a diary. The diaries came to 7,079 readings from 171 people.

The median was 97.7°F (36.5°C), which is below the 98.6°F (37°C) most of us were taught to expect.

Instead of using a clinical cutoff, the team compared each person against their own normal for that hour of the day.

Anyone whose temperature climbed 1.4°F (0.8°C) or more above their own baseline counted as fever-high. That line left 72 people above it and 99 below.

People who said earlier doses had given them a fever ran hot here too. The two groups also differed on a stool test for gut inflammation. This was higher in the fever-high group two weeks after the shot and tilted the same way beforehand.

Many gut bacteria grow whip-like tails to move around. Those tails are built from a protein the immune system treats as a sign that bacteria are on the move. The protein is called flagellin.

The team read the RNA in each stool sample to see which bacterial genes were switched on. In the fever-high group the flagellin genes were running harder, before the vaccine as well as after. Those gut microbiomes carried a wider variety of flagellins to start with.

One genus accounted for most of that. Waltera, a member of the Lachnospiraceae family, made 67.2 percent of the flagellins that were more active in fever-high people. The family as a whole made 76.3 percent.

That genus was also the only one reliably more common in the fever-high group.

The same thing turned up in a second group of people. Among 1,014 Canadian adults in the CHILD Cohort Study who answered questions about side effects after a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, the ones who reported any had more Waltera in their stool.

Measuring flagellin isn’t the same as showing it does something, so the team ran two experiments.

First they grew human colon cells in a dish and bathed them in stool extracts from the participants. Extracts from fever-high people pushed those cells to release more of an inflammatory signal, and the amount tracked how much active flagellin each sample held.

Then they transplanted human microbiomes into germ-free mice and vaccinated them. A mouse with an inflammatory reaction doesn’t run a fever. Its temperature drops instead.

Mice carrying a fever-high person’s gut bacteria dropped further, and how far they dropped tracked the fever that person had run.

Mice bred without the two receptors that detect flagellin still reacted, though less, so flagellin carries part of the signal and something else carries the rest.

The animal work was done at Georgia State University, and the gut cells and the additive cultures were handled with Institut Pasteur in Paris.

Diet changed the same thing. In culture, the common thickener carboxymethylcellulose helped Waltera grow, and raised the amount of inflammatory flagellin it made.

In tanks seeded with stool from two donors, that thickener on its own barely shifted those bacteria. A mix of it and 13 other additives of the kind that go into ultra-processed foods raised both the bacteria and the flagellin they made.

Mice fed a Western-style diet, high in fat and low in fiber, reacted harder to the vaccine than mice on standard chow. Their temperatures dropped further, and a day later they carried more of a second inflammatory signal.

Among the 171 people, 46 said they ate a plant-based diet. Temperatures rose less in that group than in the 125 omnivores.

The gap held even after the team adjusted for gender, age, body mass index, dose, fever medication, earlier fevers and starting antibody levels.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most, Ley pointed at how fast diet changed what the bacteria produced in the laboratory. “That gives us a plausible way in which diet could influence inflammatory immune tone through the microbiome,” she said.

None of this reached the part of the vaccine response that protects you. Fever-high participants carried somewhat higher antibody levels before and after their shot, but the booster lifted both groups by the same amount,. Diet made no difference either.

The mice ended up in the same place. Germ-free animals given fever-high or fever-low microbiomes finished with matching antibody levels, and the ones that cannot detect flagellin made antibodies anyway.

“This suggests that the mechanisms that make someone feel ill after vaccination are at least partly distinct from those responsible for generating protective antibodies,” Ley told Earth.com.

The human diet data are the thinnest part of the work. Participants picked from broad categories rather than filling in a food diary, and Ley said as much herself.

“But the dietary findings in people are much more preliminary: we only have broad, self-reported dietary categories, so we cannot conclude that diet caused the differences in fever that we observed,” she said.

The group was narrow by design. Everyone was a healthy adult between 18 and 40, with a median age of 25 years. Anyone immunocompromised, living with a gut disorder or recently on antibiotics was left out.

Huus and her co-authors wrote that their definitions of fever were built for research and may not meet the standard for a clinical diagnosis.

Ley said she wants to know first whether the same pattern shows up in other groups of people.

“One important next step is to determine whether the relationship between microbial flagellin and vaccine reactions holds across different populations, age groups and vaccines,” she said.

Her team also wants to know whether a person’s inflammatory tone can be shifted at all, which would take a controlled diet trial.

For now, a fever after a shot may say as much about the gut it arrives in as about the shot itself.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

Popular Weight-Loss Drugs May Have a Surprising Effect on Your Brain

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Popular weight-loss drugs may offer benefits that extend beyond metabolism, potentially helping preserve memory and other cognitive abilities.

Medications used to treat obesity and type 2 diabetes may also influence memory and other cognitive abilities. But whether drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide can actually protect the brain remains uncertain, particularly after major clinical trials failed to demonstrate a benefit in patients with early Alzheimer’s disease.

In Novo Nordisk’s EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials, semaglutide did not slow Alzheimer’s progression, even though it produced changes in several biological markers associated with the disease. Researchers from the University of Gothenburg and the University of Copenhagen have now examined the broader evidence in a review published in Science, exploring whether these medications could still help preserve cognitive function in other groups of patients.

GLP-1 medications influence biological processes involved in both metabolism and brain function. Animal experiments suggest they can affect learning and memory, but studies involving humans have produced inconsistent results. Analyses of patient health records have linked GLP-1 treatment to a reduced risk of dementia among people with type 2 diabetes, although these observational findings cannot establish that the drugs directly provide protection.

The potential benefits may differ depending on the cause of cognitive difficulties. People with obesity or type 2 diabetes sometimes experience problems with attention, planning, and mental processing speed. These difficulties are frequently associated with metabolic disturbances and poor vascular health, raising the possibility that treatments targeting these underlying problems could help maintain cognitive abilities.

Stina Börchers, the review’s first author and a researcher affiliated with both universities, believes that identifying the right patients and treatment timing will be essential.

“We need to find out whether these treatments can help people with obesity or diabetes preserve their cognitive abilities, particularly if treatment begins before cognitive problems become established. It is also important to identify which patients might benefit,” says Stina Börchers.

A central unanswered question is whether any cognitive benefits arise from direct effects on the brain, improvements in metabolic health, or both. Better regulation of blood sugar and other metabolic processes could indirectly support brain function, while GLP-1 signaling may also influence neural processes more directly. Separating these mechanisms could help researchers determine which effects are most relevant to preserving cognition.

Christoffer Clemmensen, the review’s senior author and a research group leader at the University of Copenhagen, sees an opportunity to use increasingly advanced obesity medications to investigate these connections.

“Obesity medicines are becoming increasingly sophisticated, offering new opportunities to understand how metabolic regulation influences brain function. By disentangling the direct and indirect effects of these drugs on the brain, we may ultimately be able to develop treatments specifically aimed at preserving cognitive function,” says Christoffer Clemmensen.

The researchers are calling for clinical trials that measure memory and other cognitive abilities from the beginning of treatment. Such studies could help establish whether these medications preserve cognitive function, which patients are most likely to benefit, and whether starting treatment before cognitive difficulties appear makes a difference.

They also caution that potential benefits must be weighed against risks in older or frail patients. Rapid weight loss and declining muscle mass can reduce physical functioning, potentially limiting the overall advantages of treatment. Future studies will need to account for these effects when assessing whether obesity medications can support long-term brain health.

Reference: “Obesity pharmacotherapies and cognition” by Stina Börchers, Giulia Mazzini, Henrik Zetterberg and Christoffer Clemmensen, 5 November 2026, Science. DOI: 10.1126/science.aek9987
