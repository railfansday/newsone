Entertainment 360 Buys Influencer Firm Brigade As It Expands In Creator Economy

The talent management firm's acquisition of the Max Stein-run boutique adds a client roster of Substackers and podcast players.

Entertainment 360 has accelerated its drive into the creator economy by acquiring Brigade, the creative management firm focused on digital talent.

Terms of the deal were not disclosed, but the acquisition brings elite digital tastemakers, including Substack writers, podcasters and influencers, to the Los Angeles-based talent management company run by a collective of partners as it moves beyond traditional film and TV representation.

Brigade founder Max Stein will become a partner at Entertainment 360 and bring his team and clients with him to help the combined company tap a growing influencer universe and manage and monetize celebrity creators coveted by brand marketers.

“The ways audiences discover and engage with entertainment have changed, and so have the needs of the talent building careers today,” the Entertainment 360 partners said in a statement. “Max recognized this evolution early and he and his exceptional team have built a remarkable business representing exactly the kind of original, forward-thinking talent we have always sought to work with. Bringing Brigade into 360 expands what we can offer our clients and strengthens our ability to create extraordinary opportunities for artists across an increasingly diverse entertainment landscape.”

Entertainment 360 will look to Brigade to help sign digital talent fostered by Stein and others to brand deals and partnerships, and new business opportunities in publishing, podcasts and traditional media.

“I’ve always been drawn to people with a singular point of view, whose work and ambitions don’t fit neatly into one category,” said Stein in his own statement. “Our clients have built remarkable careers by following their own taste and instincts, and our job has been to help them turn those ideas into lasting businesses. Entertainment 360 shares that philosophy and brings an extraordinary depth of experience, relationships, and resources. Joining forces gives our clients access to many more opportunities, while allowing us to preserve the personal approach that has always defined Brigade.”

The Brigade acquisition comes as YouTube stars, podcasters and other digital creators increasingly rise to the fore in Hollywood and a fast-shifting entertainment ecosystem. Brigade’s clients include fashion writer Leandra Medine Cohen; Feed Me newsletter writer Emily Sundberg; Chris Black and Jason Stewart, How Long Gone podcast hosts; stylist Danielle Goldberg; Karley Sciortino, the I Want Your Sex writer and producer; TV personality Zanna Roberts Rassi; and After School newsletter founder Casey Lewis.

The acquisition follows Entertainment 360’s recent hires and expanding into lifestyle and digital business representation in an evolving entertainment industry.

Connecticut solar projects directly support Veterans Affairs campus

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Connecticut Department of Veterans Affairs (DVA), Connecticut Green Bank and their partners recently marked the completion of solar installations serving DVA’s main campus and skilled nursing facility in Rocky Hill, Connecticut.

The projects will deliver renewable electricity and reduce energy costs at the campus. The Green Bank developed the projects through its Solar Marketplace Assistance Program Plus (Solar MkAP+). Kearsarge Energy has partnered with Connecticut Green Bank as the long-term owners of these projects as well as nine others and will also oversee operations and maintenance. Greenskies Clean Energy was the engineering, procurement and construction contractor for the two DVA installations.

“To Gov. Lamont and your administration, thank you for having the vision for the GreenerGov CT initiative, providing reliable, environmentally friendly and cost-saving energy for residents and businesses in Connecticut, which is one of your top priorities, along with countless others, enabling Connecticut’s 3.6 million residents to thrive,” said Ron Welch, a retired brigadier general with the U.S. Army, and commissioner of the DVA. “Our team at the Connecticut Department of Veterans Affairs thanks you for your full support of this solar field project and for all you do through your significant partnerships to sustain and improve the environment and the quality of life for Connecticut residents and beyond.”

The two systems have a combined capacity of 1.404 MWDC and are projected to generate approximately 1.84 million kWh of electricity annually. The systems are projected to offset 85% of electricity use for the skilled nursing facility and 35% of the electricity use at the main campus building.

“This project brings together two important commitments: advancing sustainability in state government and supporting the people who have served our nation,” said Michelle Gilman, commissioner of the Department of Administrative Services. “DAS is proud to support this project and the broader Greener Gov CT partnership.”

The two arrays built at the Rocky Hill campus are 604.8 kWDC and 799.2 kWDC.

American Airlines Plans To Bring Back First Class Mini-Meals On Shorter Flights—Starting With New York–Chicago

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This is a very good change. A flight can take up much of your afternoon without crossing American’s 900-mile meal threshold, especially when you include getting to the airport, boarding, and a connection. A small meal makes first class more worthwhile.

JonNYC reports that American Airlines is bringing back light bites and mini-meals on 700–899-mile domestic first class flights, starting on NYC/ORD.

American used to have a service between a basket of packaged snacks and a full meal. Before the pandemic, shorter flights could get something plated that would tide you over.

In 2016 I reported plans for plated morning items, including yogurt and breakfast cake. And my 2018 menu coverage included Lite Bites cheeses with cracked black pepper cheddar, cave-aged cheddar, Appalachian Tomme, and smoked Gouda.

American’s mainline standard is a snack basket on 500–899-mile flights during meal times, with meals starting in the 900-mile range.

United actually raised its meal threshold from 800 to 900 miles in January 2024. American would be moving in the other direction, offering something more substantial on flights where Delta and United generally hand out snacks.

Alaska is still the standard to beat. They already feed passengers on shorter flights, and I’ve found their domestic first class food better than competitors’.

American also used to have meal “exception markets”: shorter routes that got meals for competitive reasons. My January 2020 service guide listed Chicago–Dallas, Chicago–Boston, Chicago–New York, Los Angeles–Denver, and LaGuardia–Atlanta, among others.

I like a broad 700–899-mile rule better than making customers depend on whether their particular city pair merits an exception, though the exception markets got real meals and some were even shorter like Chicago–Washington National at just 612 miles which still wouldn’ qualify for a lite bite under this distance band.

The airline’s product turnaround has been surprising, and surprisingly fast. Their returning coach food reservation program, announced with an initial Dallas–Fort Worth–Los Angeles test, should help passengers get the item they actually want. Mostly, though, I like that American seems to be loading enough food that there’s still something left when the cart reaches my aisle.

Mortgage Demand Jumps 6.2% as Rates Fall For Fourth Straight Week

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mortgage applications rose last week, as interest rates decreased for the fourth week in a row, hitting their lowest levels since mid-September, as a positive jobs report and the results of the midterm election bolster the outlook.

For the week ending Nov. 6 the Mortgage Bankers Association's Market Composite Index—a measure of total mortgage loan application volume—increased 6.2% on a seasonally adjusted basis from one week earlier.

The Purchase Index, which measures the volume of mortgage applications to purchase a single-family home, increased 8% from one week earlier after seasonal adjustment. Purchase applications were down 18% from a year ago.

According to MBA calculations, the average contract interest rate for 30-year fixed mortgages with conforming loan balances is 7.21%, a slight decrease from last week's rate of 7.3%. Rates averaged 5.99% a year ago.

Mortgage rates dropped to 7.18% last week from 7.21% the week before, according to a separate survey from Freddie Mac.

“Mortgage rates declined for the fourth straight week,” said Joel Kan, MBA’s vice president and deputy chief economist. “The declining trend in rates helped to spur stronger application activity, although the pace of purchase and refinance applications remain considerably lower than a year ago."

In recent weeks, high interest rates had pushed more buyers toward riskier Adjustable Rate Mortgages. However, this past week, ARM applications decreased to 10.9% of the total. applications, down from 11.4% the previous week.

FHA and VA loans saw "particularly strong growth" this past week, said Kan.

The share of FHA loan applications decreased to 15.5% from 16% the prior week. The VA share of total applications increased to 12% from 11.2% the prior week. And the USDA share of total applications decreased to 0.5% from 0.6% the week prior.

The refinance share of mortgage activity remained unchanged at 39.8%, but was 57% lower than the same week one year ago, as homeowners remain locked into their lower interest rates.

October's positive jobs report, which showed that the U.S. economy added 165,000 nonfarm jobs in October, has increased the odds that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates when it next meets in December.

CME Group's FedWatch has the likelihood of an additional rate hike at around 77%.

The positive jobs report news was compounded by broad Democrat wins in the midterm elections. Democrats, many of whom ran on a message of affordability, won the majority in both the House and the Senate. Voters aged 18 to 34 and men aged 18 to 49 had ranked housing costs as a top political issue in a rcent CNBC survey.

Trump's former Federal Housing Commissioner Frank Cassidy called the midterms a "wake-up call."

"Everyone needs to get serious about tackling and addressing housing affordability. It can't be ignored anymore," he told Realtor.com®.

SpaceX/xAI is developing a custom version of Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale AI system that replaces conventional NVLink cable assemblies with a massive 84-layer printed circuit board (PCB), in part for use in orbital data centers, reports SemiAnalysis. The system is codenamed Kessel and retains 36 Vera CPUs, 72 Rubin GPUs, and nine NVLink switch trays, but introduces a different solid-state interconnect architecture. While SpaceX/xAI will continue to buy Nvidia hardware directly from the company, Elon Musk's company will source other components independently, which could give it greater control over its hardware supply chain.

The Kessel system retains the fundamental configuration of Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 platform, which comprises 72 GPU packages distributed across 18 compute trays and nine NVLink switch trays. However, instead of Nvidia's conventional arrangement with internal NVLink cable cartridges, SpaceX plans to position compute and switch trays back-to-back around a passive PCB midplane. The architecture resembles a smaller version of Nvidia's Kyber design with 576 AI GPUs (albeit in 144 packages), which also relies on a PCB-based interconnect rather than conventional cable assemblies, SemiAnalysis notes. Interestingly, the design is aimed at both terrestrial and orbital deployments, according to the analysts.

Arguably the most distinctive feature of Kessel is its 84-layer PCB midplane, which uses ultra-low-loss M10 copper-clad laminates (CCLs). By contrast, Nvidia's Kyber architecture relied on a backplane based on a considerably more complex 156-layer PCB featuring M9 CCLs. The reduction in layer count by nearly half could substantially improve manufacturability and production yields, but the more advanced copper-clad laminate will almost certainly increase material costs. In any case, an 84-layer PCB is a device that is exceptionally hard to build.

The move to a PCB midplane could simplify system assembly by eliminating numerous high-speed cable connections, but it would not necessarily reduce manufacturing costs. An 84-layer PCB that relies on ultra-low-loss M10 laminate is an exceptionally expensive component that requires sophisticated manufacturing processes and precise quality control. To put an 84-layer PCB number into context, modern high-end server motherboards use 15 – 20 layers, whereas server development boards use more than 20 layers to virtually eliminate any electrical issues with the board itself. Furthermore, manufacturing yields of an 84-layer PCB could become a major obstacle, as defects in internal layers may render an entire board unusable. Consequently, the economic viability of Kessel will depend on whether savings from cable elimination and independent component procurement can offset the cost of its complex midplane.

Perhaps more importantly, Kessel represents a departure from Nvidia's conventional system-level procurement model. Instead of purchasing Nvidia's assembled L6 compute boards or even L10 trays that Nvidia plans to sell with its Vera Rubin generation, SpaceX reportedly plans to acquire AI accelerators, CPUs, switches, and networking components directly from Nvidia and procure other components from third-party suppliers (one can wonder how easy it will be for SpaceX/xAI to procure memory amid today's shortages). This would enable SpaceX to select its own PCB manufacturers, component vendors, and manufacturing partners, optimize its hardware for its infrastructure (which will be important for orbital deployments), and perhaps reduce intermediary costs. Meanwhile, the company would assume greater responsibility for system design, validation, manufacturing quality, and supply-chain management, which is something that most hyperscalers have been doing in recent years.

For Nvidia, the implications are less straightforward. Although direct sales of CPU and AI GPU packages could reduce the company's revenue from complete compute-board assemblies, the arrangement could also encourage other large customers to develop their own Nvidia-based systems and eventually deploy more Nvidia-based machines, something that may be important in a world where custom silicon developed by hyperscalers is rapidly gaining traction. However, to make this happen, Kessel must be a smashing success that will offer clear advantages over systems designed by Nvidia.

Siri co-founder backs Helix for its voice ID tech

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For years, websites have relied on technology like CAPTCHA to prove that the user on the other end is human. More recently, social media platforms have turned to photos or videos to verify users’ identities. Now, a startup called Helix is betting that voice technology can do the same job, without requiring users to hand over sensitive personal information.

The voice technology, which can run directly on a user’s device, has attracted backing from Siri co-founder Dag Kittlaus, who will also act as an advisor.

The company raised $6 million in funding in a round led by Chicago Ventures, with participation from BAM Ventures, Aglaé Ventures, Corazon Capital, Tusk Ventures, Yonder Ventures, Rule30, and Brown Alumni Group.

The startup was founded by Alex Oberg, Anna Dorofiyenko, and Mike Hall in 2025. Oberg previously worked at Ticketmaster, which acquired his startup; Dorofiyenko led data science teams at Live Nation and Zillow; and Hall has worked at Nike and Tinder, where he helped develop the initial matching algorithm.

At Ticketmaster, Oberg played a role in creating Verified Fan, which is designed to keep bots out of the platform. As AI advanced, he started thinking along the same lines for a new solution.

“As AI started to come up in the last two years, I was realizing that it’s going to make it increasingly difficult to determine who’s human and who’s not on the Internet. Identity on the Internet’s always been about proxies for identity, right? It’s your email, it’s your phone, it’s your IP address, it’s your device fingerprint, it’s your device ID,” Oberg said.

He noted that with the rise of AI agents like Instinct and Muse, the industry needs to rethink human identity on the internet and rely on biometric signals like voice, which is where Helix comes in.

Its core technology can run directly on a device to determine whether someone is human and estimate their age. The company is also running pilots to explore whether voice can be used a biomarker to detect conditions such as adrunkenness or distress.

The company can deploy its technology to replace or supplement CAPTCHA and help block bot attacks. Helix said that because its model can run locally even on an iPhone 13, the cost of deploying the technology is minimal. The startup said one of its customers is Zeel, an in-home healthcare provider that uses Helix’s tech to verify the identities of providers visiting customers’ homes.

Oberg said verifying workers, such as cab drivers, is another use case the startup is exploring. He also said that age-verification regulations for social media platforms around the world are forcing users to trade privacy for access. Helix’s voice technology, he said, could offer an alternative.

“There’s like an avalanche of regulation coming down because essentially society has decided children should not be able to use the Internet as adults. That has created a bit of a strawman argument that everyone needs to trade their privacy for the sake of security and child safety. We are that company that’s saying, nope, don’t agree. It’s a four-second voice challenge,” he said.

The CEO also emphasized the limitations of Helix’s technology, noting that it cannot reliably detect whether someone is lying. He added that its voice technology doesn’t identify individual speakers and claimed that even if the company stores a voiceprint, it cannot be used to reconstruct a person’s voice.

AI-powered fraud is also a growing problem in phone calls. But Helix believes its technology isn’t yet capable of reliably detecting AI agents during live calls.

Siri co-founder Kittlaus said that voice identification technology has finally reached a point where it works reliably, positioning Helix to scale as AI-powered fraud increases. He also said that Helix’s work to compress its models gives the startup a cost advantage.

“What folks at Helix are doing is, instead of teaching computers how to understand speech, these guys are understanding, what the speech of the speaker actually means, what’s going on with them, who they are they lying, are they impaired? That side of it is fascinating to me within the larger picture that I just described,” he told TechCrunch.

AI visibility can hide evidence gaps that weaken buyer recommendations as requirements get specific, making decision readiness a crucial GEO metric.

For much of the past two years, the AI search conversation has focused on visibility. Teams want to know whether their brand is mentioned, where it appears in recommendations, how often competitors surface, and whether they are being cited across ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Google, and other AI surfaces.

Those are useful metrics, but I have increasingly questioned whether they tell us enough about the corresponding business outcome.

Nearly two years ago, I began using the term “eligibility gates” to describe the detailed decision criteria that determine whether a company or product remains qualified as a buyer moves through the decision process. I later expanded on that idea when I wrote Decision Coverage: whether an organization has exposed enough information and evidence for AI to evaluate, compare, qualify, and confidently recommend.

That led to two related arguments tied to previous articles. In my article Beyond Content Parity, I argued that using tools and processes that simply match competitor content may create parity without creating any decision advantage. In Beyond Brand Sovereignty, I argued that the knowledge AI needs often exists beyond the marketing site, scattered across product, sales, support, engineering, legal, customer success, and external sources.

A recent project allowed me to test those ideas against an actual business problem.

The company did not have an AI visibility problem. It frequently appeared in AI-generated consideration sets. The concern was what happened afterward, when prospective buyers continued the conversation and began asking more specific questions about requirements, workflows, integrations, outcomes, evidence, and competitive differences.

The question was not “How do we get seen in AI?” It was, “Why, with all this visibility, is our lead pipeline shrinking?” The reality we found was that as AI took a prospective buyer further down the consideration set, they became less competitive.

To prove my thesis and identify the problem, we created 38 detailed, criteria-based prompts across several product categories and buyer segments, and evaluated them on five AI surfaces, yielding 190 responses.

These were intentionally different from broad “best vendor” prompts. They reflected the kinds of requirements buyers use to narrow a field: workflow fit, integrations, regulatory requirements, interoperability, operational conditions, analytics, platform breadth, and other decision criteria. I had to create a detailed explanation of their purpose as diagnostic-only because the brand and their SEO agency argued that no human would ever use that complex a prompt. We actually found that some prompts were even longer after talking to actual procurement managers.

The initial numbers looked excellent – even better than their current visibility scorecard. Our research found the company was mentioned in 98.4% of evaluated responses and appeared as either a primary recommendation or shortlisted option 93.2% of the time. For many GEO programs, that would be the headline of the week and champagne for everyone. The brand was almost always visible and almost always treated as a serious option. But visibility was not the problem we were investigating.

When we evaluated those same responses against the decision criteria in the prompts, 73.7% had weak or missing evidence supporting important claims or recommendations about the company. In 15.8% of runs, an important association, decision criterion, competitive distinction, or discovery signal contained what we classified as a Significant or Critical Gap. The more we refined our evidence-scoring criteria, the higher this number rose.

The critical finding was that AI systems generally knew the company belonged in the conversation. However, they were far less consistent at substantiating why it should remain there once the buyer began applying specific eligibility criteria. That is the distinction that matters and explains their declining lead pipeline.

There is a meaningful difference between asking which vendors operate in a market and asking which vendors can satisfy a specific combination of technical, operational, regulatory, and business requirements.

A model may know a company is a legitimate vendor, understand its main products, and have enough general information to put it on a shortlist. But once the buyer asks how an integration works, whether a workflow is supported, what happens under a particular operating condition, what limitations apply, or what evidence supports an outcome claim, the information requirements become more demanding. Those are not merely additional keywords. They are evidence requirements.

That explains how a brand can achieve a 98.4% mention rate and 93.2% primary-or-shortlist rate while still having weak or missing evidence in nearly three-quarters of the responses. The numbers are not contradictory. They measure different stages of the decision process.

No one can dispute that AI visibility gets the company into the consideration set. Decision readiness determines whether AI has enough information to keep it there.

See also: B2B Buyers Choose A Vendor Before They Reach Out – 3 Ways To Be Visible When It Counts

One of the more important findings was that weak evidence did not necessarily mean the company lacked the capability. In many cases, the capability appeared to exist, but the available information did not explain or substantiate it well enough to satisfy the AI surface’s confidence level.

A marketing bullet might claim an end-to-end process without ever showing the actual process, connections, or how the data flows between systems. We found efficiency claims but did not have any “proof” from internal tests or customer testimonials of the efficiency gain.

Sometimes the information was simply fragmented. A model might need to connect a product page to an integration page, then to another interoperability page, before it could infer how the pieces satisfied the buyer’s requirement. While an AI system could or should be able to make those multi-part connections, each additional inference creates another opportunity for incomplete representation or for a competitor with clearer evidence to become the safer recommendation.

We also found that the strongest content was not necessarily the longest or most buzzword-integrated, but rather content that made the relationships among capability, workflow, buyer requirements, and outcome explicit.

An interesting finding was that only first-party evidence was not inherently a weakness. Many claims lacked third-party validation, but this was not necessarily flagged as a defect. We found that authoritative product documentation may be the best evidence that a feature or integration exists. However, every claim involving superiority, effectiveness, adoption, scale, measurable outcomes, or competitive differentiation did benefit from independent corroboration by customers, industry, government, analysts, or other parties.

That means some evidence gaps are not content-writing problems at all, but does anyone else agree with the evidence?

See also: Schema, LLMs & The Low Bar For ‘Evidence’ In GEO

The findings reinforced the concern I raised in my earlier work about how easy-button AI content-generation systems create “me-too content parity.” As I explained to this brand, most of the GEO efforts to date were what I would call SEO-adjacent GEO. It is a newly rebranded SEO process that leverages AI speed for traditional content-gap analysis. Teams look at competitor coverage, People Also Ask questions, common prompts, and pages receiving citations, then create content to close those gaps. There is nothing inherently wrong with that. In this case, it appears to have worked extremely well at establishing this company’s category presence. The limitation appears when everyone says roughly the same thing, and you don’t offer details.

If every vendor claims to be end-to-end, highly integrated, AI-enabled, scalable, flexible, and focused on efficiency, those statements may help establish that each belongs in the category. They do much less when the model has to determine what “end-to-end” covers, what “integration” connects, what the AI actually does, or what evidence supports the efficiency claim.

Parity can help a company qualify for the conversation without providing enough information to survive deeper evaluation.

See also: AI Gives You The Vocabulary. It Doesn’t Give You The Expertise

Running the prompts was relatively straightforward. There wasn’t a tool or off-the-shelf framework for what we were trying to measure. We needed to build a multi-variable evidence scorecard capable of determining whether the model had enough support to evaluate the company against the criteria that actually mattered in the buying decision.

That meant defining what counted as strong evidence, what first-party documentation was enough, and when independent corroboration became important. The bigger challenge was thinking beyond the “we are great” organizational mindset to determine when an evidence gap or omission was serious enough to affect consideration or selection. That turned out to be even harder than getting SEOs to agree on ranking factors.

Those judgments then had to be translated into a repeatable scoring system and applied consistently across the prompts and five AI surfaces. It also required conversations beyond content. Sales had to help identify criteria and objections that affect deals. Product had to validate capabilities and limitations. Customer-facing teams helped identify real outcomes. In some cases, customers themselves were the best source for understanding what evidence mattered.

This is why I do not believe there will be one universal GEO evidence scorecard that every organization can download. The methodology can be reusable if you can honestly define the decision criteria and what constitutes sufficient evidence, then evaluate whether AI can connect the brand to those criteria.

As we evaluated eligibility criteria and evidence requirements, many findings no longer looked like conventional content gaps. Looking at this massive evidence gap matrix as one manager exclaimed, “that no human will ever read,” it was obvious this would become a team effort to make the changes. Content teams cannot make up content, nor will they necessarily know how everything works.

Some of the knowledge required to close these gaps is organizational in nature. We had to pry it out of product, engineering, and sales, and then run it through marketing and compliance. In some cases, we had to loop in external and partner relations when stronger third-party or attributable proof was required beyond what we could create.

The content team still has a critical role: organize that knowledge, make relationships explicit, create coherent evidence paths, and expose information in ways humans and AI systems can understand.

Most importantly, this project validated and reinforced my belief that measuring only the first AI response is insufficient. True, those broad PAA-like prompts are valuable for generating a list of options worth considering. The problem is the current tools cannot maintain state like a human workflow and aggregate the sequence of prompt turns most users leverage. Each subsequent prompt digs deeper into the criteria for consideration, maintaining preferences, and memory, and culminating in the multivariate result that I affectionately call prompt math.

This means that GEO measurement needs to become more sophisticated and account for both visibility and decision readiness. Many tools now have answer accuracy monitoring, which is a step forward. I argue that we need to put more effort into the element that is much closer to the business problem organizations should actually care about.

There is an obvious danger here because once marketers conclude that AI systems need evidence, we will probably see an explosion of “evidence-shaped content”: longer pages, superficial diagrams, weak surveys, synthetic research, and AI-generated case studies created primarily because someone believes those formats will influence an LLM, just like the explosion of Reddit submission services. That misses the point. The objective is not to manufacture artifacts that appear persuasive to AI, but to provide real content that reduces uncertainty and risk for the buyer.

If the information would not help a knowledgeable customer make a better decision, it probably should not exist merely to improve a GEO score.

As noted, the company had already achieved what many GEO programs would consider an amazing success: 98.4% visibility and 93.2% primary-or-shortlist consideration, yet 73.7% of those same responses had weak or missing evidence. Unfortunately, our analysis could not prove that this evidence gap caused every lost sale or every subsequent AI exclusion. What it does show is that a visibility-only measurement model would have missed the weakness we were asked to investigate.

Any company can succeed in entering the conversation but fail to offer enough credible evidence to remain qualified as the conversation becomes more specific.

Is Pilates Effective For Weight Loss? Instructors Share How To Incorporate It Into Your Routine

It’s nearly impossible to scroll through social media right now without seeing some celebrity or influencer rave about the perks of Pilates. But even with all the buzz surrounding this popular practice, details are a little fuzzy on whether Pilates is an effective workout for weight loss.

First off, know that the health and fitness benefits of having a Pilates practice are so much more than that. This form of exercise is known for improving your posture, breathing, flexibility, and core strength. As Claudette Sariya, CPT, a personal trainer and fitness educator based in New York City, notes, “any movement that you can enjoy regularly and consistently can help you lose weight.”

So, doing Pilates (or any workout for that matter) to stay active paired with a nutrition plan is likely to help you with weight loss. But it probably won't help expedite your weight loss in the same way that, say, lifting heavy weights would. (We'll get into why!)

The focus of Pilates is not weight loss, experts point out. "Pilates should be considered part of your self-care routine," says Jacquelyn Brennan, CSCS, pilates instructor and co-founder of Chicago-based Pilates and Coffee. "We all need to move our spines more, move our joints through every range of motion, and everyone needs to be breathing better."

All of those healthy practices together can aid weight loss, though. Here's everything to know about doing Pilates when you're trying to lose weight.

Meet the experts: Gabbi Berkow, RD, CPT, is a nutritionist and Pilates instructor. Jacquelyn Brennan, CSCS, is a Pilates instructor and co-founder of Chicago-based Pilates and Coffee. Claudette Sariya, CPT, is a personal trainer and fitness educator based in New York City. Sonja R. Price Herbert is a certified Pilates instructor and founder of Black Girl Pilates. Nofar Hagag is a Pilates instructor and creator of the Nofar Method.

One form of Pilates isn't superior to another to drop pounds, experts agree. "For weight loss, I don’t think one type of Pilates is necessarily best,” says Berkow. Yet, there are different flavors—and finding one that you enjoy and will keep coming back to can, in turn, help you commit to a fitness routine and lose weight.

When someone mentions Pilates, they're often referring to Romana’s Pilates, which features exercises that Joseph Pilates (the founder) developed, explains Gabbi Berkow, RD, CPT, a nutritionist and Pilates instructor. Some studios are dedicated to classical or Romana’s Pilates (which is what Berkow is trained in) but there are studios that feature different Pilates-based workouts, which means they use Pilates-inspired moves but don't follow the classical version to a T. Some Pilates classes take place on a mat (a.k.a. mat Pilates), while others occur on a reformer, which is basically a sliding, spring-loaded platform.

You also might hear the term "athletic Pilates," but that is usually a description of the pace, says Sonja R. Price Herbert, a certified Pilates instructor and founder of Black Girl Pilates. These classes are designed to be fast-paced and high-intensity, and may even have some high-intensity interval training (HIIT) exercises and elements in the mix, which would make them better for weight loss due to the way HIIT training can help you burn more calories during and after the workout.

But if you really want to maximize your Pilates practice for weight loss and strength, it’s a good idea to use both the reformer and Cadillac machines, says Nofar Hagag, a Pilates instructor and creator of the Nofar Method. “By incorporating cardio-focused movements—such as squats, planks, and Pilates burpees, your heart rate increases, boosting calorie burn and metabolism,” she says. “These high-energy moves are paired with strength-training exercises, like side leg lifts and abs teasers, to build lean muscle and improve body composition.” (Reminder: Loading your muscles with resistance is key for muscle growth—and more muscle mass helps you burn more calories at rest and, in turn, lose weight.)

What you also want when it comes to Pilates and weight loss: an instructor who knows what they’re doing, is committed to safe movements, and who will challenge you, says Berkow. “You want to make sure your Pilates class is a workout. It shouldn’t be just a series of slow, easy stretches. Pilates has to contribute to your total daily calorie burn for it to make a difference in weight loss,” she says.

If you’ve ever gone to a class, you know how insane the core work is (seriously, the famed Hundreds exercise is not for the faint of heart...or abs).

While you cannot spot-reduce fat, Pilates can help you learn how to properly engage your pelvic floor and deep core muscles, says Brennan. "Pilates will teach you how to engage your muscles correctly, which will lend to increased activation during core exercises and lead to [the appearance of a] 'tighter tummy,' " she says.

However, to see abs definition, you do have to lose the fat that sits on top of your muscles, which requires proper nutrition, says Berkow. Think: a diet rich in whole, fresh foods and a limit on overly-processed foods and snacks.

There is some research to suggest that you can lose weight doing Pilates—but it's tough to give an exact number of pounds considering so many individual factors play into weight loss, such as genetics, sleep and stress, lifestyle habits, and more.

A meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials published in Frontiers in Physiology in 2021 found that pilates “dramatically” reduced body weight and body fat percentage in adults with overweight or obesity. And the longer the person practiced Pilates, the bigger the weight loss. Older research has had mixed results, though, with one small study concluding Pilates was indeed helpful for weight loss, while another found it had no impact on weight loss in post-menopausal women.

Again, traditional Pilates isn’t a modality designed for weight loss. Generally, traditional classes do not pack enough of a cardio or strength training component, Herbert says: “It is not a steady-state [cardio modality] like running, jumping, or walking. Pilates will, however, deliver far more benefits than just a lower number on a scale that may indirectly help with weight loss. For instance, Pilates involves breath work, encouraging deeper belly breaths that are in direct contrast to the shallow breathing many of us do on the regular. “Shallow breathing leads to increased cortisol levels, which contributes to weight gain. One could say that through pilates and learning to breathe better, you’d have lower cortisol—and thus, stress—levels, something that can help you lose weight,” Brennan says.

Feeling better, both mentally and physically, will also encourage you to take care of your body, says Berkow—and Pilates can definitely be a key part of achieving an overall weight-loss goal.

World Children's Day 2026: 6 ways to help kids feel heard

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The backpack hits the floor, and "How was your day?" gets a one-word answer: "Fine." Kids don’t always say what’s on their mind, but a little time and attention may help them open up.

World Children’s Day is a good moment for parents, caregivers, and teachers to ask kids how they’re feeling and listen without trying to fix anything. The day honors children’s rights, including the right to be heard.

Here’s a look at what the day is about, plus a few activities that can help kids feel understood, at home or in the classroom.

World Children's Day is a global day of action for children, run by UNICEF. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 20. This year’s theme is “My day. My rights. My future.” It invites children to tell world leaders what kind of future they want.

The day celebrates children and the rights they’re born with. In many places, children “take over” high-profile roles in media, sports, and government for the day. Landmarks light up blue to show support for children's rights.

November 20 marks two milestones for children’s rights. On this date in 1959, the UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of the Child, a statement of shared ideals. Thirty years later to the day, it adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1989, a treaty that nations can commit to follow.

The Convention sets out the rights every child has, including the right to be safe, to play, and to have their opinions taken seriously. Nearly every country has formally agreed to follow it, and it’s the most widely ratified human rights treaty in history.

Countries around the world mark World Children’s Day. Many also celebrate a separate Children’s Day, most notably June 1, International Children's Day.

Some countries do pick dates of their own. Turkey celebrates on April 23, and India on November 14. The dates differ, but the idea is the same: it’s a day dedicated to the youngest members of a community.

You don’t need a big event to mark World Children’s Day. A meta-analysis on children and teens linked understanding emotions with better social skills and fewer behavior problems. Kids can build that understanding in everyday moments, like the ones below.

Kids may not know they have rights, but those who learn about them may feel more confident speaking up. UNICEF’s child-friendly version of the Convention explains the rights in plain language.

Try: Read one or two rights together, then ask which feels most important to them.

Not every child has the words for how they feel. Invite them to sketch their day from morning to night, then have them walk you through it.

If they’re up for it, ask which part they loved most and which part felt hard. A drawing gives kids something concrete to point to as they talk, and that can feel easier than answering a broad question such as “How are you feeling?”

Try: At home, pin the drawing to the fridge so your child can talk about it again if they’d like. In class, create an “Our Day” wall where kids can choose to share their drawings and spot the moments they had in common.

When a child melts down, get curious about the feeling underneath. Help them put it into words: “It looks like you’re frustrated that it’s not your turn. Is that right?” Then listen carefully to their answer before you reassure them. “You’re fine” can sound dismissive, even when you mean well. When you talk about feelings this way, kids often learn to do it too.

Try: Post a chart of faces showing common feelings where kids can see it, and invite them to point to or say how they feel at the start of the day. Welcome any details they want to share.

💙 Once a big feeling has a name, here are coping skills for kids to try.

Set aside a few minutes at dinner or morning circle time for everyone to share one high, one low, and one thing they’re thankful for. Take a turn yourself so kids feel more at ease sharing.

Just listen as kids share, and try not to talk them out of a hard feeling. Kids tend to manage feelings better when you respond with warmth instead of brushing them off.

Try: Pass around a talking object, such as a small stuffed animal. Whoever holds it has the floor, grown-ups included.

Kids can sit, lie down, or stand, and they can stop whenever they’d like.

Try: Belly breathing is a gentle place to start. Have your child lie down with a stuffed animal on their belly and watch it rise and fall as they breathe slowly.

Pick a book where a character is going through difficult feelings, then pause and ask, “What do you think she’s feeling?”

One player who struggled to fill gaps in Wanderers’ attack in the 2024/25 season was Carlos Forbs. The Old Gold brought him in on loan from Ajax, where he had struggled to hit the ground running after an exciting youth career at Manchester City.

Forbs’ time at Molineux did not go as planned. However, over the past 18 months or so, the Portuguese winger has hit flying form and is catching the eye in Europe.

At Wolves. Forbs played only 11 times across all competitions and contributed with one assist. He played just 16 minutes of Premier League football over the second half of his 2024/25 season and upon the return to Ajax, they decided to cash in.

Forbs was sold to Belgian side Club Brugge, and the 22-year-old has started to show why he was so highly rated in his youth. He performed well in his debut season with 11 goals and 12 assists in 51 games across all competitions and has taken things to another level this term.

In 15 appearances, Forbs has already notched 13 goal contributions with five goals and eight assists. He is starring in the Jupiler Pro League, but his success is really shown by his form in the UEFA Champions League.

Scott Parker’s West Ham Training Plans Hit By International Call-Ups

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Much has been made of the two-week international break giving Scott Parker his first proper opportunity to work with his West Ham players. Having taken charge just before the Cardiff City match, the new Hammers manager has barely had time to get his feet under the table, let alone implement his preferred tactics.

However, the truth is that Parker’s supposed two-week opportunity on the training pitch is nowhere near as useful as it sounds. West Ham have had ten first-team squad members called up for international duty, leaving the manager without several players who would ordinarily be involved in his starting XI.

Mads Hermansen, Tomáš Souček, Konstantinos Mavropanos, Manor Solomon, Joël Piroe and Mohamadou Kanté have all been selected for senior international duty. Edvin Austbø, Divine Mukasa, Airidas Golambeckis and Finlay Herrick were also named in age-group squads, although Herrick has subsequently withdrawn from England’s Under-20 squad, with the FA citing ‘injury’ as the reason.

That leaves Parker with a rather makeshift group of players to work with, although it should at least provide opportunities for others to press their claims for a starting place.

Assuming everybody is fit and available, the following is a possible starting eleven Parker could assemble on the training ground during the international break.

It’s not a bad group of players by any means, and the likes of Lewis Orford and Josh Ajala should welcome the chance to impress their new manager. However, it’s a long way from the team Parker is likely to select when West Ham face Preston North End.

Even something as straightforward as drilling the defence is going to be difficult. Whilst Morato can slot into a makeshift back four alongside Kilman, the likelihood is that Mavropanos will be Parker’s preferred centre-back when everybody is available.

And even if Mavropanos were to suffer an injury, I’d expect Golambeckis to be considered ahead of Morato. Unfortunately, both players are away representing their countries, meaning Parker cannot even work with what I suspect will be his first-choice defensive partnership or its most likely alternative.

It’s a similar story further forward, where Mukasa, Kanté, Austbø and Piroe are all missing. These are players Parker will need to assess and integrate into his preferred system, yet he’ll have precious little opportunity to do so before the Preston match.

Of course, there are positives. Parker can still work on his overall shape, passing patterns and defensive organisation, whilst giving some of the younger players an opportunity to impress.

But the idea that West Ham’s new manager has been handed two uninterrupted weeks to transform his team is somewhat misleading. He has time on the training pitch, certainly, but not with many of the players he’ll actually need when the Championship resumes.
