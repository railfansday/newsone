Quentin Tarantino's Western Thriller Masterpiece Is Officially Free on Streaming

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Brian VanHooker is a Pop Culture Writer specializing in Celebrity Interviews and In-Depth Oral Histories in the areas of Comedy, TV, Film, Cartoons, Comic Books, 80s/90s Nostalgia, Fatherhood, Retro Games and Food (especially Pizza). He was the Senior Features Writer at Cracked and a Staff Writer at MEL. He has written for Inverse, Inside Hook, Maxim, Men's Health, Fatherly, SFGate and more. He's the co-creator of the comic book/cartoon Barnum & Elwood and The Tramp, an award-winning comedy pilot starring John O’Hurley. He also hosts a TMNT interview podcast called Turtle Tracks and was once called a “Good Guy” by Mr. T.

As far as Quentin Tarantino movies go, just about everyone loves Pulp Fiction. Reservoir Dogs and Once Upon a Time… in Hollywood are also widely regarded, while the Kill Bill films and Django Unchained each have a pretty fervent fanbase. But with so many great entries in his filmography, it’s inevitable that at least a few of his movies would get lost in the shuffle. One of those often overlooked films is his 2015 Western thriller, which may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s still an excellent film that combines the strengths of two of his previous entries.

The Hateful Eight, which is currently free to stream (with ads) on Tubi, takes place in the 1870s in the wintry Wyoming Territory. Kurt Russell is John "the Hangman" Ruth, a bounty hunter bringing in Daisy Domergue (Jennifer Jason Leigh), a gang member with a big price on her head. He’ll collect his bounty in the town of Red Rock, with a stopover at a place called Minnie's Haberdashery on the way, but Ruth’s driver (James Parks) has to stay ahead of an impending blizzard.

On the way to Minnie’s, Ruth’s carriage picks up two unexpected passengers, both of whom lost their horses in the snow and will surely die in the blizzard. First there’s Major Marquis Warren (Samuel L. Jackson), a fellow bounty hunter and former Union Army general​​​​​​​ who keeps a personal letter to him from Abraham Lincoln in his breast pocket. The second unexpected passenger is Chris Mannix (Walton Goggins), the new sheriff of Red Rock and the son of a Confederate war hero. While Ruth is suspicious of both of them, he decides to let them join him on the way to Minnie’s.

Once they arrive at Minnie’s, however, Minnie isn’t there. Instead, the haberdashery has apparently been left​​​​​​​ in the care of a Mexican man named Señor Bob (Demián Bichir) while Minnie is visiting her mother. Also present are three other men​​​​​​​ that Warren, a frequent guest of Minnie’s, doesn’t recognize. As the entire group is snowed in for the night, it’s eventually revealed​​​​​​​ that nobody is quite who they say they are and bloodshed ultimately ensues.

Like I said, The Hateful Eight is not top-tier Tarantino. Tarantino’s best work has generally come when he’s charting new territory for himself. Inglourious Basterds​​​​​​​ was his first war movie, and with its heist-like plot and unabashed joy in rewriting history, it showed what Tarantino could do with that genre. Django Unchained was his first Western, and, with its heavy influence from Spaghetti Westerns and Tarantino’s trademark talkative, over-the-top characters and depiction of extreme violence, it showed what a Tarantino Western looks like.

The Hateful Eight doesn’t do that. In fact, it can pretty easily be summed up in classic Hollywood this-meets-that fashion: The Hateful Eight is Django Unchained meets Reservoir Dogs.

It being another Western with broad, loud, talkative characters, it has the same feel as Django Unchained. Originally, The Hateful Eight was a Django sequel called Django in White Hell, but Tarantino found that Django didn’t fit the story. And while Django isn't in it, it’s clear that this movie is in the same ballpark.

As for the Reservoir Dogs part of it, it is narratively similar, with it being tightly contained primarily in one location, with a bunch of untrustworthy characters who don’t really know each other very well. It’s also a bit of a whodunit, just like Reservoir Dogs is with its “Who’s the rat?” plot thread.

None of this is to say that The Hateful Eight isn't a good movie — not at all. The Hateful Eight is an expertly crafted film which begins with a slow burn and delivers surprise after surprise once the bullets start flying. The movie blends a lot of genres, with elements of a Western, a thriller, and even a bit of broad comedy, but it's most effective as a mystery. Not only does the plot slowly unravel as Ruth tries to figure out who he can trust, but each of the characters does, too​​​​​​​.

Speaking of those characters, there are no good guys in the movie, but there are a lot of bad guys you end up rooting for because they are incredibly entertaining to watch. Samuel L. Jackson’s character is joyously funny, and Kurt Russell’s hangman is a mean grouch with a strange but consistent set of principles. Also, Walton Goggins, who was previously in Django, racks up another memorable Tarantino character in this movie. (Let’s hope he shows up in Tarantino’s 10th flick and takes a break from those damn Walmart commercials already).

While it stays firmly within previously charted territory for Tarantino, The Hateful Eight is very entertaining and very funny. It has all the hallmarks of a great Tarantino movie, with lots of fun characters, over-the-top violence, and unexpected plot twists. It may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s a really good time.

This Photographer Built a DIY Digital Rangefinder Using Two Old Canon Cameras

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This Photographer Built a DIY Digital Rangefinder Using Two Old Canon Cameras

Earlier this year, photographer and creator Mr. 50mm built a DIY digital rangefinder camera using parts he salvaged from a FED 5 Soviet-era rangefinder and a Panasonic GF3 Micro Four Thirds camera. Mr. 50mm is back with an even better DIY digital rangefinder, and this time it’s APS-C.

Unlike the Micro Four Thirds-based camera Mr. 50mm dubbed the “RGM-79” earlier this year, the new “C7M” uses a larger APS-C image sensor. Mr. 50mm is using the imaging pipeline from the original Canon EOS M mirrorless camera, which Canon unveiled way back in 2012.

The Canon EOS M features an 18-megapixel APS-C CMOS image sensor, up from the 12.1-megapixel Micro Four Thirds sensor Mr. 50mm salvaged from a Panasonic GF3.

A DIY digital rangefinder requires two donors, though. Rather than the FED 5 rangefinder Mr. 50mm used before, he’s sticking with two Canon cameras this time around. The donor rangefinder is the Canon 7, a rangefinder series that started in 1961 with the original Canon 7 and ended in 1967 with the Canon 7sZ.

Although Mr. 50mm hasn’t finalized everything or released a build video yet — it’s in the works — the C7M sure does look impressive thus far. Mr. 50mm is especially impressed with the Canon 7’s finder, which he describes as a “marked upgrade compared to a Fed camera.”

“The bar wasn’t that high, but the Canon 7 does a lot of things very, very well. You get a brighter finder, you get projected and selectable frame lines, and they’re parallax-corrected,” the builder explains.

Further, the Canon 7 uses a Leica M39 screw mount, which is great news for Mr. 50mm. It’s a widely used mount with a ton of lens options on the market.

While the Canon 7 doesn’t have many controls, that isn’t a downside for Mr. 50mm.

“It doesn’t have that many dedicated controls, which helps reduce the amount of controls I need to either port or figure out if I’m okay to live without. In the end, the digital camera really just has to be able to do two things. It has to accommodate the rangefinder that I’m gonna put on top, and it has to be able to accommodate the mount that I’m using,” he explains.

“Unfortunately, it isn’t just as easy as disassembling an EOS M and then jamming on new parts,” Mr. 50mm explains. “There [were] quite a few modifications that needed to be done to the camera to make this work, which I’ll discuss probably in depth in another video.”

Ultimately, as the video above shows, he solved the biggest challenges. He says the C7M is “mostly done” and, impressively, it only took a couple of weeks to build from start to finish. Despite a damaging drop during the build, it came together pretty well.

There’s always a catch, though. There are some rangefinder calibration issues left to figure out, and not everything aligns perfectly yet. Mr. 50mm says he needs to trim back some more material from the EOS M’s parts, although he isn’t sure how much they have left to give.

“Eventually, once things get a bit more ironed out, I’ll draft up a tutorial on how you can actually make your own,” the photographer says.

So while the C7M isn’t totally finished yet, it looks very cool and even more promising. Digital rangefinder cameras are expensive, but building one using donor parts and 3D-printed components makes the format significantly more accessible, assuming someone is comfortable with the build process itself.

Although there isn’t a build tutorial yet, it’s still worth considering the potential cost to build a C7M. The Canon EOS M body is around $260 used, not much more than a Canon 7 rangefinder body. If someone has access to a 3D printer, printing the other parts won’t cost much. That’s much less than a brand-new Leica digital rangefinder, for sure.

Kubernetes Multi-Tenancy 2026: Comparing Namespaces, Capsule, and vCluster

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 21 • Originally published at blog.saaro.net

The more teams work on a Kubernetes cluster, the louder the question about proper isolation becomes. Platform teams in 2026 face a fundamental decision: Should multiple teams share the same cluster – and if so, with what level of strictness? The answer determines not only the security architecture but also operational costs and developer team velocity.

The CNCF Annual Survey 2025 found that 82 percent of container users run Kubernetes in production [2]. With growing adoption, the pressure to share clusters efficiently without teams interfering with each other also increases. Three approaches have become established in 2026: simple namespace isolation, soft multi-tenancy with Capsule, and virtual clusters via vCluster.

The classic approach is logical separation via namespaces. Each team gets one or more namespaces assigned with ResourceQuotas, LimitRanges, NetworkPolicies, and RBAC roles. The configuration is standardized and can be rolled out automatically using Kyverno or GitOps templates [1].

The advantage is obvious: no additional infrastructure, no new control planes, no extra effort in cluster operations. The cost per tenant is minimal.

The downside: namespaces share one API server, one etcd, the same set of CRDs and webhooks – and by default the same nodes and the same kernel [3]. The Kubernetes documentation itself calls this model "soft multi-tenancy." A container escape or a vulnerability in kubelet can break the boundaries between tenants. For internal teams with trusted workloads, this is often acceptable; for multi-tenant SaaS platforms or security-critical environments, it is not enough.

Capsule (CNCF sandbox project) takes namespace isolation to the next level. Instead of configuring each namespace individually, Capsule introduces a Tenant custom resource that groups namespaces, bundles resource quotas, and centrally enforces network policies and ingress rules [4].

The key added value: tenant administrators can independently create namespaces within their quota – without the platform team having to intervene each time. At the same time, Capsule prevents one tenant from accessing another tenant's resources. A kubectl get pods --all-namespaces from a tenant user shows only their own namespaces [1].

Capsule is particularly suitable for internal platforms with multiple teams, different cost centers, and standardized quota profiles. Configuration is declarative via YAML and can be integrated into GitOps pipelines. Disadvantage: kernel isolation remains unchanged – Capsule is and remains a soft multi-tenancy approach.

When namespaces and Capsule are not enough, virtual clusters come into play. vCluster (Loft Labs, open source) creates a full Kubernetes API server per tenant with its own etcd, its own CRDs, and its own RBAC root – as a workload in the host cluster [5].

Each tenant sees a full-fledged Kubernetes cluster, can define their own ClusterRoles, install their own CRDs, and even run their own admission webhooks. That is the strength of the model: cross-tenant conflicts over CRDs or cluster-wide resources are impossible.

Version 0.27 (2026) introduced "Private Nodes": tenant nodes can participate directly in the virtual cluster with their own CNI, own CSI, and own networking, without other tenants seeing them [5]. This creates a level of isolation that previously only separate clusters could provide – but without their operational costs.

The price: higher resource consumption (each virtual API server needs CPU and RAM) and more complex troubleshooting, since the host and tenant levels are decoupled.

Practice shows: most organizations end up with a hybrid strategy [3]. For internal development teams with trusted workloads, Capsule or well-configured namespace isolation is sufficient. For SaaS tenants, PCI-DSS environments, or GPU workloads with inseparable tenants, vCluster or dedicated node pools are the right choice.

The cost question is not trivial: a separate EKS/GKE/AKS standard cluster costs around $0.10 per hour just for the control plane [3]. With ten tenants, that is roughly $730 per month – plus operational overhead for patches, upgrades, and monitoring. Multi-tenancy on a single cluster saves these costs but requires investment in the right toolchain.

Kubernetes multi-tenancy is no longer a niche topic in 2026 but a core competency for every platform team. Namespaces remain the foundation, Capsule adds thoughtful policy control, and vCluster offers a real alternative to dedicated clusters. The right choice depends on security requirements, the number of tenants, and the budget. One thing applies to all approaches: without automated provisioning, monitoring, and clear RBAC boundaries, multi-tenancy is a security risk – not an efficiency gain.

NVIDIA releases Nemotron-Labs-Sigma, an open continuous diffusion language model family

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NVIDIA has released Nemotron-Labs-Sigma, an open-weight family of continuous diffusion language models. The release ships 14 checkpoints in two sizes, 3B and 8B: base, instruct and few-step distilled variants (NFE 2, 4, 8 and 16), plus a self-speculative variant per size. All weights are published on Hugging Face under the NVIDIA Nemotron Open Model License, which permits commercial use, so teams can download, fine-tune and serve the models in their own environments.

Continuous diffusion differs from both familiar approaches. An autoregressive model writes text token by token, always moving from left to right. Discrete diffusion models such as LLaDA or Dream diffuse over discrete token IDs. Sigma instead denoises token representations in a learned 16-dimensional embedding space and generates text blockwise, 64 tokens per block: each block passes through several denoising steps before it is emitted, and the model then moves on to the next block. The training lineage is unusual as well — the models were warm-started from an autoregressive checkpoint trained on roughly 1 trillion tokens, then continued with about 0.3 trillion tokens of diffusion training.

The figures below are per NVIDIA's own internal evaluations and are not independently verified. Sigma-8B base averages 71.16 on NVIDIA's 6-task suite, against 70.22 for NLD-8B, 58.74 for Dream-7B, 41.78 for LLaDA-8B and 68.91 for the autoregressive Qwen3-8B. The 8B instruct variant reaches 91.96 on GSM8K. The known weak spot is AIME25 at 17.00, so competition-level mathematics remains clearly behind the family's other results, a useful reminder that these numbers are self-reported.

On speed, NVIDIA reports about 120-140 tokens per second on a single H100 in bfloat16. The self-speculative 8B variant decodes up to 3.46x faster than the autoregressive average on GB200 with SGLang, with a peak of 4.20x per NVIDIA. The context window is 4,096 tokens, extended to 8,192 tokens for the 8B instruct variant.

IMPACT: diffusion language models generate whole blocks in parallel instead of moving token by token from left to right. The distilled variants trade a few extra compute steps for fewer network round-trips, which matters for self-hosted inference stacks where latency budgets are tight. Since NVIDIA does not host a first-party API, everything runs on your own infrastructure via vLLM, SGLang or Docker Model Runner, with commercial use allowed under the license. For teams that already operate their own GPU fleet, that removes a dependency on external endpoints entirely.

MariaDB support timeline 2026–2029: which LTS should your servers run?

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 21 • Originally published at srvscripts.com

MariaDB changed its release model in 2024 and the consequences are now fully visible: instead of every version being supported for five years, there are short-lived rolling releases and, once a year, a long-term-support release that gets three years. For a hosting provider that means the “install and forget for half a decade” habit is gone, and the fleet needs a version policy rather than a version. This guide lays out the dates as they stand on 29 September 2026 and turns them into a policy you can apply.

Short answer: As of late September 2026, MariaDB 10.6 is unsupported, 10.11 runs until February 2028, 11.8 until June 2028, and 11.4 and 12.3 until May and June 2029; 13.0 is a rolling release leading to a 13.3 LTS around mid-2027. Run an LTS with at least eighteen months of support left: 11.8 on cPanel until WHM adds 12.3, and 12.3 on DirectAdmin and standalone servers. Never put a .0, .1 or .2 rolling release on a customer-facing server, and keep the maintenance patch level current because that is where the security fixes are. (Reviewed October 2026.)

In short: As of late September 2026, MariaDB 10.6 is unsupported, 10.11 runs until February 2028, 11.8 until June 2028, and 11.4 and 12.3 until May and June 2029; 13.0 is a rolling release leading to a 13.3 LTS around mid-2027.

Each yearly series has four minor versions. The .0, .1 and .2 releases are rolling: supported roughly until the next one ships, meant for early adopters and testing. The .3 release is the LTS, supported for three years. So 12.0, 12.1 and 12.2 were stepping stones and 12.3 is what production runs. The same pattern is under way with 13.0, released on 15 September 2026, which will lead to a 13.3 LTS around mid-2027.

Do not run a rolling release on a customer-facing server. They are fine for a staging box where you want to see what the next LTS will break.

Note the oddity: 11.4 outlives 11.8 by nearly a year because the LTS windows were set at different points in the model change. If you want the longest runway from an 11.x install, 11.4 is it; if you want the newer features (VECTOR, VEC_DISTANCE_*, better JSON), 11.8 is the last 11.x LTS.

The quarterly maintenance releases came out on 24 August 2026 across every supported series. Check each server against the current patch level:

cPanel on AlmaLinux 8 and 9 offers MySQL 8.0 and 8.4 and MariaDB 10.5 through 11.8 in the WHM upgrade workflow; on AlmaLinux 10 the list narrows to MySQL 8.4 and MariaDB 10.11, 11.4 and 11.8; Ubuntu 24.04 gets MySQL 8.0 and 8.4 and MariaDB 10.6 through 11.8. 12.3 is not available in WHM yet. New cPanel installs default to 10.11.

DirectAdmin’s CustomBuild tracks 10.6, 10.11, 11.4, 11.8 and 12.3, plus MySQL 8.0 and 8.4, and dropped MySQL 5.7 and MariaDB 10.3 in 1.693. It expects you to move through LTS versions in order rather than skipping.

Standalone servers using the upstream repository can run anything on the list.

Run an LTS that has at least eighteen months of support remaining. That single rule produces sensible decisions:

New builds: 11.8 on cPanel until WHM adds 12.3, and 12.3 on DirectAdmin and standalone. Pin the choice in your provisioning scripts:

Put the end-of-life dates into the same calendar or monitoring system that tracks certificate expiry and operating-system EOL. A simple cron that compares the running major version against a small table and emails when the remaining support drops below a threshold is enough:

Feed that into whatever fleet inventory you keep; the MySQL health snapshot script already records the version per server so the comparison can be done centrally.

Reading “12.3.3” as a minor point release that can wait. Within an LTS series the third number is the maintenance release, and those carry the security fixes. A server on 12.3.0 is missing every fix since May. Keep the patch level current through the normal package update cycle; on cPanel that is upcp and /scripts/update-packages, on DirectAdmin it is da build mariadb after da build update, and on standalone servers it is dnf update MariaDB-server followed by a restart in a maintenance window.

I battle rocks and falling temperatures while searching for a lost item on trail.

My plans are rarely flexible, until they are. I checked in with Pebbles last night to see what she, Rebar, and Greasetrap had planned (Soaked completed his hike the previous day, huge congrats to him!). Not a big deal, I could just swap them. However, my section south of Swatara Gap called for 24 miles of hiking. I would need to start early and hike flat out all day to make it before sunset. I knew that isn’t really their style, which is valid. I had made up my mind and was sticking to my plan. I was up early and decided to head towards town to buy a coffee. While enjoying my hot beverage in my car I decided to take one last look at both itineraries. I might not be able to hike with them, but simply flipping my current section might allow me to pass them early in the day. Eventually I have to earn my trail name, right? I set a course for Swatara Gap and was on trail close to 8:00AM.

I texted my change in plans to Pebbles as I made my way down the trail. No sooner had I switched my phone into airplane mode and put it back in my pack than 3 hikers appeared ahead. Ca-caw! The group chimed. Ca-caw! I called back. Soon we were catching up and discussing our future hiking plans and Rebars new kicks. His previous Altras had taken a beating on the trail, I recall him putting some kind of indestructible adhesive on them during a previous encounter. He now had a snazzy new pair of Altras and was clearly enjoying the upgrade. After chatting for a few minutes we agreed to keep in close contact since I’ll soon be skipping ahead of them to close out my hike. The trail crosses the Swatara Creek on the 19th century Waterville Bridge, an interesting piece of industrial era engineering.

As I approached the bridge, I felt my phone vibrate. I had left it on since I had parked my car in a lot that requires calling the state park if you intend to leave your vehicle there overnight. I thought I might get a call back since I left a message. Instead I saw that it was Pebbles calling me. “So, you know how you like treasure hunts? Well, Rebar left a dry bag at the last campsite…”. I think I could make a living doing this professionally given the dozens of items reported missing on FarOut. Drive to the nearest trailhead, hike out and retrieve the item, drive it up the trail or ship it back to its owner, profit. Of course, for friends I’ll stick with the rate of a few guava candies. A few miles later I was staring at a bag resting neatly by a fire pit, it was definitely Rebar’s, my easiest find yet!

The trail had leveled off onto another long ridge, but the rocks made sure things weren’t too easy for me. The rocks and cold became the theme of the day. I had endured many cold days but not one single time had I ended a day with my sleeping layers still on. Hiking always warmed me up a ton and often after 30-minutes of hiking I’d shed them and hike in my shorts and sun hoodie. The climb out of Swatara Gap warmed me up and had me considering stopping to make the adjustment. But I wanted to see how I felt once the climbing stopped and I was just moving along the mostly level ridge. My temperature returned to a comfortable level quickly, so I was content to keep moving and keep the layers on. There were a handful of views along the ridge, but otherwise minimal points of interest aside from some boulder fields.

Faster now, but the builder says the Optane sticks still need fans of their own.

A homelabber detailed improvements to the second-gen Optane AI build in a follow-up post on Reddit, showing the addition of two RTX 3090s to run the 2.8-trillion-parameter Moonshot Kimi K3 model at 3.11 tokens per second. Transferring almost half the model onto a RAM drive added a significant 41% to generation speed and 26% to prompt evaluation. The extra fans are still needed, though, as the persistent Optane memory gets very hot.

That speed is not the most impressive, but it comes with 16.8 tokens per second for prompt evaluation, with 45 of 94 shards, or 414 GB out of 865 GB, living on a RAM drive. Such an arrangement is not new to enthusiasts who would set aside system memory for extra-fast performance in a pseudo-non-volatile manner — it’s still volatile, but appears like a disk — although not everyone has that much RAM sitting around free these days. With both GPUs but no RAM drive, the build ran at 2.20 tokens per second for generation and 13.3 for prompt evaluation.

45 of 94 shards (414 GB of 865 GB) in a tmpfs RAM drive, the rest in Optane

Builder’s measurements; llama.cpp, DAX on for the Optane portion.

Even a quick read of the details makes it clear that the GPUs aren’t holding the model: two RTX 3090s, at 24 GB apiece, only have a total of 48 GB of VRAM, which is about 5.5% of the 865 GB model file. That said, the model is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) type, where its weights are split into multiple “experts” with only a few run per token. Any given token will have “routed” experts selected for it, while “shared” experts run on every token.

For this model, with 896 experts, 16 are chosen per token, with two shared, with an active 104B parameters out of its 2.8T. All 2.8T parameters would take about 5.6 TB at 16-bit precision. Moonshot’s own release, which uses the 4-bit MXFP4 format, comes to about 1.56 TB, and the Q2_K file used here shrinks that to 865 GB, an average of under 3 bits per parameter. The 865 GB figure is likely in gibibytes (GiB), but for clarity we’re using the GB values as written by the homelabber.

The configuration here has the routed-expert tensors stay on the CPU side, originally in Optane, and now in Optane plus the RAM drive. The RTX 3090s handle attention, or how each token weighs the rest of the context, and the dense feed-forward network layer that isn’t split into experts, where every token passes through it. They also manage the shared experts, other active-dense components, the KV cache, and the stored context of the current conversation, with the GPU compute buffers. Basically, the parts on the GPUs are “used on every token,” and the improvements there come from the high level of GPU compute and memory bandwidth.

DRAM is a lot slower than VRAM, but it’s still capable of giving a decent speedup. The new build involves using Linux’s tmpfs, which, per the kernel documentation, “is a file system which keeps all of its files in virtual memory.” Those shards now live in memory, which admittedly is not ideal when the original build was designed to save money by using Optane instead. With those shards in DRAM, which the builder has said is faster than Optane, that part of the model no longer has to be read from the slower memory; the workload is decidedly distributed.

The speed that came with the GPUs is within the range of what the builder expected, guessing “somewhere between 2 and 4 tokens per second” in a prediction from the original build thread. The GPU install had to wait, in part, for modifications to the case, which required cutting out some of the metal and relocating the PSU to make room for the GPUs and their cooling. Cooling remains a huge issue, with “the improved throughput [not changing] the original thermal warning,” and the two 12W blower fans for the Optane DIMMs are kept in place. Add in dual 350W-class GPUs, and the changes are understandable.

Things won’t stop here, as more optimizations are possible, including “customized quantization,” which may be similar to the techniques used by lawyer Nick Mermiges. Quantization selected per tensor or layer, or by where the data lives, might reduce bandwidth and capacity pressures for the slower tiers; the setup “might be faster yet.” Speed, or tokens per second alone, is not enough, and output quality must be taken into consideration in any future plan.

While legacy automakers tap the brakes on electric vehicles, one startup is hitting the accelerator. Earlier this month, electric truck maker Harbinger raised $240 million in a Series D round, capping a banner stretch that includes a $300 million order from FedEx and a new defense contract with the US military.

Harbinger is stacking up wins by skipping the consumer EV hype cycle altogether and focusing on the unglamorous workhorses that keep the economy running: Class 4–6 delivery vans and box trucks. The company’s thesis is simple: go after the overlooked commercial segments where legacy trucks are weak and giant automakers can’t be bothered to compete, CEO John Harris tells me.

“We’re not solving the big obvious problem because everyone’s going to solve that problem, and as a result, the margins get kind of ground down to zero,” Harris says. “We’re solving problems that — they’ve just been there forever and you no longer think of this as a problem, you just think of it as like part of existence. It’s like a tax on your life.”

Founded in 2021 by veterans of Canoo, QuantumScape, and Faraday Future, Harbinger aims to fill a structural gap in the auto market, in which most passenger EV platforms lack the durability to scale up while the heavy-duty platforms are too heavy and expensive to scale down.

“In auto, you have Class 1 companies and you have Class 8 companies,” Harris says. “And everything in between is kind of a wasteland... It is much, much, much too small for a big company to serve.”

Which is not to say the industry hasn’t tried. Ford and GM both took shots at electrifying the medium-duty segment with the E-Transit and BrightDrop delivery vans, respectively, but sluggish sales and the loss of federal incentives forced them to discontinue or cancel those models.

Harris says that big automakers are ill-suited to task, with their over-reliance on third-party suppliers to build all the necessary components. The effort becomes too spread out and expensive, with not enough return on your investment. That’s why Harbinger is attempting a more vertically integrated approach, he says, assembling its own battery packs and drive units in house — with inverters to follow.

“If you are not vertically integrated in auto, you don’t build viable products,” Harris says. “We believe that electric vehicles are only viable when they’re competitive on acquisition price with combustion vehicles.”

Of course, Harris and his fellow cofounders have tasted failure in their previous ventures. Harbinger’s CTO Phillip Weicker cofounded EV startup Canoo, which ran out of cash and filed bankruptcy in 2025. Harris himself spent a year at tumultuous Chinese EV company Faraday Future before moving on to Xos Trucks and then Palmer Luckey’s Anduril.

“I was an engineer at Faraday. I wasn’t in charge of anything,” Harris says. “So it’s not like, ‘John, why did Faraday not succeed?’ I can tell you lots of reasons, but it wasn’t because the plastic part that I designed didn’t work. It was because the plastic part that I designed was going into a vehicle for which the world customer base was like three people.”

Those companies “fundamentally didn’t work,” he adds, “because of the market fit issue and the leadership issue. Not because the engineering didn’t work or electrification wasn’t ready or they didn’t have enough capital.”

Harbinger’s approach is more pragmatic, offering both battery-electric and plug-in hybrid powertrains for its medium-duty trucks. The company is not pursuing heavy-duty Class 8 trucks like the Tesla Semi, however, as Harris argues the economics of electric long-haul trucking are unworkable.

“Class 8 electrification to me is one of those markets. I completely reject the notion that that’s a good fit for electrification for the majority of the market,” he says. “We can put [the Tesla Semi] on the watch list with the Cybertruck.”

With companies like Amazon and FedEx making serious moves to secure more electric delivery vehicles, Harbinger is well-positioned to be a key supplier. And with diesel prices skyrocketing as a result of the war with Iran, more companies are waking up to a reality in which EVs can help bring down their costs.

Harbinger isn’t just building delivery trucks — it’s also branching into energy storage and military technology. For example, the company supplies battery power systems to Airstream and partners with American Rheinmetall and Forterra to build self-driving hybrid supply vehicles for the US Army. Harbinger also recently bought Phantom AI to bring advanced driver-assistance features to commercial fleets, in the hopes of giving companies a clear, measurable return on their safety investments.

With millions in fresh capital and major contracts already on the books, Harbinger has cleared the first major hurdle that tripped up its founders’ past ventures. Now comes the hardest part in the auto business: proving it can actually build them at scale.

“Auto is a big enough opportunity that it can get people’s attention,” Harris says. “It’s also punishingly difficult. So if you can make something that works in auto, it will be phenomenal in every other segment, because every other segment, the competition is essentially weaker.”

She said she was forced to cancel the project, adding: “I worked for quite four years, five years for nothing because they stole from me, they stole, you know, the movie and I could not do it. So it was very hard.”

Bertuccelli turned to the directors in the room. “It’s a fight against you, against yourself, because you need to work, you need even when you are depressed or you have doubts, you need to continue, because the only thing that makes you a director is that you go on and you never, you know, you never stop your desire,” she said.

She is now writing the new feature. It follows a French woman living in Mumbai who is slowly going blind and sets off on one last journey with her son, who is half Indian and half French. Bertuccelli plans to come to India to make it.

“It’s my first time in India, but for so long I’m admiring your country, your culture,” she said.

Bertuccelli is also finishing a second documentary alongside “The Violin Lesson,” which follows Rachel Routier, a violin teacher in Pantin, outside Paris, and is headed for French cinemas after airing on France Télévisions in September. She hopes to screen “The Violin Lesson” in Mumbai in the coming weeks, along with either “The Tree” or “Since Otar Left.”

During two terms as president of La Scam, the French authors’ society for documentary makers, Bertuccelli created the Golden Eye documentary prize at Cannes, whose Indian winners include Payal Kapadia’s “A Night of Knowing Nothing” and Shaunak Sen’s “All That Breathes.” She also founded La Cinémathèque du documentaire in Paris, which hopes to stage a retrospective of Indian documentary.

Asked by Kumar whether documentary carries a social responsibility, Bertuccelli said: “For sure there is a big responsibility because the film can change the life of somebody, you know?”

She pointed to “School of Babel,” which followed a class for children newly arrived in France. The film was widely seen by school groups and shifted young viewers’ attitudes toward immigrant classmates, she said, and it helped protect such classes at a time when some were being closed. “Because I said with this film to all the Education Ministry: ‘So I made a film to show that France is great, but don’t touch it. Now you can’t destroy this,'” she said.

Kumar later asked where cinema stands in the age of AI. Bertuccelli allowed that the technology can be useful for small tasks, such as sound work.

“But I hate AI. Not just because, okay, it’s dangerous for us, we will forget how to have memory, how to think by ourselves. Also because they steal, they stole the film, you know, they stole the author rights. But also more because it’s an ecological, horrible, horrible thing,” she said.

Documentary, she argued, is cinema’s last line of defense, because “there is a contract with you that when you see a documentary, there is reality, there is truth.” She added that “the 21st century will be the one of documentary.”

Bertuccelli spent around 15 years as an assistant to directors including Krzysztof Kieślowski, Bertrand Tavernier and Otar Iosseliani before making her own documentaries. Her fiction debut, “Since Otar Left,” shot in Georgia, won the Critics’ Week grand prize at Cannes and the César for best first film. Her documentary work includes “School of Babel,” “Latest News From the Cosmos” and “Jane Campion, the Cinema Woman.” “The Tree,” starring Charlotte Gainsbourg, played IFFI more than 15 years ago, but Bertuccelli could not attend as she was eight months pregnant at the time.

She closed the session by pointing back to the Lumière brothers, whose operators traveled the world filming everyday life.

“Cinema was born documentary and it will die documentary,” she said.

The life and legacy of legendary Egyptian artist Mohamed Mounir is set to be explored in a biopic spearheaded by The Film Committee at Media City Qatar.

The Film Committee has acquired the rights to document Mounir’s life story, with partners on the project set to include a leading regional studio and other partners, which have yet to be unveiled.

The project was announced at the Film Committee’s Doha Industry Days, just hours after the Qatari body gave an update on previously announced biopic Caesar, about Syrian whistleblower Farid Al-Madhhan, announcing Taim Hasan as it star. Mounir’s career spans nearly five decades across music, film and television. Watch on Deadline

His 1981 breakthrough album ‘Shababeek’ established him as one of Egypt’s most distinctive artists, with his work characterized by its fusion of Nubian folk and Arabic pop with Western genres like jazz, blues, and reggae.

He is known to his fans across the Middle East and beyond as “El King” in reference to his 1998 album and play ‘El Malek Howwa El Malek’ ((The King is The King).

Also working in cinema, his some 20 credits including collaborations with late Egyptian filmmaker Youssef Chahine on films including Egyptian Story (1982) and Destiny, which played in Out of Competition in Cannes in 1997 and was then selected as Egypt’s Oscar entry.

Mounir was born into a Nubian family in a village in Southern Egypt, where he spent his early childhood. His family moved to Cairo, after the village was resettled due to the construction of the Aswan Dam in the 1960s, where his singing voice was discovered when he sang at a family gathering.

The Film Committee said the biopic will trace Mounir’s journey from his humble beginnings to becoming one of the Arab world’s most celebrated and influential artists, revealing untold details, surprises, stories and anecdotes.

“Mohamed Mounir’s music holds a special place in the lives of audiences across Egypt and the Arab world,” said Film Committee at Media City Qatar Chair Hassan Al Thawadi.

“Bringing his story to the big screen is an opportunity to celebrate his artistry, the cultural heritage his work represents and its lasting social relevance, a story that connects generations of fans across the region and has the power to resonate with audiences around the world,” he continued.

“This project also builds on our recent investment in extraordinary life stories from the region and reflects our ambition to develop a world-leading slate of biographical films that brings these stories to audiences globally.”

Alongside the feature film, plans include a behind-the-scenes documentary featuring interviews with Mounir and those who have lived and worked alongside him, as well as documenting the development and production of the biopic.

With development in the early stages, the hunt is now on for an actor to play Mounir.
