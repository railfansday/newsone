Germany's Early Start Pension: Can it fix retirement woes?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Germany plans to pay €10 a month into investment accounts for children to encourage long-term retirement saving. Economists say the measure may help build wealth but won't solve the country's pension funding challenge.

Germany's pension system is under growing strain as demographic change leaves a shrinking workforce funding the pensions of an increasing number of retirees.

In the 1960s, for instance, six workers contributed to the pay-as-you-go public retirement system for each pensioner. Today, that figure has fallen to just two contributors per retiree.

According to the Federal Statistical Office, Germany's fertility rate is currently 1.32 children per woman,and has remained below the 2.1 needed to maintain a stable population since the 1970s.

As a large cohort of baby boomers reaches retirement age, fewer young people are entering the workforce. Germany's working-age population is shrinking by around 1% each year due to demographic change alone, according to the German bank LBBW.

To keep up, nearly a quarter of the federal budget, over €123 billion ($135 billion), is transferred to the statutory pension system each year to help finance pension payments.

Now to help ease the pressure on the pension system, Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government plans to implement a raft of reforms, including a supplementary, capital-funded savings scheme known as the Frühstartrente, or Early Start Pension.

Under the plan, the state would pay €10 per month into an investment account for every child and teenager between the ages of six and 18.

Beneficiaries would not be able to access the money until they reach retirement age, though current plans would allow the funds to be transferred into private retirement accounts. Investment returns would be tax-free during the accumulation phase and taxed only when funds are withdrawn.

Assuming an average annual return of 7%, the €1,440 contributed by the government over 12 years would grow to around €53,000 by retirement, according to government projections.

Parents and relatives would be able to make additional contributions. If they invest an extra €50 per month, the account could grow to as much as €300,000 by retirement.

Starting to save early makes sense because of the power of compound interest, Axel Börsch-Supan, a professor of economics at the Technical University of Munich, told DW.

"But it will not solve Germany's pension problems, which stem from the pay-as-you-go system and costly policies such as the pension level guarantee and retirement at 63," he added, referring to a provision that allows workers with at least 45 years of pension contributions to retire at age 63 with a full pension.

Börsch-Supan argues that government subsidies under the program are likely to benefit higher-income families more than lower-income households.

"That's a waste of taxpayer money, because higher-income individuals are already well-positioned to provide for their own retirement and that of their children," he said.

The Ifo Institute, one of Germany's leading economic think tanks, shares a similar assessment.

It argues that the scheme effectively means "the government hands out money indiscriminately, only to reclaim it through taxes from the same citizens."

The government, however, argues that the plan gives every child a head start in the capital markets regardless of their family's financial circumstances and helps them build retirement savings and long-term wealth.

Similar capital market-based savings and investment accounts for children exist in several countries, including the United Kingdom, Israel, Canada and Singapore.

Many of these programs include government contributions, matching payments or tax incentives designed to encourage long-term saving and asset accumulation from an early age.

But Germany's plan differs slightly as it links state-funded childhood investment accounts directly to retirement provision.

The government also hopes the program will boost financial literacy among children and young adults by familiarizing them with investing, saving and capital markets.

Johannes Geyer, deputy head of the department of public economics at the German Institute for Economic Research (DIW Berlin), says there is little evidence the Early Start Pension will fundamentally change how people engage with financial markets.

"The idea is that people will develop a connection to the capital markets through their experiences with their investment portfolios," he told DW. "But this is not very convincing. We neither know what experiences the future will bring nor what conclusions people will draw from them."

Geyer stresses that the program's impact will not be visible for decades because participants cannot access the funds until retirement.

"If we start now with the 2020 and 2021 cohorts, we're talking about the 2080s. That's a bit too far in the future to be able to draw meaningful conclusions."

In the meantime, he says, the scheme's most immediate effect may be to strengthen ties between families and financial institutions, particularly if account holders make additional contributions.

Opposition parties, including the Greens and the Left Party, have criticized the plan, arguing it would reinforce social disparities and boost inequality since better-off parents could pay more into accounts.

Yet Geyer says the measure won't increase inequality per se. "Sure, wealthy families are more likely to top up their contributions more substantially. But they probably would have done that anyway," he underscored.

"A more interesting question is whether the measure can also encourage people with limited financial means to invest in stocks," he said.

Geyer points out that Germany cannot entirely escape the costs of demographic decline and an aging society.

To address the challenge, he calls for a moderate increase in pension contributions, a broader contributor base that includes groups such as the self-employed, and policies aimed at attracting more qualified workers to the labor market.

Börsch-Supan, who specializes in the economics of aging, says the government's primary goal should be to stabilize the pay-as-you-go statutory pension system.

"A transition to a fully funded pension system would actually be desirable, but it would initially place a heavy double burden on the younger generation, because they would have to save for themselves while also paying contributions for their parents," he explained.

The expert also suggests other measures like restoring the sustainability factor, which is designed to link annual pension hikes to demographic shifts, and linking the retirement age to life expectancy.

Mass General Brigham spinout Aevius raises $22m to make medicine computable

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Aevius’s four co-founders all helped build Modella AI, which AstraZeneca bought in January. Eric Topol, the cardiologist and digital medicine researcher, chairs its scientific advisory board.

Aevius co-founders, from left: Long Phi Le, Rowland Pettit, Ming Yang (Max) Lu and Faisal Mahmood

Aevius, a Boston clinical AI startup spun out of Mass General Brigham, has raised $22m led by Insight Partners. The company announced the round on 17 November in a press release. Chief executive Rowland Pettit told Axios Pro it is a seed round.

The money will go on model and product development, hiring and commercial deployment, the company said. It plans to add engineering, clinical and commercial staff. It did not give a valuation or name any customers.

“Our mission is to make medicine computable,” Pettit, a physician-scientist and co-founder, said in the release.

Pettit founded Aevius with chief technology officer Ming Yang (Max) Lu, Faisal Mahmood and Long Phi Le. All four were part of the founding team behind Modella AI, a cancer-focused medical AI startup. AstraZeneca bought Modella in January, as Brian Gormley reported for The Wall Street Journal. The drugmaker already uses AI to find trial patients.

Eric Topol, the cardiologist and digital medicine researcher, chairs Aevius’s scientific advisory board.

“The value of medical AI will be measured by how well it promotes accuracy,” Topol said in the release.

Aevius builds AI models that learn from structured, unstructured and specialty medical data. From a patient’s records, they build a refreshable multimodal patient representation, in the company’s words. It is a computable picture of the patient’s condition and trajectory that updates as new information arrives.

Organisations can build several applications on that one representation, the company said. They can forecast outcomes, find clinically similar patients, sort patients into groups and track change over time. Aevius is selling to health systems, payers and life sciences companies.

“Most AI agents today piece together their picture of a patient from text, one question at a time,” Lu said in the release.

He said Aevius instead builds a native representation of each patient’s whole history. Researchers, care teams and AI agents can then take on new questions without starting over.

Mass General Brigham said Aevius brings together research developed at the hospital system with a team that has taken medical AI to market. Dylan Morris, a managing director at Insight Partners, said clinical data is among the hardest kinds of data to put to use. Insight has also backed AI recruiting startup Metaview and sales tax startup Numeral.

Need to solve a problem? Neuroscience says try the Jack Reacher Method

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There’s a point in Lee Child’s book Make Me (the 20th in his Jack Reacher series, for those keeping score) where Reacher is trying to solve a problem and decides to back-burner it: Instead of actively working the problem, he sets it aside, trusting his subconscious to eventually make the right connections.

Which it does.

Sound kinda hokey?

Actually, no. Neuroscience shows you tend to be at your most creative when you’re not trying to be creative. Actually, you’re at your most creative when you’re not trying to do anything at all.

Researchers at Washington University in St. Louis used PET scans to determine which parts of the brain lit up during different types of activity. To establish baselines, some participants were put in the scanner and told to do nothing, what the researchers called “resting state.” Surprisingly, resting state resulted in mental energy spikes across various brain regions.

In fact, mental energy turned out to be higher in resting state than active state, where participants were given mental tasks.

A few years later, neuroscientist Nancy Andreasen made resting-state activity (she called it “free floating periods of thought”) her focus. No tasks. No inputs. No intentional creativity. No nothing; just vegging. 

As Andreasen writes in The Creating Brain: The Neuroscience of Genius:

Most investigators made the convenient assumption that the brain would be blank or neutral during “rest.” From introspection I knew that my own brain is often at its most active when I stretch out on a bed or sofa and close my eyes.

We found activations in multiple regions of the association cortex. We were not [seeing] a passive silent brain during the resting state, but rather a brain that was actively connecting thoughts and experiences.

Andreasen termed that activity REST: random episodic silent thinking. During REST, Andreasen says, your brain “uses its most human and complex parts, areas known to gather information and link it all together, in potentially novel ways.”

That’s why you sometimes get ideas while taking a walk. Or taking a shower. Or doing dishes. Or driving. When you let your mind wander, when you don’t actively focus on anything specific, your brain makes all sorts of seemingly unconnected associations.

You think about the future. You think about the past. You speculate. You synthesize. You test and discard. 

As Andreasen writes, “When the brain/mind thinks in a free and unencumbered fashion, it uses its most human and complex parts.”

In simple terms, if you want to be creative, stop trying to be creative. Stop actively trying to work the problem. Do something relatively mindless, and let your mind do some work in the background.

As Steven Johnson wrote in The New York Times:

The seemingly trivial activity of mind-wandering is now believed to play a central role in the brain’s “deep learning,” the mind’s sifting through past experiences, imagining future prospects, and assessing them with emotional judgments: that flash of shame or pride or anxiety that each scenario elicits.

Granted, REST, or mind-wandering, or whatever you want to call it, is easier said than done.

For one thing, stimulation and “focus” are always within easy reach. Plus, it can feel unproductive, and even oddly uncomfortable, not to fill time with some sort of activity.

How many people sit in a waiting room and just wait? How many people sit on a plane and just stare at the seatback in front of them? How many people take a walk without listening to something? How many people don’t listen to something while they’re driving? How many people work out without earbuds? (While it’s just one data point, the other day I went to the gym and counted 18 of 20 people wearing headphones. One of the two not wearing headphones was me.)

Sitting and thinking in a “free and unencumbered fashion”? Sounds boring.

And it is boring, at least at first. It’s also tough to stick with, especially if you decide to sit in a chair and just stare out the window.

So try this instead. Take a walk. (Then you’ll be double-dipping: A study published in the Journal of Experimental Psychology found that your creative output increases by as much as 60% when you’re walking.)

Just walk. Don’t think about thinking. Don’t think about what you should be doing, should be listening to, should be focused on. Let your mind wander. 

In all likelihood, your thoughts will soon drift to a problem you have. Or a challenge you’re working through. Your thoughts will shift forward and backward in time. You’ll sift through hypothetical possibilities and outcomes.

You’ll actively, without trying, connect ideas, thoughts, and experiences. 

When you’re REST-ing, you won’t have to come up with ideas. Ideas will come to you, because that’s how your brain is wired.

The BMW iX5 is a big deal for the German automaker. It’s the company’s first electric X5, a model that has traditionally been one of its best-sellers, and it’s also the first electric BMW assembled in the United States.

In other words, the iX5 has a lot on its shoulders, so it has to deliver. Thankfully, the automaker crammed a massive, 144-kilowatt-hour battery under the SUV’s body, and now a new test from Out of Spec Testing reveals just how much range is hidden in the EV’s pack, even after the instrument cluster shows there’s nothing more to give.

The test, which was performed on a closed course, started when the battery still had 4% energy left. The car’s displays said that the driver should recharge at the next opportunity, which makes a lot of sense in the real world, but the SUV still drove normally. At 2% state of charge, the car’s brain limited the power output to roughly 350 horsepower, down from approximately 450 hp, but that’s still plenty.

At 1%, the iX5 still delivered plenty of performance, and the driver still managed to maintain speeds between 35 and 55 miles per hour. The same speeds were still possible when the battery level reached zero, but a turtle icon appeared on the dash, accompanied by a yellow power meter and a warning message telling the driver to avoid heavy acceleration and overtaking.

All this being said, the iX5 did not slow down. It soldiered on, covering 8.4 miles after the battery level had reached 0% and consuming almost 3 kWh from the battery that was supposedly empty. The pedal started to feel a little softer at the 10-mile mark, and the first big power cut came at 10.7 miles, when the car stopped taking accelerator inputs and started slowing down.

But that wasn’t the end of the test. The iX5 was restarted and, surprisingly, continued driving at speeds of up to 55 mph. After consuming 7.4 kWh from the reserve, the iX5 eventually shut down, slowing down to a standstill and automatically engaging Park. It drove a total of 21.8 miles after the instrument cluster first showed that the battery had 0% energy left.

A second restart attempt was unsuccessful, but it goes to show that BMW put quite a big buffer on the iX5’s battery. It also means that the SUV’s total battery capacity is somewhere around 151 kWh.

Range-wise, the 2027 BMW iX5 60 xDrive, the only version currently available, can drive up to 456 miles on a full battery, according to the EPA. In a recent comparison with the Lucid Gravity, Porsche Cayenne, and Rivian R1S, the iX5 covered 411 miles at 70 mph, beating all of its competitors by at least 43 miles.

ROCKVILLE, Md., Nov. 23, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Transforming artificial intelligence into operational value and a driver of competitiveness through secure, reliable, and scalable solutions. It is with this positioning that Expert.ai is now presenting itself to the market with a new identity, targeting businesses, the public sector, and the healthcare industry. This evolution builds on the objectives of its 2026-2028 Strategic Plan and is supported by a stronger organizational structure.

Practical AI, more efficient processes, measurable value

Organizations are under growing pressure to stay competitive while modernizing the technology that powers their operations. In this context, Expert.ai is redefining the role of AI beyond an experimental tool, but a strategic asset for accelerating business transformation. By helping organizations adopt and integrate AI, Expert.ai delivers Vertical AI solutions tailored to the specific needs of each industry, focusing on critical business processes where the cost of error is highest.

More Than 30 years of experience with a vision for the future of AI

The Expert.ai Group's value proposition centers on implementing secure, scalable, explainable and reliable AI that meets the stringent compliance requirements of highly regulated industries, including banking, financial services and insurance, public sector and healthcare, life sciences; industrial, media and publishing. Building on more than 30 years of experience delivering AI solutions, Expert.ai serves as a partner for the development, adoption and integration of artificial intelligence into core enterprise-level processes. Expert.ai's work has also gained international recognition. In October 2026, Expert.ai was named to the FinTech Global InsurTech100 and selected by two North American insurance companies for underwriting and policy issuance.

The neurosymbolic approach to efficient, reliable and governable Vertical AI

Vertical AI builds on the expertise Expert.ai has developed through three decades of hands-on experience and hundreds of successful implementations, integrating proprietary technologies with the most advanced and effective techniques and models available on the market. Through its neurosymbolic approach, Expert.ai leverages the diverse capabilities of AI, optimizing and orchestrating them to address specific objectives and requirements: neural machine learning models, structured knowledge, business logic and semantic capabilities. This results in solutions that ensure accuracy, consistency, explainability, speed and predictable costs.

A new brand identity and logo, together with redesigned digital channels, reflect the Expert.ai Group's evolution and ambitions.

Expert.ai's new logo reflects the Group's continued commitment to excellence and technological innovation, qualities recognized by its customers and partners. "Expert" stands as a leading reference in AI thanks to the unique expertise and capacity for innovation, is united with "AI" by a green dot that represents the human element, an essential part of any ambitious transformation journey.

A stronger organization to support customers in today's challenges and tomorrow's opportunities

The new brand identity is accompanied by an organizational strengthening aligned with the 2026-2028 Strategic Plan, which is aimed at consolidating the customer base and expanding into new industries and geographies by leveraging industry-specific expertise and extending its market reach through commercial and strategic partnerships. The Group includes Ised (100% owned by Expert.ai), that strengthens its presence in the public sector and healthcare, and Fusion AI Labs, in which Expert.ai holds a 51% stake and which focuses on applied research. Expert.ai also holds a 40% stake in Villanova.ai.

Umberto Pardi has been appointed Group General Manager, working alongside Chairman and CEO Dario Pardi in the company's day-to-day management. Gabriele Donino has been appointed Chief Operations Officer, with responsibility for coordinating the business functions and geographic regions where the Group operates, as well as overseeing operations and delivery. Fabio Santini has been appointed Chief Solution and Innovation Officer, responsible for guiding the Group through the evolution of its technology portfolio, directing targeted investments in R&D and driving the development of new solutions.
