Trump says renewed Iran strikes ‘highly unlikely’ if talks progress but warns he could still strike

President Donald Trump said he is "highly unlikely" to restart open war with Iran, but warned he could still strike the country as Washington tries to reach a deal with Tehran to end the conflict.

The now more than eight-month-old war, which stoked inflation and sent oil prices surging, has seen repeated bursts of fighting separated by ceasefires and peace talks for months. It was widely speculated that Trump might restart open conflict after the midterm elections, where his Republican Party lost badly to Democrats.

Trump's comments came during an interview with CNN, where he said restarting the war is unlikely as long as talks to constrain Iran's nuclear program and reopen the Strait of Hormuz make progress.

"It depends on whether or not they sign a very strong agreement," Trump said. "The answer is highly unlikely it's going to be necessary. They're a failing nation."

Trump, however, left the door open for additional strikes if he deems it necessary. The president reiterated his threats to strike bridges and power plants in Iran if the war restarts.

"I've got three aircraft carriers here. The other one just arrived. We have three. They're fully locked and loaded," the president said. "We have 38 bridges in our site that we can knock down in literally one hour."

An end to the war and reopening of the Strait of Hormuz would likely ease oil prices, though finding a truce has proven difficult. All previous attempts to end the war and reopen the strait have been met with disappointment.

Trump is likely to come under increased pressure to end the conflict, however, now that Democrats have won control of both the House and the Senate. Some Republicans are also likely to be eager for an end to the war, given the role high gas prices played in their drubbing at the ballot box.

The president has demanded Iran fully open the Strait of Hormuz and agree to never develop a nuclear weapon as part of any deal.

Later in the day on Truth Social, Trump posted that "Task Force Iron Gate is in full force and effect in the Islamic Republic of Iran."

"Coupled with history's greatest Naval Blockade, very little gets into, or out of, Iran!" he said.

Central Command announced Task Force Iron Gate on Saturday, describing it as an initiative aimed at "preventing the movement of advanced weapons systems, dual-use technologies, sanctioned materials, and illegal revenue streams across Iran's land borders and the Caspian Sea."

The U.S. has maintained a naval blockade of Iranian ports for months.

Nvidia investigates CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with alleged Chinese spy

📅 2026-11-08  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia has opened an investigation into co-founder and CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with a businesswoman who intelligence agencies say is a Chinese spy.

U.S. intelligence agencies have expressed concerns that the woman, Alice Huang, who is affiliated with a cloud company and an Nvidia customer called Megaspeed, has been passing information to Beijing, according to reports in The New York Times and the Financial Times.

Huang told Nvidia staff in an email on Sunday that he had an affair with the woman and that Nvidia's board of directors had opened an independent investigation into the spying allegations.

"I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did," Huang wrote in the message. "I am deeply sorry."

An Nvidia spokesperson said that Huang was committed to continuing as CEO of Nvidia without interruption.

In a statement, the Nvidia board said it would investigate the allegations that Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort.

"While the Board has no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised, the Board's inquiry will examine all available information," Nvidia's board said in a statement.

Read Huang's email to Nvidia employees and the company board of directors' statement here.

The allegations come as Nvidia has become the center of a trade dispute between China and the U.S. as AI is seen as increasingly critical to national security.

The U.S. restricts exports of Nvidia's most advanced AI chips to China, though it has approved exports of certain older, slower models.

Megaspeed, which Ms. Huang is affiliated with, has been accused of helping restricted Chinese companies acquire Nvidia AI chips. Nvidia representatives previously said that Megaspeed had already been investigated for evading export controls.

Nvidia's Huang has pushed for broader AI chip exports to China, saying it is better for the U.S. if the world uses and builds on American technology. He has also aligned with President Donald Trump on American artificial intelligence policy and its infrastructure buildout.

In his note to staff, Huang said he was "proud and grateful" to be an American.

Three House Republican committee chairs sent a letter to Huang on Wednesday asking him to sit for a closed-door briefing with lawmakers on Nov. 18.

Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings on Nov. 17 and will hold a major conference in Washington, D.C., at the end of the month.

Manchester United do not have a football team, more a collection of adrenaline junkies. Over time, it will not be enough.

So deficient are Michael Carrick's players that they surely can't keep their manager in a job for much longer. For the most part United are shambolic, a mess. At times they play football so devoid of intelligence and competence that it's almost impossible to fathom. They look uncoached.

Yet here they were for the second night running at Old Trafford, completing a lap of honour at full-time while their supporters sang the name of Carrick as he completed his own journey in front of the Stretford End towards the tunnel.

United – it's worth pointing out – are third bottom of the Premier League. They haven't won in the league since August and have conceded more goals across all competitions – 30 – than any team bar Crystal Palace in Europe's top divisions.

But at Old Trafford they will cling to anything to help them look in the mirror without laughing and currently the currency is comebacks. There was one against Roma in the Champions League last Tuesday and here – quite remarkably – there were two in one game.

None of it should really mean anything in the grand scheme. United are rotten, capable of playing effectively only when something gives them an energy spike with which to roll. It makes for quite hypnotic theatre but has inadequacy and desperation at its core.

2027 Kawasaki Jet Skis deliver a supercharged, eye-opening performance

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 8, 2026 5:21 PM EST

In 1964, then-Pontiac division president John Z. Delorean (yes, that Delorean, of Back to the Future movie car fame) invented the muscle car category by bolting the big-block V8 engine from the company’s full-size cars into its lighter intermediate-sized chassis to create the legendary Pontiac GTO.

Kawasaki has done the same thing for its Jet Skis for the 2027 model year by installing the 310-horsepower supercharged 1.5-liter engine from its bigger Ultra models into the sporty, agile Jet Ski STX hull. The result is a truly eye-opening performance with what the company says is its quickest Jet Ski yet.

Kawasaki established the personal watercraft industry with the 1973 introduction of the 27-horsepower JS400 Jet Ski. Since that time, however, the one-time industry leader has slipped in popularity compared to its rivals in the market.

The company’s 2027 plan to recapture customers includes an embrace of high performance. Kawasaki has rolled out five new models for 2027, but the stars of the show are the high-performance Jet Skis meant to excite customers.

The original Jet Ski employed a smokey 400 cc 2-stroke engine that grew to 800 cc over time. Those early Jet Skis were tricky stand-up models that delivered responsive handling but presented a learning curve that saw newcomers falling off repeatedly as they learned to master the machine.

The solution was the introduction of the more stable sit-down models that traded responsiveness for stability. It was a trade that so many riders were willing to make that eventually Kawasaki phased out the stand-up models entirely.

The industry shifted to cleaner 4-stroke engines in the ‘90s, and 1999 saw the arrival of the 1.5-liter engine based on Kawasaki’s sport motorcycle powerplants in its Jet Skis, packing 150 horsepower. In 2007, the engineering team bolted a supercharger to the same engine, producing 250 horsepower.

Now, thanks to the technology of Eaton’s Twin Vortices System supercharger design (which debuted on the 2009 Chevrolet Corvette ZR1), power has reached 310 horsepower. Packed into the agile STX sit-down hull to create the STX 310 model, the big motor lets riders rip across the water at 65 mph.

This drivetrain employs a 4-into-2-into-1 exhaust with dual water mufflers to minimize backpressure, maximizing the engine’s power and efficiency. The 160-mm jet pump has a three-blade impeller whose blade angles are optimized for peak acceleration and thrust.

Kawasaki let us ride the top-of-the-line STX 310RX, which lists for $20,199. There is a slightly less-well-equipped STX 310R that goes just as fast for $18,799. The test unit featured a 7-inch display panel containing the speedometer, tachometer, supercharger boost level, fuel level, trim setting, power mode setting, and compass. The rear-view camera on the STX lets the rider keep an eye on skiers, wakeboarders, or tube riders behind the watercraft.

We’ve gotten familiar with electronically controlled modes in modern cars, trucks, and motorcycles, and they are also present here in the Jet Ski STX 310RX. They are Smart Learning Operation (SLO), Lower Power Operation (LPO), Middle Power Operation (MPO), and Full Power Operation (FPO).

I started riding in MPO, thinking that would be a good get-acquainted scenario and I was pleasantly surprised at the STX 310’s speed. I guess I shouldn’t have been, considering that it has 310 horsepower. After a bit of that, I switched to FPO, and found the throttle response much touchier and acceleration quicker–perfect for big fun.

MPO limits peak power to 80 percent, while FPO unleashes all 310 horses. LPO makes the throttle response even more sluggish and further restricts maximum power to 60 percent. SLO mode requires a special yellow key and is aimed at true beginners, who, realistically, probably should be learning on something other than this supercharged beast.

Other gadgets include the Launch Control system, which automatically adjusts the jet pump’s trim over the course of an acceleration run, keeping it optimized as the boat’s attitude changes during the run. There’s also a handy one-touch 5-mph cruise control, good for complying with no-wake zones.

Hot-dogging on the STX is a good time, thanks to a hull design that was created for corner carving, without much risk of pitching the rider into orbit mid-corner. There are also some nice practical features, like a Neutral that preserves some degree of steering capability.

Jet Skis have no rudder, relying on thrust from the engine to steer them, so when the throttle is released, or the boat is shifted to neutral, you’d expect it to be adrift. But Kawasaki employs a thrust reverser that lets the STX simultaneously have no forward thrust while also having some steering capacity. That reverser also provides for a Reverse position that lets the ride back the STX.

Reverse is one of the amenities that led to the demise of original-style stand-up Jet Skis, which aren’t suited to such features. Kawasaki tried briefly to revive the format in 2017, but says that its new stand-up 2027 models are Back to the Future to stay this time.

I was pleasantly surprised to find that long-dormant stand-up riding skills were still sufficient for me to take off on the first try without falling.

Kawasaki offers two versions of its classic stand-up model for 2027, the Jet Ski SX-R 160 and SX-X 160. These are powered by 160-hp naturally aspirated versions of the 1.5-liter engine, with the difference between them being that the SX-R has a traditional-style moveable handlebar that starts off lying flat and the rider lifts it as they stand.

The SX-X has a fixed motorcross-style handlebar, giving riders a choice between the two styles. The test model was the SX-X motocrosser. I’d never ridden one like that before, but I found the fixed bar seemed to make it easier to pull myself up as the ski got underway.

However, after falling off, as one does on these things, you have to reach up from the water to reattach your wrist lanyard to the ignition interlock. This is the thing that shuts the ski off when you fall so that it doesn’t leave you behind, and you have to reconnect it to restart the machine.

But with the fixed bar up there pretty high from the water, I was happy to have my 35-inch sleeve length. Someone with more normal-length arms might find it hard to reach the switch while floating in the water alongside.

Just as I’d remembered stand-ups, the SX model is tremendously agile and fun for turns and jumps. And just as I remembered, it is easy to fall off in the process. Once it starts to roll onto its side, adding power might get you to ride out of it, or, as in my case, it might just make you hit the water while going even faster!

It doesn’t matter which handlebar style you prefer because both the SX-X 160 and SX-R 160 are $11,999. Getting one of these would allow you to cultivate some old-school riding skills while taking advantage of some throwback value. That was another key component of the original Pontiac GTO’s muscle car legend, so it is good to see in the Kawasaki showrooms for 2027.

NAR sees pent-up demand waiting on lower mortgage rates, better sentiment

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NAR economist Lawrence Yun said pent-up housing demand could be released if mortgage rates ease, inventory improves, and confidence rebounds. NAR data show existing-home sales near 4M, first-time buyers at 22%, and months’ supply at its highest since 2015.  AI Summary

The U.S. housing market is building “significant” pent-up demand that could be released if mortgage rates ease, inventory improves and consumer confidence rebounds, National Association of Realtors’ (NAR) chief economist Lawrence Yun told attendees Friday at NAR’s NXT in New Orleans.

Yun contrasted today’s sales environment with the year 2000, noting that the U.S. population has grown from 281 million to 350 million and payroll jobs from 132 million to 159 million over that period. Housing wealth has ballooned from $7 trillion to $35 trillion, yet existing-home sales are tracking near 4 million this year, well below the 5.2 million homes sold in 2000, he said, citing NAR data.

“Affordability is tough, but people’s incomes are rising, and if somehow mortgage rates go down, think about the potential housing demand that could be released to the marketplace,” Yun said.

On the supply side, Yun said inventory has risen as sales have slowed. Months’ supply is now at its highest level since 2015, and the median home took 33 days to go from listing to contract in September, according to NAR.

“Homes are staying on the market a little longer than before,” Yun said. “With longer days on market, you have to be realistic to the market condition. Otherwise, buyers will reject the homes on the market.”

Yun pointed to consumer psychology as a key missing ingredient. Consumer sentiment has fallen to roughly half its 2000 level and is now worse than during the Great Recession, even as the labor market has added jobs.

“If we can boost confidence levels, maybe that can also change the dynamics,” Yun said.

Jessica Lautz, NAR deputy chief economist and vice president of research, presented findings from the trade group’s newly released 2026 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers. First-time buyers made up 22% of all buyers, near the series low, and the median age of first-time buyers held at a record-high 40.

Those numbers underscore how younger households are delaying homeownership, even as they form households and enter peak earning years. For lenders and real estate brokerages, that means fewer entry-level transactions and more emphasis on move-up or equity-rich buyers.

“First-time home buyers are struggling,” Lautz said. “They’re not struggling just with housing affordability. They’re also struggling with rent. They’re struggling with student loan debt, and a rising issue for first-time home buyers is childcare costs.”

Lautz noted that sellers reported staying in their homes for a median of 12 years before selling, the longest tenure on record for the survey.

“The golden handcuffs of low interest rate mortgages is true. People are holding on to those properties for longer periods of time,” she said. “It’s why we need to build more housing inventory—especially affordable housing inventory—for the potential first-time home buyers who are out there.”

Despite technology and direct-to-consumer platforms, use of licensed professionals in transactions remained strong, according to NAR’s survey. A reported 90% of buyers purchased through an agent, the highest share in four decades, and a record 92% of sellers used an agent.

“Sellers want agents to price that home competitively, find that qualified buyer, help with marketing, and make sure they get to the closing table in a time frame that they need to do so,” Lautz said.

For real estate agents and brokers, NAR’s data point to a market where transaction volume is constrained more by affordability, rates and sentiment than by underlying demand. A larger population, more jobs and higher housing wealth suggest that activity could accelerate quickly if mortgage rates retreat and confidence improves.

For lenders, a 22% first-time buyer share and a median first-time buyer age of 40 highlight both near-term headwinds and an eventual backlog of delayed demand. Households that are renting longer and juggling student debt and childcare may respond strongly to any easing in rates, pricing or down payment constraints.

For builders and developers, Lautz’s comments reinforce the policy and business case for adding affordable inventory. With existing owners locked in by sub-4% mortgages and staying put for a median 12 years, new construction remains one of the few levers to ease the entry-level shortage.

Yun’s and Lautz’s remarks come as the industry also adjusts to post-settlement commission rule changes and elevated borrowing costs, making accurate pricing, counseling on affordability and clear communication with consumers more central to winning business in 2027 and beyond.

Hunters Can’t Really Improve Wild Whitetail Genetics. Here’s Why

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It was the year we’d been waiting for at the family deer camp. All summer and fall we pulled trail camera photos of more—and bigger—bucks than had ever been seen on the property. It was a far cry from the early 90s, when the bucks my dad and uncles did spot tended to have a signature narrow spread with tall, skinny tines. Now, nearly 30 years later, we’re finally seeing a caliber of deer we weren’t even sure the property was capable of supporting. It’s taken a lot of intensive habitat improvement and discipline, but it’s working.

Still, there was probably a faster way to get here. It should have taken years, not decades, to improve our whitetail habitat. And there are still things we’re trying to figure out—like how to kill those bucks now that we’ve got them. So, I decided to find out what a lot of whitetail hunters get wrong by talking to some hunters who have been succeeding at the management game for a long time.

Typically, a cull buck is one that deer managers have identified as genetically inferior and should be removed from the wild herd. This might be a deer whose antlers are too small or too funky to keep in the gene pool. Studies show that killing these deer solely on the basis of selective harvest (rather than for the traditional reasons of fun, meat, etc.) is ultimately a waste of a good buck tag.

“Many hunters simply believe that you can influence antler quality through genetics in the wild,” says Kip Adams, chief conservation officer of the National Deer Association (formerly QDMA). “We know very clearly that yes, behind a fence—when you can pick which buck is doing the breeding and which doe is doing the breeding—[you] absolutely can influence the genetics. In the wild, it’s very clear we cannot do that.”

This is for a few reasons. For one, does are contributing half the antler DNA to buck fawns. (If you can identify which does have favorable antler genes from your treestand, please share with the rest of the class.) Also, the rut doesn’t lead to quite as many fawns as you might think.

“It’s not like the biggest buck out there dominates all the does and sires the most fawns,” Adams says, noting that the average U.S. fawn recruitment right now is .59, or less than one fawn per doe. “Research shows that most bucks sire very few fawns during the course of their life. We can’t dominate the gene pool that way.”

“We can’t predict what a buck’s son will have—from an antler standpoint—by looking at a buck’s antlers,” says Adams.

Let’s say that again for the hunters in the back: Just because a buck has big antlers doesn’t mean his offspring will. And just because a buck has small antlers doesn’t mean his offspring will have small antlers. (Part of that goes back to the fact that you can’t tell what kind of antler genes a doe is carrying around.)

That all boils down to Adams’ original point: A hunter cannot influence the genetics of a wild deer herd. Selective buck culling to optimize antler size doesn’t work. So, what can hunters do, if they want to shoot bigger bucks? It’s a simple two-part plan rooted in the classic QDM refrain. First, let deer get older.

“We tend to see and hear about more bigger bucks than ever before because older deer are encompassing a higher percentage of the total buck harvest today than ever before,” Adams says. “Hunters today are letting deer get older at unprecedented levels, and that’s the reason that, across the whitetails’ range, we see, hear of, photograph, and shoot bigger deer than ever before.”

The 2021 Deer Report reveals that during the 2019-20 deer season, 39 percent of all antlered bucks harvested were at least 3.5 years old. That’s the highest national average ever recorded.

The second thing you can do if you want to increase antler size? Increase the available nutrition for deer. This means increasing habitat quality, decreasing the number of deer using the habitat (that is, shooting more does), or both.

It’s all good and well to recommend passing bucks to let them mature, but what does that look like on the ground? Some of the most successful big deer hunters follow this advice, and with good results.

Brothers Brent and Justin Cearlock, two of the Illinois hunters behind the world-record whitetail, enjoy considerable success year after year. Part of this is because of where they live, in the prime big-buck county of eastern Illinois. But it’s also because they pass more bucks than they shoot, even with two buck tags apiece per season.

“I have no intention of shooting a deer younger than five, unless it has just a substantially huge rack,” says Justin, who mostly relies on trail camera history to help him establish age estimates. “I don’t care if I think the deer is four. I’m just going to let him get to five, just to see what happens.”

This past season, Brent made the call on a stud buck that didn’t meet this age threshold. Instead of picking up his bow that morning, he grabbed his phone and shot some video.

“I passed a phenomenal deer—the biggest buck I’ve ever let walk in my life,” Brent says. “He was already in the mid-160s, flirting with the 170-inch mark as a 4-year-old deer. I called him Will, because I didn’t know if I would have the willpower to pass him.”

The guys could only imagine what Will might look like in fall 2021. Brent ended up killing a lower-scoring buck that same day: a deer that was at least seven years old, and that he had history with.

“We’re very fortunate to have access to the properties we do. If you don’t have good properties, you’re not going to shoot big deer,” Brent says. “But we do put a lot of effort into it and we pass deer that most people probably wouldn’t pass. There are a lot of guys that say they’ll pass a deer like that. But when they’re standing there and it’s getting dark, they’re like, I’m shooting that deer.”

Brent didn’t regret his decision—the older buck meant a lot to him—but he couldn’t help feeling torn when he learned a neighbor shot Will a few weeks later.

“Passing a big one comes with risk, and I am genuinely happy for the other hunter to get a great buck like that,” Brent says. “But I’d be lying if I said part of me isn’t sincerely disappointed that we just lost what we thought might be our No. 1 target buck next year.”

Often the decision to pass a deer is fraught because of the property itself. Other hunters with permission and neighboring hunters are just two of the many factors that reduce a deer’s odds of surviving into the next season. And the Cearlocks don’t begrudge anyone who wants to shoot younger bucks: They’ve been those guys themselves, earlier in their hunting careers. But the knowledge that a neighbor might shoot a buck isn’t enough to make them abandon their management strategy in the heat of the moment.

“There’s a lot of things that can happen,” Brent acknowledges. “But if you shoot them, they’re definitely not going to make it to five.”

This past deer season, Rick Dahl secured access to a new whitetail property in central Missouri. Dahl believes the new farm, which is in the same county as the parcels he currently hunts, actually holds better potential for bigger bucks. The new property is promising for a few reasons, including its superior soil quality, fewer neighbors to compete with, and the anecdotal evidence that big bucks are being harvested nearby. The fact that the new property doesn’t have any shooter bucks on it yet doesn’t seem to faze him.

“The food supply is very limited on the new farm,” Dahl says, who is a lifelong Missouri deer hunter and chairman of the NDA board. “[But] it’s in the right area and, if we give the deer there what they need to eat, time will tell whether we’ll be able to produce a bigger-antlered deer there.”

Although the property has fertile soil, Dahl says the food supply is limited because it hasn’t been managed for deer yet. A mature forest canopy has prevented understory growth, and many of the tillable fields were used as cattle pasture. Dahl’s no biologist, but he still knows good soil is important for growing big deer.

Dahl conducted a trail camera survey late last year, which revealed a relatively even age-class distribution of deer—and some clues to support his hunch that nutrition is currently a limiting factor for antler growth. The photos of yearling bucks seem to be mostly spikes, instead of fork-horns. And, with the exception of one deer with an extra point, not a single buck on the property seemed to have more than eight points.

“That was really interesting to me,” Dahl says, who noticed four bucks in the 4- and 5-year-old age class that probably scored 140 inches. “It takes a big 8-pointer to score like that. So, there’s clearly an 8-point genetic thing going on there. I will be really interested to see what transpires over the next three to five years as we begin to introduce more food.”

What confused me about this particular endeavor—investing time and money in a whole new property that’s going to need careful timber, habitat, and food plot management—is that Dahl currently has a 4.5-year-old minimum age limit agreement with his hunting buddies on the excellent properties they’ve already got. Why not just raise the age limit for shooter bucks to five, like the Cearlock brothers?

“I’m at the point where I think five and a half is the right age [for me],” Dahl says. “But I also realize that everybody’s at a different stage in the maturation process that we all go through as hunters. My hunting partner is a perfect example. He hasn’t shot a lot of bucks. And if we say we’re not going to shoot anything less than five years old, well, what are we doing? We just went from five deer out there we can hunt to two. At some point you’ve restricted yourself. And for me, if there’s only one deer on the farm that I’m willing to shoot, then the question becomes, ‘Is it really fun anymore?’ And maybe that deer jumps the fence and gets shot by the neighbor on October 30. What do you do? Are you just done?”

Happily for Dahl, he doesn’t have to pick. He can tinker with the new farm, and see if those nutritional improvements eventually support the 4.5-year-old bucks he’s interested in.

The Northeast isn’t exactly known for dedicated deer management. This is a combination of multiple factors, including high hunter numbers in states like Pennsylvania and poorer soil quality. But Neil Dougherty, the president of NorthCountry Whitetails consulting services, finds the challenges of creating QDM programs for clients that much more rewarding.

“Iowa is like Disneyland—it’s fake,” Dougherty laughs. “It’s such a wonderful place in those areas where deer have the ability to explode like that, but it’s not the everyman’s hunt. It’s such a foreign thing to most people who are trying to play this deer game.”

When Dougherty examines a deer property, he takes into account 17 different factors—from native habitat to huntability—to determine the property’s potential. He can almost always improve a property to help “unlock the genetic potential” of the deer that are there. But he’s not a magician, either.

“There’s a magic intersection with the amount of food that’s produced on a property, and the amount of deer that are there to consume that food,” Dougherty says, “The problem is, in some big woods country, that could be eight deer per square mile. That kind of hunting sucks.”

While almost all the properties Dougherty works on need some serious rehabilitation of the natural habitat, the biggest problem with a new property usually lies not with the land, but the client.

“When I show up, there’s almost always a hunting strategy that’s flawed,” Dougherty says. “Just about every property has a hunting strategy that does not lend itself to the intersection of the hunter coming in contact with 5-year-old deer. [Those hunters] just haven’t been exposed to 5-year-old deer. They don’t understand what it takes to speak the same language.”

If you broke down those flaws, Dougherty estimates poor understanding of the wind is to blame about 40 percent of the time. That’s a big improvement compared to even 10 years ago. Today’s hunters are generally much more sophisticated at hunting downwind, he says, and being aware of steady or swirling wind patterns.

“Access is the biggest issue I’m finding. It’s super hard to get out of these spots—getting in isn’t that hard to do, but getting out in the dark and not blowing up the area? That requires a lot of discipline. And sometimes we’re limited by the physical characteristics of a property.”

While Dougherty is a habitat expert, his consulting work involves much more than recommending where to put food plots and what to plant in them. At the end of the day, he’s teaching clients how to hunt.

“I would encourage people to not get caught up in this whole genetic thing,” Dougherty advises. “Learn what your [deer] potential is on the property you hunt, know what you’re doing when it comes to habitat, and just be happy with what you’re doing.”

So, even if the stars align and you end up with big deer consistently using your property, the question remains: Can you kill one of them?

As it turns out, I couldn’t last fall. When I did finally get the biggest buck on the place in front of me, I choked. Painful lesson though it was, it was a reminder that managing for mature deer isn’t like shooting fish in a barrel. Sure, it can be a lot easier when you’ve got 5,000 acres and no neighbors or public land for miles around. But that’s unrealistic for most hunters. Even some basic habitat work and personal discipline can improve the number and quality of deer where you hunt.

Agent instructions: in the repo or in the tool?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are two places to put the standing rules you want a coding agent to follow: in a file in the repo, or built into the tool that runs the agent. I've ended up with both, and they trade off differently.

In the repo, the rules are versioned. They show up in pull requests, the whole team sees them, and they travel with a clone. The cost is that you write and maintain them per project, and they're only as good as the last person who edited them.

In the tool, they work in every project and for every agent with no setup. The cost is that they're invisible: nobody reviews a diff of them, and they can clash with how a particular repo does things.

Styx does the second kind. It builds in guidance from a few public repos: Ponytail for coding, Impeccable for interfaces, design-critique for reviewing a design, and architecture-designer for system design. Only the core rules are included, each applies only to that kind of work, and your project's own design system and rules come first.

That last part is the rule I'd defend: when the two disagree, the repo wins, because it knows the project and the tool doesn't.

Do you agree, or do you let the tool override the repo?

Your Mac has been keeping a file on your family

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since macOS 13 shipped in 2022, every Mac has run two daemons whose only job is to build a graph about you. Who you are, who you know, how those people relate to you and to each other, how you communicate, where you go, what you do and when. The daemons are called knowledgeconstructiond and intelligenceplatformd, and the closest thing Apple has ever documented about them is a two-sentence man page.

A security duo going by The Bishop and The Knight took the system apart in October and published the anatomy. The graph lives in ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform, fifty-one plaintext SQLite databases protected by macOS's Full Disk Access permission. The core one, graph.db, is a triple store: 181 entity classes, 278 predicates, and a confidence score on every row. What goes into it is basically everything the OS can see. Contacts. Photos, with faces. Calendar titles and attendees. Family Sharing, including the age of every member. The emergency contact in your Medical ID. Events and people Siri Suggestions digs out of your mail and messages. Apps donated through App Intents. Beside the graph sit tables for who appears with whom in your photos, a communication log without content, location and movement, and a stream of device signals: screen locked, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging, ambient light.

Then there is the part the HN headline picked up: a pair of XGBoost classifiers, shipped as CoreML models, that decide who counts as family. They read 144 features and output a probability for seventeen relationship columns per person, recomputed every day. Twenty of the features are identity: same last name, shared home address, an iCloud Family flag, age difference. Forty are communication patterns. Eighty-four come from photographs.

One shipped feature name is worth reading twice: photosPersonPosteriorProbabilityGivenSaturdayNight. A real input to a real classifier, and its job is deciding whether someone is your sister.

The scores look mundane until you look at what produced them. A mother scored 0.960, the ceiling of that column. Nobody typed it. The post counts nine independent signals that land on the same person: the partner column, a composite score, a household entity, the Family Sharing token, an iCloud flag, top twelve-month contact, a Photos face cluster, a Contacts record, a reachable handle. The model joined them and concluded "partner" for someone the owner had never labeled.

The joins are where it stops being about you. From one store, the authors worked out that there is a child in the household and roughly how old, which daycare the child attends along with both phone numbers, where the family will be and between which hours on a given Thursday, and which password manager is installed. A relationship is intelligence one level above the rows it was inferred from.

Deletion does not fully retract any of it. The live graph drops a contact, but a second table, expired_stable_graph, keeps the retracted rows with their confidence and sources, about forty-eight hours by the authors' measurement, longer on their own machines. The scorecard still scored two people a day after the graph had dropped them, people it knew only from a calendar invitation.

The authors also run a thought experiment that applies to any personalized system: what happens when this file ends up in the wrong hands, paired with a local model that has had its guardrails stripped. They call the graph a privacy single point of failure, and they play the scenario out in a second post.

To be fair to Apple: everything happens on device. The databases sit behind the Full Disk Access permission, so other apps cannot read them without the user approving it. Apple's researchers published a paper on the graph techniques back in 2023. And the same store exists on iOS, where a forensics tool called iLEAPP has been reading it since September.

The fair criticism is narrower and harder to wave off. The depth of the inference is undocumented. The kinship classifier is mentioned nowhere. And the graph does not just hold your data; it holds data about everyone who has ever emailed you, appeared in your photos, or been invited to a calendar event, and none of those people were asked.

The authors open-sourced a dashboard called iKnow that reads your own graph locally, if you want to see the file your Mac keeps on you.
